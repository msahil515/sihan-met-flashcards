1Schools of psychology: structuralism, functionalism, cognitive approach
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q11, Q92, Q45

From scratch

Psychology became a separate science in 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt opened the first formal psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig. Wundt called his system voluntarism and studied immediate conscious experience with trained introspection (careful self-observation under controlled stimulus conditions). Everything that follows in the "schools" story is a series of reactions: each new school defined itself by what it rejected in the previous one.

Structuralism (Edward Bradford Titchener, Wundt's English student, at Cornell from 1892) asked what the mind is made of. Like a chemist breaking water into hydrogen and oxygen, Titchener broke consciousness into three elements: sensations (the elements of perception), images (the elements of ideas) and affections (the elements of feeling). Each element had attributes such as quality, intensity, duration and clearness. His method was strict analytic introspection, and observers had to avoid the stimulus error (reporting the object, "an apple", rather than the raw sensations, "red, round, shiny"). Structuralism died with Titchener in 1927 because introspection was unreliable (two labs could not agree), it ignored children, animals and abnormal minds, and it had no practical use.

Functionalism (William James, Principles of Psychology, 1890, Harvard; then John Dewey, James Rowland Angell and Harvey Carr at the University of Chicago) asked what the mind is for. Inspired by Darwin, functionalists treated consciousness as an adaptive tool that helps the organism survive. They described consciousness as a continuous stream, not a heap of elements, and they widened psychology to include animals, children, individual differences, mental testing and education. Dewey's 1896 paper on the reflex arc is often called the founding document of the Chicago school. Functionalism never vanished; it was absorbed into applied, educational and evolutionary psychology.

After functionalism came behaviourism (John B. Watson, 1913 manifesto), which threw out consciousness entirely and studied only observable stimulus and response, treating the mind as a "black box". The cognitive approach was the reply to behaviourism, in the "cognitive revolution" of the 1950s and 1960s: Miller's "magical number seven" (1956), Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior, Newell and Simon's computer models, and Ulric Neisser's book Cognitive Psychology (1967), which named the field. Neisser is called the father of cognitive psychology.

The cognitive approach studies the internal mental processes between stimulus and response: attention, perception, memory, language, thinking, problem solving and decision making. Its core metaphor is information processing: input, encoding, storage, retrieval, output, just as in a computer. Later cognitive models include connectionism or parallel distributed processing (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986), in which knowledge is stored as weighted links among many simple, neuron-like nodes that are activated in parallel; and Collins and Loftus's spreading-activation semantic network (1975). Example: a cognitive psychologist explains a phobia by the person's catastrophic interpretation of a racing heart, while a behaviourist explains it by a conditioned association.

MCQ traps

  • Titchener vs James: Titchener is the rival functionalism was reacting against, so he is the classic distractor on any functionalism question.
  • Wundt vs Titchener: Wundt founded experimental psychology (Leipzig, 1879); the label "structuralism" is Titchener's. If the option says "founder of structuralism", pick Titchener.
  • G. Stanley Hall founded the APA (1892) and the first American lab (Johns Hopkins, 1883), but he is not the functionalism answer.
  • Cognitive vs psychoanalysis: both deal with what is "inside", but psychoanalysis is about unconscious drives; cognitive psychology is about information processing.
  • Network vs connectionist model: "network model" sounds right in plain English, but the textbook term for interconnected nodes with parallel processing is connectionist (PDP).

2History: tabula rasa, stream of consciousness, first Indian psychology lab ★
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q27, Q73, Q42

From scratch

Tabula rasa ("blank slate") is the central claim of British empiricism. John Locke, in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), argued that the newborn mind is like "white paper, void of all characters", with no innate ideas. All knowledge comes from experience, through two routes: sensation (input from the external world) and reflection (the mind observing its own operations). Simple ideas combine into complex ones by association, an idea that led to Hume, Hartley, James Mill and finally to behaviourism and learning theory. Locke is therefore the classic "nurture" philosopher.

The opposing camp is rationalism / nativism: Plato (knowledge is innate and "recollected"), Rene Descartes (innate ideas such as God and geometry; also mind-body dualism and "cogito ergo sum") and Immanuel Kant (the mind brings innate categories, such as space, time and causality, that organise experience). Kant's view later fed into Gestalt psychology. Example for recall: a Lockean would say a child fears snakes only after a bad experience or being told; a nativist would say some readiness to fear snakes is built in.

Stream of consciousness was coined by William James in The Principles of Psychology (1890), chapter "The Stream of Thought". James rejected Wundt and Titchener's picture of consciousness as a chain of separable elements. He said consciousness is: (1) personal (every thought belongs to someone), (2) always changing (no state recurs exactly), (3) sensibly continuous (it flows like a river, without gaps even after sleep), (4) about objects other than itself, and (5) selective (it chooses among objects, which is attention). He also distinguished "substantive" resting places and "transitive" flights between them. The phrase was later borrowed by novelists (Joyce, Woolf) for a writing style.

First Indian psychology laboratory and department: psychology in India began at the University of Calcutta. Under Vice-Chancellor Sir Ashutosh Mookerjee and philosopher Brajendra Nath Seal, an experimental psychology laboratory was set up in 1915, run by Narendra Nath Sengupta (trained under Munsterberg at Harvard). The first independent Department of Psychology followed in 1916. Girindrasekhar Bose joined it, earned the first Indian doctorate (D.Sc.) in psychology, became the first head of the independent department (1929), founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society (1922) and corresponded with Freud. Sengupta also founded the Indian Journal of Psychology and the Indian Psychological Association (1925) with Bose. Mysore (M.V. Gopalaswamy, 1924) and Patna followed.

3Perception theorists: Titchener and Bruner's New Look ★
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q9, Q10

From scratch

Two very different ideas of what perception is get tested together. The first is structuralist. Edward B. Titchener (A Text-Book of Psychology, 1909-1910) held that consciousness is built from three elements: sensations, images and affections. A perception, for him, is not a single element but a complex of sensory and imaginal elements, fused and given meaning by context. This is his context theory of meaning: when you see a clock, the bare visual sensations are joined by images (the remembered sound of ticking, the idea of time), and that added context is what makes the sensations "mean" a clock. Example: hearing a few notes and instantly "seeing" the singer in your mind is sensation plus image, hence perception.

The second is the New Look in perception, launched in the late 1940s by Jerome Bruner with Leo Postman and Cecile Goodman. Its claim: perception is not a passive copy of the stimulus; it is actively shaped by needs, values, motives, expectations and personality. The perceiver brings "hypotheses" to every stimulus (Bruner's later hypothesis theory and perceptual readiness, 1957).

The famous study is Bruner and Goodman (1947), "Value and need as organizing factors in perception". Ten-year-old children adjusted a circle of light to match the size of coins (1 to 50 cents) and of neutral cardboard discs of the same sizes. Coins were judged larger than discs, more valuable coins were overestimated more, and poor children overestimated more than rich children. So economic deprivation (need) affects perceived size. Related New Look work: Bruner and Postman on perceptual defence (taboo words need longer exposure to be recognised) and the anomalous playing cards (a red six of spades) showing expectancy; Schafer and Murphy (1943) showing that reward makes one face of an ambiguous figure-ground profile more likely to be seen; McClelland and Atkinson showing hungry subjects "see" more food in ambiguous slides.

The needs-shape-perception idea is the motivational side of top-down processing (the perceiver's knowledge and state guide what is seen), the opposite of bottom-up, data-driven processing (Gibson's direct perception, feature detectors). Critics later showed some New Look effects were response bias, but the core idea survives in research on motivated perception.

4Gestalt laws of perceptual organisation
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q52

From scratch

Gestalt psychology was founded in Germany by Max Wertheimer with Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Kohler. The starting point was Wertheimer's 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon: two lights flashed in quick succession are seen as one light moving, a motion that exists in neither light alone. Their slogan: "the whole is different from (more than) the sum of its parts." This was a direct attack on Titchener's elementism. "Gestalt" means form or configuration. In 1923 Wertheimer listed the principles by which the brain automatically groups elements into wholes.

Figure-ground is the most basic organisation: we split a scene into a figure (the object, which seems in front, has a shape and contour) and a ground (the shapeless background behind it). Edgar Rubin (1915) showed it with the reversible vase-faces figure: you see either two faces or a vase, never both at once. The grouping laws are: Proximity: elements near each other in space or time are grouped (six dots spaced as three pairs look like three pairs). Similarity: elements alike in colour, size or shape are grouped (alternating rows of red and black dots look like rows). Closure: we fill gaps to perceive a complete figure (a circle with a gap is still seen as a circle; the Kanizsa triangle). Good continuation / continuity: we follow smooth, continuous lines rather than abrupt turns (two crossing curves are seen as two lines, not four pieces). Common fate: elements moving together in the same direction are grouped (a flock of birds, a "wave" in a stadium). Pragnanz (law of good figure, simplicity) is the master law: we perceive the simplest, most stable, most regular organisation possible (overlapping rings are seen as five circles, the Olympic logo, not complex odd shapes).

Later additions (Stephen Palmer, 1990s) are common region (items inside the same boundary group) and connectedness (physically linked items group). The Gestaltists explained these laws by isomorphism: brain fields mirror the structure of experience. Their influence extends to Kohler's insight learning in chimpanzees (Sultan and the sticks), Lewin's field theory, and Gestalt therapy (Fritz Perls, which is a separate thing).

Example from real life: a page of text is readable because proximity groups letters into words, and closure lets you read faded print. Logo designers use closure (the WWF panda) and figure-ground (the arrow in the FedEx logo).

MCQ traps

  • "Close together" means proximity, not closure. The word "close" in the stem tempts you to closure; closure is about completing gaps.
  • "In space and time" is the proximity wording; time is included (two taps close in time are heard as a pair).
  • Similarity vs common fate: if the shared feature is motion direction, it is common fate, even though moving together is a kind of "similarity".
  • Pragnanz is the overall law, sometimes called "law of good figure" or "law of simplicity"; do not mistake it for closure.
  • Gestalt founders vs Gestalt therapy: Wertheimer, Koffka, Kohler are perception; Fritz Perls is therapy. Rubin (figure-ground) was Danish, not a founder.
  • Phi phenomenon vs beta movement: both are apparent motion; Wertheimer's 1912 paper is the phi phenomenon.

5Attention: the Stroop effect
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q33

From scratch

John Ridley Stroop (1935, Journal of Experimental Psychology) showed people colour words printed in coloured ink. In the key condition the word and ink conflict (the word RED printed in blue ink), and the task is to name the ink colour ("blue") and ignore the word. People are markedly slower and make more errors on these incongruent trials than on neutral trials (coloured rectangles or XXXX) or congruent trials (RED in red ink). The delay is the Stroop interference effect. Reading the word aloud while ignoring the ink is barely affected, an asymmetry that matters.

The explanation is about attention and cognitive control. Reading is a highly practised, automatic process: fast, effortless and hard to suppress. Colour naming is slower and more controlled. When the two point to different responses, the automatic word response must be inhibited while attention is held on the relevant dimension (ink). Models include the speed-of-processing account (the word arrives first and must be suppressed), the automaticity account (Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977, automatic vs controlled processing), and Cohen, Dunbar and McClelland's (1990) connectionist model, where stronger word pathways win unless top-down attention boosts the colour pathway. So the Stroop effect is the classic demonstration of selective attention failing against automatic processing.

Clinically, the Stroop is a standard test of executive function: response inhibition, interference control and selective attention. It relies on the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the anterior cingulate cortex (conflict monitoring). Performance is impaired in frontal lobe lesions, ADHD, schizophrenia and dementia. Versions include the Golden Stroop Color and Word Test and the Victoria Stroop. The emotional Stroop asks people to name ink colours of emotional words: anxious patients are slower on threat words ("spider" for spider phobics, "trauma" words in PTSD), which shows attentional bias toward threat. Other variants: numerical Stroop, spatial Stroop.

Example: in an exam hall, if a sign says "EXIT" in red and an arrow points the other way, the momentary hesitation you feel is Stroop-like conflict between automatic reading and the task you are attending to.

  • "Memory retrieval" is tempting because word meaning is retrieved from memory, but no recall task is involved.
  • "Language development" is only there because reading is used; the effect measures attention in literate adults.
  • If the options include executive function / response inhibition and attention, both are defensible; in basic MCQs "selective attention" is the textbook answer.
  • The effect is strongest when naming ink, not when reading the word; a reversed stem is a trap.

6Memory: serial position, interference, amnesia, context-dependent memory
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q71, Q95, Q76, Q63, Q82

From scratch

Serial position effect. If you hear a list of 15 to 20 words once and recall them in any order (free recall), accuracy plotted by position forms a U-shaped curve. The first items are recalled well (primacy effect) and so are the last few (recency effect); the middle sags. The curve was first noted by Ebbinghaus (1885) and analysed by Murdock (1962); Glanzer and Cunitz (1966) separated the two parts. Primacy reflects long-term memory: early items get more rehearsal. Slowing the presentation rate improves primacy only. Recency reflects short-term memory: the last items are still in the buffer. A 30-second distractor task (counting backwards) abolishes recency and leaves primacy intact. This double dissociation supported Atkinson and Shiffrin's (1968) multi-store model.

Interference theory says forgetting happens because memories compete, not just because traces fade (decay, Ebbinghaus's curve of forgetting). Retroactive interference: new learning disrupts recall of old learning (after learning your new phone number you cannot recall the old one). Proactive interference: old learning disrupts recall of new learning (you keep writing last year's date in January). Mnemonic: "pro" = forward (old pushes forward onto new); "retro" = backward (new reaches back onto old). Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924) showed less forgetting after sleep than after waking activity, evidence for interference over decay. Underwood (1957) showed proactive interference explains much forgetting in the lab. Release from PI (Wickens) occurs when the category of the material changes.

Amnesia is named by its direction relative to the onset (injury, illness). Anterograde amnesia: inability to form new long-term memories after onset. The classic case is H.M. (Henry Molaison), who lost both hippocampi in 1953 surgery (Scoville and Milner, 1957); he could not learn new facts but kept old memories, short-term memory and procedural learning (mirror drawing). Retrograde amnesia: loss of memories formed before onset, usually graded so that recent memories are lost and older ones spared (Ribot's law), as after head injury or ECT. Korsakoff syndrome (chronic alcohol use, thiamine/B1 deficiency, damage to mammillary bodies and dorsomedial thalamus) gives severe anterograde plus some retrograde amnesia and confabulation, usually following Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia). Dissociative amnesia (DSM-5-TR) is psychological, not organic: loss of autobiographical information after trauma.

Context-dependent memory is a case of Tulving's encoding specificity principle (Tulving and Thomson, 1973): recall is best when cues at retrieval match those present at encoding. Godden and Baddeley (1975) had divers learn words underwater or on land; recall was about 40% better when learning and testing places matched. State-dependent memory is the internal version (Goodwin and colleagues, alcohol; mood-dependent memory, Bower). Example: you forget why you walked into the kitchen, then remember once you go back to the bedroom.

MCQ traps

  • Direction swap: "interference from new information" is retroactive; students pick proactive because "new" sounds forward-looking.
  • "Inability to retrieve old memories" is retrograde; it is the decoy on any anterograde question.
  • Made-up labels like "list position effect" or "order recall effect" sound plausible; the recognised names are serial position and primacy-recency.
  • Principle of congruity (Osgood and Tannenbaum) is an attitude-change theory; it is not about matching study and test rooms.
  • Korsakoff vs Wernicke: Wernicke's is the acute triad; Korsakoff's is the chronic amnesic stage. Korsakoff's hallmark is anterograde amnesia with confabulation.
  • The hippocampus, not the amygdala, is the "new memories" structure (IHBAS Q43 tests this too).

7Semantic network models of memory
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q45

From scratch

Semantic memory (Tulving, 1972) is your store of general knowledge: facts, word meanings and concepts, with no record of when or where you learned them ("a canary is a bird"). It contrasts with episodic memory (personal, time-and-place-stamped events). Network models try to explain how this knowledge is organised and searched. The core idea: every concept is a node, and concepts are joined by links (associations). Remembering means moving through, or activating, this web.

Collins and Quillian (1969): the hierarchical network model. Concepts sit in a tree arranged by category level: ANIMAL at the top, BIRD and FISH below it, CANARY and OSTRICH below BIRD. Links are of two kinds: "is a" links (canary is a bird) and property links (bird can fly; animal has skin). The model assumes cognitive economy: a property is stored only once, at the highest node it applies to ("has skin" is stored at ANIMAL, not repeated at CANARY). Their evidence was the sentence verification task: people verified "A canary can sing" (same level) faster than "A canary can fly" (one level up) faster than "A canary has skin" (two levels up). Reaction time grew with the number of links traversed.

The model failed on two findings. (1) The typicality effect: "A robin is a bird" is verified faster than "A penguin is a bird", though both are one link away. (2) Some category statements violate the hierarchy: "A chicken is an animal" is verified faster than "A chicken is a bird", even though ANIMAL is further up. Pure link-counting cannot explain either.

Collins and Loftus (1975): spreading activation model. They dropped the strict hierarchy. Concepts form a looser web where link length reflects semantic relatedness (short link from FIRE ENGINE to RED and TRUCK, longer to SUNSET). When a node is activated, activation spreads outward along links, weakening with distance and time. Closely related, typical items are reached quickly, which explains typicality. It also explains semantic priming: Meyer and Schvaneveldt (1971) showed people decide that "nurse" is a word faster after seeing "doctor" than after "bread", because "doctor" has already sent activation to "nurse". Example: hearing "ambulance" makes "hospital" and "siren" pop to mind faster.

Connectionist / Parallel Distributed Processing (PDP) model (Rumelhart, McClelland and the PDP group, 1986). Knowledge is a network of simple neuron-like units joined by weighted connections, but a concept is not one node: it is a pattern of activation distributed across many units, and memory lives in the connection weights. Processing runs in parallel, learning means adjusting weights (e.g. backpropagation), and damage produces graceful degradation rather than total loss of one fact. A rival non-network account is the feature comparison model (Smith, Shoben and Rips, 1974), which compares lists of defining and characteristic features.

MCQ traps

  • "Network model" vs "connectionist model": both sound right for "nodes in a network". IHBAS keyed connectionist, the formal textbook name for interconnected-node, parallel architecture. If the stem says "hierarchical" or "cognitive economy", the answer is Collins and Quillian instead.
  • Spreading activation belongs to Collins and Loftus (1975), not Collins and Quillian (1969). The pair names share "Collins", so examiners swap them.
  • Typicality effect is evidence against the hierarchical model and for spreading activation; do not list it as support for Collins and Quillian.
  • Semantic vs episodic: network models are about semantic memory (Tulving). "Remembering your 10th birthday" is episodic and is not what these models describe.
  • "Associative model" is a loose historical idea (Aristotle, British associationists), not the formal node-and-weight model.
  • Priming is unconscious and implicit; it is the classic experimental support for spreading activation, not for levels of processing (Craik and Lockhart, 1972).

8Classical vs operant conditioning, extinction
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q65, Q77, Q100, Q7

From scratch

Classical (Pavlovian, respondent) conditioning was discovered by Ivan Pavlov (Nobel 1904 for digestion; conditioning work published 1927, Conditioned Reflexes). An unconditioned stimulus (UCS), food, naturally produces an unconditioned response (UCR), salivation. A neutral stimulus, a bell, is repeatedly presented just before the food. After pairings the bell becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that alone produces salivation, now called the conditioned response (CR). The learner is passive: an involuntary reflex comes under the control of a new signal. Best timing is delay conditioning (CS starts before and overlaps UCS); backward conditioning (UCS before CS) works poorly. Watson and Rayner (1920) used it to condition fear in Little Albert (white rat + loud noise). Rescorla (1968) showed what matters is the CS predicting the UCS (contingency), not mere pairing; Garcia showed taste aversion can form in one trial with a long delay (biological preparedness).

Operant (instrumental) conditioning starts with Thorndike's Law of Effect (1898, cats in puzzle boxes): responses followed by satisfying consequences are stamped in. B.F. Skinner (1938, The Behavior of Organisms) systematised it with the Skinner box. Here the learner is active: a voluntary response is strengthened or weakened by its consequence. Reinforcement increases behaviour, punishment decreases it. Positive means adding a stimulus, negative means removing one. So negative reinforcement (removing a headache by taking a pill) increases pill-taking; it is NOT punishment. Complex behaviour is built by shaping (reinforcing successive approximations). Skinner's philosophy is radical behaviourism: even thoughts are behaviour controlled by environmental contingencies.

Schedules of reinforcement: continuous reinforcement gives fastest learning; partial schedules are fixed ratio (FR, piecework), variable ratio (VR, slot machine), fixed interval (FI, scalloped pattern, monthly salary) and variable interval (VI, checking email). VR gives the highest, steadiest responding and the greatest resistance to extinction (the partial reinforcement extinction effect).

Extinction means the learned response weakens when its support is removed. Classical: the CS is presented repeatedly without the UCS (bell, no food), so salivation fades. Operant: the response is no longer reinforced (lever-pressing, no pellet), so it fades, often after a brief extinction burst. Extinction is new inhibitory learning, not erasure: after a rest the response returns (spontaneous recovery), it can return in a new context (renewal, Bouton) or after the UCS alone (reinstatement). Related phenomena: generalisation (responding to similar stimuli) and discrimination (responding only to the trained one).

Clinical use. Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) for OCD (Victor Meyer, 1966; refined by Edna Foa): the patient faces the obsessional cue and is prevented from ritualising, so anxiety habituates and the cue-anxiety link extinguishes; the negatively reinforced ritual also extinguishes. Systematic desensitisation (Wolpe, 1958) instead uses reciprocal inhibition / counterconditioning: relaxation is paired with a fear hierarchy. Token economy (Ayllon and Azrin, 1968) is operant.

9Sheldon's somatotypes and Rotter's locus of control
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q8, Q58

From scratch

Type theories sort people into discrete categories. The oldest is Hippocrates (about 400 BCE) and Galen's four humours: sanguine (blood, cheerful), choleric (yellow bile, irritable), melancholic (black bile, sad) and phlegmatic (phlegm, sluggish). Ernst Kretschmer (1921, Physique and Character) linked body build to psychosis: pyknic (short, round) with manic-depressive illness, asthenic / leptosomic (thin) with schizophrenia, athletic (muscular), and dysplastic (mixed, disproportionate).

William Sheldon (1940, Varieties of Human Physique; 1942, Varieties of Temperament) refined this using photographs of thousands of male college students. He named three body components after the embryonic germ layers and paired each with a temperament:

  • Endomorph (soft, round, digestive viscera dominate) → viscerotonia: relaxed, sociable, loves food, comfort and affection, even-tempered.
  • Mesomorph (muscular, broad-shouldered) → somatotonia: assertive, energetic, adventurous, loves physical activity and dominance, noisy, callous, competitive, aggressive.
  • Ectomorph (thin, fragile, large nervous system relative to mass) → cerebrotonia: restrained, introverted, hypersensitive, private, intellectual, prefers solitude.

Each person is rated 1 to 7 on all three, so a somatotype is a three-digit code: 7-1-1 is an extreme endomorph, 1-7-1 an extreme mesomorph, 4-4-4 balanced. Sheldon reported correlations near .80, but his ratings were made by the same people who knew the physiques (rater bias); later studies found weak links. Remember it as historically important, scientifically weak. Modern "types" you should keep separate: Jung's introvert/extravert (1921), Friedman and Rosenman's Type A / Type B (1959, coronary-prone; hostility is the toxic element), Type C (cancer-prone, suppressed emotion), Denollet's Type D (distressed: negative affectivity plus social inhibition).

Julian Rotter: locus of control. Rotter's social learning theory (1954) says behaviour potential depends on expectancy (will this act get reinforced?) and reinforcement value (how much do I want the reward?) in a given psychological situation. Locus of control (Rotter, 1966) is a generalised expectancy about who controls reinforcement. Internals believe outcomes follow from their own effort and ability ("I failed because I did not study"). Externals believe outcomes depend on luck, fate, chance or powerful others ("the paper was unfair"). It is a continuum, not a type. Measured by the Rotter I-E Scale (1966): 29 forced-choice pairs, 23 scored plus 6 fillers; higher score = more external. Internals tend to seek information, resist influence, achieve more and cope better; externality is linked to depression and helplessness.

Extensions: Levenson's IPC scale splits externality into Powerful others and Chance; Wallston's Multidimensional Health Locus of Control applies it to health behaviour.

10Positive psychology: savoring, broaden-and-build, flow ★
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q13, Q56

From scratch

Positive psychology was launched by Martin Seligman in his 1998 APA presidential address and the 2000 American Psychologist special issue with Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. It studies what makes life worth living: positive emotions, positive traits and positive institutions, rather than only disorder. Key frameworks: PERMA (Seligman, 2011: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment); the VIA classification (Peterson and Seligman, 2004: 24 character strengths under 6 virtues); hedonic well-being (Diener's subjective well-being: life satisfaction plus high positive and low negative affect) vs eudaimonic well-being (Ryff's six dimensions, e.g. purpose, autonomy).

Savoring (Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff, 2007, Savoring: A New Model of Positive Experience) is the capacity to attend to, appreciate and enhance positive experiences. It works across three time frames: anticipation (savoring the future: looking forward to a holiday), savoring the moment (present), and reminiscence (past: re-living a happy memory). Four savoring processes: basking (receiving praise, pride), thanksgiving (gratitude), marveling (awe), luxuriating (physical pleasure). Strategies that amplify it: sharing with others, memory building, sharpening the senses, absorption, self-congratulation; "kill-joy thinking" dampens it. Example: eating a mango slowly, noticing its taste, telling a friend about it.

Broaden-and-build theory (Barbara Fredrickson, 1998; fully stated 2001). Negative emotions narrow the thought-action repertoire to specific survival actions (fear → flee). Positive emotions do the opposite: they broaden momentary thought-action repertoires (joy → urge to play; interest → explore; contentment → savor and integrate; love → all of these within relationships). Over time this broadening builds durable personal resources: physical, intellectual, social and psychological (resilience, skills, friendships). Two corollaries: the undoing hypothesis (Fredrickson and Levenson, 1998: positive emotions speed cardiovascular recovery after negative arousal) and the upward spiral (resources produce more positive emotion). Her 2.9:1 "positivity ratio" (Fredrickson and Losada, 2005) was mathematically discredited (Brown, Sokal and Friedman, 2013); the broaden-and-build core stands.

Flow (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, 1975, Beyond Boredom and Anxiety; 1990, Flow) is a state of complete absorption in an activity. It occurs when both challenge and skill are high and balanced. Features: clear goals, immediate feedback, intense concentration, merging of action and awareness, a sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, distorted sense of time, and an autotelic quality (the activity is its own reward). Off-balance states: high challenge with low skill = anxiety; low challenge with high skill = boredom or relaxation; both low = apathy. Example: a sitar player lost in a raga, or you in a mock test pitched at exactly your level.

11Stress: Lazarus transactional model, appraisal, job stressors ★
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q89, Q40, Q44

From scratch

Three classic ways of thinking about stress. (1) Stress as response: Walter Cannon (1915/1932) described the fight-or-flight response (sympathetic arousal, adrenaline) and coined homeostasis. Hans Selye (1936; The Stress of Life, 1956) described the General Adaptation Syndrome: alarm (shock and countershock), resistance (sustained adaptation, cortisol via the HPA axis), exhaustion (resources depleted, illness). He separated eustress (good) from distress. (2) Stress as stimulus: Holmes and Rahe (1967) Social Readjustment Rating Scale, 43 life events scored in Life Change Units (death of spouse = 100); Kanner, Lazarus and colleagues (1981) argued daily hassles predict health better than major events.

(3) Stress as transaction: Richard Lazarus (1966, Psychological Stress and the Coping Process) and Lazarus and Susan Folkman (1984, Stress, Appraisal, and Coping). Stress is neither the event nor the response but a relationship between person and environment that the person appraises as taxing or exceeding their resources and endangering well-being. Lazarus showed this in film studies (Speisman et al., 1964): the same disturbing film caused less arousal when the soundtrack framed it intellectually or with denial. Appraisal, not the event, decides stress.

Primary appraisal asks "Is this relevant to me, and what is at stake?" The event is judged irrelevant, benign-positive, or stressful; stressful appraisals take three forms: harm/loss (damage already done), threat (anticipated harm) and challenge (potential for gain or growth). Secondary appraisal asks "What can I do about it? Do I have the resources to cope?" It evaluates coping options, resources (skills, money, social support) and likely outcomes. Reappraisal updates both as new information arrives. "Primary" and "secondary" do not mean first and second in time or importance; they interact. Example: an IHBAS entrance next month. Primary: "This matters, it could decide my career" (threat or challenge). Secondary: "I have mocks, notes and two months" (adequate resources, so lower stress).

Coping follows appraisal: problem-focused coping changes the situation (making a study schedule) and fits controllable stressors; emotion-focused coping changes how you feel (relaxation, reappraisal, seeking comfort, avoidance) and fits uncontrollable ones. Allostasis (Sterling and Eyer, 1988; McEwen, 1998) is "stability through change": the brain anticipates demands and adjusts stress hormones (HPA axis cortisol, sympathetic adrenaline); chronic wear from this is allostatic load.

Job stressors (Robbins' organisational behaviour model). Organisational sources: task demands (workload, time pressure, job design), role demands (role conflict, role ambiguity, role overload), interpersonal demands, organisational structure and leadership, life-stage of the organisation. Environmental sources: economic, political and technological uncertainty. Individual sources: family problems, personal economic problems, personality. Models: Karasek's (1979) job demand-control model (high demand plus low control = high strain); Siegrist's (1996) effort-reward imbalance; Maslach's burnout (emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation/cynicism, reduced personal accomplishment).

12Applied psychology: psychologist vs psychiatrist, I/O, biopsychosocial model, reading
General / Cognitive · IHBAS 2024 Q1, Q31, Q47, Q61, Q70

From scratch

Clinical psychology is the branch devoted to the assessment, diagnosis, treatment and prevention of mental disorders, combining science and practice. Lightner Witmer opened the first psychological clinic (University of Pennsylvania, 1896) and coined the term "clinical psychology" (1907, journal The Psychological Clinic). The scientist-practitioner (Boulder) model dates from 1949.

Psychologist vs psychiatrist. A psychiatrist is a medical doctor (in India MBBS plus MD/DNB Psychiatry) who diagnoses, does physical workups and prescribes medication, ECT and other physical treatments. A clinical psychologist is not a medical doctor; in India they hold an RCI-recognised professional qualification (traditionally M.Phil Clinical Psychology, now also the professional MA/MSc route) and must be registered with the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI Act, 1992; recognised as a mental health professional under the Mental Health Care Act, 2017). They do psychometric assessment (IQ, personality, neuropsychological testing) and psychotherapy (CBT, behaviour therapy, etc.), and typically cannot prescribe (a few US states such as New Mexico, 2002, and Louisiana, 2004, allow specially trained psychologists limited prescription rights). Other team members: psychiatric social worker, psychiatric nurse, counselling psychologist (milder adjustment problems).

Industrial-organisational (I/O) psychology applies psychology to the workplace to optimise performance and well-being. Founders: Hugo Münsterberg (Psychology and Industrial Efficiency, 1913, father of industrial psychology) and Walter Dill Scott (advertising, selection). The Hawthorne studies (Western Electric, 1924 to 1932, Elton Mayo) showed social factors and being observed change productivity (Hawthorne effect). Taylor's scientific management (1911) was engineering, not psychology. Areas: personnel psychology (selection, training, appraisal), organisational psychology (motivation, leadership, culture, job stress) and human factors/ergonomics.

Biopsychosocial model: George Engel (1977, Science, "The need for a new medical model: a challenge for biomedicine"), building on general systems theory. He attacked the biomedical model's reductionism and argued that illness arises from interacting biological (genes, neurotransmitters, physical illness), psychological (thoughts, emotions, coping, personality) and social (family, culture, poverty, stigma, support) factors. Example: depression after job loss in someone with a family history and poor support. It is the umbrella under which the diathesis-stress model (Zubin and Spring, 1977, for schizophrenia) sits.

Cognition behind reading. Reading is built on spoken language. Children first have oral vocabulary and grammar, then develop phonological awareness (hearing and manipulating phonemes) and learn the alphabetic principle (letters map onto speech sounds). The Simple View of Reading (Gough and Tunmer, 1986) states Reading comprehension = Decoding x Language comprehension. Coltheart's dual-route model has a lexical route (whole-word recognition, needed for irregular words like "yacht") and a sublexical grapheme-phoneme route (needed for non-words); damage gives surface vs phonological dyslexia. Phonological deficit is the core of specific learning disorder with impairment in reading.

13Festinger's cognitive dissonance
Social · IHBAS 2024 Q12, Q62

From scratch

Leon Festinger (1957), A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance. Dissonance is an unpleasant state of psychological tension (a drive state, like hunger) that arises when a person holds two or more cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, knowledge of one's own behaviour) that are psychologically inconsistent. "I smoke" and "smoking causes cancer" are dissonant. Because the state is aversive, the person is motivated to reduce it, just as a hungry person is motivated to eat.

Magnitude of dissonance depends on (1) the importance of the cognitions, (2) the ratio of dissonant to consonant cognitions, and (3) how little external justification exists for the behaviour. The less justification you had, the more dissonance you feel.

Ways to reduce dissonance: (1) change the attitude ("smoking is not that harmful"), (2) change the behaviour (quit), (3) add consonant cognitions ("smoking keeps my weight down"), (4) trivialise the inconsistency ("everyone dies of something"). Because behaviour is usually harder to undo than an attitude, people most often change the attitude to fit what they already did. This is the core surprise of the theory: behaviour can drive attitudes, not just the reverse.

Classic experiment: Festinger and Carlsmith (1959), the $1 vs $20 study. Participants did a boring peg-turning task and were then paid either $1 or $20 to tell the next participant it was fun. Later, the $1 group rated the task as more enjoyable. The $20 group had sufficient external justification ("I lied for the money"); the $1 group did not, so they reduced dissonance by changing their private attitude. This is induced (forced) compliance and the principle of insufficient justification.

Other paradigms: post-decision dissonance (Brehm, 1956: after choosing between two similar appliances, people rate the chosen one higher and the rejected one lower, "spreading of alternatives"); effort justification (Aronson and Mills, 1959: a harsh initiation makes a dull group seem more attractive); insufficient deterrence (Aronson and Carlsmith, 1963: children given a mild threat not to play with a toy later devalued it more than children given a severe threat). Field example: When Prophecy Fails (Festinger, Riecken and Schachter, 1956), a doomsday cult that proselytised more after the prophecy failed.

Rival account: Daryl Bem's self-perception theory (1967) says no aversive arousal is needed: we infer our attitudes by watching our own behaviour, as an observer would. Self-perception explains attitude change best when attitudes are weak or vague; dissonance explains it best when behaviour clearly contradicts a strong attitude. Later revisions (Cooper and Fazio's "new look", 1984) stress personal responsibility and foreseeable aversive consequences.

  • Free choice matters. Dissonance is strongest when the act feels chosen. Under heavy coercion, external justification is high and dissonance is low, so "forced" in a stem means induced compliance with minimal pressure, not a gun to the head.
  • Bem vs Festinger. "Attitudes inferred from behaviour without arousal" is self-perception, not dissonance.

14Tajfel's social identity theory
Social · IHBAS 2024 Q14

From scratch

Henri Tajfel and John Turner (1979) proposed social identity theory (SIT). Its central claim: part of a person's self-concept (the social identity) comes from knowing they belong to social groups, together with the value and emotional significance attached to that membership. You are not only "Sihan, a careful student" (personal identity) but also "an Indian", "a psychology student", "a Delhi person" (social identities).

Three psychological processes drive it: (1) Social categorisation: we sort people, including ourselves, into groups (us vs them), which exaggerates similarity within and differences between groups. (2) Social identification: we adopt the identity, norms and emotions of the group we belong to. (3) Social comparison: we compare our in-group with out-groups, and because people want positive self-esteem, they seek positive distinctiveness, i.e. seeing their group as better. This produces in-group favouritism and can produce out-group derogation and prejudice.

The minimal group paradigm (Tajfel, Billig, Bundy and Flament, 1971). Bristol schoolboys were divided on a trivial basis (supposedly preferring Klee vs Kandinsky paintings, actually random). They never met their group, had no history or conflict, and gained nothing personally. Yet when allocating points, they favoured their own group, often choosing to maximise the difference between groups even at the cost of their group's absolute gain. Mere categorisation was enough to cause in-group bias. This is the key evidence that SIT does not need real competition.

Contrast with Sherif's realistic conflict theory. Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment (1954) showed that competition for scarce resources creates hostility between groups (Eagles vs Rattlers), and that superordinate goals (fixing the water supply together) reduce it. SIT adds that even without real conflict, identity alone creates bias. Self-categorisation theory (Turner, 1987) extends SIT, explaining when personal vs social identity becomes salient.

Responses to a low-status identity: individual mobility (leave the group), social creativity (redefine the comparison, e.g. "we are poorer but more hospitable"), and social competition (challenge the higher-status group directly). Practical relevance: prejudice, communal conflict, sports fandom, and reducing bias via the contact hypothesis (Allport, 1954) and common in-group identity model (Gaertner and Dovidio).

  • Festinger's social comparison vs SIT's social comparison. In SIT, the comparison is between groups to achieve positive distinctiveness.

15Attribution biases: actor-observer, FAE, halo effect
Social · IHBAS 2024 Q15, Q51, Q54

From scratch

Attribution is how we explain the causes of behaviour. Fritz Heider (1958), the founder, distinguished internal (dispositional) attributions (personality, ability, effort) from external (situational) ones (luck, task difficulty, other people). Jones and Davis (1965) proposed correspondent inference theory (when do we infer a trait from an act?), and Harold Kelley (1967) the covariation model using consensus, consistency and distinctiveness: low consensus + high consistency + low distinctiveness points to an internal cause. Bernard Weiner added stability and controllability, applied to achievement.

Fundamental attribution error (FAE), named by Lee Ross (1977), also called correspondence bias (Jones and Harris, 1967): when explaining other people's behaviour, we overestimate dispositional causes and underestimate situational ones. In Jones and Harris's study, readers judged an essay writer to hold pro-Castro views even when told the writer was assigned that side. Example: a classmate arrives late and you think "irresponsible", ignoring the traffic jam. FAE is weaker in collectivist cultures (Miller, 1984, comparing Indian and American participants).

Actor-observer effect (Jones and Nisbett, 1971): we attribute our own behaviour to situations but others' behaviour to dispositions. "I was late because of traffic; he was late because he is careless." Explanation: perceptual salience (as actors we see the situation; as observers we see the person) and more information about our own varied behaviour. It differs from FAE in that it is a comparison of self vs other.

Self-serving bias: success is attributed internally ("I am smart"), failure externally ("the paper was unfair"). It is driven by self-esteem, so it depends on outcome valence, unlike the actor-observer effect, which depends on perspective.

Halo effect: Edward Thorndike (1920) found that officers' ratings of soldiers on separate traits were too highly correlated. One salient positive trait (attractiveness, confidence) spreads to judgements of unrelated traits (intelligence, honesty). Its reverse is the horns (devil) effect. Dion, Berscheid and Walster (1972) showed "what is beautiful is good". In assessment and interviewing, the same phenomenon is called halo error: rating a candidate on all dimensions based on one attribute. Other rater errors: central tendency (rating everyone average), leniency/severity, contrast error (rating relative to the previous candidate), similar-to-me error. Halo is a bias in impression formation, close to implicit personality theory.

MCQ traps

  • FAE vs actor-observer. If the stem mentions both "own behaviour external" and "others' internal", the answer is actor-observer. FAE only concerns judging others.
  • Self-serving bias looks right for "own behaviour external", but it hinges on success vs failure, not self vs other.
  • Halo vs primacy. "Effect of initial impressions on later judgements" is the primacy effect, not halo. Halo is one trait spreading to other traits.
  • Halo vs stereotyping. "Judging others based on group membership" is a stereotype, not halo.
  • Halo vs contrast error. Contrast error is judging relative to the previous ratee; central tendency is clustering in the middle; halo is "one single attribute".

16Groups: groupthink, social loafing, social facilitation, bystander effect
Social · IHBAS 2024 Q4, Q22, Q34, Q57, Q79

From scratch

Social facilitation. Norman Triplett (1898), often called the first social psychology experiment, found cyclists rode faster with others and children wound fishing reels faster in pairs. Robert Zajonc (1965) resolved contradictory findings with drive theory: the mere presence of others raises arousal, which strengthens the dominant response. On simple or well-learned tasks the dominant response is correct, so performance improves; on complex or new tasks it is wrong, so performance worsens (social inhibition). Zajonc even showed it in cockroaches. Cottrell (1968) proposed evaluation apprehension; Baron proposed distraction-conflict.

Social loafing. Max Ringelmann (1913) found that individuals pulled a rope with less force per person as group size increased (the Ringelmann effect). Latané, Williams and Harkins (1979) named it social loafing: people exert less effort on a collective task where individual output is pooled and unidentifiable. Explained by the collective effort model (Karau and Williams, 1993). Reduced when contributions are identifiable, the task matters, groups are small and cohesive. It is weaker in collectivist cultures and among women. Key contrast with facilitation: in facilitation your performance is individually evaluated; in loafing it is pooled.

Groupthink. Irving Janis (1972), analysing the Bay of Pigs fiasco, defined groupthink as a mode of thinking in highly cohesive groups where the striving for unanimity overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives. Antecedents: high cohesion, insulation, a directive leader, high stress, no procedures for evaluating options. Symptoms: illusion of invulnerability, illusion of unanimity, collective rationalisation, belief in the group's inherent morality, stereotyped views of out-groups, direct pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, and mindguards. Remedies: a devil's advocate, an impartial leader, outside experts, subgroups, a "second-chance" meeting.

Bystander effect. Triggered by the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York (reported as 38 witnesses not helping). Darley and Latané (1968), in the seizure-over-intercom study, found that 85 percent helped when they believed they were alone, but only 31 percent when they thought four others heard. So the more bystanders, the less likely any one person helps, and the slower help comes. Their five-step decision model: notice the event, interpret it as an emergency, assume responsibility, know how to help, decide to help. Causes: diffusion of responsibility (main cause), pluralistic ignorance (others' calm makes you think it is not an emergency, as in the smoke-filled room study, Latané and Darley 1968), and audience inhibition / evaluation apprehension.

Related group phenomena: group polarisation (Moscovici and Zavalloni, 1969; discussion makes the group's initial leaning more extreme; the "risky shift" of Stoner, 1961); deindividuation (Festinger, Pepitone and Newcomb, 1952; Zimbardo, 1969; anonymity in crowds lowers self-awareness and restraint).

MCQ traps

  • Groupthink is not peer pressure or brainstorming. It is a collective decision process that discourages dissent; "peer pressure" is too generic.
  • Facilitation vs loafing in one question: "improved performance in presence of others" is facilitation; "less effort when working with others" is loafing.
  • Bystander direction. People help more when alone. The option "more likely to help in a group" reverses it. "Less likely to notice emergencies in a group" is tempting but the effect is about helping, not noticing.
  • Mechanism vs phenomenon. The cause of the bystander effect is diffusion of responsibility; social loafing also involves diffused accountability but is about effort on group tasks, not helping.
  • Polarisation vs groupthink. Polarisation is about extremity of attitudes; groupthink is about suppressing dissent to keep harmony.

17Social influence: normative vs informational, conformity, compliance, obedience, reactance, roles
Social · IHBAS 2024 Q25, Q26, Q41, Q94

From scratch

Two reasons we yield to others (Deutsch and Gerard, 1955): Normative social influence is going along to be liked, accepted and avoid rejection; it typically produces public compliance without private acceptance. Informational social influence is going along because we believe others are correct, especially when the situation is ambiguous, a crisis, or others are experts; it produces genuine private acceptance (internalisation). Kelman (1958) described three levels: compliance, identification, internalisation.

Conformity is changing behaviour or beliefs to match a group norm, without anyone directly asking. Muzafer Sherif (1935), using the autokinetic effect (a still light in a dark room seems to move), showed that estimates converged into a group norm: informational influence in an ambiguous task. Solomon Asch (1951, 1956), using unambiguous line-judgement tasks with confederates giving wrong answers, found about 37 percent conformity on critical trials and about 75 percent of participants conformed at least once: normative influence. Conformity rose with group size up to about 3 to 4, and dropped sharply if even one ally dissented or responses were private.

Compliance is changing behaviour in response to a direct request from someone without special authority. Techniques: foot-in-the-door (Freedman and Fraser, 1966: small request first), door-in-the-face (Cialdini et al., 1975: large request refused, then smaller one), low-ball (agree, then the cost rises), that's-not-all. Cialdini's principles: reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity.

Obedience is following a direct command from an authority figure. Stanley Milgram (1963), Yale: participants as "teachers" were told to give increasing "shocks" (up to 450 V) to a learner; 65 percent went to the maximum. Obedience fell when the learner was close, the experimenter was absent, or peers rebelled. Milgram explained it with the agentic state.

Power of roles: Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971). Healthy college men randomly assigned as guards or prisoners in a mock prison quickly adopted their roles: guards became abusive, prisoners passive and distressed; the planned two-week study was stopped after six days. It illustrates how social roles and situations shape behaviour, linked to deindividuation (uniforms, mirrored sunglasses). It has been criticised for demand characteristics and Zimbardo's own role as superintendent. Do not confuse with Sherif's Robbers Cave (intergroup conflict) or Milgram (obedience).

Reactance (Jack Brehm, 1966): when people perceive a threat to a behavioural freedom, they experience an aversive motivational state that pushes them to restore the freedom, often by doing the forbidden thing or rejecting the persuasive message (the boomerang effect). Example: a "Do not touch" sign increases touching; heavy-handed anti-smoking warnings can strengthen a teen's urge to smoke. Reactance explains why too much pressure undermines compliance.

  • Normative vs informational swap. "Desire to be correct and accurate" is informational; "desire to be accepted and liked" is normative. "Perceived power and authority" points to obedience.
  • Conformity vs compliance. Conformity: behaviour changes due to group pressure, nobody asks; compliance: a direct request. "Both refer to obedience" is wrong because obedience needs an authority's command.
  • Garbled names. Options like "Milgram's message experiment" (Milgram did the small-world letter study, not about roles) or "Zilstein's shock research" are decoys. Power of roles = Zimbardo.
  • Robbers Cave is about realistic group conflict, not roles.
  • Reactance is resistance, not compliance. "Tendency to follow norms without question" or "comply with direct requests" are the opposite of reactance.

Developmental · IHBAS 2024 Q2, Q64, Q72

From scratch

Erik Erikson (Childhood and Society, 1950; Identity: Youth and Crisis, 1968) was a neo-Freudian ego psychologist. He kept Freud's idea of stages but shifted the focus from psychosexual drives to psychosocial tasks, and extended development across the whole life span (8 stages, from birth to death). His principle is epigenetic: each stage has its time of ascendancy, and stages unfold in a fixed order.

Each stage is defined by a psychosocial crisis, a tension between a positive (syntonic) and a negative (dystonic) pole. Healthy resolution is a favourable ratio leaning positive (some mistrust is adaptive), and yields a basic virtue (ego strength). Unresolved crises can be revisited later.

Adolescence and identity. Erikson coined identity crisis and the psychosocial moratorium (a socially sanctioned delay to experiment). James Marcia (1966) operationalised it into four identity statuses by crossing exploration (crisis) and commitment: achievement (explored, committed), moratorium (exploring, not committed), foreclosure (committed without exploring, e.g. adopting parents' career choice), diffusion (neither). Marcia is a frequent follow-up MCQ.

  • Erikson vs Freud: psychosocial vs psychosexual; 8 lifespan stages vs 5 stages ending at puberty; ego vs id focus.
  • Erikson's identity vs Marcia's statuses: Erikson describes the crisis; Marcia classifies outcomes.
  • Erikson vs Piaget vs Kohlberg: psychosocial vs cognitive vs moral development.
  • Industry vs inferiority (school age) is often confused with initiative vs guilt (preschool). Initiative = planning and starting play activities; industry = mastering school skills.
  • Intimacy (young adult) comes before generativity (middle adult), which comes before integrity (late adult).

  • Virtue matching: fidelity goes with identity, care with generativity, wisdom with integrity; examiners scramble these.
  • "Role confusion" is also called identity diffusion; do not mix with Marcia's "diffusion" status, which is an outcome category.

19Piaget's cognitive stages and Vygotsky's ZPD
Developmental · IHBAS 2024 Q16, Q32

From scratch

Jean Piaget (Swiss, 1896 to 1980; key works 1936 to 1952) founded genetic epistemology and the stage theory of cognitive development. The child is an active constructivist, a "little scientist". Knowledge is organised into schemas (schemes). Development is driven by adaptation, which has two parts: assimilation (fitting new experience into an existing schema: calling a cow a "doggy") and accommodation (changing the schema to fit the new experience: creating a new "cow" schema). The balance between them is equilibration; mismatch causes disequilibrium, which motivates growth. Organisation is integrating schemas into coherent systems.

Four stages, invariant in order, universal across cultures, with qualitatively different thinking:

Criticisms: Piaget underestimated infants (Baillargeon's violation-of-expectation studies show object knowledge earlier), underestimated the role of culture and instruction, and not everyone reaches formal operations. Neo-Piagetians (Case, Pascual-Leone) linked stages to working-memory capacity.

Lev Vygotsky (Russian, 1896 to 1934; Thought and Language, 1934; Mind in Society, 1978) proposed the sociocultural theory: cognition develops first between people (interpsychological) and then within the child (intrapsychological). Culture supplies psychological tools, especially language. The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is the gap between what a child can do alone (actual developmental level) and what they can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other (MKO) (potential level). Teaching should target this zone. Scaffolding (a term from Wood, Bruner and Ross, 1976, not Vygotsky himself) is temporary support that is gradually withdrawn as competence grows. Vygotsky saw private speech (talking aloud to oneself) as a self-regulation tool that becomes inner speech, whereas Piaget saw it as egocentric speech that simply fades.

Example: a 6-year-old can complete a 20-piece puzzle alone, and a 40-piece one when a parent hints "look for edge pieces first". The 20-to-40 range is the ZPD; the hints are scaffolding.

20Brain areas: prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, medulla
Biopsych · IHBAS 2024 Q3, Q28, Q43, Q74

From scratch

The brain is taught in three layers (MacLean's older "triune" framing is still used in Indian textbooks): the hindbrain (medulla, pons, cerebellum) keeps you alive, the limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus, hypothalamus, cingulate) handles emotion, motivation and memory, and the cerebral cortex (four lobes) handles thought. Exam questions almost always pair one function word with one structure, so the job is to own the function-to-structure map cold.

Frontal lobe and prefrontal cortex (PFC). The frontal lobe is the largest lobe and sits behind the forehead. Its front part, the prefrontal cortex, runs the executive functions: planning, problem-solving, reasoning, working memory, inhibition of impulses, shifting set, and social judgement. Behind it lie the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus, voluntary movement) and, on the left, Broca's area (speech production; damage gives expressive, non-fluent aphasia). The classic case is Phineas Gage (1848): an iron rod destroyed his frontal cortex, his intellect and memory survived, but he became impulsive, profane and unreliable. Frontal damage produces the dysexecutive syndrome (poor planning, perseveration on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, disinhibition, apathy). Prefrontal dysfunction is implicated in ADHD and schizophrenia (hypofrontality).

Amygdala. An almond-shaped nucleus in the medial temporal lobe, part of the limbic system. It detects threat and drives fear and aggression, fear conditioning, and the emotional tagging of memories (why emotional events are remembered vividly). Kluver-Bucy syndrome (1937): removal of the amygdala/temporal lobes in monkeys produced tameness, loss of fear, hyperorality and hypersexuality. Joseph LeDoux described a fast "low road" (thalamus to amygdala) and slow "high road" (thalamus to cortex to amygdala) for fear. In PTSD and anxiety the amygdala is overactive and the PFC fails to calm it.

Hippocampus. A seahorse-shaped structure inside the medial temporal lobe. It consolidates new declarative (explicit) memories from short-term to long-term storage and supports spatial memory (O'Keefe's place cells, Nobel 2014; London taxi drivers have enlarged posterior hippocampi, Maguire 2000). Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), operated on by Scoville in 1953 and studied by Brenda Milner, lost both hippocampi and developed severe anterograde amnesia: old memories and IQ intact, procedural learning (mirror drawing) intact, but no new facts or events. The hippocampus is not where memories are permanently stored (that is distributed cortex); it is the gateway that forms them. It shrinks with chronic cortisol and is an early site of Alzheimer's damage.

Medulla oblongata. The lowest part of the brainstem, continuous with the spinal cord. It controls vital, life-sustaining autonomic functions: heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, and reflexes such as swallowing, coughing, sneezing and vomiting. Motor pathways cross over (decussate) here, which is why the left hemisphere controls the right body. Damage to the medulla is usually fatal; a person can survive cortical death in a persistent vegetative state because the medulla keeps breathing going. The pons above it relays signals and regulates sleep and arousal; the cerebellum behind it coordinates balance and fine movement.

21Neurotransmitters: acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin
Biopsych · IHBAS 2024 Q59, Q80, Q91

From scratch

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger released from the axon terminal of one neuron into the synaptic cleft, where it binds receptors on the next cell. The sequence is: action potential reaches the terminal, calcium enters, vesicles fuse and release transmitter, transmitter binds receptors (excitatory or inhibitory effect), then the signal is ended by reuptake into the presynaptic neuron or enzymatic breakdown. Otto Loewi (1921) proved chemical transmission with his frog-heart experiment ("Vagusstoff", later identified as acetylcholine). Most psychiatric drugs work by altering one of these steps: agonists mimic or boost a transmitter, antagonists block it.

Acetylcholine (ACh) was the first neurotransmitter discovered. It is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction, so it triggers skeletal muscle contraction, and it is the main parasympathetic transmitter. In the brain, cholinergic neurons from the basal forebrain (nucleus basalis of Meynert) project to the hippocampus and cortex and support learning, memory and attention. Example: in Alzheimer's disease these neurons degenerate, so drugs such as donepezil (acetylcholinesterase inhibitors) are given to keep ACh in the synapse longer. Curare blocks ACh receptors (paralysis); black widow venom floods ACh release (spasms); botulinum toxin blocks its release; myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune attack on ACh receptors (muscle weakness).

Dopamine (DA) is a catecholamine involved in reward, pleasure, motivation and voluntary movement. Key pathways: the mesolimbic pathway (ventral tegmental area to nucleus accumbens) is the reward circuit, studied after Olds and Milner (1954) found rats would press a lever endlessly to stimulate it; nearly all addictive drugs raise dopamine here. The nigrostriatal pathway (substantia nigra to striatum) controls movement; its loss causes Parkinson's disease (treated with L-dopa). The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia links excess mesolimbic dopamine to positive symptoms; typical antipsychotics block D2 receptors and can cause Parkinson-like side effects. Modern view (Schultz): dopamine signals reward prediction error, not pleasure itself.

Serotonin (5-HT, 5-hydroxytryptamine) is made from tryptophan in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem. It regulates mood, appetite, sleep, impulsivity and aggression. Low serotonin is linked to depression, OCD, and impulsive suicide; SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline) block its reuptake. Serotonin is also the precursor of melatonin. LSD acts on 5-HT2A receptors.

Other transmitters you must place: GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter (anxiety, benzodiazepines boost it); glutamate the main excitatory (learning via long-term potentiation, NMDA receptors); norepinephrine arousal, alertness, fight-or-flight; endorphins natural pain relief.

  • Glutamate vs ACh for "learning and memory". Glutamate is also memory-linked (LTP), but the "muscle contraction" clue locks it to ACh.
  • Serotonin vs dopamine for "pleasure". Serotonin is mood and wellbeing; reward and pleasure circuitry is dopamine.
  • Norepinephrine for "reward". NE is arousal and alertness; it is a decoy for reward questions.
  • GABA for "sleep". GABA promotes sedation, but the textbook triad "mood, appetite, sleep" is serotonin.
  • Parkinson's vs Alzheimer's. Parkinson's = dopamine loss (substantia nigra); Alzheimer's = acetylcholine loss (basal forebrain).
  • Dopamine is not only "pleasure". If a stem mentions movement disorders or antipsychotic side effects, it is still dopamine.

22Endocrine vs exocrine glands, adrenal hormones, HPA axis
Biopsych · IHBAS 2024 Q6, Q23, Q44

From scratch

The body has two communication systems. The nervous system is fast, electrical and point-to-point. The endocrine system is slower, chemical and broadcast: glands release hormones into the bloodstream, which carries them to distant target organs with matching receptors. Glands are split by how they deliver secretions. Exocrine glands have ducts that carry non-hormonal secretions to a surface or cavity: sweat, saliva, tears, sebum, digestive enzymes, milk. So exocrine glands are called duct glands. Endocrine glands are ductless glands that release hormones directly into blood: pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenals, pineal, gonads, and the islets of the pancreas. The pancreas is mixed: exocrine for enzymes into the intestine, endocrine for insulin and glucagon.

Major endocrine glands. The pituitary is the "master gland" but is itself controlled by the hypothalamus, the true link between brain and endocrine system. The anterior pituitary releases growth hormone, prolactin, TSH, ACTH, FSH and LH; the posterior pituitary releases oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH), which are made in the hypothalamus. The thyroid secretes thyroxine (metabolic rate; hypothyroidism mimics depression, hyperthyroidism mimics anxiety; congenital deficiency causes cretinism). The pineal secretes melatonin (sleep and circadian rhythm). Gonads secrete testosterone, estrogen and progesterone.

Adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys and have two parts. The outer adrenal cortex makes steroid hormones: glucocorticoids such as cortisol (stress hormone, raises blood glucose, suppresses inflammation and immunity, peaks in the morning), mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone (salt and water balance), and small amounts of sex hormones. The inner adrenal medulla is really part of the sympathetic nervous system and makes the catecholamines epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine, the hormones of Walter Cannon's (1915) fight-or-flight response: heart rate, blood pressure and blood sugar rise within seconds.

Two stress pathways. The fast SAM axis (sympathetic-adrenal-medullary): hypothalamus to sympathetic nerves to adrenal medulla to adrenaline, in seconds. The slower HPA axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal): the hypothalamus releases CRH, which makes the anterior pituitary release ACTH, which makes the adrenal cortex release cortisol, in minutes to hours. Cortisol then feeds back to the hypothalamus and pituitary to switch the axis off (negative feedback). Hans Selye's (1936) General Adaptation Syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion) describes what happens when this system is pushed for too long. In depression the feedback often fails (non-suppression on the dexamethasone suppression test), and chronic cortisol damages the hippocampus.

Homeostasis vs allostasis. Walter Cannon coined homeostasis: keeping internal variables around a fixed set point (like a thermostat). Sterling and Eyer (1988) proposed allostasis, "stability through change": the brain actively anticipates demands and adjusts set points, regulating its stress response through hormone release (HPA and SAM). Bruce McEwen added allostatic load: the cumulative wear and tear from repeated or chronic activation (hypertension, immune suppression, hippocampal atrophy).

  • Melatonin as adrenal. Melatonin is pineal.
  • Homeostasis for stress regulation. Homeostasis is passive fixed-point balance; active, anticipatory regulation of stress hormones is allostasis.
  • Neuroplasticity or circadian rhythm. Both are real brain processes but neither means regulating the stress response through hormones.
  • Pituitary as the top of the HPA chain. The chain starts at the hypothalamus (CRH), not the pituitary.

23Neuroplasticity and neural pruning
Biopsych · IHBAS 2024 Q19, Q50

From scratch

Neuroplasticity (brain plasticity) is the brain's ability to change its structure and function throughout life in response to experience, learning and injury. The old view (Ramon y Cajal, early 1900s) was that the adult brain was fixed. That view fell from the 1960s onward: Rosenzweig, Bennett and Diamond showed rats raised in enriched environments grew thicker cortex and more synapses than rats in impoverished cages; Merzenich showed monkey cortical maps reorganise after a finger is lost; Maguire (2000) showed London taxi drivers have larger posterior hippocampi. The cellular basis was predicted by Donald Hebb (1949): "neurons that fire together wire together" (Hebbian learning), confirmed by long-term potentiation (LTP), discovered by Bliss and Lomo (1973) in the hippocampus, a lasting strengthening of synapses after repeated stimulation, dependent on glutamate NMDA receptors.

Plasticity works at several levels: synaptic plasticity (strengthening or weakening of existing synapses, LTP and LTD), structural plasticity (growth of new dendrites, spines and synapses, called synaptogenesis), neurogenesis (birth of new neurons, which in adults is limited mainly to the hippocampal dentate gyrus), and functional reorganisation (cortical remapping, for example the visual cortex of blind Braille readers processing touch). Clinically, plasticity is why stroke rehabilitation, constraint-induced movement therapy, and cognitive remediation work, and why children recover language better after left-hemisphere damage than adults. Plasticity is greatest during critical or sensitive periods (Hubel and Wiesel, Nobel 1981: kittens with one eye sewn shut in early life permanently lost vision in that eye) but continues at a reduced level in adulthood.

Neural (synaptic) pruning is the elimination of unused or weak neural connections. The developing brain overproduces synapses: synaptic density peaks in the first few years (visual cortex around age 1, prefrontal cortex later), roughly double the adult number. Then a "use it or lose it" process removes connections that are not activated, while active ones are strengthened and myelinated. Peter Huttenlocher's work (1979 onward) documented this rise and fall. Pruning continues through adolescence, especially in the prefrontal cortex, which is why grey matter thins in the teens and why executive control matures last (into the mid-20s). Microglia physically remove the tagged synapses.

Pruning is therefore a form of plasticity: the brain sculpts itself by subtraction, making circuits more efficient, just as a gardener cuts branches so the plant grows better. Example: infants can discriminate speech sounds of every language, but by about 10 to 12 months they lose distinctions not used in their home language (Werker), because those circuits were pruned. Too much pruning in adolescence is one leading hypothesis for schizophrenia (Feinberg 1982; complement gene C4 findings, Sekar 2016); too little pruning has been linked to autism.

MCQ traps

  • "Strengthening of neural pathways" for pruning. Strengthening is LTP or Hebbian learning; pruning is removal. They happen together but are not the same thing.
  • "Growth of new neurons" for pruning or plasticity. That is neurogenesis, a narrower process.
  • "Brain cell death" for pruning. Pruning removes connections (synapses, dendrites), not whole neurons; cell death is apoptosis.
  • "Increase in brain size". Pruning reduces grey matter; brain size is not the definition of anything here.
  • "Brain's protective coating" for neuroplasticity. That is the meninges or myelin; plasticity means changeability.
  • "Only in childhood". Plasticity is greatest in critical periods but lifelong; an option saying it stops in childhood is wrong.

24Korsakoff's syndrome, prosopagnosia, myopia
Biopsych · IHBAS 2024 Q63, Q76, Q83, Q67

From scratch

Korsakoff's syndrome was described by the Russian psychiatrist Sergei Korsakoff (1887). It is a chronic amnestic disorder caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, most often from chronic alcohol abuse (alcohol plus poor diet impairs thiamine absorption). It usually follows Wernicke's encephalopathy (Carl Wernicke, 1881), an acute emergency with the triad of confusion, ataxia and ophthalmoplegia (eye movement paralysis, nystagmus). Together they are called Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. Damage is to the mammillary bodies and the medial (dorsomedial) thalamus, part of the diencephalic memory circuit (Papez circuit).

The core features of Korsakoff's: severe anterograde amnesia (cannot form new memories), significant retrograde amnesia with a temporal gradient (recent years lost, childhood spared, Ribot's law), confabulation (honest, unintentional filling of memory gaps with invented stories), lack of insight, and apathy, while general intelligence and procedural memory are relatively preserved. Example: a patient greets the doctor as if for the first time every morning and, asked what he did yesterday, describes a fishing trip that never happened. Treatment is immediate thiamine; Wernicke's stage is partly reversible, Korsakoff's largely is not. In DSM-5-TR it falls under alcohol-induced major neurocognitive disorder, amnestic-confabulatory type.

Anterograde vs retrograde amnesia. Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new memories after the onset (H.M. after hippocampal removal; Korsakoff's). Retrograde amnesia is the loss of memories formed before the onset (common after head injury, usually shorter). Direction is relative to the injury: antero = forward, retro = backward.

Prosopagnosia (Greek prosopon = face, agnosia = not knowing), also called face blindness, was named by Joachim Bodamer (1947). It is a selective inability to recognise familiar faces, sometimes even one's own in the mirror, despite normal vision and intact recognition of the same people by voice, gait or clothing. It results from damage to the fusiform face area (fusiform gyrus, ventral occipito-temporal cortex, usually right or bilateral; Kanwisher 1997 identified the FFA with fMRI), or is developmental (present from birth in about 2% of people). It is a type of visual agnosia: a failure of recognition (meaning), not of sensation. Oliver Sacks's The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat describes a related agnosia.

Myopia (nearsightedness) is a refractive error in which distant objects look blurred while near objects are clear. The eyeball is too long (or the cornea too curved), so light focuses in front of the retina. It is corrected with concave (diverging, minus) lenses. Hypermetropia (hyperopia, farsightedness) is the reverse: eyeball too short, focus behind the retina, near objects blurred, corrected with convex lenses. Presbyopia is age-related loss of lens flexibility after about 40, making near vision hard. Astigmatism is an irregularly curved cornea or lens producing distortion at all distances.

  • Wernicke's aphasia vs Wernicke's encephalopathy. Same surname, entirely different disorders.

25Depression and bipolar disorder
Abnormal · IHBAS 2024 Q5, Q46

From scratch

Mood disorders (DSM-5 splits them into two chapters: Depressive Disorders and Bipolar and Related Disorders) are disorders in which a sustained disturbance of emotion, not a passing feeling, colours thinking, behaviour and body functions. The key word is episode: a block of time (weeks) in which the person is clearly different from their usual self.

Major depressive disorder (MDD): DSM-5-TR requires five or more of nine symptoms for at least two weeks, nearly every day, and at least one must be either (1) depressed mood or (2) anhedonia (markedly reduced interest or pleasure). The other seven: significant weight or appetite change, insomnia or hypersomnia, psychomotor agitation or retardation, fatigue or loss of energy, worthlessness or excessive guilt, poor concentration or indecisiveness, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicide. A useful mnemonic is SIG E CAPS (Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicide) plus mood. Example: a 28-year-old who for a month feels empty, has stopped painting (which she loved), wakes at 4 a.m., has lost 4 kg and thinks she is a burden: classic MDD with early morning awakening and anhedonia.

Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) is a milder but chronic depressed mood for at least 2 years in adults (1 year in children and adolescents) with two or more symptoms. Think "low-grade, long-lasting". Premenstrual dysphoric disorder and disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (children 6 to 18, severe temper outbursts) are also in the depressive chapter in DSM-5.

Bipolar I disorder needs at least one manic episode (depression is common but not required). A manic episode is a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood plus increased energy or activity lasting at least 1 week (or any duration if hospitalised), with three or more of: inflated self-esteem or grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas or racing thoughts, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, and risky pleasurable activity (spending sprees, sexual indiscretion). Mnemonic DIG FAST. Bipolar II needs at least one hypomanic episode (at least 4 days, no marked impairment, no psychosis, no hospitalisation) plus at least one major depressive episode. Cyclothymic disorder is 2 years of fluctuating hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria.

Causes: biological accounts stress the monoamines (the monoamine hypothesis: low serotonin and norepinephrine; Schildkraut 1965 for catecholamines), HPA-axis hyperactivity (high cortisol), and strong heritability (bipolar is among the most heritable disorders, roughly 60 to 85 percent). Psychological accounts: Beck's cognitive triad (negative view of self, world, future, 1967), Seligman's learned helplessness (1975) and its reformulation as hopelessness/attributional style (Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale 1978: internal, stable, global attributions for failure), and Freud's "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917: anger turned inward after loss). Treatment: SSRIs and CBT for depression; lithium (Cade 1949) and mood stabilisers for bipolar, since antidepressants alone can flip a bipolar patient into mania.

  • "Persistent sadness and loss of interest" with bipolar disorder offered: tempting because bipolar includes depression, but the stem describes only one pole, so the answer is depression.
  • "Alternating episodes of mania and depression" is the classic textbook definition of bipolar, but DSM-5 Bipolar I does not require a depressive episode at all; one manic episode is enough. Know both statements.
  • Bipolar II is not "milder bipolar": the depressive episodes are often more frequent and disabling. The distinction is hypomania vs mania only.
  • "Mood swings" as an option in a GAD or depression item is a distractor; it points loosely to bipolar or cyclothymia, not to anxiety.
  • Durations get swapped: 2 weeks (MDD), 1 week (mania), 4 days (hypomania), 2 years (dysthymia and cyclothymia).
  • Learned helplessness is Seligman (dogs, 1967 to 1975); the cognitive triad is Beck. Do not attribute the triad to Ellis (ABC model, REBT).

26Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)
Abnormal · IHBAS 2024 Q35, Q66

From scratch

Anxiety is a future-oriented state of apprehension (the body and mind preparing for possible threat), while fear is the immediate alarm reaction to a present danger. Anxiety disorders are diagnosed when this response is excessive, persistent and impairing. Freud called the diffuse kind "free-floating anxiety": anxiety not tied to any specific object, and that phrase is the heart of GAD.

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), DSM-5-TR criteria: (A) excessive anxiety and worry (apprehensive expectation) occurring more days than not for at least 6 months, about a number of events or activities (work, health, money, family); (B) the person finds it difficult to control the worry; (C) the worry is associated with three or more of six symptoms (only one needed in children): restlessness or feeling keyed up, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance. (D) it causes significant distress or impairment and is not due to a substance or another disorder. ICD-10 used a similar picture (with autonomic symptoms) over "several months"; older DSM-III used 1 month, raised to 6 months in DSM-III-R.

Example: a bank clerk who for the past year worries daily that she will be fired, that her son will have an accident on his bike, that the family will run out of money, though nothing is actually wrong. She cannot "switch it off", sleeps badly, her shoulders ache and she snaps at her husband. No panic attacks, no specific feared object: GAD.

Why it persists: Borkovec's avoidance theory of worry (1990s) proposes that worry is a verbal-linguistic activity that suppresses vivid imagery and autonomic arousal, so it is negatively reinforced (it feels like it "protects" the person). Wells' metacognitive model distinguishes Type 1 worry (about external events) from Type 2 "worry about worry". Dugas' intolerance of uncertainty model is the third major account. Barlow describes a general biological and psychological vulnerability. Neurobiologically, GABA underactivity is implicated (hence benzodiazepines, which enhance GABA-A) and the amygdala is hyper-reactive. Treatment: CBT, applied relaxation, SSRIs/SNRIs, buspirone.

  • "Fear of fear" looks like "anxiety about anxiety", but it is the classic description of panic disorder (Goldstein and Chambless 1978), where people dread the next attack.
  • "A desire to check that the environment is safe" is OCD checking compulsions, not GAD. GAD worriers rarely perform rituals.
  • "Continual flashbacks" is PTSD re-experiencing; GAD worry is about the future, not reliving the past.
  • Mania, hallucinations, mood swings are distractors drawn from bipolar and psychotic disorders; anxiety disorders do not include them.
  • Duration trap: GAD is 6 months (same as specific and social phobia in adults); panic disorder needs 1 month of persistent concern; acute stress disorder 3 days to 1 month.
  • Symptom count trap: 3 of 6 in adults but 1 in children. Muscle tension is distinctive to GAD; palpitations and choking are panic features.

  • Q35: which is a symptom of GAD → Persistent, excessive worry; it is criterion A itself, while mania, hallucinations and mood swings belong to other disorders.

27Types of delusions
Abnormal · IHBAS 2024 Q36

From scratch

A delusion is a fixed, false belief held with firm conviction despite clear contrary evidence, and not explained by the person's culture or religion (Jaspers, General Psychopathology, 1913, gave the classic criteria: certainty, incorrigibility, impossibility or falsity of content). It is a disorder of thought content, unlike hallucinations (perception) or formal thought disorder (form, e.g. loosening of associations). Jaspers also separated primary delusions (arising out of the blue, "un-understandable", e.g. delusional perception, one of Schneider's first-rank symptoms) from secondary delusions (understandable from mood or another experience, e.g. guilt delusions in severe depression).

Delusions are classified by theme. Persecutory (the commonest): the belief that one is being plotted against, followed, poisoned, spied on, harassed, discriminated against or obstructed. Grandiose: belief in exceptional ability, wealth, identity or a special relationship with a deity or famous person (typical of mania). Reference: neutral events, remarks, TV programmes or newspaper articles are believed to be specifically about or aimed at oneself. Erotomanic (de Clerambault's syndrome, 1921): the belief that another person, usually of higher status, is secretly in love with one. Delusional jealousy (Othello syndrome, Todd and Dewhurst 1955): conviction that one's partner is unfaithful, common in alcohol use disorder. Nihilistic (Cotard's syndrome, 1880): belief that one's body, organs, the world or oneself does not exist or is dead or rotting, seen in psychotic depression. Somatic: false beliefs about bodily functions or sensations (infested with parasites, emitting a foul odour). Control / passivity: one's thoughts, feelings or actions are imposed by an outside agency ("made" acts), a Schneiderian first-rank symptom. Thought insertion, withdrawal and broadcasting are related first-rank phenomena. Misidentification: Capgras (a familiar person replaced by an identical impostor) and Fregoli (strangers are a familiar persecutor in disguise). Guilt/sin and poverty delusions are mood-congruent with depression.

Example: a man refuses food at home because he is certain his neighbours have bribed his wife to poison him and the police are in on it: persecutory delusion. If he also believes a news anchor is sending him coded warnings: delusion of reference.

Where they occur: schizophrenia (often bizarre), delusional disorder (DSM-5: one or more delusions for at least 1 month, functioning otherwise not markedly impaired; subtypes erotomanic, grandiose, jealous, persecutory, somatic, mixed, unspecified), psychotic mania (grandiose) and psychotic depression (guilt, nihilistic). DSM-5 dropped the requirement that delusions be non-bizarre in delusional disorder; "bizarre" is now a specifier.

  • "Discriminated against" can pull you toward a social-psychology answer, but in a psychopathology item it is a persecutory theme (DSM-5 glossary wording: "conspired against, cheated, spied on, followed, poisoned, maliciously maligned, harassed, or obstructed").

28Somatic symptom disorder; malingering vs factitious disorder
Abnormal · IHBAS 2024 Q78, Q68

From scratch

DSM-5 (2013) replaced the old "Somatoform Disorders" chapter with Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders. The family is united by prominent bodily symptoms or health concerns causing distress. Historically this goes back to hysteria (Charcot, Janet, Breuer and Freud) and to Briquet's syndrome (multiple unexplained complaints), which became somatization disorder in DSM-III.

Somatic symptom disorder (SSD), DSM-5-TR: (A) one or more somatic symptoms that are distressing or disrupt daily life; (B) excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours related to the symptoms, shown by at least one of: disproportionate and persistent thoughts about seriousness, persistently high anxiety about health, or excessive time and energy devoted to the symptoms; (C) the symptomatic state is persistent, typically more than 6 months. The big DSM-5 change: the symptoms do not have to be medically unexplained; what defines SSD is the excessive psychological response. Textbook MCQ framing (and older DSM-IV somatization) still describes it as "physical symptoms without a medical cause", and that is what entrance keys usually expect. Example: a woman with mild back pain and stomach complaints visits eight doctors in a year, reads about cancer every night and has stopped working.

Related disorders: illness anxiety disorder (old hypochondriasis, preoccupation with having a serious illness with minimal or no somatic symptoms, 6 months), conversion disorder / functional neurological symptom disorder (altered motor or sensory function, e.g. paralysis, blindness, non-epileptic seizures, incompatible with neurological disease; la belle indifference may be seen), psychological factors affecting other medical conditions, and factitious disorder.

The decisive questions for the faking cluster are: is the production of symptoms conscious? and what is the motive? In SSD and conversion, symptoms are not intentionally produced. In factitious disorder (DSM-5: imposed on self or imposed on another), the person deliberately falsifies or induces signs of illness, but the motive is internal: to assume the sick role, with no obvious external reward. Its severe form is Munchausen syndrome (Asher 1951); factitious disorder imposed on another (a caregiver making a child ill) was called Munchausen by proxy (Meadow 1977). Malingering is not a mental disorder in DSM-5; it is a V/Z code ("other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention"): intentional production of false or grossly exaggerated symptoms motivated by external incentives such as avoiding duty, work or prosecution, obtaining compensation or drugs. DSM flags suspicion when there is a medicolegal context, discrepancy between claims and findings, poor cooperation, or antisocial personality disorder.

29Dissociative disorders: DID, dissociative amnesia, depersonalization/derealization
Abnormal · IHBAS 2024 Q69, Q93, Q88

From scratch

Dissociation is a disruption in the normally integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation and behaviour (DSM-5-TR). The concept comes from Pierre Janet (1889, desagregation), who studied hysterical patients at the Salpetriere under Charcot; Freud later preferred repression. Dissociation is generally understood as a defence against overwhelming trauma, and dissociative disorders are strongly associated with childhood abuse. DSM-5 lists: dissociative identity disorder, dissociative amnesia (with or without fugue), depersonalization/derealization disorder, and other specified/unspecified.

Dissociative identity disorder (DID), formerly multiple personality disorder (renamed in DSM-IV, 1994): (A) disruption of identity characterised by two or more distinct personality states (or an experience of possession in some cultures), with discontinuity in sense of self and agency; (B) recurrent gaps in recall of everyday events, personal information or trauma; (C) distress; (D) not part of accepted cultural or religious practice; (E) not due to a substance. The host identity is the one most frequently encountered, who usually carries the person's legal name, and is often passive, dependent, guilty and depressed. Alters (alternate identities) may differ in age, sex, handwriting, vision and even physiological responses; switching is often triggered by stress. Typical alters include child alters and protector/persecutor alters. Famous cases: The Three Faces of Eve (Thigpen and Cleckley 1957) and Sybil. The sociocognitive model (Spanos 1994; Lilienfeld) argues alters are role enactments shaped by therapists and media; the posttraumatic model sees them as trauma-driven.

Dissociative amnesia: an inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, inconsistent with ordinary forgetting, and not due to brain injury or substance. Types: localized (a specific period, the commonest, e.g. no memory of the hours of an assault), selective (some parts of the event), generalized (whole life history, rare), plus systematized and continuous. Procedural and general knowledge are spared, unlike organic amnesia. Specifier with dissociative fugue: purposeful travel or bewildered wandering with amnesia for identity, sometimes adopting a new identity (fugue is no longer a separate diagnosis in DSM-5).

Depersonalization/derealization disorder: persistent or recurrent experiences of depersonalization (unreality, detachment, or being an outside observer of one's own thoughts, feelings, body or actions; "I feel like a robot, as if watching myself in a film") and/or derealization (the surroundings seem unreal, dreamlike, foggy, lifeless or visually distorted). Crucially, reality testing remains intact: the person knows it is a feeling, not a fact, which separates it from psychosis. Brief episodes are common in the general population under fatigue or stress.

30Autism (DSM-5 core criteria); Charcot and hypnosis
Abnormal · IHBAS 2024 Q18, Q39, Q55, Q85, Q97

From scratch

Autism was first described by Leo Kanner (1943, "autistic disturbances of affective contact") and, independently, Hans Asperger (1944). The word itself was coined by Eugen Bleuler (1911) for withdrawal in schizophrenia. DSM-5 (2013) merged autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder and PDD-NOS into a single autism spectrum disorder (ASD), placed under neurodevelopmental disorders (with ADHD, intellectual disability, communication and learning disorders).

DSM-5 core criteria are a dyad (DSM-IV had a triad, with communication separate): (A) persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction, all three required: (1) social-emotional reciprocity (failure of back-and-forth conversation, reduced sharing of interests), (2) nonverbal communicative behaviours (poor eye contact, gestures, facial expression), (3) developing, maintaining and understanding relationships. (B) restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, at least two of four: stereotyped or repetitive movements, speech or object use (echolalia, lining up toys); insistence on sameness and inflexible routines; highly restricted, fixated interests; hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input (new in DSM-5). (C) present in the early developmental period; (D) clinically significant impairment; (E) not better explained by intellectual disability. Specifiers: with or without intellectual impairment, with or without language impairment, associated medical or genetic condition, catatonia, and severity levels 1 to 3 ("requiring support", "substantial support", "very substantial support"). So IQ is not a criterion: many autistic people have average or high IQ. Example: a 4-year-old who does not respond to his name, rarely points to share interest, flaps his hands, and screams if the route to school changes.

Assessment tools: ADOS-2 and ADI-R (gold standard), CARS (Childhood Autism Rating Scale, Schopler and colleagues, 1980, 15 items, US), M-CHAT (screening toddlers), and in India the ISAA (Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism, NIMH Secunderabad / Ministry of Social Justice, 2009), 40 items in six domains, used to certify autism and grade disability for government benefits. CGAS (Children's Global Assessment Scale) is a general functioning scale and CBCL (Achenbach) a broad behaviour checklist.

Charcot and hypnosis: Jean-Martin Charcot (1825 to 1893), the French neurologist at the Salpetriere in Paris, studied hysteria (now conversion disorder). He showed that hysterical symptoms such as anaesthesia (loss of feeling) in limbs, paralysis and fainting could be produced and removed by hypnosis and suggestion, which argued that they were psychological rather than caused by structural damage (though Charcot himself thought only hysterics could be hypnotised, a view opposed by the Nancy school of Liebeault and Bernheim, who held hypnosis was simply heightened suggestion in normal people). Freud studied with Charcot in 1885 to 1886; Janet developed dissociation from this work. Lineage: Mesmer (animal magnetism, 1770s) → James Braid (coined "hypnosis", 1843) → Charcot and Bernheim → Breuer and Freud (cathartic method, Studies on Hysteria, 1895).

Hypnosis is a state of focused attention and reduced peripheral awareness whose chief characteristic is increased suggestibility, i.e. heightened responsiveness to suggestions about perception, memory and action. Theories: Hilgard's neodissociation theory (1977, the "hidden observer", consciousness splits into streams) versus sociocognitive / role-enactment theories (Barber, Spanos, Sarbin). Hypnotisability is measured with the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scales (Weitzenhoffer and Hilgard). Hypnosis does not give superhuman strength, and hypnotically "recovered" memories are unreliable (more confidence, not more accuracy).

31Defense mechanisms and Freud's id, ego, superego
Therapy · IHBAS 2024 Q30, Q49

From scratch

Sigmund Freud proposed the structural model of personality in The Ego and the Id (1923). It sits on top of his earlier topographical model (1900: conscious, preconscious, unconscious). The structural model divides the psyche into three agencies that are always negotiating with one another.

The id is present from birth, is entirely unconscious, and is the reservoir of instinctual drives (libido or Eros, and the aggressive or death drive, Thanatos). It runs on the pleasure principle: immediate gratification, no logic, no sense of time. Its mode of thinking is primary process (wish-fulfilling imagery, as in dreams). The ego develops out of the id through contact with the outside world. It runs on the reality principle and secondary process thinking (logic, planning, delay of gratification). It is partly conscious, partly unconscious, and it is the executive: it mediates between the id's desires, the superego's moral demands, and the constraints of reality. The superego forms around age 5 to 6, at the resolution of the Oedipus complex, by internalising parental and social standards. It has two parts: the conscience (punishes with guilt) and the ego ideal (rewards with pride). It runs on what is sometimes called the morality principle and can be as irrational and absolute as the id.

When the demands of id, superego and reality clash, the ego experiences anxiety (Freud named reality, neurotic and moral anxiety). To protect itself, the ego uses defense mechanisms: unconscious strategies that distort or deny reality to reduce anxiety. Freud described several; his daughter Anna Freud systematised them in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936). George Vaillant later arranged them in a maturity hierarchy: psychotic (denial of external reality, distortion, delusional projection), immature (projection, acting out, passive aggression), neurotic (repression, displacement, reaction formation, intellectualisation, rationalisation) and mature (sublimation, humour, altruism, suppression, anticipation).

Core mechanisms with examples: Repression (the basic defense): pushing a threatening memory or wish out of awareness, e.g. no memory of a childhood trauma. Projection: attributing your own unacceptable impulse to someone else, e.g. a man who is attracted to a colleague accuses her of flirting with him. Displacement: redirecting an impulse from a threatening target to a safer one, e.g. shouted at by the boss, you shout at your younger brother. Reaction formation: converting an impulse into its exaggerated opposite, e.g. excessive sweetness toward a hated sibling. Rationalisation: giving a plausible but false reason, e.g. "I did not want that job anyway" (sour grapes). Sublimation: channelling an impulse into a socially valued outlet, e.g. aggression into surgery or boxing; Freud saw it as the only fully healthy defense. Regression: retreating to an earlier developmental stage. Denial: refusing to accept an external reality. Intellectualisation: discussing a painful event in cold abstract terms. Identification with the aggressor (Anna Freud): taking on the traits of a feared person.

  • "Self" as an option belongs to Rogers or Jung, not to Freud's structural model.
  • Projection vs displacement: look for whether the feeling is attributed to someone (projection) or discharged on someone (displacement).
  • Defenses are unconscious by definition; a stem describing deliberate avoidance points to suppression or coping, not a classic defense.
  • The systematiser of defenses is Anna Freud (1936), not Sigmund; the maturity hierarchy is Vaillant.
  • Structural model (id/ego/superego, 1923) is not the topographical model (conscious/preconscious/unconscious, 1900).

32Rogers' client-centred core conditions
Therapy · IHBAS 2024 Q48

From scratch

Carl Rogers founded non-directive therapy (Counseling and Psychotherapy, 1942), renamed it client-centred therapy (Client-Centered Therapy, 1951) and later person-centred approach. It is a pillar of humanistic psychology, the "third force" alongside psychoanalysis and behaviourism (with Maslow).

His theory of personality: every organism has an actualising tendency, an innate drive toward growth and self-actualisation. Each person builds a self-concept. Children need positive regard from others; when love is given only under conditions of worth ("I love you when you get good marks"), the child learns to disown experiences that do not fit. The gap between the self-concept (or real self) and actual experience is called incongruence; the gap between real self and ideal self produces anxiety and maladjustment. The goal is the fully functioning person.

Therapy therefore does not interpret, advise or teach. It provides a relationship in which the client's own actualising tendency can resume. In his 1957 paper "The necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic personality change" Rogers listed six conditions; three, provided by the therapist, are the famous core conditions: (1) Congruence or genuineness: the therapist is real, transparent, not hiding behind a professional facade. (2) Unconditional positive regard (UPR): warm, non-judgemental acceptance of the client as a person, with no conditions of worth. This directly undoes the conditions of worth that created the problem, which is why it is usually called the critical or defining feature. (3) Accurate empathic understanding: sensing the client's inner world "as if" it were one's own, without losing the "as if" quality, and communicating that understanding back. The other three conditions: two persons in psychological contact, the client is in a state of incongruence (vulnerable, anxious), and the client perceives the therapist's UPR and empathy at least to a minimal degree.

Key technique: reflection of feeling (and restatement, clarification). Example: client says "Nobody at home ever listens to me." Therapist: "You feel unheard and alone, even with your own family." No advice, no interpretation of childhood causes. Rogers also pioneered recording sessions for research and the Q-sort technique to measure self versus ideal-self discrepancy.

33Bandura (Bobo doll) and theorist-to-theory matching
Therapy · IHBAS 2024 Q20, Q7

From scratch

Albert Bandura showed that people learn by watching others, without performing the behaviour or being reinforced themselves. This is observational learning (also modelling, vicarious learning). In the Bobo doll experiments (Bandura, Ross and Ross, 1961, 1963) nursery-school children watched an adult model either attack an inflatable Bobo doll (hitting it with a mallet, kicking, saying "Sock him in the nose") or play quietly. Children who saw the aggressive model imitated the specific novel acts. In the 1965 version, the model was rewarded, punished or had no consequence; children who saw the model punished imitated less, but when later offered rewards to imitate, all groups reproduced the acts equally. That proved the learning versus performance distinction: learning happened regardless; reinforcement only affected performance.

Bandura's four processes of observational learning: attention, retention, reproduction (motor capability) and motivation. Related concepts: vicarious reinforcement and vicarious punishment; reciprocal determinism (person, behaviour and environment influence each other); self-efficacy (1977, belief in one's ability to succeed). His theory was renamed social cognitive theory in 1986. With Richard Walters he wrote Social Learning and Personality Development (1963), which is why "Bandura and Walters" = later social learning theory.

John Dollard and Neal Miller (Yale), in Social Learning and Imitation (1941) and Personality and Psychotherapy (1950), translated Freudian ideas into Hullian drive-reduction learning theory. Their four elements of learning are drive, cue, response, reward. They described approach-avoidance conflicts and the frustration-aggression hypothesis (with Doob, Mowrer and Sears, 1939). Because their work came first and still required reinforcement of the imitator, it is called early social learning theory.

B. F. Skinner developed operant conditioning and the philosophy of radical behaviourism: even private events (thoughts, feelings) are behaviour governed by environmental contingencies, with no need for mental causes. Abraham Maslow is the humanistic theorist of the hierarchy of needs and self-actualisation. J. B. Watson is founder of (methodological) behaviourism (1913).

34Hypnosis
Therapy · IHBAS 2024 Q39, Q55

From scratch

Hypnosis is a procedure in which a hypnotist suggests changes in sensation, perception, thought or behaviour, and a participant responds. Its chief characteristic is increased suggestibility: heightened responsiveness to suggestion together with narrowed, focused attention and reduced critical judgement. Induction usually involves relaxation and focusing on one stimulus, so arousal goes down, not up. EEG during hypnosis resembles relaxed wakefulness, not sleep.

History in order: Franz Anton Mesmer (1770s, Vienna and Paris) claimed cures through "animal magnetism"; a 1784 French commission (including Benjamin Franklin) attributed the effects to imagination. James Braid, a Scottish surgeon, coined "hypnosis" (from Greek hypnos, sleep) in 1843 and recast it as focused attention. Jean-Martin Charcot at the Salpetriere in Paris showed in dramatic demonstrations that hysterical symptoms (paralysis, anaesthesia, loss of feeling in limbs, fainting) could be produced and removed by hypnosis and suggestion; he believed only hysterics could be hypnotised. The rival Nancy school (Liebeault and Bernheim) argued hypnosis is simply suggestion and normal people are hypnotisable, and Bernheim won that debate. Freud studied with Charcot (1885 to 1886), used hypnosis with Breuer (Studies on Hysteria, 1895, Anna O. and the cathartic method), then abandoned it for free association. Pierre Janet developed dissociation theory from hypnotic work.

Two modern theories. Neodissociation theory (Ernest Hilgard, 1970s): consciousness splits; a "hidden observer" remains aware of pain while the hypnotised person reports none (ice-water experiments). Social-cognitive or role theory (Theodore Sarbin, Nicholas Spanos, Barber, Kirsch): hypnotised people are enacting the role of a hypnotised person, driven by expectations and motivation, not an altered state. Hypnotisability is a stable trait measured by the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale (Weitzenhoffer and Hilgard) and the Harvard Group Scale; roughly 10 to 15 percent are highly hypnotisable.

What hypnosis can and cannot do: effective for pain relief (hypnoanalgesia), as an adjunct in anxiety, IBS and habit control; produces posthypnotic suggestion and posthypnotic amnesia. It does not reliably enhance memory accuracy (it increases confidence and false memories, hence hypnotically refreshed testimony is suspect), does not give superhuman strength, and generally cannot make people act against their values. Example: told "your arm is becoming light and will float up", a highly hypnotisable person's arm rises and feels involuntary.

35Double bind (Bateson) and countertransference
Therapy · IHBAS 2024 Q98, Q99

From scratch

Double bind: introduced by Gregory Bateson with Don Jackson, Jay Haley and John Weakland in "Toward a theory of schizophrenia" (1956), from the Palo Alto (later Mental Research Institute) group. A double bind is a communication in which a person, usually a child, receives two contradictory messages at different levels from a significant other, typically a verbal message contradicted by a non-verbal one, while a third, implicit injunction forbids commenting on the contradiction or leaving the field. Whatever the child does is wrong. Classic example: a mother says "Come and give me a hug, I love you", then stiffens and pulls away when the child approaches; if the child withdraws she says "Don't you love your mother?" Bateson proposed that repeated exposure could contribute to schizophrenia. The causal claim is no longer accepted (schizophrenia is understood through the stress-vulnerability model with strong genetic loading), but the concept shaped family systems and communication therapy.

Related family-theory concepts for schizophrenia, often put together in MCQs: schizophrenogenic mother (Frieda Fromm-Reichmann, 1948: cold, domineering, rejecting), marital schism and marital skew (Theodore Lidz), pseudomutuality and pseudohostility (Lyman Wynne), and expressed emotion (George Brown, Vaughn and Leff: criticism, hostility, emotional overinvolvement), which predicts relapse, not onset, and is the one with strong empirical support.

Transference (Freud): the patient unconsciously redirects feelings from earlier significant figures (usually parents) onto the therapist. Positive transference = affection, idealisation; negative transference = hostility, distrust. Analysis of transference is central to psychoanalysis. Countertransference (Freud, 1910): the therapist's emotional reactions to the patient, originally from the therapist's own unresolved conflicts. Freud saw it as an obstacle to be overcome by the analyst's own analysis; later writers (Paula Heimann, 1950; Racker; Winnicott's "Hate in the countertransference", 1949) treated it as a useful clue to the patient's inner world. It can be positive countertransference (over-liking, rescuing) or negative countertransference (irritation, boredom, dislike, hostility toward the patient).

The key to all four transference terms: first ask whose feelings (patient = transference, therapist = countertransference), then ask which direction (warm = positive, hostile = negative). Example: a therapist feels impatient and cold toward a demanding client who reminds him of his critical father: negative countertransference.

36Exposure and response prevention (ERP) and its principle
Therapy · IHBAS 2024 Q100

From scratch

Exposure and response prevention (ERP, also ExRP) is the first-line psychological treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder. It was pioneered by Victor Meyer (1966, Middlesex Hospital, London, "apotrepic therapy") and developed by Isaac Marks, Stanley Rachman and especially Edna Foa (University of Pennsylvania). Its rationale comes from Mowrer's two-factor theory (1947, 1960): fear of a stimulus is acquired by classical conditioning and maintained by operant (negative) reinforcement, because escape or avoidance reduces anxiety. In OCD, obsessions raise anxiety and compulsions (washing, checking, mental rituals) bring quick relief, which negatively reinforces the compulsion and prevents the person from ever learning that the feared outcome would not occur.

ERP has two parts. Exposure: systematic, prolonged contact with the feared stimulus, in vivo or imaginal (touching a "contaminated" doorknob, leaving the stove unchecked). Response prevention: the client refrains from the ritual and from subtle neutralising or reassurance seeking. Exposure is usually graded up a hierarchy using SUDS (subjective units of distress, Wolpe's 0 to 100 scale). Example: a patient with contamination fears touches a bathroom floor, does not wash for an hour, and records SUDS falling from 85 to 30 over the session and lower again across sessions.

The principle behind it is habituation and extinction. Habituation is the decrease in response to a repeated or prolonged stimulus: anxiety, if not escaped, rises, peaks and then declines on its own (within-session habituation), and starts lower each session (between-session habituation). Extinction is the weakening of a conditioned response when the CS occurs without the UCS (respondent extinction), and the weakening of the ritual when it no longer produces relief (operant extinction). Foa and Kozak's emotional processing theory (1986) adds that the fear structure must be activated and then corrected with disconfirming information. The newer inhibitory learning model (Michelle Craske, 2008, 2014) argues that extinction creates a new "safe" association that competes with the old one, with expectancy violation as the key; this is why relapse can happen through spontaneous recovery, renewal and reinstatement.

Contrast with systematic desensitisation (Joseph Wolpe, 1958), based on reciprocal inhibition or counterconditioning: a hierarchy is climbed while the client is deeply relaxed (Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation), so a response incompatible with anxiety replaces it. ERP uses no competing response; the anxiety is allowed to run its course. Flooding (in vivo, maximal exposure from the start) and implosion (Stampfl, imaginal and exaggerated, with psychodynamic themes) are also extinction-based exposure methods.

37Reliability vs validity
Assessment · IHBAS 2024 Q75

From scratch

Every psychological test is judged on two core psychometric properties. Reliability is the consistency of a measure: if you measured the same person again, under similar conditions, would you get the same score? Validity is the accuracy or meaningfulness of a measure: does the test actually measure what it claims to measure, and do the inferences drawn from its scores hold up? The modern framework comes from classical test theory (Spearman, early 1900s), in which every observed score X = true score T + error E. Reliability is the proportion of observed-score variance that is true-score variance, so it runs from 0 to 1; most clinical tests aim for 0.80 or higher, and 0.90 for high-stakes individual decisions.

Types of reliability. (1) Test-retest: give the same test twice and correlate the scores; the result is a coefficient of stability, threatened by practice and memory effects and by real change over the interval. (2) Parallel or alternate forms: two equivalent versions given to the same people; a coefficient of equivalence. (3) Split-half: split one test into two halves (usually odd versus even items), correlate them, then correct upward with the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula, because a half-length test underestimates reliability. (4) Internal consistency: Kuder-Richardson formulas (KR-20, KR-21, Kuder and Richardson 1937) for dichotomous items, and Cronbach's alpha (Cronbach 1951) for multi-point items. (5) Inter-rater (inter-scorer) reliability: agreement between two scorers, indexed by percentage agreement, Cohen's kappa (chance-corrected) or the intraclass correlation. The standard error of measurement (SEM) translates reliability into a band of error around an individual's score: the higher the reliability, the smaller the SEM.

Types of validity. (1) Face validity: the test looks like it measures the construct to the test-taker; it is not a technical form of validity. (2) Content validity: the items adequately sample the whole domain, judged by experts (for example, an anxiety scale that covers cognitive, somatic and behavioural symptoms). (3) Criterion-related validity: scores correlate with an external criterion, either concurrent (criterion measured at the same time, such as a new depression scale against a psychiatrist's diagnosis today) or predictive (criterion measured later, such as an entrance test against final-year marks). (4) Construct validity (Cronbach and Meehl 1955): the test behaves as theory predicts; evidence includes convergent validity (high correlation with measures of the same construct) and discriminant/divergent validity (low correlation with unrelated constructs), formalised in Campbell and Fiske's (1959) multitrait-multimethod matrix. Messick (1989) argued all validity is ultimately construct validity.

The relationship. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. An unreliable test cannot be valid, because random error cannot correlate with anything; the validity coefficient can never exceed the square root of the reliability coefficient. But a test can be perfectly reliable and still invalid. The classic picture is the dartboard: tight clustering away from the bullseye is reliable but not valid; tight clustering on the bullseye is both. Example: a bathroom scale that always reads 5 kg too heavy is reliable (consistent) but not valid (inaccurate).

Do not confuse test validity with research validity. Internal validity (Campbell and Stanley 1963) is whether the independent variable truly caused the change in the dependent variable; external validity is whether findings generalise to other people, settings and times. Generalizability theory (Cronbach et al. 1972) is an extension of reliability theory that splits error into multiple sources (raters, occasions, items).

38Rorschach and projective tests
Assessment · IHBAS 2024 Q17

From scratch

A projective test presents an ambiguous, unstructured stimulus and asks the person to interpret it. The projective hypothesis holds that, because the stimulus has no single correct meaning, people impose their own needs, conflicts, defences and thought organisation onto it. Lawrence Frank (1939) coined the term "projective methods". The approach grew from psychoanalytic theory, and these tests are contrasted with objective (self-report) inventories like the MMPI, which have structured items, fixed response options and standardised scoring.

The Rorschach Inkblot Test. Swiss psychiatrist Hermann Rorschach published it in Psychodiagnostik (1921). It has 10 symmetrical inkblot cards: five achromatic (black and grey: I, IV, V, VI, VII), two black-and-red (II, III), and three fully chromatic (VIII, IX, X). Administration has two phases: the free association (response) phase, where the person says what each blot might be, and the inquiry phase, where the examiner asks where and what made it look that way. Responses are coded for location (W whole, D common detail, Dd unusual detail, S white space), determinants (form F, colour C, human movement M, shading, texture), content (human H, animal A, anatomy etc.), popular responses (P) and form quality (how well the percept fits the blot). Poor form quality and unusual verbalisations index disordered thinking, which is why the test is classically described as assessing thought processes and perceptual-cognitive organisation. The most used scoring system is John Exner's Comprehensive System (1974 onward), now succeeded by R-PAS (Rorschach Performance Assessment System, Meyer et al. 2011). Exner's Perceptual-Thinking Index is used to detect psychotic thought disorder. Earlier systems: Beck, Klopfer, Hertz, Piotrowski, Rapaport-Schafer.

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT). Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan (1935), Harvard, based on Murray's need-press theory. 31 cards (30 pictures plus one blank card, card 16); typically about 20 are given in two sessions. The person tells a story: what led up to the scene, what is happening, what the characters feel and think, how it ends. It assesses needs, motives, interpersonal themes rather than thought organisation. Children's versions: CAT (Bellak, animal figures), and the Indian adaptation by Uma Chowdhury (Indian TAT, CAT).

Other projectives, using Lindzey's (1959) classification by response type: association (Rorschach, Holtzman Inkblot Technique with 45 blots, two parallel forms, one response per card; Jung's word association test), construction (TAT, CAT), completion (Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank, Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Study), choice or ordering (Szondi test), and expression (Draw-a-Person, Machover 1949; House-Tree-Person, Buck 1948; Kinetic Family Drawing).

Strengths and criticisms. Projectives are hard to fake, bypass defensiveness and give rich idiographic material. But they have been criticised for weak inter-scorer reliability, inconsistent norms and modest validity (Lilienfeld, Wood and Garb 2000). The Comprehensive System and R-PAS were built specifically to improve reliability and norms. Example: a patient who sees "a bleeding face melting into the walls" on card II with poor form quality suggests perceptual distortion; a TAT story full of failure and punishment suggests a theme of guilt.

39Rater errors: halo, leniency, central tendency
Assessment · IHBAS 2024 Q54, Q15

From scratch

Whenever one person rates another (interviews, performance appraisals, rating scales, clinical impressions), the ratings are contaminated by systematic biases in the rater, not just random noise. These rater errors lower the reliability and validity of rating scales and are a standard topic in psychological assessment (Anastasi, Guilford) and in industrial-organisational psychology.

Halo effect (halo error). Named by Edward Thorndike (1920), who found that military officers' ratings of soldiers on separate traits (physique, intelligence, leadership, character) were far too highly correlated. Definition: the tendency to let one salient trait or a general overall impression colour the ratings of all other, unrelated traits. If a candidate is attractive and articulate, the interviewer also rates them as intelligent, honest and hard-working. The negative version is the horn (reverse halo) effect: one bad trait drags all ratings down. In social psychology the halo effect is a person-perception bias (Asch's central traits such as warm/cold work similarly; Dion, Berscheid and Walster 1972 showed "what is beautiful is good").

Leniency (generosity) error: rating everyone too favourably, crowding scores at the high end. Severity (strictness) error: rating everyone too harshly. Central tendency error: avoiding extremes and putting everyone in the middle ("average" on every item), often because the rater does not know the ratee well or wants to avoid justifying extreme ratings. All three restrict the range of ratings, so they reduce discrimination between people.

Other errors. Contrast error: rating someone relative to the person rated just before (an average candidate looks excellent after a weak one), or rating others as opposite to oneself. Similar-to-me error: rating higher those who resemble the rater in background or attitudes. Logical error (Guilford): rating traits similarly because the rater believes they logically go together (e.g., assuming the intelligent are also responsible). Proximity error: items close together on the form get similar ratings. Primacy (first-impression) and recency errors: early information or the most recent events dominate the judgment.

Remedies. Behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS), forced-choice and forced-distribution methods (counter leniency and central tendency), rating all ratees on one trait at a time (counters halo), multiple independent raters, and rater training. Example: a ward supervisor who rates a nurse highly on punctuality, empathy and documentation simply because she is cheerful is committing halo error; one who gives every nurse 3 out of 5 is committing central tendency error.

  • "Judging others based on group membership" is stereotyping, not halo.

41Graphs and frequency distributions ★
Biostats / Research · IHBAS 2024 Q21, Q53, Q38, Q37

From scratch

Descriptive statistics begins by organising raw data. Raw scores are the original, unprocessed measurements. Listing each value with how often it occurs gives a frequency distribution. When there are many distinct values, scores are collapsed into class intervals (e.g., 40 to 44, 45 to 49) to form a grouped frequency distribution. Each interval has stated limits (40 and 44), exact or real limits (39.5 and 44.5) and a midpoint (42). Common rules (King and Minium, Garrett): use roughly 10 to 20 intervals of equal width, and choose convenient widths such as 2, 3, 5 or 10. Because the researcher chooses the width and starting point, a set of raw scores does not result in a unique set of grouped scores: the same data can be grouped many ways, and grouping loses information (you no longer know exact scores within an interval).

Kinds of frequency. Frequency (f) is the count in a category. Cumulative frequency (cf) is the running total up to the upper limit of each interval, used for percentiles and medians. Relative frequency is f divided by N, expressed as a proportion (or multiplied by 100 as a percentage). Relative frequencies are especially useful when comparing distributions with different numbers of cases: if Class A has 200 students and Class B has 50, raw counts are not comparable, but proportions put both on the same base. Cumulative relative frequency (cumulative percentage) combines both ideas.

Graphs. (1) Histogram: a series of adjacent rectangles, one per class interval, with height equal to frequency; bars touch because the variable is continuous (interval or ratio data). (2) Bar graph: separated bars for discrete or categorical (nominal, ordinal) data such as diagnosis type. (3) Frequency polygon: points plotted at interval midpoints joined by straight lines, closed at both ends; best for comparing two distributions on one graph. (4) Ogive: the cumulative frequency (or cumulative percentage) curve, S-shaped, plotted at upper exact limits; used to read percentiles and the median. (5) Pie chart: sectors showing parts of a whole for categorical data. (6) Stem-and-leaf display (Tukey 1977): keeps raw values while showing shape.

Scatterplot (scatter diagram). Used for bivariate correlational data: each person is one point, X score on the horizontal axis and Y score on the vertical. An upward-sloping cloud means positive correlation, downward means negative, a shapeless cloud means near zero; the tighter the cloud around a line, the stronger the relationship. Francis Galton used such plots in the 1880s to study regression, and Karl Pearson formalised the correlation coefficient r. Scatterplots also reveal curvilinearity (e.g., the inverted-U of the Yerkes-Dodson law) and outliers that r alone would hide.

Shapes. Normal (bell curve, symmetric, mean = median = mode), positively skewed (tail to the right, mean above median), negatively skewed (tail to the left), and kurtosis: leptokurtic (peaked), mesokurtic (normal), platykurtic (flat).

42Research designs
Biostats / Research · IHBAS 2024 Q24, Q29, Q60, Q81, Q86

From scratch

A research design is the plan that links a question to the data. The central divide is between experimental designs, which can establish cause and effect, and non-experimental designs (correlational, survey, observational, case study), which describe or predict but cannot prove causation. In a true experiment the researcher (1) manipulates an independent variable (IV), (2) measures its effect on a dependent variable (DV), and (3) controls other influences, chiefly through random assignment. Example: 60 patients with depression are randomly assigned to CBT or a waitlist; if the CBT group improves more on the BDI, the improvement can be attributed to CBT.

Randomisation vs random sampling. Random sampling (random selection) is how people are drawn from the population into the study, so every member has an equal chance of being included; it protects external validity (generalisation). Randomisation (random assignment) is how participants already in the study are allocated to treatment groups so each has an equal probability of any group; it equates groups on all pre-existing variables, known and unknown, and protects internal validity. R. A. Fisher (1935, The Design of Experiments) made randomisation the foundation of experimental design. Stratification divides the population into subgroups (strata) first, then samples within each.

Confounding variables. A confound is an extraneous variable that is related to both the IV and the DV, so its effect cannot be separated from the IV's. If the CBT group happened to be younger and the young recover faster, age confounds the result. Controls: random assignment, holding variables constant, matching, counterbalancing (for order effects in within-subjects designs), blinding (single-blind, double-blind against expectancy and demand characteristics), and statistical control (ANCOVA). A quasi-experiment (Campbell and Stanley 1963; Cook and Campbell 1979) has manipulation or comparison groups but no random assignment, so confounds remain plausible.

Developmental designs. To study change with age: a cross-sectional design tests different age groups at one time (20-, 40-, 60-year-olds in 2026). It is quick and cheap but confounds age with cohort: the groups differ in generation, schooling, nutrition and historical experience, so differences may be cohort differences, not ageing (this is why early cross-sectional studies exaggerated IQ decline). A longitudinal design follows the same people over time, directly showing intra-individual change and stability, but it is slow and costly, and suffers attrition (dropout), practice effects and time-of-measurement effects. K. Warner Schaie's Seattle Longitudinal Study used a cross-sequential (sequential) design, combining both, to separate age, cohort and time effects.

Other designs. Correlational: measures how variables co-vary (r), no manipulation, cannot rule out third variables or reverse causation. Case study: intensive study of one person (Freud's Anna O, H.M.), rich but low generalisability. Between-subjects (different people per condition) vs within-subjects / repeated measures (same people in all conditions, needs counterbalancing). Single-case experimental designs (ABA, ABAB, multiple baseline) are used in behaviour therapy.

43Chi-square df and p-value
Biostats / Research · IHBAS 2024 Q84, Q90

From scratch

The chi-square test (χ²), introduced by Karl Pearson (1900), is a non-parametric test for frequency (count) data in nominal or ordinal categories. It compares observed frequencies (O) with the expected frequencies (E) predicted by the null hypothesis: χ² = Σ (O minus E)² / E. If observed counts are close to expected, χ² is small; large discrepancies give a large χ² and a small p-value.

Two main uses. (1) Goodness of fit (one variable): do observed counts fit a hypothesised distribution? Example: are patients equally spread across four OPD days? Here df = k minus 1, where k is the number of categories (so 4 days gives df = 3). (2) Test of independence (two variables in a contingency table): is gender related to diagnosis? Expected frequency for each cell = (row total × column total) / N, and df = (number of rows minus 1) × (number of columns minus 1). A 2 × 2 table has df = 1; a 3 × 4 table has df = 2 × 3 = 6. The logic: once the row and column totals are fixed, only (r minus 1)(c minus 1) cells are free to vary. Assumptions: independent observations (each person counted once), and expected frequency of at least 5 in each cell (in practice no more than 20% of cells below 5). For a 2 × 2 table with small expected counts, use Yates' correction for continuity or Fisher's exact test. The chi-square distribution is positively skewed and its mean equals its df. Effect size for association: phi (2 × 2) or Cramér's V.

The p-value. In null hypothesis significance testing (Fisher; Neyman and Pearson), you assume H0 (no effect, no association). The p-value is the probability of obtaining data as extreme as, or more extreme than, those observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true: P(data | H0). If p is less than the pre-set alpha (usually 0.05), the result is "statistically significant" and H0 is rejected. Example: χ²(1) = 6.2, p = .013 means that if gender and diagnosis were truly unrelated, a discrepancy this large would arise only about 1.3% of the time by sampling error.

What p is not. It is not the probability that H0 is true, not the probability that the result was "due to chance", not 1 minus the probability the alternative hypothesis is true, and not the size or importance of the effect. A tiny effect in a huge sample can have p below .001. That is why the APA (and the ASA statement, 2016) asks for effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside p.

Related terms. Alpha = probability of a Type I error (rejecting a true H0, false positive). Beta = probability of a Type II error (failing to reject a false H0, false negative). Power = 1 minus beta, the probability of detecting a real effect; it rises with larger N, larger effect size and higher alpha (Cohen recommended 0.80).

MCQ traps

  • (N minus 1) is the df for goodness-of-fit with N categories or for a one-sample t; it is not the contingency-table formula.
  • (N minus 2) is the df for correlation or independent-samples t, a different test.
  • (columns minus 2)(rows minus 2) is a made-up formula; always subtract 1.
  • "The probability that the null hypothesis is true" is the most popular wrong p-value definition; p is about the data given H0, not H0 given the data.
  • p is not the effect size and not power; a significant result can be trivial in size.
  • Chi-square is non-parametric and uses frequencies, never means.

  • Q90: What is the p-value? → The probability of obtaining the observed data if the null hypothesis is true; it is a conditional probability of the data, not of the hypothesis.
44Meta-analysis
Biostats / Research · IHBAS 2024 Q87

From scratch

Meta-analysis is a quantitative method for statistically combining the results of many independent studies on the same question to produce an overall estimate of an effect. The term was coined by Gene V. Glass (1976), who called it "the analysis of analyses". Its landmark use in clinical psychology was Smith and Glass (1977), which pooled about 375 psychotherapy outcome studies and found that the average treated client was better off than about 75% of untreated controls (mean effect size around 0.68); Smith, Glass and Miller (1980) extended this to 475 studies (effect size about 0.85). This was a direct empirical reply to Hans Eysenck's (1952) claim that psychotherapy was no better than spontaneous remission; Eysenck in turn dismissed meta-analysis as "mega-silliness" and "garbage in, garbage out".

How it works, step by step. (1) Define a precise question (e.g., does CBT reduce depression compared with waitlist?). (2) Conduct a systematic literature search with explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria (reported using PRISMA guidelines). (3) Extract a common effect size from each study: Cohen's d or Hedges' g for mean differences (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large), r for correlations, odds ratio or risk ratio for binary outcomes. (4) Pool them as a weighted average, giving more weight to larger, more precise studies (inverse-variance weighting). (5) Assess heterogeneity (Cochran's Q, the I² statistic) and choose a fixed-effect model (one true effect, differences are sampling error) or a random-effects model (true effects vary across studies). (6) Run moderator analyses (does the effect differ by age, severity, therapist training?). (7) Check publication bias.

Displays and biases. A forest plot shows each study's effect and confidence interval plus the pooled diamond. A funnel plot plots effect size against study precision; asymmetry suggests publication bias, because small non-significant studies tend to stay unpublished. Robert Rosenthal (1979) named this the file-drawer problem and proposed the fail-safe N (how many null studies would be needed to cancel the effect).

Systematic review vs meta-analysis. A systematic review is the structured, reproducible search and appraisal of all relevant studies; a meta-analysis is the statistical pooling step that may (or may not) follow. A narrative review summarises studies in words, without systematic search or statistics. Meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials sit at the top of the evidence hierarchy (Cochrane Collaboration), which is why they guide evidence-based practice and clinical guidelines (e.g., NICE). Criticisms: "apples and oranges" (mixing studies that measure different things), garbage in garbage out (poor-quality studies), and publication bias.

Key point: meta-analysis uses secondary data; it never collects new primary data and is not one large new experiment. Example: a 2026 meta-analysis of 40 RCTs on mindfulness for anxiety reporting pooled g = 0.45, I² = 60% (moderate heterogeneity) and a funnel plot hinting at missing small negative studies.

  • A systematic review is not automatically a meta-analysis; only the statistical pooling makes it one.
