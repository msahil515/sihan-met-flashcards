1 · Introduction to Neuropsychology

Neuropsychology is the study of brain function and behaviour.

Neuroplasticity

  • Experience expectant — development according to expected growth pattern.
  • Experience dependent — plasticity dependent on new learning, based on new experiences.

History of the mind–brain question

  • Mentalism (Aristotle) — a non-material psyche is responsible for behaviour.
  • Dualism (Descartes) — the body is like a machine (only controls reflex); the mind controls the machine.
  • Materialism (Darwin) — the mind is just our brain; neuroplasticity is based on evolution, the capacity to change.

Research findings

  • Florenz and Galls challenged localization: removed the cortex of 3 dogs.
  • Young children can recover fast from brain damage (stem cells migrate).
  • In adults, new neurons (2%) are formed in the hippocampus and striatum.
  • Functioning improves with physiotherapy.

2 · Neuropsychological Tests: goals, categories, approaches

Neuropsychological testing allows for an in-depth analysis of functional limitation associated with brain dysfunction, and is required for diagnosis by some diagnostic criteria for neurological disorders.

Goals (Schaefer et al., 2023)

  • Establish a baseline.
  • Gauge an individual's cognitive strengths and weaknesses and aid in treatment planning.
  • Assist in establishing diagnoses.
  • Establish lateralization and possible etiology of brain lesions.
  • Track progress in rehab.
  • Determination of disability, return to work/school, driving ability or legal purposes.

Categories assessed

Abstract · Attention · Daily activities · Emotional Distress · Language · Memory · Motor skills · Orientation · Sensation · Perception · Visuospatial reasoning.

Sensitivity and Specificity

Key. Sensitivity = a test's ability to correctly identify people who have a disease. Specificity = a test's ability to correctly identify people who do not have a disease.

Approaches to Neuropsychological tests

3 · Halstead–Reitan Neuropsychological Test Battery

Founder note. The Halstead-Reitan battery arose from one of the first neuropsychology labs in America; it proposed the theory of "biological intelligence".

Introduction

  • Created one of the first neuropsychology labs in America.
  • First neuropsychological test to have a factor-analytic process.
  • Was made to test head trauma.
  • Created: 1935. Time taken: 6–8 hours.
  • 1956: The Halstead-Reitan Test Battery begins to emerge as the premiere test battery in neuropsychology.
  • Now it can detect tremors, cerebrovascular accidents, infections, degenerative diseases, learning disabilities, neurological disorders.
  • Proposed theory of "biological intelligence".

Extra information

  • Total 7 scores.
  • Removed: critical flicker fusion test, time sense test.
  • For ages: 15+.
  • Based on T-score value.
  • 6-point score.

Scales / Measures

  1. Category test — measures abstract reasoning and concept formation; requires the examinee to find the rule for categorizing pictures of geometric shapes.
  2. Tactual performance test (Seguin) — measures kinesthetic and sensorimotor ability; requires a blindfolded examinee to place blocks in the appropriate cutout on an upright board with the dominant hand, then non-dominant hand, then both hands; also tests for incidental memory of blocks (score: total time, memory, location).
  3. Seashore Rhythm Test — measures attention and auditory perception; requires the examinee to indicate whether paired musical rhythms are same or different.
  4. Speech sound perception test — measures attention and auditory-visual synthesis; requires the examinee to pick from four choices the written version of taped nonsense words.
  5. Finger tapping test — measures motor speed; requires the examinee to tap a telegraph-key-like lever as quickly as possible for 10 seconds.

Other measures

  • Grip strength — requires the examinee to squeeze as hard as possible; separate trials with each hand.
  • Trail making, parts A, B — requires the examinee to connect numbers (part A) or numbers and letters in alternating order (part B) with a pencil line under pressure of time.
  • Tactile form recognition — requires the examinee to recognize simple shapes (e.g., triangle) placed in the palm of the hand.
  • Sensory-Perceptual Exam — requires the examinee to respond to simple bilateral sensory tasks, e.g., detecting which finger has been touched.
  • Aphasia Screening test — tasks include naming a pictured item (e.g., fork), repeating short phrases.
  • Supplementary: WAIS-III, WRAT-3, MMPI-2, memory tests such as Wechsler Memory Scale-III or Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test.

4 · Other Neuropsychological Tests

Luria-Nebraska Test Battery

Founder note. Luria-Nebraska — started in 1978, standardized by Golden in 1980. The AIIMS and NIMHANS batteries are based on this.
  • 269 items (0, 1, 2 scale); T scores are used.
  • In some areas 2 scores are assigned to the same task: speed and accuracy.
  • Can check any organic damage.
  • Total 14 scales: motor, rhythm, tactile, visual, receptive speech, expressive speech, writing, reading, arithmetic, memory, intellectual scales, pathognomonic — left and right hemisphere.

The Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS) / Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS)

Founder note. Developed by American psychologist Edgar Arnold Doll (1935).
  • Most widely used test to measure adaptive behaviour in existence; can be used from birth until death.
  • For younger children, there is a semi-structured interview with parents.
  • Domains: communication, daily living skills, socialization, motor skills, maladaptive behaviour (optional).
  • Can check for Autism Spectrum Disorder, deficits in adaptation, genetic disorders, traumatic brain injury.
  • Age 7–90: given only 3 subtests (not the motor skills subtest). Age 0–7: given all 4 subtests.

The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST)

Founder note. Given by E.A. Berg in 1948 (60 cards); Heaton modified it in 1981 to a total of 64 cards.
  • Order: Colour → Form → Number.
  • Can break rapport if the client is unable to answer correctly.
  • Bilateral prefrontal dysfunction patients are excited to give the test even if they give wrong answers.
  • Checks for perseveration.
  • Scored using Milner's criteria.

The Bender-Gestalt Test (BGT)

Founder note. Developed in 1938 by Lauretta Bender.
  • Tests: hand-eye coordination, perseveration, prefrontal damage.
  • Involves 13 designs for children less than 8 years and 12 designs for age 8 years and above.
  • Memory recall process; global scoring system.
  • Clients are given a lot of sheets, but asked to draw on only one.
  • Visuospatial difficulties identified (lesions in right parietal lobe).

Seguin Form Board Test

Founder note. Indian adaptation by J. Bharatraj; norms by S. K. Goel.
  • Part of the Halstead-Reitan test battery (1947).
  • Client is asked to draw the board from memory.
  • Earlier it was used to test eye-hand / visuomotor coordination.
  • Earlier given as 10 blocks of Gesell figures.

The Benton Visual Retention Test (BVRT)

Founder note. Developed by Arthur Benton.
  • Currently: 5th edition; includes 10 designs.
  • Clients are required to see and copy 10 designs immediately out of memory.
  • Time taken: 5 minutes.

The Seashore Test

Founder note. The tests were developed by Carl E. Seashore.
  • Used in the Halstead-Reitan Battery.
  • 3–5 errors considered normal.
  • Measures musical ability and cerebral function.
  • Seashore Rhythm Test — a subtest of the Seashore Measures of Musical Talent that measures generalized cerebral function; age-related problems occur.

Stroop Test

  • Test of selective attention, response conflict and inhibition; errors indicate dysfunction.
  • Because of conceptual primacy, colour takes longer to recognize.
  • No age bias.
  • For checking smaller lesions, more items need to be added.
  • Indicative of frontal functioning; biggest test is 4 block/trial.

Tower Tests

Tests for frontal, planning and executive dysfunction. A measure of means-end analysis. Requires the client to reach the final goal in the least number of moves possible.

TestApparatusLeast number of moves possible
Tower of LondonUtilizes balls5
Tower of HanoiUtilizes rings6
  • Dependent on working backwards; one has to visualize; repeat-state avoidance.
  • Checks for inhibition of conflicting responses and working memory.
  • Prepotent: goal-subgoal conflict.

The Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF)

Founder note. Developed by Rey in 1941; standardized by Osterrieth in 1944.
  • Measures visuospatial construction ability, visual memory, and executive functioning by the prefrontal lobe.
  • 3 stages: copy, immediate recall, delayed recall.
  • Uses one-for-one diagram.
  • Checks for: fine motor coordination, visuospatial memory, planning and organization, attention and coordination.
  • Age-related bias may be present.

Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB)

  • Computerized test. Time taken: 40–45 mins.
  • Checks for dementia; frontal, temporal, hippocampal dysfunction.
  • Checks for organic as well as non-organic damage.
  • Independent of language and not sensitive to gender.
  • Subtests: psychomotor coordination and motor speed; reasoning and planning abilities; spatial memory; attention and vigilance.

Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)

  • Basic exam of cognitive screening; 11 items.
  • A score of 23/30 and lesser is considered pathological.
  • Domains: orientation, repetition, verbal recall, attention, calculation, language and visual construction.
  • Made in 1975, standardized in 1995. Time taken: 5–10 mins.
  • Used for people with neurocognitive disorder.
  • Drawbacks: heavily verbal; the test has a "floor effect" (can't tell high deterioration).

The Postgraduate Institute of Brain Dysfunction (PGIBBD)

Founder note. Given by Prashad and Verma, 1990; 2007.
  • Language: Hindi; available for all. For ages: 20–59 years. 19 domains.
  • 5 subtests:
    • PGI Memory scale: immediate, recent, very short term, long term, verbal, non-verbal.
    • Bhatia short scale: Koh's block design, Alexander Pass Along.
    • VAIS: 1. info, 2. digit span, 3. arithmetic, 4. verbal comprehension.
    • Nahor Benson test: organic brain pathology.
    • Bender Gestalt Test.

The Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS)

  • Given for the first time in 1945. 4th edition: 2009.
  • Working memory deficits indicate frontal lobe damage; long-term memory deficits indicate temporal lobe damage.
  • Checks for auditory, visual, visual working, immediate, and delayed memory.
  • Does not check much of: verbal memory or verbal working memory and remote memory.
  • Time taken: 80 minutes.
  • Checks for neurocognitive disorders (Korsakoff).

The AIIMS Neuropsychological Battery

Founder note. Given by Surya Gupta, 2000 — a Hindi adaptation of the Luria-Nebraska test.
  • Training only given by the author; very expensive.
  • 160 items; ages 15–80 years.
  • Used to assess cognitive function; has versions for younger individuals.
  • Scales: motor, tactile, visual, receptive speech, expressive speech, reading, writing, arithmetic, memory, intellectual processes, left hemisphere, right hemisphere, pathognomonic.

The NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery

Founder note. Version used: 2004 by Dr. Rao. Inspired by Luria-Nebraska.
  • The test is available but the manual is not.
  • Developed on the principles of localization and lateralization.
  • Checks for attention, visual search, mental search, psychomotor deficit, working memory (frontal lobe damage).
  • Also checks for temporal (BVRT) and parietal lobe (BGT) damage.
  • Includes 19 tests.
  • Popular tests: tower test, Stroop task, complex figure, finger tapping, WCST.

Stanford-Binet Test-5

  • Follows Cattell-Horn-Carroll theory; given in 2003.
  • 10 subtests; deviation IQ used; uses factor analysis.
  • Age: 2–85 years.

5 factor scores

  • Fluid reasoning — matrices, absurdities, inductive reasoning.
  • Knowledge — procedural, general knowledge, missing details, vocabulary, difficult words.
  • Quantitative reasoning — arithmetic problem, word problem, counting.
  • Visuospatial reasoning — puzzles, direction problems.
  • Working memory — words, sentences, short visual info.

Raven's Progressive Matrices

  • Coloured Progressive Matrices (36 cards).
  • Advanced Progressive Matrices (48 cards).
  • Standard Progressive Matrices (60 cards).
  • Power and speed test; measures Fluid Intelligence.
  • Mean = 100; SD = 15.

Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT)

Founder note. By Raymond Cattell.
  • Group test; speed and power test; 3 forms; only non-verbal test.
  • Different from other tests in reading, experience, language, speed/tempo, environmental experiences.
  • Can be given in groups.

The Kaufman Adolescent and Adult Intelligence Test (KAIT)

Founder note. Kaufman tests by Alan Kaufman.
  • Age: 11–85+.
  • Based on Cattell-Horn-Carroll theory (fluid and crystallized intelligence) and some part of Piaget's theory.
  • Culture biased.

Cognitive Assessment System-II (CAS-II)

  • Based on PASS theory (Das, Naglieri).
  • Only for adolescents; used for scholarship tests.
  • Contains the Naglieri Nonverbal Aptitude test.
  • Mean: 100, S.D.: 3.

The Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (KABC)

  • Based on PASS theory.
  • 4 global scales: Simultaneous, Successive, Mental Processing, Achievement.
  • Culture biased.

The Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test (KBIT)

  • Age: 4–90 years; both verbal and nonverbal.
  • Time taken: 20 minutes.
  • Mean: 100, SD: 15.

Culture Fair Tests

Draw A Person Test (DAPT)

  • 1926; performance test to measure observation and conceptual thinking.

Leiter International Performance Scale

  • Performance test; made for African groups; elimination of verbal instruction; no time limit.
  • Tests: reasoning, visualisation, attention, memory.

6 · Indian Intelligence Tests

Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence

  • Given in 1940; normed for ages 11–16 years. Performance test.
  • Includes:
    1. Koh's block design — speed and power.
    2. Alexander Pass along — speed and power.
    3. Pattern drawing — speed and power.
    4. Immediate memory: digit span — verbal and power.
    5. Picture construction.
  • Different norms for rural and urban population.
  • Can be only administered individually.
  • Not completely culture fair.

Binet Kamat Test

  • Bombay Karnataka version of the Binet Simon Scale; based on the 1916 Simon Binet test.
  • Used ratio IQ.
  • Ages: 3–22 years, separate norms.
  • Measures: language, reasoning, conceptual thinking, social intelligence, memory, visuomotor performance.
  • Heavily verbal — can't check subnormal intelligence.
  • Items are not appropriate to current times; easy to administer.
  • Severe and profound mental retardation.

Kulshreshtha Intelligence Test

  • 1960; Hindi adaptation of the Stanford-Binet test.
  • Ages: 2–22 years; each age level = 6 items.
  • Verbal and performance tests; uses ratio IQ.

Tests based on Wechsler's test

Wechsler Adult Performance Intelligence Scale (WAPIS)

Founder note. Made by Prabha Ramalingaswami (1974).
  • 11 subtests (from WAIS): 6 verbal, 5 non-verbal.
  • Picture completion and picture arrangement have been modified.

Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC)

Founder note. Arthur J. Malin of Nagpur reformed Wechsler's scales.
  • Ages: 6–15 years; 2–2.5 years.
  • 11 subtests:
    • 6 verbal: i. information, ii. general comprehension, iii. arithmetic, iv. analogy and similarity, v. vocabulary, vi. digit span.
    • 5 non-verbal: i. block design, ii. picture completion, iii. object assembly, iv. symbol coding, v. maze.

7 · Personality Tests: overview

  • Projective tests began in 1800.
  • 1st test: Word Association test — Galton, Kraepelin, Jung.
  • 1916: Carl Jung's first standardized word association test.
  • 1917: 1st objective test — Woodworth's Personal Data Sheet.

Types / categories of personality assessment

  • Observational tests — rating scales and interview technique. Rating scales: judgement about personality in some categories. Interviews: structured and unstructured.
  • Personality inventories (self-report: forced choice).
  • Non-projective tests, Observation.

Bell's Adjustment Inventory

  • 1934; to diagnose adjustment problems.
  • Home: satisfaction/dissatisfaction; health: shyness, introversion.
  • Student form and occupation form; 140 Qs.
  • Adapted in Indian languages.

California Personality Inventory

  • 1957, 1987; work-related inventory.
  • Measures behaviour in the organizational setting.

8 · Projective Tests

Association techniques

Rorschach Inkblot Test

Founder note. 1921, by Herman Rorschach; he created it while playing klexography.
Projective vs objective. The Rorschach is a projective association technique studying unconscious thoughts, unlike objective self-report inventories (MMPI, 16PF). Scoring is subjective and system-dependent (Exner etc.), not criterion-keyed.

Administration: 10 cards, 7×10 inches, shown one at a time in sequence.

CardTheme
iParanoia
iiViolence, communication, dominance
iiiSexual preference
ivFather card
vHostility, castration complex, schizophrenia
viIntimate relationships
viiMother card
viiiEmotions / complex situations
ixUncertainty / ambiguity
xLife situation / oral fixation

Phases:

  • Performance proper.
  • Enquiry: ask what, why, where it was seen.
  • Testing the limits: done to give participants with weird responses another chance.
  • Studies unconscious thoughts.
  • Average number of responses: 20–25; per card: 3–5.
  • Focus on: content of the story, feeling or tone, subject's behaviour apart from the responses.
  • Scoring system: Levey, Beck S., Hertz, Klopfer, Exner (1974). Latest scoring system by Exner: 1993.
  • Scoring based on: location, determinant, form quality, content, popular responses.

Blacky's Picture Test

  • Based on Freud's psychosexual stages; for children ages 5 and older; 11 cartoon stimuli.
  • In addition to requesting a story for each card, the examiner also presents multiple-choice questions based on stages of psychosexual development.

Word Association Test

Founder note. Also known as free association technique. First systematically described by Galton (1879); introduced into the psychological laboratory by Wundt; first applied to the study of abnormality by Emil Kraepelin; adapted for use as a projective technique by Carl Jung.

Construction tests

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

Founder note. Given by Henry Murray and Christina Morgan (1935, 43); based on Murray's theory of personology.
  • Can be both idiographic and nomothetic.
  • 19 cards, for male, female, boys, girls; total cards that can be given: 20.
  • Blank card: Card no. 16.
  • Bellack's scoring system is used.

Analysis: Hero · Needs of the hero · Thema · Outcome.

Needs of the hero:

  • Needs motivated by desire for power, property, prestige, knowledge, creativity: achievement, acquisition, aggression, construction, counteraction, dominance, exposition, recognition, understanding.
  • Needs motivated by affection, admiration, sympathy, love, dependence: affiliation, deference, nurturance, sex, succorance.
  • Needs motivated by desire for freedom, change, excitement, play: autonomy, change/travel adventure, excitation/dissipation, play.
  • Miscellaneous needs: abasement, blame avoidance, cognizance, harm avoidance, passivity, rejection, retention, sentience.

Presses:

  • Press of deprivation: acquisition, retention.
  • Press descriptive of an empty, alien, or rejecting environment: lack of things/opportunities/friends, human support; loss; rejection; uncongenial environment.
  • Press of coercion and restraint: dominance; imposed task, duty or training.
  • Press descriptive of a hostile aggressive environment: aggression.
  • Press of danger, injury, death: affliction, death of hero, physical danger, physical injury.
  • Press of friendliness, sympathy, respect, dependent, love: affiliation, deference, nurturance, sex, succorance.
  • Miscellaneous press: birth of offspring, claustrum, cognizance, example, exposition, luck.

3 levels of interpretation: descriptive level, interpretive level, diagnostic level.

Rosenzweig Picture Frustration Study

  • Consists of 24 cartoon pictures each portraying two persons in a frustrating situation.
  • Responses are scored as to whether, and to what degree, they indicate that the subject exhibits aggression towards the source of the frustration; assumes blame or guilt as the cause of frustration; or justifies, minimizes or denies the frustration.
  • The score is based on a total of nine factors derived from combinations of three types of aggression and three directions of aggression.
  • Three types of aggression: obstacle dominance, ego defense, need persistence.
  • Three directions of aggression: extragression, imagression, intragression.
  • What to ask: current situation; thoughts and feelings of the character; preceding events; outcome.

Choice or ordering tests: Szondi

Founder note. Developed by Leopold Szondi in 1935.
  • Intended to reveal a person's innermost suppressed traits, the parts of yourself you have pushed down into your subconscious.
  • Number of photos: 48. The test involves looking at a series of 48 photos of people with distinct expressions on their faces.
  • The patient picks the friendliest faces and the unfriendliest, the idea being that a person identifies with like people and that their choices from the pictures would represent traits of themselves.

Completion tests

Sentence Completion Test

  • Semi-projective. 1940: first given by Rohde and Hidreth.

Sack's Sentence Completion Test

Founder note. By Joseph M. Sacks.
  • Developed to explore specific clusters of attitudes or significant areas of an individual's life.
  • Number of items: 60.
  • 4 subscales: family, sex, relationships, self concepts — each measured on 15 different attitudes such as fears, guilt, and goals.
  • A rating sheet with the four appropriate stems rearranged under the 15 attitude headings allows a clinician to rate the examinee's responses on a continuum that ranges from no significant disturbance to severely disturbed.

Expressive tests

House Tree Person Test (HTP)

  • Asks for the drawing of a house, a tree, and a person, in order to evaluate them together and globally.
  • Person: who is the person; how old he is; what does he do best; what does he like least.
  • Tree: what kind of tree it is; how old it is; what season it is; has someone tried to cut it down; who waters it; does it have enough light to grow.
  • Usually administered on children.

Draw A Man Test

Founder note. Goodenough, 1926. Scoring: Chen-Sea (2000).
  • Patients are given a blank piece of paper (8.5×11) entitled "Draw an Entire Man" and a pencil, and are asked to draw an entire man from memory.

Kinetic Family Drawing

Founder note. Burns and Kaufman, 1970.
  • Helps assess a child's self-concept and family dynamics.
  • The child is asked to draw a picture of their family, including themselves, "doing something".

9 · Non-Projective Tests (Non Factor Analytic)

Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)

Founder note. Given by Hathaway & McKinley (1989); ages 18 and above.
  • 567 items.
  • 1943: 1st test of validity scales. 1989: Revision. MMPI-A published in 1992.
  • Uses the criterion keyed method (criterion validity).
  • T scores. Time taken: 40–45 minutes.

Validity Scales

  • CNS — cannot say.
  • L — Lie scale.
  • F — Infrequency scale, faking bad (1st half).
  • K — Defensiveness / Evasiveness.
  • Fb — Faking bad (last half).
  • VRIN — Variable Response Inconsistency.
  • TRIN — True Response Inconsistency.
  • F-K — Honesty of test.
  • S — Superlative self presentation.
  • Fp — Faking psychopathology in a clinical setting.
  • Fs — Overreporting somatic symptoms.
Why it matters. People scoring high on the K scale show high adjustment, insight, higher socioeconomic status, education. People scoring low on the K scale: masochistic tendencies.

Clinical Scales

  • Hs — Hypochondriasis
  • D — Depression
  • Hy — Hysteria
  • Pd — Psychopathic deviate
  • Mf — Masculinity Femininity
  • Pa — Paranoia
  • Pt — Psychasthenia
  • Sc — Schizophrenia
  • Ma — Hypomania
  • Si — Social Introversion

Other Scales

  • 28 Harris-Lingoes subscales.
  • Content scales: Butcher, Graham, Williams & Ben-Porath (1990).
  • Content component scales (Ben-Porath & Sherwood, 1993).
  • Personality Psychopathology Five scales (PSY-5; Harkness et al., 2002).
  • Restructured clinical scales (Tellegen et al., 2003).

Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory (MCMI)

Founder note. Given by Theodore Millon.
  • 4th edition based on DSM-5.
  • 5th grade reading level; made for age 18+.
  • 195 true or false questions. Time taken: 25–30 mins.

Scales

  • 15 personality pattern scales.
  • 10 clinical syndrome scales.
  • 5 validity scales: 3 modifying indices, 2 random response indicators.
  • 45 Grossman personality facet scales.

Clinical scales — severe

  • SS — thought disorder
  • CC — major depression
  • PP — delusional disorder
  • A — generalized anxiety
  • H — somatic symptom
  • N — Bipolar
  • D — Persistent depression
  • B — Alcohol use
  • T — Drug Use
  • R — PTS

Validity Scales

  • X — disclosure
  • Y — desirability
  • Z — debasement
  • V — invalidity
  • W — inconsistency

Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

Founder note. By Isabel Myers and Katherine Briggs; made by the theoretical method (Carl Jung).
  • Highly used by organizations.
  • Interest inventory: Ipsative (every single option has the same value).
  • Types: extroversion vs introversion; sensing vs intuiting; thinking vs feeling; judging vs perceiving.
  • Criticism: little scientific evidence; weak validity; incomprehensive and vague.

Allport, Vernon & Lindzey — Study of Values Scale

  • Ipsative; given in 1931.
  • Based on six value orientations: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, religious.
  • 240 points; interest inventory.

Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS)

Founder note. By Ellen Edwards; derived from Murray's theory.
  • Interest inventory: ipsative.
  • Validity scales: social desirability, consistency.
  • 225 items; used in vocational training.
  • Based on Murray's needs: Achievement, Deference, Order, Exhibition, Autonomy, Affiliation, Intraception, Succourance, Dominance, Abasement, Nurturance, Change, Endurance, Heterosexuality, Aggression.

Holland's Interest Inventory

  • Based on Holland's Theory of Vocational Types or the RIASEC model.
  • Vocational Types: Realistic (R), Investigative (I), Artistic (A), Social (S), Enterprising (E), Conventional (C).

Multiphasic Personality Questionnaire (MPQ)

Founder note. Developed by Murthy (1964); the Indian version of MMPI-2.
  • Age: 18 and above.
  • 100 statements, forced choice type.
  • 2 validity scales: K scale, Repressor Sensitiser.
  • 7 clinical scales: Anxiety, Manic, Psychopathic deviation, Depression, Paranoid, Schizophrenia, Hysteria.

10 · Non-Projective Tests (Factor Analytic)

16 PF

Founder note. By Raymond Cattell; made using factor analysis.
  • Type of traits: temperamental, ability, dynamic (bipolar).
  • Surface traits are a combination of source traits.
  • Oblique design used for factor analysis.
  • Gave the specification equation R = f(S,P).
  • Identified 11 second-order factors.
  • Used multiple abstract variance analysis to measure heritability of traits.

16 source traits

Warmth · Reasoning · Emotional stability · Dominance · Liveliness · Rule conscious · Social boldness · Sensitivity · Vigilance · Abstractness · Privateness · Apprehension · Open to change · Self reliance · Perfection · Tension.

Types of data

Key. Objective measurement: the participant should not know what is being measured.
  • L data — D, J, K, P factors are a part of L data.
  • Q data
  • T data
  • Global 5 scale — created out of second-order factors.
  • Response style scales: acquiescence; impression management (narcissists will show a lot of social desirability); infrequency.

NEO-PI

Founder note. By Costa and McCrae; based on the five factor model / big five model.
  • Year: 1978.
  • No lie scale, no validity scales.
  • Number of items: 240; orthogonal rotation used.
  • Three versions: NEO-PI (1985), NEO-PI-R (1992), NEO-PI-3 (2005).
  • Domains measured by NEO-PI: Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism, Openness, Conscientiousness. Each domain has 6 facets.
  • The psychoticism scale of EPI correlates negatively with agreeableness and conscientiousness scales.

Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ)

Founder note. By Eysenck.
  • Theory: Type → Trait → Habit → S-R (smallest).
  • Focuses on nature (biological disposition) only.
  • 108 items; current version: EPQ-R (1985).
  • Can measure 22 personality disorders.
  • Three dimensions (PEN):
    • Psychoticism – Impulse control (gave this dimension after working at Maudsley hospital).
    • Extroversion – Introversion.
    • Neuroticism – Emotional Stability.
  • 4th dimension: Lie scale.
Reliability vs validity check. Note the distinct validity-scale families across objective tests: MMPI uses criterion-keyed validity scales (L, F, K, VRIN, TRIN…); MCMI uses X/Y/Z/V/W; EPPS uses social desirability + consistency; MPQ uses K + Repressor-Sensitiser; the 16PF uses response-style scales (acquiescence, impression management, infrequency); NEO-PI has no validity/lie scales, whereas the EPQ adds a Lie scale as its 4th dimension. Don't confuse a validity scale (detects faking) with a clinical/content scale (measures the trait).
