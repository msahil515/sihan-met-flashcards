In 1913 a twenty-six-year-old German psychologist named Wolfgang Kohler sailed to Tenerife, in the Canary Islands, to take charge of a small research station that the Prussian Academy of Sciences kept there for the study of chimpanzees. He expected a short posting. Then the First World War broke out, the sea lanes closed, and he stayed until 1920. For six years he had little to do but watch apes think.

His most gifted subject was a chimpanzee called Sultan. One day Kohler placed a banana outside the bars of Sultan's cage, beyond reach. Inside the cage lay two hollow bamboo sticks, one thin enough to slide into the other, but each too short to reach the fruit on its own. Sultan tried one stick, then the other. He pushed one stick out with the second, so that the first touched the banana, but he could not draw it in. After more than an hour Kohler gave up and left a keeper to watch. Sultan sat on a box, idly playing with the two sticks. By chance he found himself holding them in a line, and he pushed the thin one into the end of the thick one. According to Kohler's record, he jumped up at once, ran to the bars and raked in the banana with his new long stick. When the sticks came apart, he joined them again without hesitation.

What had happened inside Sultan? Kohler was sure he knew: the ape had suddenly seen the situation in a new way, the two sticks reorganised in his perception into one tool. But in the very year Kohler reached Tenerife, a young American at Johns Hopkins University, John B. Watson, was telling psychologists that "inside" questions of this kind had no place in a science at all; all that could be studied was what the animal did. In Vienna, Sigmund Freud would have asked a different question again: what hidden wish does the behaviour serve? Forty years later, a cognitive psychologist would describe Sultan as an information-processing system searching a "problem space", and today a researcher trained in the Indian tradition might ask about the state of attention in which the solution arrived.

Same ape, same sticks, five different stories. That is what this chapter is about. Between about 1900 and 1960 psychology was divided into rival schools, each with its own idea of what psychology should study, how it should study it, and what a human being is. You will meet each school in turn, see why it arose, what it discovered, and why it declined or was absorbed. Remember Sultan; you will meet him again at the end of the chapter.

2.1 Schools, Systems and ParadigmsLower yield · 3 Qs

Chapter 1 ended with the first two schools of scientific psychology, structuralism and functionalism. This chapter picks up the story where they left it. Before we meet the newcomers, though, it helps to be clear about what a "school" actually is, because the word is used loosely, and the vocabulary of the field matters as much as its content.

What makes a school of thought

A school of thought is a group of psychologists who share a common set of assumptions about what psychology should study (its subject matter), how it should study it (its method), and what kind of explanation counts. A school usually has a recognisable founder or leader, a statement of its programme (often called its manifesto), a university or laboratory that serves as its home, a journal in which its members publish, a stream of students who carry its ideas into other institutions, and, just as important, a rival school against which it defines itself. Behaviourism, for example, had Watson as its founder, his 1913 paper as its manifesto, and structuralism's introspection as its enemy.

Two classic books gave the age of schools its shape. Robert Sessions Woodworth (1869 to 1962) of Columbia University surveyed the rivals in Contemporary Schools of Psychology (1931), and Edna Heidbreder (1890 to 1985) described Seven Psychologies (1933). Notice their dates. The schools flourished roughly between the 1890s and the 1950s; after that, as you will see in Section 2.7, psychology gradually traded rival schools for overlapping "perspectives".

Two related words are worth separating. A system of psychology is an organised and internally consistent body of assumptions, methods and theory that tries to cover the whole field; a theory is narrower, an explanation of one set of facts (a theory of forgetting, a theory of colour vision). Courses called "Systems and Theories" study both: the great systems, and the theories they produced.

Kuhn's paradigms and a preparadigmatic science

Why did psychology split into warring schools when physics and chemistry did not? A helpful answer comes from a physicist turned historian, Thomas Samuel Kuhn (1922 to 1996). In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962), Kuhn argued that a mature science works within a paradigm: a shared framework of assumptions, problems, methods and model solutions that all workers in the field accept. Under a paradigm, scientists do what Kuhn called normal science: puzzle-solving within the accepted framework. Over time, results accumulate that the paradigm cannot explain (anomalies). When they pile up, the field enters a crisis; a new framework appears; and if it wins, the result is a scientific revolution, or paradigm shift. Newton's physics giving way to Einstein's is Kuhn's model case.

A field that has no single accepted paradigm, but several competing schools, each with its own problems and methods, is in Kuhn's term a preparadigmatic science. Kuhn himself noticed this in the social sciences. In the preface to his book he recalled a year spent among social scientists and how struck he was by the number and extent of their open disagreements about what the legitimate problems and methods even were. The age of schools in psychology fits that description well: a structuralist and a behaviourist did not merely disagree about results, they disagreed about what counted as data. Whether psychology has since acquired a paradigm (or whether the cognitive revolution was a true Kuhnian revolution) is still debated, and we return to the question at the end of Section 2.7.

2.2 Behaviourism: From Watson's Manifesto to SkinnerMedium yield · 9 Qs

In 1913, structuralism (Wundt and Titchener) and functionalism (James, Dewey and Angell) were still arguing about consciousness: what its elements were, or what it was for. Both relied on introspection, the trained observation of one's own experience. A young professor at Johns Hopkins decided that the whole argument was a mistake. Psychology, he said, should stop studying consciousness altogether. To understand how anyone could say such a thing in 1913, and be applauded for it, you need to see the ground that had been prepared.

The ground prepared: animal psychology and Russian objective psychology

The first preparation came from the study of animals. After Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882) argued for continuity between humans and other species, animal minds became a serious topic. George John Romanes (1848 to 1894) collected stories of clever animals (his anecdotal method), and Conwy Lloyd Morgan (1852 to 1936) answered with his canon of 1894: never interpret an action as the result of a higher mental faculty if it can be explained by a lower one (both are covered in Chapter 1). Jacques Loeb (1859 to 1924), a physiologist who taught Watson at Chicago, went further and explained animal movement as tropisms, forced, mechanical orientations toward or away from light, gravity and chemicals, with no mind required. And Edward Lee Thorndike (1874 to 1949), in his 1898 dissertation Animal Intelligence, put hungry cats in puzzle boxes and showed that they escaped not by reasoning but by gradual trial and error, stamped in by satisfying consequences: his law of effect (Chapter 6). If animal behaviour could be studied so precisely without asking what the animal was conscious of, why not human behaviour too?

The second preparation came from Russia, where a tradition of objective psychology had grown out of physiology. Ivan Mikhailovich Sechenov (1829 to 1905), in Reflexes of the Brain (1863), argued that all acts of conscious and unconscious life are reflexes in their origin. Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (1849 to 1936), who won the 1904 Nobel Prize for his work on digestion, discovered around the turn of the century that his dogs began to salivate to signals that preceded food; he called these conditioned reflexes and spent the rest of his life studying them (Chapter 6). Vladimir Mikhailovich Bekhterev (1857 to 1927) studied what he called association reflexes, conditioning motor responses such as withdrawing a limb from an electric shock, and named his whole approach reflexology; his Objective Psychology appeared between 1907 and 1912. Bekhterev's motor conditioning, more than Pavlov's salivation, gave Watson a method he could use on people.

The third preparation was home-grown. American functionalism had already pulled psychology toward practical questions (education, testing, industry) and toward the study of what organisms do to adapt. Behaviourism, in a sense, was functionalism with the mind removed.

Watson's manifesto (1913)

John Broadus Watson (1878 to 1958) grew up poor in South Carolina, earned his doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1903 with a study of learning in rats, and moved to Johns Hopkins in 1908. He had never been comfortable with introspection; he later admitted that he hated serving as a subject in introspective experiments and preferred animals, which could not be asked what they were experiencing. Early in 1913 he gave a lecture at Columbia University, published that March in Psychological Review as "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It". It is the founding document of behaviourism, the school that defines psychology as the science of observable behaviour.

Key study: "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" (1913)

Watson's paper opens with a sentence every student of the history of psychology should know almost by heart: psychology as the behaviourist views it "is a purely objective experimental branch of natural science. Its theoretical goal is the prediction and control of behavior. Introspection forms no essential part of its methods." He added that the behaviourist "recognizes no dividing line between man and brute".

The paper made four claims. (1) Subject matter: behaviour, not consciousness. (2) Method: objective observation, never introspection. (3) Goal: prediction and control, which made psychology useful to education, business and medicine. (4) Continuity: the same principles apply to humans and animals. In later books Watson phrased the goal precisely: given the stimulus, to predict the response, and given the response, to identify the stimulus that produced it. Watson was elected President of the American Psychological Association in 1915, only two years later; the manifesto had found an audience ready for it.

What methods did Watson allow? Four: observation, with or without instruments; testing (he liked tests as samples of behaviour, not as measures of mental capacities); the verbal report method, in which speech is treated as behaviour, so that what a person says counts as data but is not taken as a window on consciousness; and, from 1915, the conditioned reflex method borrowed from Pavlov and Bekhterev.

Watson's programme: thinking, emotion and the "twelve infants"

A science of behaviour has to say something about the things introspectionists cared about, such as thought and feeling. Watson had an answer for each, and his answers show you the spirit of the school.

Thinking as subvocal speech. Watson argued that thinking is nothing but talking to oneself so quietly that the movements cannot be seen: thought is subvocal speech, small movements of the tongue and larynx (he called them laryngeal habits). This view is called peripheralism, because it locates mental life in the peripheral muscles rather than in central brain processes. It led Watson to divide behaviour into explicit (overt) responses that anyone can see, and implicit (covert) responses such as subvocal speech, which cannot be seen by an observer but can, in principle, be measured with instruments. This is exactly the sense in which modern textbooks say covert behaviour is measurable but not directly observable, and why the "mental processes" in today's definition of psychology (the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes) are internal, covert processes.

Was Watson right about thought? A dramatic test came in 1947, when Scott Smith and his colleagues at the University of Utah gave Smith himself a dose of curare large enough to paralyse every skeletal muscle, keeping him alive on a respirator. If thinking were movement of the speech muscles, Smith should have been unable to think. He reported afterwards that he had remained fully aware, had understood what was said to him and could remember it. Peripheralism did not survive.

Emotion. Watson held that infants show just three unlearned emotional reactions: fear (to loud noises and sudden loss of support), rage (to hampering of movement) and love (to stroking and gentle handling). Every other emotion, he argued, is built from these three by conditioning.

Environmentalism. Watson's most quoted words appeared in his popular book Behaviorism (1925). "Give me a dozen healthy infants, well-formed, and my own specified world to bring them up in and I'll guarantee to take any one at random and train him to become any type of specialist I might select: doctor, lawyer, artist, merchant-chief and, yes, even beggar-man and thief, regardless of his talents, penchants, tendencies, abilities, vocations, and race of his ancestors." This is environmentalism in its purest form: the belief that behaviour is shaped almost entirely by experience rather than heredity. What is usually left out is the next sentence, in which Watson conceded that he was "going beyond my facts", adding that so had the advocates of the contrary view, and they had been doing it for thousands of years. Consistent with this, Watson first accepted, and then by the mid-1920s rejected, the idea of human instincts.

Little Albert. To show that emotions are conditioned, Watson and his graduate student Rosalie Rayner (1898 to 1935) conditioned fear in an eleven-month-old infant, "Albert B.", in 1920, pairing a white rat with a loud noise made by striking a steel bar behind his head. Albert came to fear the rat, and the fear generalised to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat and a Santa Claus mask. The study, its method and its ethical problems are told in full in Chapter 6. The same year, Watson's affair with Rayner became public; he was forced to resign from Johns Hopkins, married Rayner, and spent the rest of his working life in advertising at the J. Walter Thompson agency. His academic career had lasted barely twenty years, but the school he named outlived him by decades.

Woodworth is remembered for the S-O-R formula and a quip often credited to him: psychology first lost its soul, then its mind, then consciousness.

Rationalism is a philosophical position (Descartes), not a school Watson founded. Functionalism belongs to James, Dewey and Angell; structuralism to Titchener.

Three meanings of "behaviourism"

As the school matured, it became clear that "behaviourism" could mean three different things, and the three are easily confused. The difference lies in what each says about the mind.

Neobehaviourism: logical positivism and operationism

Watson's crusading behaviourism dominated American psychology from about 1913 to 1930. Historians of psychology usually describe what followed as a second stage, neobehaviourism (about 1930 to 1960), and a third, sociobehaviourism or social learning theory (about 1960 onward). The neobehaviourists, Tolman, Hull, Guthrie and Skinner, kept Watson's demand for objectivity but wanted to make behaviourism more rigorous and more theoretical. They drew on two ideas from the philosophy of science.

The first was logical positivism, the philosophy of the Vienna Circle of the 1920s, which held that a statement is meaningful only if it can be verified by observation (or is true by definition). Talk of unobservable "minds" was therefore not false but meaningless unless it could be tied to observables. The second was operationism, proposed by the American physicist Percy Williams Bridgman (1882 to 1961) in The Logic of Modern Physics (1927): a scientific concept means nothing more than the set of operations used to measure it. The psychologist S. S. Stevens brought the idea into psychology in 1935. The legacy is the operational definition that every research methods course still teaches: "hunger" in a rat experiment is defined as "twenty-three hours without food", and "intelligence" as "the score on this test".

Edward Chace Tolman's purposive behaviourism. Edward Chace Tolman (1886 to 1959) of the University of California at Berkeley was the behaviourist who let cognition in by the back door. He called his system purposive behaviourism and set it out in Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men (1932), a book he dedicated, with typical humour, to the white rat. Tolman insisted on studying molar behaviour, whole goal-directed acts such as a rat running a maze or a person going home, rather than Watson's molecular behaviour, the individual muscle twitches and glandular secretions that make up an act. Molar behaviour, Tolman observed, "reeks of purpose": a rat that finds its usual route blocked takes another, so what it has learned is not a chain of movements but where the food is.

To explain this without returning to introspection, Tolman introduced the intervening variable in the mid-1930s: an unobserved process inferred between the observable stimulus and the observable response and defined operationally by the conditions that produce it. Hunger, expectancy and demand are intervening variables. On one side sit the independent variables (environmental stimuli, hours of deprivation, heredity, previous training, age); on the other, behaviour; in between, the processes that link them. In effect, Watson's S-R formula became S-O-R, the formula Woodworth had used to insert the organism between stimulus and response.

Tolman's most famous intervening variable was the cognitive map, a mental representation of the layout of the environment, which he described in "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" (1948). The best evidence for it was latent learning, learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to show it. In the classic experiment of Tolman and Charles Honzik (1930), rats that explored a maze for ten days without food made many errors; once food appeared in the goal box on day 11, their errors fell by the very next day to the level of rats rewarded from the beginning. They had been learning the maze all along (the full study is in Chapter 6). For Tolman, rats learn what leads to what (expectancies), not stimulus-response habits.

Clark Leonard Hull's hypothetico-deductive system. If Tolman was the playful neobehaviourist, Clark Leonard Hull (1884 to 1952) of Yale University was the system-builder. Hull admired Newton's Principia and wanted a psychology built the same way, by the hypothetico-deductive method: state a small set of postulates, deduce precise testable predictions (theorems) from them, test the predictions experimentally, and revise the postulates when they fail. His Principles of Behavior (1943) made him for two decades the most cited psychologist in the world.

At the centre of Hull's theory is drive reduction. A biological need (for food, water, relief from pain) produces a drive (D), an aroused state that energises behaviour; any response that reduces the drive is reinforced. Reinforced repetitions build habit strength, written SHR, the strength of the learned bond between a stimulus and a response, which grows with the number of reinforced trials. Habit alone does not produce behaviour; it must be energised by drive. Hull's 1943 formula for reaction potential, the tendency to make the response, was therefore multiplicative:

SER = SHR × D

The multiplication matters: if either habit or drive is zero, there is no behaviour, however strong the other. A well-trained but completely satiated rat does not run the maze. Later versions added incentive motivation (K, the size of the reward) and stimulus intensity (V), and subtracted inhibition (fatigue-like reactive inhibition and conditioned inhibition). Hull's students, especially Kenneth Spence, Neal Elgar Miller and O. Hobart Mowrer, carried his ideas into many fields. One famous offshoot was John Dollard and Neal Miller's Personality and Psychotherapy (1950), which translated Freud's ideas into Hull's vocabulary of drive, cue, response and reinforcement; it is usually called the early social learning theory, as distinct from the later social learning theory of Albert Bandura and Richard Walters (1963).

Edwin Ray Guthrie's contiguity theory. Edwin Ray Guthrie (1886 to 1959) of the University of Washington offered the simplest theory of all, in The Psychology of Learning (1935). His one law was contiguity: a combination of stimuli that has accompanied a movement will, on its recurrence, tend to be followed by that movement. Reinforcement is not needed; what matters is only that stimulus and movement occur together. Learning is one-trial learning: a stimulus-movement association reaches full strength on its first pairing. Why, then, does practice help? Because a skill is made of many movements, each attached to many stimuli, and practice attaches the act to more and more of the situations in which it must occur. Guthrie also gave practical advice on breaking habits (the threshold, fatigue and incompatible-response methods, Chapter 6).

Burrhus Frederic Skinner's radical behaviourism. Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990) of Harvard became the most famous psychologist of the twentieth century; a 2002 survey ranking the most eminent psychologists placed him first, ahead of Piaget and Freud. Skinner distinguished respondent behaviour, elicited by a preceding stimulus as in Pavlov's experiments, from operant behaviour, emitted by the organism and controlled by its consequences. His tools (the operant chamber or "Skinner box", the cumulative recorder) and findings (reinforcement schedules, shaping) are covered in Chapter 6. Here what matters is his system.

Skinner was a radical behaviourist (Table 2.2) and also an anti-theorist. In a 1950 paper he asked whether theories of learning were necessary at all and answered no: psychology should describe functional relations between environmental events and behaviour, without inventing intervening variables, drives or cognitive maps. This is why Skinner's approach is sometimes called descriptive behaviourism, or the "empty organism" approach (a description Skinner disliked, since he never denied that the organism has insides; he denied that inner events explain anything). His major books are worth knowing by title and year: The Behavior of Organisms (1938); Walden Two (1948), a novel about a utopian community run on reinforcement principles; Science and Human Behavior (1953); Verbal Behavior (1957), which treated language as operant behaviour shaped by a verbal community; and Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971), which argued that the idea of an autonomous, free person is a pre-scientific fiction we can no longer afford.

Zing-Yang Kuo, the behaviourist who abolished instinct. The most extreme environmentalist of all was a Chinese psychologist trained at Berkeley, Zing-Yang Kuo (1898 to 1970). In "Giving Up Instincts in Psychology" (1921) he argued that the concept of instinct explains nothing. He backed the claim with an elegant experiment (1930): kittens reared alone, kittens reared watching their mothers kill rats, and kittens reared together with rats. Most of the kittens that watched their mothers became rat-killers, while only a small minority of those raised alongside rats ever killed one, and some seemed to treat rats as companions. "Rat-killing instinct," Kuo concluded, depends on rearing.

Criticism, decline and legacy

By the 1950s behaviourism had won American psychology, and it was beginning to lose. The criticisms came from several directions at once. It could not explain language: Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior, discussed in Section 2.6, argued that children produce sentences they have never heard and never been reinforced for. It ignored biology: two of Skinner's own former students, Keller and Marian Breland, reported in 1961 that the animals they trained for commercial shows kept "misbehaving", drifting back toward instinctive food-getting actions (instinctive drift), and John Garcia showed that rats learn some associations far more easily than others (Chapter 6). Its central concepts strained against its own evidence: Tolman's rats seemed to carry maps in their heads, and Kohler's apes seemed to reason. And it seemed to many to leave out exactly what makes us human: purpose, meaning and freedom. The humanists (Section 2.5) attacked from that side, the cognitive psychologists (Section 2.6) from the other.

Yet behaviourism did not so much die as dissolve into the rest of psychology. Its demand for objective, operationally defined, replicable measurement became simply the way psychology is done. Its laws of learning remain the core of Chapter 6. And it gave clinical psychology one of its most effective toolkits.

Clinical link

Behaviour therapy applies learning principles to psychological problems. Mary Cover Jones (1896 to 1987) removed a small boy's fear of rabbits in 1924 by gradually bringing a rabbit closer while he ate. Joseph Wolpe (1915 to 1997), in Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition (1958), developed systematic desensitisation: relaxation, which is incompatible with anxiety, is paired with an imagined hierarchy of feared situations. (The method is Wolpe's, not Skinner's, Watson's or Pavlov's.) Skinner's operant principles produced token economies and applied behaviour analysis, now widely used with children with autism. The therapies themselves are taught in the Clinical book.

2.3 Gestalt Psychology and Kurt LewinHigh yield · 16 Qs

Behaviourism rebelled against structuralism by throwing out consciousness. A second rebellion, in Germany, began a year earlier and took the opposite route: it kept consciousness but threw out the idea that consciousness is built from elements. Where Titchener had analysed experience into sensations, images and feelings, and Watson analysed behaviour into reflexes, the Gestalt psychologists argued that both had made the same mistake. They had taken the whole apart and assumed that the parts were more real than the whole.

Wertheimer and the phi phenomenon (1912)

Max Wertheimer (1880 to 1943) was born in Prague and trained under Oswald Kulpe at Wurzburg. As the story is usually told, in the summer of 1910 he was travelling by train to a holiday in the Rhineland when an idea about seen motion struck him so forcefully that he got off at Frankfurt, bought a toy stroboscope (a device that creates the impression of movement from a series of still pictures), and began experimenting in his hotel room. Historians suspect the story has been polished over the years, but what followed is well documented. At the Psychological Institute in Frankfurt, Wertheimer used a tachistoscope (an instrument that flashes visual displays for precisely controlled intervals) to study apparent motion. His two main observers were younger psychologists, Wolfgang Kohler and Kurt Koffka. The three of them became the founders of Gestalt psychology, and Wertheimer's 1912 paper on the experimental study of seen motion is the school's founding document.

The experiment was simple. Two lines were flashed one after the other in slightly different positions. When the interval between them was very short (around 30 milliseconds), observers saw two lines at once; when it was long (around 200 milliseconds), they saw one line and then the other. But at intervals in between (around 60 milliseconds), they saw a single line move from one position to the other. Wertheimer called this apparent movement the phi phenomenon. (He distinguished "pure phi", a sense of motion without any moving object, from the smooth movement of the object itself, later called beta movement; the details belong to Chapter 4.)

Why did such a small effect launch a school? Because the motion was not in either stimulus. Each line, analysed on its own, was stationary. An elementist, adding up two static sensations, could never arrive at movement. The perceived motion was a property of the whole pattern of stimulation in time, and it was as real in experience as anything else. If something as basic as seen motion could not be built from elements, perhaps nothing in perception could.

The idea was not entirely new. In 1890 the Austrian philosopher Christian von Ehrenfels (1859 to 1932), whose lectures Wertheimer had attended in Prague, had described form qualities (Gestaltqualitaten): a melody played in a different key consists of entirely different notes, yet we hear the same melody. The melody, then, is something more than its notes. Ehrenfels, however, treated the form quality as an extra element added to the sensations. Wertheimer's radical step was to reverse the order: the whole comes first, and the parts get their character from their place in it. The German word Gestalt means form, shape or configuration, an organised whole.

Kohler: insight, transposition and isomorphism

Wolfgang Kohler (1887 to 1967), born in Reval (now Tallinn in Estonia), took his doctorate in Berlin under Carl Stumpf and was the school's scientist and chief spokesman. You have already met him on Tenerife (1913 to 1920). His observations there, published in German in 1917 and in English as The Mentality of Apes (1925), challenged Thorndike's trial-and-error account of animal problem-solving. Thorndike's cats, Kohler argued, had been put in puzzle boxes whose mechanisms they could not see; of course they fumbled. Give an animal a problem whose elements are all in view, and it may solve it by insight: a sudden reorganisation of the perceptual field so that the relations needed for a solution are seen. Sultan's two sticks, and the boxes his companions stacked to reach fruit hung from the roof, were Kohler's showpieces. Insightful solutions, he noted, appear suddenly, are carried out smoothly once found, are retained, and transfer readily to similar problems. Insight learning as a type of learning is treated in Chapter 6.

Key study: Kohler's chickens and transposition (1918)

The question. When an animal learns to choose one of two stimuli, does it learn the absolute properties of the stimulus (this particular shade of grey) or a relation between stimuli (the darker of the two)?

The method. Kohler trained hens to peck grain from the darker of two grey papers; grain on the lighter one was out of bounds. Then he gave them a test pair: the old "correct" darker grey, now beside an even darker new grey.

The result. Most of the time the hens pecked at the new, darker grey, the one they had never been rewarded on, and ignored the shade that had been reinforced. Kohler found the same with chimpanzees.

The conclusion. The animals had learned a relation ("the darker one"), not an absolute stimulus. Kohler called this transposition, by analogy with a melody transposed to a new key. It was a direct challenge to the stimulus-response view that a specific stimulus becomes bonded to a response. The criticism: Kenneth Spence (1937) later showed that a stimulus-response account combining generalisation gradients of excitation and inhibition could predict transposition too, and that transposition breaks down when the test pair is far from the training pair.

Kohler's most ambitious idea was physiological. In Die physischen Gestalten ("Physical Gestalten", 1920) he proposed isomorphism: the organisation of experience corresponds in structure (not in literal copy) to the organisation of electrical field processes in the brain. A perceived circle does not mean a circle drawn on the cortex, but a pattern of brain activity with the same relational order. The theory was tested in the 1950s. Karl Lashley and his colleagues laid strips of gold foil across the visual cortex of monkeys (1951), and Roger Sperry inserted mica plates and tantalum wires into the cortex of cats (1955), to short-circuit or block any such electrical fields. Perception was essentially unaffected. The field version of isomorphism did not survive, although the broader idea that experience and brain activity share a structure lives on in cognitive neuroscience.

Kohler became director of the Psychological Institute at the University of Berlin in 1922, the most prestigious post in German psychology. When the Nazis began dismissing Jewish professors in 1933, he published a newspaper article in their defence, one of the last openly critical pieces to appear in Germany, and resisted the regime's interference with his institute until he resigned and emigrated in 1935. He spent the rest of his career at Swarthmore College in Pennsylvania and was President of the American Psychological Association in 1959.

Koffka: development and the behavioural environment

Kurt Koffka (1886 to 1941) was the school's great communicator. His Growth of the Mind (German 1921, English 1924) applied Gestalt ideas to child development, arguing that infants perceive organised wholes from the beginning rather than assembling them from sensations. In 1922 he introduced Gestalt psychology to American readers in an article in the Psychological Bulletin titled "Perception: An introduction to the Gestalt-theorie". The article was so influential that for years Americans assumed Gestalt psychology was only about perception, which the founders found frustrating. Koffka moved to Smith College in Massachusetts in 1927, and his Principles of Gestalt Psychology (1935) is the school's most systematic textbook.

Koffka's most memorable lesson is a German legend he retold in the Principles. On a winter evening, in a driving snowstorm, a man on horseback rode across a wide, wind-swept plain and arrived at last at an inn. The innkeeper, astonished, asked where he had come from. The rider pointed back across the plain. "Do you know," said the innkeeper, "that you have ridden across Lake Constance?" The rider dropped dead at his feet. In what environment, Koffka asks, did the rider's behaviour take place? In the geographical environment, the physical world, he crossed thin ice over deep water. But he behaved as if he were crossing solid ground, because his behavioural environment, the world as perceived, was a frozen plain. Behaviour, Koffka argued, is governed by the behavioural environment. It is one of the cleanest arguments in psychology for studying the world as the person experiences it.

The three founders, together with the neurologist Kurt Goldstein and the psychiatrist Hans Gruhle, launched the school's journal, Psychologische Forschung ("Psychological Research"), in 1921.

The central ideas of Gestalt psychology

The whole is different from the sum of its parts. This is the school's slogan, and it is usually quoted as "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts". Koffka himself thought "greater" was the wrong word: in the Principles he wrote that it is more correct to say the whole is something else than the sum of its parts, because summing is a meaningless procedure, whereas the whole-part relationship is meaningful. A square drawn with four lines is not "more" than four lines; it is a different kind of thing, with properties (squareness, closedness, an inside) that none of the lines possesses. Textbooks use both versions for the Gestalt principle.

Field theory. The Gestalt psychologists borrowed the concept of a field from nineteenth-century physics, where Faraday and Maxwell had shown that electrical and magnetic events are properties of a whole field of forces rather than of isolated particles. Field theory in psychology holds that perception, thought and behaviour are determined by the whole field of interacting forces present at the moment, not by isolated stimuli. Iron filings around a magnet do not each "decide" where to go; the field organises them.

Organisation and Pragnanz. Because experience is organised into wholes, the Gestalt psychologists set out the laws by which the organisation happens. Wertheimer's 1923 paper on the laws of perceptual organisation described proximity, similarity, closure, good continuation and common fate, all governed by an overarching law of Pragnanz: perceptual organisation will always be as "good" (simple, regular, symmetrical, stable) as the conditions allow. Figure-ground organisation, first analysed by the Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin (1886 to 1951) in 1915, was adopted as a cornerstone: the figure is seen as a thing, nearer, with a clear contour and shape, usually smaller and more memorable, whereas the ground seems shapeless, diffuse, farther away and to continue behind the figure. The laws themselves, with demonstrations, are taught in Chapter 4.

Productive thinking. Wertheimer's last book, Productive Thinking (published in 1945, two years after his death), brought Gestalt ideas to problem-solving and education. He distinguished reproductive thinking, the blind application of a memorised method, from productive thinking, in which the thinker grasps the structure of the problem and sees why a solution works. His favourite example came from a school classroom. Children taught the formula for the area of a parallelogram (base times height) by rote could apply it to the textbook figure but were helpless when the figure was turned on its side. A child who saw that the triangle "sticking out" at one end could be moved to fill the gap at the other, turning the parallelogram into a rectangle, understood the formula and could never be fooled by a rotation. Wertheimer also interviewed Albert Einstein at length about how the theory of relativity had been discovered. Karl Duncker's work on functional fixedness (1945) continued this line (Chapter 8).

Kurt Lewin: field theory applied to the person

The Gestalt founders worked mainly on perception and thinking. It was a younger colleague at the Berlin Institute, Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947), who carried field theory into motivation, personality and, finally, social life. Lewin's starting point was that behaviour at any moment is a function of the whole psychological situation. He expressed it in a formula that appears in his Principles of Topological Psychology (1936):

B = f(P, E)

Behaviour (B) is a function (f) of the person (P) and the environment (E). The person and the environment are not independent: they form one interdependent field, which Lewin called the life space (LSp), the totality of facts that determine a person's behaviour at a given moment. So the formula is often written B = f(LSp). The life space contains the person and the psychological environment, the environment as it exists for that person (Koffka's behavioural environment again), not the physical world as a geographer would map it. A rule a student has never heard of is not in her life space; an exam she fears is, even though it is a week away.

To describe the life space, Lewin borrowed from a branch of mathematics called topology, which deals with regions, boundaries and connections rather than distances and sizes; hence topological psychology. The life space is divided into regions (activities, goals, groups) separated by boundaries, some of which act as barriers. Movement from one region to another, in thought or action, is called locomotion. Because topology alone cannot express direction and strength, Lewin added two dynamic concepts. A valence is the attracting (positive) or repelling (negative) quality a region has for the person; a vector is a force acting on the person, with a direction and a strength, toward or away from a region. Lewin later added hodological space (from the Greek hodos, path), a geometry of paths that represents the routes, including detours, by which a person can reach a goal. Figure 2.2 puts these terms together.

Tension systems and the Zeigarnik effect. Lewin proposed that an intention to reach a goal sets up a tension system within the person, which is discharged only when the goal is reached. One prediction follows directly: unfinished tasks should leave tension behind them and so stay active in the mind. According to a story told by his students, Lewin noticed this one evening at a Berlin restaurant, where a waiter remembered every detail of the unpaid orders but could not recall what had been ordered at a table that had already settled its bill. His student Bluma Zeigarnik (1900 to 1988) tested the idea in 1927. Participants were given a series of simple tasks, half of which were interrupted before completion. Afterwards they recalled the interrupted tasks nearly twice as often as the completed ones. Better memory for interrupted than for completed tasks is the Zeigarnik effect. A second student, Maria Ovsiankina (1898 to 1993), showed in 1928 that people given the chance spontaneously go back to interrupted tasks and finish them, the Ovsiankina effect. Remember the pair this way: Zeigarnik, people remember unfinished tasks; Ovsiankina, people resume them.

Contemporaneity. A further principle of Lewin's field theory is that only the present field can influence present behaviour. The past matters only to the extent that it has left traces in the life space now; the future, only as present expectations. This principle of contemporaneity is the most direct "here-and-now" idea in Gestalt-derived psychology. Lewin also analysed conflict as a balance of valences (approach-approach, avoidance-avoidance and approach-avoidance, Chapter 10).

Group dynamics and action research. Lewin, who was Jewish, left Germany in 1933, worked briefly at Cornell University and then at the Iowa Child Welfare Research Station, and in 1945 founded the Research Center for Group Dynamics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. There he turned field theory to groups, which he saw as dynamic wholes whose properties are not the sum of their members'. He coined the term group dynamics. With Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White (1939) he compared boys' clubs run under autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire leadership (Social book). He also founded action research, research carried out in partnership with the people affected by a social problem, in a repeating cycle of planning, acting, observing the results and reflecting, then planning again. A summer workshop he led in 1946 gave rise to the T-group (training group) and the National Training Laboratories. His three-stage model of change (unfreezing, moving, refreezing) is still taught in management courses. Lewin is widely called the father of modern social psychology, and his motto summarises his career: "There is nothing so practical as a good theory."

Exam trap

Life space = the person plus the psychological environment (not society and group, not person and group, not environment and group). Lewin founded action research, group dynamics and modern experimental social psychology; he did not found psychoanalysis (Freud), psychophysics (Fechner) or functionalism (James, Dewey, Angell). Zeigarnik = recall of interrupted tasks; Ovsiankina = resumption of interrupted tasks. Topological = regions and boundaries; hodological = paths.

Emigration, legacy, and a different "Gestalt"

With the rise of the Nazis, all four leading figures, Wertheimer (1933, to the New School for Social Research in New York), Koffka (already at Smith from 1927), Kohler (1935) and Lewin (1933), ended up in the United States. There they met a psychology dominated by behaviourism, taught at small colleges rather than major research universities, and wrote in a second language. Gestalt psychology never became a dominant American school. Its influence, however, was enormous and lasting. Its account of perceptual organisation is still taught almost unchanged. Its insistence on insight and understanding prepared the ground for cognitive psychology (Section 2.6). And through Lewin, his students (Leon Festinger among them) and Gestalt-minded social psychologists such as Fritz Heider and Solomon Asch, it shaped modern social psychology.

One thing Gestalt psychology did not produce is Gestalt therapy. Gestalt therapy was developed in the 1940s and 1950s by Frederick Salomon Perls (Fritz Perls, 1893 to 1970), a German-born psychoanalyst, with his wife Laura Perls and the writer Paul Goodman; the book Gestalt Therapy (1951) was written by Perls, Ralph Hefferline and Goodman. It is an experiential, humanistic therapy that emphasises awareness in the here and now, personal responsibility, and the completion of "unfinished business" (a phrase that echoes Zeigarnik's interrupted tasks). Its best-known technique is the empty chair, in which the client speaks to an imagined person, or a disowned part of the self, placed in an empty chair, and then changes seats to answer. Perls had worked with the holistic neurologist Kurt Goldstein, and he borrowed the word Gestalt and a general holism, but little else. The Gestalt-trained psychologist Mary Henle published a careful comparison in 1978 and concluded that the connection between the two is largely one of name. The therapy itself is taught in the Clinical book.

Exam trap

Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Kohler, Koffka) is not Gestalt therapy (Perls). The empty chair technique belongs to Gestalt therapy, not to psychoanalysis, CBT or person-centred therapy. Questions sometimes blur the two. An assertion-reason item states "Fritz Perls was a proponent of the Gestalt perspective" with the reason "Gestalt focuses on the wholeness of individuals' experiences in the present moment"; the key treats both as true and the reason as the correct explanation, because "Gestalt perspective" is used loosely there to include the therapy. Similarly, one Panwar item keys "here-and-now" to Gestalt psychology: the idea is most at home in Gestalt therapy (and in existential-humanistic therapy), and within Gestalt psychology proper it corresponds to Lewin's principle of contemporaneity. If the options separate Gestalt psychology from Gestalt therapy, choose the therapy.

2.4 Psychoanalysis as a School: The First ForceMedium yield · 6 Qs

Behaviourism and Gestalt psychology were both born in university laboratories, as quarrels with structuralism about how to study normal minds. Psychoanalysis came from somewhere else entirely: the consulting room of a Viennese doctor treating patients whose symptoms made no medical sense. It never really belonged to academic psychology, yet it changed the way the whole twentieth century thought about the mind. This section tells its history as a school; Freud's theory of personality (the stages, defences and dynamics) is taught in full in the Personality book.

From neurology to hysteria: Charcot, Bernheim and Breuer

Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) did not set out to be a psychologist. He qualified in medicine at the University of Vienna in 1881 after years of research in the physiological laboratory of Ernst Brucke, a leader of the mechanistic school of German physiology, where he studied the nerve cells of fish and other animals under the microscope. Unable to support a family on a researcher's pay, he went into private practice in 1886 as a neurologist, a specialist in diseases of the nerves. Many of the patients who came to him suffered from hysteria, a condition (today classed among the conversion and dissociative disorders) in which people developed paralyses, blindness, loss of feeling or fits with no detectable damage to the nervous system.

Three encounters shaped what Freud did with these patients. From October 1885 to February 1886 he studied in Paris with Jean-Martin Charcot (1825 to 1893), the most famous neurologist in Europe, who showed at the Salpetriere hospital that hysterical symptoms could be produced and removed under hypnosis. That meant the symptoms had a psychological, not an anatomical, cause. Charcot's pupil Pierre Janet (1859 to 1947) went on to explain hysteria as dissociation, a splitting off of some mental functions from the rest of consciousness into what he called the subconscious; his ideas ran parallel to Freud's, and the two later disputed priority. In 1889 Freud visited Hippolyte Bernheim (1840 to 1919) at Nancy, the rival French school, which taught that hypnosis was simply heightened suggestibility; Freud was struck by evidence that patients could later recall what had happened under hypnosis when pressed, which suggested that forgotten memories were not lost but kept out of awareness.

The third and decisive influence was a senior Viennese physician and friend, Josef Breuer (1842 to 1925). Between 1880 and 1882 Breuer had treated a gifted young woman, known in the literature as Anna O. (her real name was Bertha Pappenheim), who suffered paralysis, disturbances of vision and speech, and periods of altered consciousness. Breuer found that when, under light hypnosis, she talked about the circumstances in which a symptom had first appeared, and expressed the feelings she had held back at the time, the symptom eased. Anna O. called the treatment "the talking cure" and "chimney sweeping". Breuer called it the cathartic method, from the Greek catharsis, purging: emotion that had been "strangulated" was released. Breuer and Freud published Studies on Hysteria in 1895, and that book is usually taken as the beginning of psychoanalysis. (Anna O. was Breuer's patient, not Freud's; she later became a pioneering social worker and campaigner for women's rights.)

Freud soon gave up hypnosis, partly because not all patients could be hypnotised. He replaced it first with a "pressure technique" (pressing on the patient's forehead and insisting that a memory would come) and then with free association, in which the patient lies on a couch and reports everything that comes to mind, however trivial, embarrassing or illogical. The places where associations stall, he argued, mark resistance, the mind's defence against painful material. By 1896 he was calling his method psychoanalysis.

The Interpretation of Dreams and the growth of a movement

In the late 1890s, after the death of his father, Freud undertook a systematic self-analysis, much of it through his own dreams. The result was The Interpretation of Dreams, which appeared in November 1899 but was dated 1900 by its publisher, a fitting debut for the new century. In it Freud argued that every dream is the disguised fulfilment of a wish, and he called the interpretation of dreams "the royal road to a knowledge of the unconscious" (dream theory is in Chapter 5). It sold only a few hundred copies in its first years. Yet a circle gathered. From 1902 a small group met at Freud's apartment on Wednesday evenings; in 1908 it became the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society, and in 1910 the International Psychoanalytical Association was founded, with Carl Jung as its first president.

International recognition came in 1909, when G. Stanley Hall invited Freud to lecture at Clark University in Worcester, Massachusetts, as part of the university's twentieth-anniversary celebrations. Freud travelled with Jung and Sandor Ferenczi, gave five lectures in German, and received an honorary doctorate, his first academic honour. William James, by then old and ill, came to hear him. The Clark lectures are the usual marker for the arrival of psychoanalysis in America.

Two models of the mind

The topographic model. In The Interpretation of Dreams (1900) Freud described the mind as having three regions, a topographic model (topography means the mapping of places). The conscious contains what we are aware of at this moment. The preconscious contains material that is not in awareness now but can easily be recalled, such as your phone number or what you ate this morning. The unconscious contains wishes, memories and impulses that are actively kept out of awareness because they are unacceptable, and that can reach consciousness only in disguised form, in dreams, slips of the tongue and symptoms. The popular image of the mind as an iceberg, mostly below the waterline, illustrates this model, though the image is not Freud's own.

The structural model. In The Ego and the Id (1923) Freud added a second map, the structural model, which describes three agencies rather than three regions. The id is the oldest and most primitive part of the personality, present at birth and entirely unconscious; it is the source of instinctual energy and operates on the pleasure principle, demanding immediate gratification and caring nothing for logic, time or morality (impulsive, irrational, pleasure-seeking: these words always point to the id). The ego develops to deal with reality; it operates on the reality principle, delays gratification, and uses realistic thought (secondary process) to find safe ways of meeting the id's demands. The superego is the internalised voice of parents and society, made up of the conscience and the ego-ideal. The ego, caught between the id, the superego and the outside world, protects itself against the anxiety these conflicts produce by means of defence mechanisms such as repression. The two models are not rivals: the ego and superego are partly conscious and partly unconscious, while the id is wholly unconscious.

Exam trap

Freud began his career as a neurologist, not as a psychiatrist, philosopher or (obviously) a psychoanalyst. Father of psychoanalysis: Freud, not Bernheim (Nancy school of hypnosis), not Adler (individual psychology), not Jung (analytical psychology). Psychosocial theory is Erikson's; psychoanalytic theory is Freud's. Gratification, irrational, impulsive = id. Freud's three anxieties are realistic, neurotic (fear that id impulses will break through and be punished) and moral; "innate anxiety" is not a technical term in any of these theories; Horney's basic anxiety and the existentialists' existential anxiety are different concepts (see Table 2.5 and Section 2.5).

Dissenters and neo-Freudians

Freud demanded loyalty to the core of his theory, above all to the sexual basis of neurosis, and some of his ablest followers broke away. Alfred Adler left in 1911 and Carl Jung in 1913 to 1914; each founded his own school. A later generation, often called the neo-Freudians, kept the idea of unconscious dynamics but shifted the emphasis from biological instincts to social relationships, culture and the ego. Strictly, Adler and Jung are "dissenters" and Horney, Fromm and Sullivan the neo-Freudians proper, while Anna Freud and Erikson are ego psychologists and Klein the founder of the object relations school; introductory textbooks usually group them all together. Psychoanalysis as the first force

As a school, psychoanalysis was unlike any other in this chapter. Its home was the clinic, not the university; its data were case histories, dreams and free associations, not experiments; and its aim was to relieve suffering as well as to understand it. Its central claims, that much of mental life is unconscious, that childhood experience shapes the adult, and that symptoms have meaning, entered everyday thought so completely that we hardly notice them. It is the first force in psychology (Table 2.1), and the broader psychodynamic perspective descended from it still explains behaviour, and many mental disorders, in terms of unconscious urges and the hidden struggle over their expression.

Its critics were just as important to the history of the field. The philosopher Karl Popper (1902 to 1994) argued that psychoanalysis was unscientific because it was unfalsifiable: any behaviour at all could be explained after the event, so no observation could ever count against it. Behaviourists dismissed its concepts as untestable, and later the humanists complained that it built a whole picture of human nature from the study of sick people. Modern psychodynamic research has answered some of these charges with controlled studies, but the debate shaped how all the later schools defined themselves.

Clinical link

Short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy, which focuses on recurring relationship patterns, the transference and avoided feelings, has a growing body of controlled evidence for depression and some anxiety and personality problems. When an interviewer asks you which psychotherapy "school" you find most convincing, remember that modern practice borrows across schools; see eclecticism in Section 2.7.

2.5 The Third Force and Beyond: Humanistic, Existential, Transpersonal and Positive PsychologyHigh yield · 16 Qs

By the 1950s, a student of psychology in America could choose between two great pictures of human nature. Psychoanalysis showed a creature driven by blind instincts and scarred by childhood. Behaviourism showed a creature shaped, like a laboratory rat, by its schedule of rewards. Both pictures were deterministic, and to a growing number of psychologists both seemed to leave out what mattered most: the capacity to choose, to grow, to create, to love, to look for meaning. The movement that grew out of this dissatisfaction called itself the third force.

The birth of humanistic psychology

Abraham Harold Maslow (1908 to 1970) began his career as an enthusiastic behaviourist. He took his doctorate at the University of Wisconsin in 1934, studying dominance and sexual behaviour in monkeys, and as a young man he believed Watson's behaviourism could solve the world's problems. Two experiences changed his mind: the Second World War, and the birth of his first child. He later said that holding his baby made him feel small and not really in control, and that anyone who had a baby could not be a behaviourist. Maslow decided to study not sick people, as Freud had done, nor rats, but the healthiest, most fulfilled people he could find. His hierarchy of needs (1943) and his concept of self-actualisation, the fulfilment of one's unique potential, are taught in Chapter 10. His books Motivation and Personality (1954) and Toward a Psychology of Being (1962) became the movement's texts, and it was Maslow who named it the third force.

Carl Ransom Rogers (1902 to 1987) came to the same place by a different road, through counselling. In Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942) and Client-Centered Therapy (1951) he argued that every person has an actualising tendency, an innate drive to grow and develop, which flourishes when others offer unconditional positive regard, empathy and genuineness, and is blocked when love is given only on "conditions of worth". His theory is phenomenological: what matters is the person's own perceived world, the phenomenal field. (Rogers's theory belongs to the Personality book and his therapy to the Clinical book.)

Two other founders deserve a place. Charlotte Buhler (1893 to 1974), a Viennese developmental psychologist who studied the course of whole human lives through biographies, brought a European lifespan perspective and was an early president of the movement's association. Rollo May (1909 to 1994) brought existential philosophy into American psychology, as you will see below.

The movement acquired the trappings of a school quickly. In 1961 Maslow and Anthony Sutich (1907 to 1976) launched the Journal of Humanistic Psychology, and the Association for Humanistic Psychology was formed in the early 1960s (sources differ on the exact year, usually giving 1961 or 1962). In November 1964 a small invitational conference at Old Saybrook, Connecticut, brought together Maslow, Rogers, May, Buhler, Gordon Allport, Henry Murray, George Kelly and others; the Old Saybrook conference is usually treated as the formal birth of humanistic psychology as a movement. In 1971 the American Psychological Association created a Division of Humanistic Psychology.

What did the humanists believe? James Bugental (1915 to 2008), the Association's first president, summarised it in five postulates in 1964:

  • Human beings, as human, supersede the sum of their parts; they cannot be reduced to components.
  • Human beings have their existence in a uniquely human context, which is relational: we live among other people.
  • Human beings are aware, and are aware of being aware; awareness is part of what it is to be human.
  • Human beings have choice, and with choice comes responsibility.
  • Human beings are intentional: they seek meaning, value and creativity, and aim at goals.

Put simply, humanistic psychology emphasises free will, conscious experience, the whole person, the innate tendency to grow, and the reaching of maximum potential. Its method is phenomenological: understand people from inside their own experience. Its view of psychological disorder follows. Disturbance arises when growth is blocked, when people deny their real feelings to win approval and their self-concept becomes incongruent with their experience. Healing comes from relationships that offer acceptance, respect for the client and belief in the person's potential, not from reinforcement contingencies (a behavioural idea) or the uncovering of unconscious conflict (a psychoanalytic one).

Phenomenology

Several schools in this chapter call their method "phenomenological", so the word needs a clear definition. Phenomenology is the philosophical method founded by Edmund Husserl (1859 to 1938) for describing experience exactly as it presents itself, before any theory about its causes is applied. Husserl's call was "back to the things themselves". His central technique was bracketing (Greek epoche): deliberately suspending one's assumptions about the external reality and causes of an experience, in order to describe the experience itself. He took from his teacher Franz Brentano the idea of intentionality, that consciousness is always consciousness of something. Phenomenology fed three streams in psychology: the Gestalt psychologists' description of naive experience (through Carl Stumpf, who also taught Husserl), the existential psychologists, and the humanists' focus on the subjective world of the client. Today interpretative phenomenological analysis is a respected qualitative research method (Research Methodology book).

Existential psychology

Humanistic psychology was optimistic: people naturally grow toward fulfilment. Existential psychology, its close relative, is more sober. It begins from the "givens" of human existence that no one escapes, death, freedom, isolation and the absence of any ready-made meaning, and asks how people live authentically in the face of them. Its roots are in European philosophy.

Soren Kierkegaard (1813 to 1855), the Danish thinker usually called the father of existentialism, analysed anxiety in The Concept of Anxiety (1844) as the "dizziness of freedom" that comes with having to choose. Martin Heidegger (1889 to 1976), in Being and Time (1927), described human existence as Dasein, literally "being there": the human being is not a mind looking at a separate world but is always already being-in-the-world, thrown into a situation not of its choosing and living toward its own death. Jean-Paul Sartre (1905 to 1980) declared that for human beings "existence precedes essence": we are not born with a fixed nature but must continually create ourselves through our choices, so that we are "condemned to be free", and to deny that freedom is bad faith. The statement that "each person must continually recreate himself; the meaning of life is never fixed but constantly changes" is pure existentialism.

Two Swiss psychiatrists turned Heidegger's philosophy into a clinical method. Ludwig Binswanger (1881 to 1966) founded Daseinsanalysis (existential analysis), which tries to understand a patient's "world-design". He described three simultaneous modes of being-in-the-world: the Umwelt (the biological and physical world around us), the Mitwelt (the world with others) and the Eigenwelt (one's own world, the relationship with oneself). The existential therapist Emmy van Deurzen later added a fourth, the Uberwelt, the spiritual world of values and beliefs. Medard Boss (1903 to 1990), who worked closely with Heidegger himself, developed Daseinsanalysis further.

The most widely read existential psychiatrist is Viktor Emil Frankl (1905 to 1997) of Vienna. Frankl survived Theresienstadt, Auschwitz and Dachau's subcamps; his wife, parents and brother died in the camps. In Man's Search for Meaning (German 1946, English 1959) he described how prisoners who held on to a reason to live, a task to finish or a person to return to, were better able to endure. He founded logotherapy (from the Greek logos, meaning), often called the "third Viennese school of psychotherapy" after Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology. Where Freud saw a will to pleasure and Adler a will to power, Frankl saw the will to meaning as the primary human motive. When it is frustrated, people fall into an existential vacuum, a sense of emptiness and boredom; when the frustration produces symptoms, Frankl called the result noogenic neurosis (from the Greek noos, mind or spirit), a neurosis arising not from psychological conflict or biology but from the spiritual dimension, from problems of meaning. Meaning, Frankl taught, can be found in three ways: by creating a work or doing a deed (creative values), by experiencing something or loving someone (experiential values), and by the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering (attitudinal values). His techniques include paradoxical intention and dereflection (Clinical book).

Rollo May brought European existentialism to American readers in Existence (1958), edited with Ernest Angel and Henri Ellenberger. In The Meaning of Anxiety (1950) he distinguished normal anxiety, which is proportionate to the threat, does not require repression and can be used constructively, from neurotic anxiety, which is disproportionate and involves repression. Existential anxiety, the anxiety of facing death, freedom and meaninglessness, is normal in this sense. Finally, the American psychiatrist Irvin David Yalom (born 1931), in Existential Psychotherapy (1980), organised the field around four "ultimate concerns": death, freedom (with its responsibility), existential isolation, and meaninglessness. Transpersonal psychology: the fourth force

Toward the end of his life Maslow came to believe that humanistic psychology itself was a transitional stage. In the preface to the second edition of Toward a Psychology of Being (1968) he wrote of a still "higher" fourth psychology, transpersonal, centred in the cosmos rather than in human needs and interests. His studies of self-actualising people had shown him that many reported peak experiences, moments of awe, unity and self-transcendence. In 1969 Maslow and Sutich founded the Journal of Transpersonal Psychology. Transpersonal psychology, the fourth force, studies experiences in which the sense of identity extends beyond (trans) the individual person: mystical and peak experiences, meditation and altered states of consciousness, spiritual development and self-transcendence. Its leading figures include the psychiatrist Stanislav Grof (born 1931), who studied non-ordinary states of consciousness, Charles Tart (born 1937), who wrote on states of consciousness, Ken Wilber (born 1949), whose spectrum of consciousness and integral theory tried to unite Western psychology with Eastern traditions, and, earlier, the Italian psychiatrist Roberto Assagioli (1888 to 1974), founder of psychosynthesis. Frankl, with his stress on self-transcendence, is often claimed as a forerunner. Because so much transpersonal psychology draws on yoga, Vedanta and Buddhism, it forms a natural bridge to Indian psychology in Sections 2.8 and 2.9.

Positive psychology

The last movement in this family is the youngest. The phrase "positive psychology" first appeared as the title of the final chapter of Maslow's Motivation and Personality (1954), but it remained a passing label. The field as it exists today was launched by Martin Elias Peter Seligman (born 1942), the psychologist who had made his name with research on learned helplessness and depression (Chapter 6). Elected President of the American Psychological Association for 1998, he used his presidency to argue that psychology had become a science of repairing damage and had forgotten its other two missions: making the lives of ordinary people more fulfilling, and nurturing talent.

Seligman later told how the idea crystallised. He was weeding his garden with his five-year-old daughter Nikki, who was throwing weeds into the air and dancing. He shouted at her. She walked away, came back, and told him that from her third birthday to her fifth she had been a whiner, that on turning five she had decided to stop, that it was the hardest thing she had ever done, and that if she could stop whining, he could stop being such a grouch. Raising Nikki, Seligman realised, was not about correcting her whining but about recognising and nurturing the strength she had just shown. Psychology, he concluded, should do the same.

In January 2000 Seligman and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934 to 2021) edited a special issue of the American Psychologist that announced positive psychology: the scientific study of positive experiences, positive individual traits and the institutions that enable them (its "three pillars"). Csikszentmihalyi was already known for his research on flow, the absorbing state of complete engagement in an activity whose challenge matches one's skill. Other landmarks followed. Character Strengths and Virtues (2004), by Christopher Peterson and Seligman, classified 24 character strengths under six virtues as a kind of "manual of the sanities" to set beside the diagnostic manuals. In Flourish (2011) Seligman proposed the PERMA model of well-being: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning and Accomplishment (detail in the Motivation and Emotion chapters).

Several research programmes are now considered part of positive psychology. C. R. Snyder's hope theory describes hope as the combination of agency (the will to reach a goal) and pathways (the ways to reach it); promoting hope is a typical positive psychology intervention. Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff (2007) studied savouring, the capacity to attend to, appreciate and deliberately enhance the enjoyment of positive experiences, past, present or anticipated; savouring is to good events what coping is to bad ones. Barbara Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory holds that positive emotions broaden attention and thinking and build lasting personal resources, and Ed Diener's research on subjective well-being measures happiness as high life satisfaction, frequent positive feeling and infrequent negative feeling. Positive psychology interventions, such as writing down three good things each day, using one's signature strengths in a new way, or delivering a gratitude letter, have been tested in randomised trials.

How does positive psychology relate to humanism? Seligman was at pains to distance his field from what he saw as the unscientific, self-help side of humanistic psychology; positive psychology is meant to be empirical. Humanistic psychologists replied, with some justice, that positive psychology had rediscovered their themes and given them new names. Most textbooks now treat humanistic psychology as its forerunner.

Exam trap

Founder of positive psychology: Seligman (with Csikszentmihalyi). Maslow coined the phrase in 1954 but did not found the field. Howard Gardner is multiple intelligences; Steven Pinker is language and evolutionary psychology; Bandura is self-efficacy and social cognitive theory. Savouring = enhancing the enjoyment of positive experiences (not avoiding negative ones, not relaxation, not reflecting on trauma). The primary focus of positive psychology interventions is building strengths such as hope, not behaviour therapy, psychoanalysis or DBT.

Criticisms of the third force

Humanistic psychology has been criticised on four main grounds. First, its concepts (self-actualisation, growth, authenticity) are vague and hard to test; Maslow's description of self-actualisers rested on a small, hand-picked sample of people he admired. Second, its optimism can seem naive in the face of real human destructiveness, a criticism made from inside the movement by Rollo May. Third, it is individualistic: by concentrating so heavily on the individual's inner world, the humanistic and phenomenological approaches miss the wider social, cultural and economic contexts that shape experience and identity, and they reflect a Western, individualist ideal of the self. Fourth, some critics argued that its celebration of self-fulfilment encouraged self-absorption. Positive psychology tried to answer the first criticism with data; cross-cultural and Indian psychologists, as you will see, have pressed the third.

2.6 The Cognitive RevolutionMedium yield · 5 Qs

The humanists attacked behaviourism for leaving out the person. A second group of critics attacked it for leaving out the mind, and this second attack proved decisive in the laboratories. Between the late 1940s and the late 1960s, the study of memory, attention, language, thinking and perception, all of which behaviourism had pushed to the edges, returned to the centre of experimental psychology. The change was so rapid and so complete that it is usually called the cognitive revolution. Why did it happen when it did?

Why behaviourism waned

Several pressures converged, and it helps to keep them separate.

Anomalies inside behaviourism. You have already met two: Tolman's rats that learned maps without reward, and Kohler's apes that solved problems by insight. A third came from Karl Spencer Lashley (1890 to 1958), once Watson's own student. At the Hixon Symposium on "Cerebral Mechanisms in Behavior" in 1948 (published in 1951), Lashley gave a paper on "The Problem of Serial Order in Behavior". A pianist playing a fast passage, or a person speaking a sentence, produces movements far too quickly for each one to be triggered by sensory feedback from the one before, as a chain of stimulus-response links would require. The sequence must be planned in advance, centrally, and organised hierarchically. In other words, behaviour requires internal plans.

The war and its machines. The Second World War posed problems that behaviourism could not answer: how radar operators could stay alert through long watches, how pilots could cope with too many dials and messages at once, how to design controls that people would not misread. Psychologists working on these problems, such as Donald Broadbent in Britain, found themselves talking about attention, capacity and the flow of information through a person.

Information, feedback and computers. In 1948 the engineer Claude Elwood Shannon (1916 to 2001) published a mathematical theory of communication that measured information in units called bits, and the mathematician Norbert Wiener (1894 to 1964) published Cybernetics, the science of control and feedback in animals and machines. A machine with a goal and a feedback loop (a thermostat, a guided missile) behaves purposefully, and nobody thinks it is mystical to say so. Then came the stored-program digital computer, and in 1950 Alan Mathison Turing (1912 to 1954) asked whether machines could think. Computers made internal states respectable. A computer's behaviour cannot be understood without reference to its program and its memory, which nobody can see. If a machine could be described in terms of stored symbols and rules for manipulating them, why not a person?

Language. The final blow came from linguistics, as you will see shortly. Seen through Kuhn's eyes (Section 2.1), these were anomalies piling up until the old framework entered a crisis.

1956 and after

If the revolution has a birthday, it is in 1956. From 10 to 12 September of that year, a Symposium on Information Theory was held at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. George Armitage Miller (1920 to 2012), one of the participants, later dated the birth of cognitive science to its second day, 11 September 1956. On that day Allen Newell (1927 to 1992) and Herbert Alexander Simon (1916 to 2001) described the Logic Theorist, a computer program (written with Cliff Shaw) that proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica, the first program to model human problem-solving. Noam Chomsky (born 1928), a young linguist, presented a paper on formal models of language. And Miller himself spoke about the limits of short-term memory, the subject of his famous 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", which showed that people can hold about seven items (between five and nine) in immediate memory, and that the limit can be stretched by grouping items into larger meaningful units, or chunks (Chapter 7).

The same year saw the Dartmouth summer research project at which John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon and others launched the field of artificial intelligence (McCarthy had coined the term in the 1955 proposal for the meeting), and the publication of A Study of Thinking by Jerome Seymour Bruner (1915 to 2016), Jacqueline Goodnow and George Austin, which analysed the strategies people use to discover concepts (Chapter 8). In 1957 Chomsky published Syntactic Structures, and in 1958 Donald Eric Broadbent (1926 to 1993) published Perception and Communication, which presented the filter model of attention (Chapter 5). Broadbent's book is regarded as the first major theory cast explicitly in information-processing terms: information flows through a series of stages of limited capacity, drawn as a box-and-arrow flow chart.

Key study: Chomsky's review of Verbal Behavior (1959)

The background. In Verbal Behavior (1957), Skinner had argued that language is operant behaviour like any other: children learn to speak because the verbal community reinforces some sounds and not others, and adult speech is controlled by stimuli and reinforcement histories.

The argument. In a long review in the journal Language (1959), Chomsky argued that this could not work. Children acquire the grammar of their language rapidly, at about the same age everywhere, from speech that is fragmentary and full of errors (later called the poverty of the stimulus). They constantly produce and understand sentences they have never heard, so these sentences cannot have been reinforced. Their errors, such as "I goed" or "two mouses", are not imitations of anything adults say; they are over-applications of rules. And when Skinner's terms (stimulus, response, reinforcement) are stretched to cover language, Chomsky argued, they lose their precise laboratory meaning and become a vague paraphrase of ordinary talk.

The conclusion. Language is rule-governed, creative and generative, and human beings must come equipped with an innate capacity for acquiring it (Chapter 8).

The criticism. Behaviour analysts, notably Kenneth MacCorquodale (1970), replied that Chomsky had misunderstood Skinner's position. Skinner never answered publicly. Whatever its fairness, the review was read as a manifesto for the new cognitive psychology, and it made Chomsky, a linguist rather than a psychologist, one of the movement's heroes.

The pieces came together quickly. In 1960 Miller, Eugene Galanter and Karl Pribram published Plans and the Structure of Behavior, which replaced the reflex arc with the TOTE unit (Test, Operate, Test, Exit), a feedback loop in which an organism compares its current state with a goal, acts, and tests again. The same year, Bruner and Miller founded the Center for Cognitive Studies at Harvard. Then, in 1967, Ulric Neisser (1928 to 2012) published Cognitive Psychology, the first textbook of the new field, which gave it its name. Neisser defined cognition as "all the processes by which the sensory input is transformed, reduced, elaborated, stored, recovered, and used". He is called the father of cognitive psychology, the study of internal mental processes such as perception, attention, memory, language, problem-solving and reasoning. (Characteristically, Neisser later criticised his own field: in Cognition and Reality, 1976, he argued that laboratory cognitive psychology had lost touch with how people use their minds in real life, its ecological validity.) The information-processing metaphor

The core idea of the new psychology is the information-processing approach: the mind is treated as a system that takes in information from the senses, transforms it, stores it, retrieves it and uses it to guide action, much as a computer takes input, runs it through a program and produces output. The approach rests on four assumptions. Mental processes can be broken into a sequence of stages. Each stage has a limited capacity. Processing takes time, so the time a task takes (the reaction time) reveals what stages it involves, an idea that revived Donders's mental chronometry from the nineteenth century (Chapter 1). And mental processes can be described as operations on internal representations (symbols and rules), at the level of the "software", without first knowing the "hardware" of the brain.

A worked example shows how the approach thinks. A fast bowler releases a cricket ball at 140 kilometres an hour, and the batsman has about half a second before it reaches him. In information-processing terms: light from the ball is registered by the eyes (sensory input); attention selects the ball from the crowd, the bowler's arm and the sightscreen (selection); the line and length of the delivery are recognised by matching them against patterns stored from thousands of earlier deliveries (pattern recognition, drawing on long-term memory); a shot is selected and its motor program retrieved (decision and response selection); and the bat moves (output). Each stage takes time, and an expert's advantage lies partly in doing some stages faster and partly in anticipating the ball before it is bowled, from cues in the bowler's run-up, which is top-down processing (Chapter 4). Forerunners: Bartlett and Piaget

The cognitive revolution was a revolution mainly in America. In Europe, the study of the mind had never stopped, and two European psychologists in particular are treated as forerunners. Frederic Charles Bartlett (1886 to 1969) of Cambridge, in Remembering (1932), asked English students to read and later recall a Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts". Their recall grew shorter, lost its unfamiliar supernatural details and was rationalised into the shape of an English story. Bartlett concluded that remembering is reconstruction guided by a schema, an organised mental structure of knowledge built from past experience (Chapter 7).

Jean Piaget (1896 to 1980) of Geneva spent half a century, from the 1920s on, studying how children's thinking develops. He called his field genetic epistemology, the study of the origins and growth of knowledge, and described how children build schemas and adapt them through assimilation (fitting new experience into existing schemas) and accommodation (changing schemas to fit new experience). Piaget studied thinking directly at a time when American psychology refused to, which is why he is often named a pioneer of cognitive psychology; in the 2002 survey of eminent psychologists he ranked second only to Skinner. His stage theory is taught in the Developmental book. Other forerunners include the Gestalt psychologists (insight and productive thinking), Tolman (cognitive maps), the Wurzburg school (thought without images, Chapter 1) and the Russian psychologist Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (1896 to 1934), who studied the relation between thought and language.

Chomsky's review was of Verbal Behavior (1957) and appeared in 1959.

Cognitive science and cognitive neuroscience

Cognitive psychology soon joined a larger enterprise. Cognitive science is the interdisciplinary study of mind and intelligence, bringing together psychology, artificial intelligence and computer science, linguistics, philosophy, neuroscience and anthropology; it acquired its own journal and, in 1979, a Cognitive Science Society.

For its first twenty years cognitive psychology mostly ignored the brain, in keeping with the "software" assumption. That changed in the late 1970s, when, as Michael Gazzaniga (born 1939) tells it, he and George Miller coined the term cognitive neuroscience in the back of a New York taxi. Cognitive neuroscience studies how mental processes are carried out by the brain, combining the experimental tasks of cognitive psychology with methods for measuring brain activity: the study of patients with brain damage, electrical recording (EEG and event-related potentials), positron emission tomography (PET) from the 1980s, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) from the 1990s, and transcranial magnetic stimulation. Its methods are taught in the Physiological book.

Connectionism and parallel distributed processing

The serial, box-and-arrow model had a problem. Brains are made of billions of slow neurons, each firing at most a few hundred times a second, yet people recognise a face in a fraction of a second. A strictly serial process with such slow parts could not be that fast. The answer, many researchers argued, was that the brain does many things at once.

Connectionism models the mind as a network of many simple, neuron-like processing units joined by connections of varying strength (weights). Each unit adds up the activation it receives and passes activation on; many units are active at the same time (parallel processing); and a piece of knowledge is stored not in one place but as a pattern of weights spread across the whole network (distributed representation). The landmark was the two-volume Parallel Distributed Processing (1986) by David Everett Rumelhart (1942 to 2011), James Lloyd McClelland (born 1948) and the PDP Research Group, which gave the approach its other name, parallel distributed processing (PDP). Figure 2.3(b) shows a simple network.

Networks learn by adjusting their weights. One idea goes back to Donald Hebb (1949), who proposed that a connection between two neurons strengthens when they are active together; another, the back-propagation rule popularised in 1986 by Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams, adjusts weights in proportion to the network's errors. Connectionist models have several brain-like properties. They degrade gracefully: damage a few units and performance worsens a little rather than failing completely. They are content-addressable: a partial cue can retrieve a whole memory. And they generalise: Rumelhart and McClelland's 1986 model of the English past tense learned regular and irregular verbs from examples, with no rules written in, and even went through a stage of over-regularising ("goed") as children do. Connectionism has deep roots, in Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts's model neurons (1943) and Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958), and today's deep-learning systems are its descendants.

Cognition enters behaviour therapy

The cognitive revolution did not stop at the laboratory door. Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021), whose Bobo doll studies you will meet in Chapter 6, moved social learning theory steadily toward cognition. In 1977 he introduced the concept of self-efficacy, a person's belief in his or her capacity to carry out the actions needed to deal with a particular situation, as a unifying explanation of how people change. Expectations, not reinforcements alone, drive behaviour. His mature system is social cognitive theory (1986). At the same time, Albert Ellis (1913 to 2007) developed rational therapy, later renamed rational emotive behaviour therapy, from 1955, and Aaron Temkin Beck (1921 to 2021) developed cognitive therapy for depression in the 1960s, both based on the idea that maladaptive thoughts produce distress. The merger of these ideas with behaviour therapy produced cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), the therapy that focuses on changing maladaptive thought patterns.

Clinical link

Psychotherapy has gone through three "waves" that mirror this chapter's history: behaviour therapy (the legacy of behaviourism), cognitive therapy and CBT (the legacy of the cognitive revolution), and a third wave of acceptance and mindfulness-based therapies, including acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), which draw explicitly on Buddhist ideas (Section 2.9). Psychoanalysis is not one of the waves of CBT. Each therapy is taught in the Clinical book.

2.7 Psychology Today: Perspectives, Eclecticism and the Enduring IssuesMedium yield · 6 Qs

By the 1970s the age of schools was over. Few psychologists now call themselves behaviourists or Gestaltists; instead they speak of perspectives, ways of approaching a question that coexist and complement one another. The difference is real. A school claimed to be the right way to do psychology; a perspective claims only to be a useful angle on a problem that other perspectives also illuminate.

The biopsychosocial model was proposed by the American psychiatrist George Libman Engel (1913 to 1999) in a 1977 paper in Science, as an alternative to the purely biomedical model of illness: any illness, physical or mental, must be understood as the product of biological, psychological and social factors acting together. It is now the standard framework of clinical psychology and psychiatry. You will also see "developmental" listed as a perspective in some textbooks, meaning the study of how people change as they mature across the lifespan; strictly, developmental psychology is a field of study that uses all the other perspectives.

Within the sociocultural approach, three terms are easily confused. Cross-cultural psychology compares psychological processes across different cultures, usually with the same measures, to find out which are universal and which vary (the work of Harry Triandis, John Berry and Geert Hofstede is typical). Cultural psychology studies, often from within a single culture, how culture and mind "make each other up", treating culture as part of the mind rather than as a variable acting on it. Indigenous psychology builds psychological concepts and theories from within a culture's own traditions instead of importing them; Durganand Sinha's call to indigenise psychology in India, which you will meet in Section 2.8, is an example.

Eclecticism

If every perspective captures part of the truth, why choose one? Eclecticism is the practice of selecting concepts, methods or techniques from different schools according to what best fits the problem in hand. Frederick Thorne argued for an eclectic approach to counselling around 1950, and Arnold Lazarus later distinguished technical eclecticism, using techniques from any school without necessarily accepting its theory (his multimodal therapy is the best-known example), from theoretical integration, which tries to combine the theories themselves. A third route looks for the common factors, such as the therapeutic relationship and the instillation of hope, that make different therapies work. Surveys repeatedly find that eclectic or integrative is the most common orientation among practising clinicians. The danger, critics point out, is a shapeless mixture with no principle for choosing.

The enduring issues

Schools come and go, but the questions that divided them do not. The historian of psychology Robert Irving Watson (1909 to 1980) argued in a 1967 paper, "Psychology: A Prescriptive Science", that since psychology lacks a single paradigm, its history is better organised by prescriptions: contrasting attitudes, held more or less strongly by different psychologists at different times, about what psychology should be and do. He listed eighteen pairs, among them conscious versus unconscious mentalism, determinism versus indeterminism, empiricism versus rationalism, mechanism versus vitalism, molecularism versus molarism, monism versus dualism, nomotheticism versus idiographicism, and peripheralism versus centralism. 2.8 Indian Psychology I: The Movement, Its Pioneers and Samkhya's Map of the MindHigh yield · 31 Qs

Every school you have met so far was born in Europe or North America, and every one of them took for granted a particular picture of the person: an individual mind, studied from the outside, whose deepest questions could in principle be settled in a laboratory. At the end of Section 2.5 you saw one consequence. Critics charged that even humanistic psychology, for all its talk of the whole person, reflected a Western, individualist ideal of the self. Yet the questions psychology asks (what the mind is, why we suffer, how attention can be trained, what the self really is) were being asked in India more than two thousand years before Wundt opened his laboratory in Leipzig.

The Bhagavad Gita is the most famous example. It opens with a warrior, Arjuna, standing in his chariot between two armies. Facing his own teachers and cousins across the field, he tells his charioteer Krishna that his limbs are failing, his mouth is dry, his body trembles and his hair stands on end, that his bow is slipping from his hand and his mind is reeling. He sits down and refuses to fight, because every course open to him seems wrong. The rest of the poem is a dialogue in which Krishna helps him understand his own mind and find his way back to action. A modern reader can hardly miss that it has the shape of a counselling session with a man paralysed by anxiety and moral conflict.

This section and the next tell two connected stories. The first is how modern, Western-style psychology came to India in the early twentieth century, and how Indian psychologists gradually moved from copying it to questioning it. The second is what the classical Indian systems actually say about the mind. We begin by separating two phrases that sound almost the same.

Psychology in India and Indian psychology

Psychology in India means the discipline as it is taught and practised in Indian universities, hospitals and clinics. For most of its first century it was Western psychology carried on in India: laboratories on the Leipzig model, theories from Europe and America, and tests translated or adapted for Indian use. Most of this book is psychology in that sense.

Indian psychology, in the sense used by the movement that took shape around the year 2000, is something else: an approach to psychology rooted in the ideas and practices of the classical Indian traditions (the Upanishads, Samkhya, Yoga, Vedanta, Buddhism and Jainism, the Bhagavad Gita and Ayurveda), joined in later centuries by Indian Sufism and, in the twentieth century, by Sri Aurobindo. It is not the psychology of Indians either; a study of how Indian students cope with examination stress belongs to cross-cultural or cultural psychology (Section 2.7). Its advocates stress that, like the psychology that came out of Germany, it is meant to be universal. Its claims about attention, the self and suffering are claims about human beings anywhere, which happen to have been worked out in India.

What makes the approach distinctive? Its leading scholars usually point to five features.

  • It is consciousness-centred. Consciousness is treated as fundamental rather than as a by-product of the brain, and a sharp line is drawn between consciousness itself, the silent witness, and the mind's changing contents and instruments (thoughts, feelings, memories), which are treated as part of nature.
  • It is holistic. Body, mind and spirit are treated as one system, and so are the person, the family and the community; health is a state of balance among them.
  • It is transformative. The aim is not only to describe the mind but to change it, toward freedom from suffering and the realisation of one's true nature (called moksha, kaivalya or nirvana in different systems). Disciplines such as yoga and meditation are its laboratory techniques.
  • It values disciplined first-person methods. Where Western psychology after Watson distrusted introspection, the Indian traditions developed elaborate methods for training and refining it, and they treat the reports of a trained observer as evidence.
  • It is relational and value-laden. The self is understood in relation to others and to dharma, the order of duties that sustains social and moral life, so ethics is part of psychology rather than outside it.

How it differs from Western psychology: it emphasises holistic and spiritual approaches, including family and community, rather than the isolated individual; it does not rely only on ancient texts without modern relevance, and it is Indian (not Western) psychology that stresses spirituality. Atman is the true self, eternal consciousness; not the physical body, not Jung's collective unconscious and not a passing state of mind.

The pioneers: how modern psychology came to India

The story of psychology in India begins in Calcutta, then the capital of British India and home of its leading university. In 1905, on the initiative of the philosopher Brajendranath Seal (1864 to 1938), the University of Calcutta introduced experimental psychology as a subject within its philosophy curriculum. Seal, a scholar of both Western science and Indian philosophy, wanted Indian students to learn the new laboratory psychology. The university's vice-chancellor, the mathematician and lawyer Asutosh Mookerjee (1864 to 1924), backed him. What was missing was someone trained to run a laboratory.

That person was Narendra Nath Sengupta (1889 to 1944; the name is also written Sen Gupta). Sengupta had studied at Harvard under Hugo Munsterberg, who had himself taken his doctorate with Wundt at Leipzig, so there is a direct line of teachers running from Wundt's laboratory to Calcutta. In 1915 Sengupta set up the first psychology laboratory in India at the University of Calcutta, modelled on the German laboratories, and in 1916 the university created a separate Department of Psychology (often called the Department of Experimental Psychology), the first in the country, with Sengupta in charge. With the sociologist Radhakamal Mukerjee he wrote Introduction to Social Psychology: Mind in Society (1928), one of the first social psychology textbooks written by Indian authors, and he later moved to Lucknow University. For his laboratory, his department and the students he trained, Sengupta is called the father, or founder, of modern psychology in India.

Among the first generation of Calcutta psychologists was a physician who would take the subject in a completely different direction. Girindrasekhar Bose (1887 to 1953; also written Girindra Shekhar Bose) had qualified in medicine and become fascinated by the mind through his own medical practice. His thesis on the concept of repression, published in 1921 as Concept of Repression, earned him what is usually described as the first doctorate in psychology awarded by an Indian university (some Indian study notes give 1916 or 1922 for this degree; 1921, the year of the thesis, is the date most histories give). He sent a copy to Freud, and the two corresponded for the next sixteen years or so. In 1922 Bose founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society in Calcutta, one of the earliest psychoanalytic societies outside Europe and North America, and in 1947 he launched its journal, Samiksha. When Sengupta left Calcutta, Bose succeeded him as head of the psychology department. He is the father of psychoanalysis in India.

Bose did not simply import Freud. His best-known idea, the theory of opposite wishes, holds that every wish comes paired with its opposite: the wish to beat goes with the wish to be beaten, the wish to be male with the wish to be female, the wish to see with the wish to be seen. One member of each pair is expressed in consciousness and behaviour while the other is repressed, and the two can change places. For Bose it was this play of opposites, rather than a fixed Oedipus complex, that lay at the centre of mental life, and he reported that the Oedipal conflict did not hold the same central place in his Indian patients as in Freud's Viennese ones. Freud read his work with interest but was not persuaded. Bose's ideas show an Indian thinker reworking psychoanalysis through his own culture's preoccupation with the reconciliation of opposites, an early example of what would later be called indigenisation.

Institutions followed the pioneers. In 1923 the Indian Science Congress opened a section for psychology and educational science. In 1925 Sengupta founded the Indian Psychological Association, and in 1926 the association's Indian Journal of Psychology, the first Indian psychology journal, began publication. Universities across the country then opened departments of their own; Table 2.11 gives the main dates.

From imitation to indigenisation

For its first fifty years, psychology in India was largely imitative. Indian psychologists, many trained in Britain or America, repeated Western experiments on Indian participants and used Western tests in translation. Did the results mean what they meant in London or Chicago? Increasingly, the answer seemed to be no.

The psychologist who did most to name the problem and chart a way out was Durganand Sinha (1922 to 1998) of the University of Allahabad. In Psychology in a Third World Country: The Indian Experience (1986) he described psychology in India as passing through four broad phases: a period before independence in which the Western academic and experimental tradition was transplanted largely unchanged; a period of rapid expansion after independence, still mostly imitative; a problem-oriented phase from about the 1970s, in which psychologists turned to India's own pressing problems (poverty, deprivation, rural development, social change); and finally a phase of indigenisation, the effort to make psychology's concepts, theories and methods fit the culture in which they are used. Indigenous psychologists distinguish two routes, a pair of terms usually credited to the Filipino psychologist Virgilio Enriquez and used by Sinha in his later writing. Indigenisation from without takes a Western concept or tool and modifies it to suit local conditions. Indigenisation from within builds concepts, theories and methods from the culture itself, from its traditions, texts, languages and everyday life.

Test adaptation is the clearest example of indigenisation from without. The pictures of the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), drawn in the United States, showed American faces, clothes and settings, and Indian respondents told thin, guarded stories about them. Uma Chowdhury (the name is also spelled Chaudhuri) of Calcutta redrew the cards with Indian figures and scenes, producing an Indian modification of the TAT around 1960 (the tests themselves are covered in the Assessment book). Two contributions from organisational psychology show indigenisation moving further, toward concepts grown from Indian conditions.

Jai B. P. Sinha, studying Indian organisations in the 1970s, found that neither the authoritarian leadership common in Indian offices nor the participative style recommended by American research worked best. In The Nurturant Task Leader (1980) he proposed the nurturant-task leader: a leader who combines a strong task orientation (clear goals, discipline, insistence on hard work) with nurturance (warmth, personal care and guidance, interest in the subordinate's growth), and who makes that nurturance depend on the subordinate's work. The style fits a culture that values hierarchy and personal relationships, and Sinha saw it as a stage through which a work group could move toward fuller participation as subordinates matured (leadership styles in general are covered in the Social book). Udai Pareek (1925 to 2010) of the Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad, was a founder of human resource development in Indian organisations; he developed the concepts and scales of role stress (he identified ten organisational role stressors, among them role overload, role ambiguity and role stagnation) and role efficacy, and, with G. Trivedi, a widely used socio-economic status scale for rural India (1964).

Psychoanalysis, too, was reworked from within. Sudhir Kakar (born 1938), a psychoanalyst influenced by Erik Erikson, wrote The Inner World: A Psycho-analytic Study of Childhood and Society in India (1978), which traced how the Indian family, with its long and intense early bond between mother and son and its many caretakers in the joint family, shapes an identity more relational than the Western ideal of the autonomous individual. In Shamans, Mystics and Doctors (1982) he studied India's traditional healers as psychotherapists in their own right. Kakar is the leading figure of a cultural psychoanalysis of India, the field Bose began.

The Indian Psychology movement

Indigenisation asked psychology to fit Indian conditions. By the 1990s a growing number of psychologists wanted something bolder: to take the Indian traditions themselves seriously as sources of psychological theory and method, on an equal footing with the Western schools. The turning point came in 2002, when a national conference on yoga and Indian approaches to psychology, held in Pondicherry (now Puducherry), closed with a statement signed by the psychologists present, the Pondicherry Manifesto of Indian Psychology. The manifesto argued that Indian psychologists had too long confined themselves to replicating Western research, that the Indian traditions contain a rich and systematic understanding of consciousness and the self, together with methods for exploring and transforming them, and that this knowledge should be developed into a modern psychology useful anywhere in the world. Notice that Figure 2.1 marks 2002 as the start of the Indian Psychology movement.

Several people carried the movement forward. Matthijs Cornelissen, a Dutch-born physician and psychologist long resident at the Sri Aurobindo Ashram, was one of its prime movers and founded the Indian Psychology Institute in Pondicherry, which runs courses and workshops grounded largely in Sri Aurobindo's integral psychology. Koneru Ramakrishna Rao (born 1932), a philosopher and parapsychologist and a former vice-chancellor of Andhra University, became its most prolific theorist; with Anand C. Paranjpe of Simon Fraser University in Canada, author of Self and Identity in Modern Psychology and Indian Thought (1998), and Ajit K. Dalal of the University of Allahabad, he edited the Handbook of Indian Psychology (2008), the movement's first comprehensive reference work, and with Paranjpe he later wrote Psychology in the Indian Tradition (2016). Cornelissen, Girishwar Misra and Suneet Varma, both of the University of Delhi, edited the two-volume Foundations of Indian Psychology (2011), one volume on theories and concepts and one on practical applications. Misra is also one of the most influential scholars of cultural and indigenous psychology in India.

The movement has its critics. Some worry that it romanticises the past, that it blurs the line between religion and science, or that claims about pure consciousness cannot be tested. Its defenders answer that the first-person methods of yoga and Buddhism can be made as disciplined as any laboratory technique, that many traditional claims (about meditation, breathing and attention, for example) are now being tested experimentally, and that Western psychology's own assumptions are no less culture-bound for being familiar. The debate is a live one, and you will find both sides in Indian journals today.

The darshanas: a map of Indian thought

Before we open the first of the classical systems, you need a map. Indian philosophy is traditionally divided into schools called darshanas, from a root meaning "to see": each is a way of seeing reality. Samkhya: purusha and prakriti

Samkhya is the oldest of the systematic Indian philosophies of mind. Its name means "enumeration", because it explains the world by counting out its basic principles, twenty-five of them. Tradition names its founder as the sage Kapila, of whom nothing certain is known. Its oldest surviving text is the Samkhya Karika of Ishvarakrishna, a short work of about seventy verses usually dated to around the fourth or fifth century CE. Its ideas, however, are much older; they run through the Upanishads, the Mahabharata and the Gita, and the Yoga of Patanjali takes them over almost entire.

The Samkhya Karika begins, as any clinician might, with suffering. Human beings, it says, are tormented by three kinds of suffering: suffering from within ourselves, in body and mind (adhyatmika); suffering caused by other beings, people and animals (adhibhautika); and suffering from natural and supernatural forces such as floods and storms (adhidaivika). Ordinary remedies, medicine or ritual, relieve suffering only for a while. The aim of Samkhya is a lasting cure, and the cure is knowledge of what we really are.

What are we? Samkhya's answer is a strict dualism of two ultimate, eternal and independent realities.

The first, purusha, is pure consciousness: the seer, the witness (sakshi). It does not act, change, think, feel or choose; it simply is aware. It has no qualities, it is neither produced nor productive, and it is untouched by anything that happens. Samkhya holds that there are many purushas, one for each living being, because beings are born, suffer and die at different times. The second, prakriti, is primordial matter or nature: unconscious, active, ever-changing and one. It is the uncaused cause from which everything that is not consciousness evolves, and it is made of three strands or qualities, the gunas, which in its unmanifest state are held in perfect balance. Samkhya explains how the world arises from prakriti by its doctrine that an effect already exists, unmanifest, in its cause (satkaryavada), as curd exists potentially in milk or oil in sesame seeds. Evolution is the manifestation of what was latent.

When prakriti comes into the presence of purusha, its balance is disturbed and it begins to evolve, unfolding in a fixed order into twenty-three products, or evolutes. The first is mahat, "the great one", which in the individual is buddhi, the intellect. From buddhi comes ahamkara, the "I-maker", the sense of being a separate self. Ahamkara then evolves in two directions. On its sattvic (luminous) side it produces manas, the sensory mind, together with the five organs of perception (jnanendriyas: hearing, touch, sight, taste and smell) and the five organs of action (karmendriyas: speech, grasping, movement, excretion and reproduction). On its tamasic (inert) side it produces the five subtle elements (tanmatras: sound, touch, form, taste and smell as pure potentials), and from them the five gross elements (mahabhutas: space, air, fire, water and earth), of which the physical world, including the body, is made. Rajas, the active strand, supplies the energy for both lines of evolution. Purusha, prakriti and the twenty-three evolutes together make the twenty-five principles (tattvas) of Samkhya. Figure 2.4 lays out the whole scheme.

Look at Figure 2.4 again and notice something that surprises most Western readers. Intellect, ego and mind, everything a Western psychologist would call mental, are all products of prakriti. They are part of nature, as material as the body, only subtler. Descartes drew the great dividing line between mind and body; Samkhya draws it between consciousness and everything else, with the mind on the side of matter. Why, then, does the mind seem conscious? Because, Samkhya and Yoga answer, it reflects the light of purusha, as a clear crystal placed beside a red flower appears red. Thinking, deciding and feeling are processes in prakriti; the awareness of them belongs to purusha alone. In modern terms, Samkhya separates the contents and operations of the mind, which cognitive psychology studies, from awareness itself, the question at the heart of what philosophers now call the hard problem of consciousness (Chapter 5).

Two images from the Samkhya Karika show how the system pictures their relation. Purusha and prakriti are like a lame man who can see and a blind man who can walk: the lame man rides on the blind man's shoulders, and together they find their way. And prakriti is like a dancer who performs for an audience and stops once she has been fully seen. Why do we suffer? Because of aviveka, non-discrimination: consciousness mistakes the activities of prakriti for itself, so that the witness says "I am angry", "I am tired", "I am a failure", when anger, tiredness and failure belong to the body and mind. The cure is viveka, discriminative knowledge, the direct realisation that the seer is not the seen. The result is kaivalya, "aloneness" or isolation: purusha rests in its own nature, free, and prakriti ceases to act for it. The same word names the goal of Patanjali's yoga (Section 2.9).

Exam trap

Purusha is passive, unchanging and conscious; prakriti is active, ever-changing and unconscious. So "Purusha is described as dynamic and ever-changing" is the false statement in a list about Samkhya; "prakriti is the cosmic material from which all beings emerge", "purusha is the essence of pure consciousness" and "prakriti evolves into the five great elements (pancha mahabhutas)" are all true. Prakriti is the primordial matter from which all forms of life emerge; it is not pure consciousness, not unaffected by change and not the intelligent self. The gunas belong to prakriti, not to purusha, atman or Brahman, and not to samadhi or samskara. Ahamkara is an evolute of prakriti, not a second principle beside it. Samkhya is dualist (many purushas and one prakriti); Advaita Vedanta is monist (Section 2.9).

The three gunas

If everything in nature, including the mind, is made of prakriti, and prakriti is made of three strands, then every thought, feeling and act is some mixture of the three. This is the doctrine of the three gunas, the best-known idea in Indian psychology. The word guna means a strand, as in the strands twisted together to make a rope, and also a quality. The three strands are always present together and never found alone, in different proportions in different things and at different times. Like the oil, the wick and the flame of a lamp, the Samkhya Karika says, they are opposed in nature yet work together to produce one light.

The first, sattva, is the strand of lightness, clarity and illumination. In the mind it shows itself as calm, balance, harmony, knowledge, contentment and goodness. The second, rajas, is the strand of energy, motion and stimulation. In the mind it shows itself as activity, passion, desire, ambition and restlessness, and, when it runs to excess, as anger, greed, anxiety and attachment. The third, tamas, is the strand of heaviness, inertia and obstruction, which covers and darkens. In the mind it shows itself as dullness, lethargy, sleepiness, confusion, ignorance and delusion. The Samkhya Karika links the three to pleasure, pain and dejection respectively, and to three functions, illumination, activity and restraint. A traditional colour code, found as early as the Shvetashvatara Upanishad, makes sattva white, rajas red and tamas black.

It is the Bhagavad Gita that turned the gunas into a full psychology of personality. The Gita is a poem of seven hundred verses in eighteen chapters, set within the epic Mahabharata; its fourteenth chapter is devoted to the gunas, and chapters 17 and 18 classify faith, food, worship, charity, knowledge, action, the doer, the intellect, resolve and happiness into sattvic, rajasic and tamasic kinds. The gunas, Krishna tells Arjuna, are born of prakriti and bind the embodied self: sattva binds by attachment to happiness and knowledge, rajas by attachment to action, born of craving, and tamas by heedlessness, laziness and sleep. From sattva comes knowledge, from rajas greed, and from tamas negligence, delusion and ignorance. Table 2.13 gathers their signatures.

A worked example shows how the scheme is used. Three friends in Mysuru face the same entrance examination next week. Ananya has a revision plan, keeps to it, eats simply, sleeps on time and feels a quiet confidence: sattva predominates. Rohan has bought six guidebooks, switches between them every hour, drinks tea after tea, argues with his roommate and lies awake with his heart racing: rajas predominates. Karthik keeps meaning to start, sleeps until noon, scrolls his phone in bed and tells himself there is no point: tamas predominates. Now apply the Gita's three kinds of happiness. Ananya's hours of revision are hard at first and sweet at the end, when the material falls into place; Rohan's late-night binge of videos is sweet at first and bitter the next morning; Karthik's oversleeping is dull and self-deceiving from start to finish. Notice two further points. First, nobody is purely one guna; the gunas describe proportions, and the same person can be sattvic in the morning, rajasic at noon and tamasic after a heavy lunch. Second, the scheme is not only descriptive but practical, since diet, routine, company and practice can shift the balance toward sattva. The Gita's ideal is the person who has gone beyond all three, the gunatita, who stays even-minded in pleasure and pain, praise and blame, honour and dishonour.

Clinical link

The gunas map surprisingly well onto clinical pictures. The person who has stopped eating properly, withdrawn from others and cannot get herself to do anything is in a predominantly tamasic state, which resembles the psychomotor slowing and anergia of severe depression; restless agitation, racing thoughts, irritability and craving are rajasic, as in anxiety and mania. Researchers have built questionnaires to measure the three gunas (David Wolf's Vedic Personality Inventory, from the late 1990s, is one), and in such studies higher sattva scores tend to go with better well-being, and higher rajas and tamas scores with more distress. The disorders themselves are taught in the Disorders book.

Exam trap

Sattva = harmony, balance, clarity, purity (not passion and desire, which is rajas; not inertia, ignorance, darkness or confusion, which are tamas). Rajas = restlessness, passion, desire, over-activity. Tamas = lethargy, inertia, darkness, dullness, ignorance. The stem "Bavisha lost her job, stopped eating properly and cannot get herself to do anything" points to tamas alone; yama is the first limb of yoga (an ethical discipline, not a mental state), pratyahara is sense withdrawal and jnana is knowledge (Section 2.9). The three gunas are sattva, rajas and tamas. Distractors that are not gunas: vata (an Ayurvedic dosha), ahimsa (non-violence, a yama), prakriti (the matter the gunas compose), ahamkara (an evolute, the I-maker), and the triads "atman, Brahman, maya", "karma, dharma, moksha" and "prakriti, purusha, kshetra".

The antahkarana: the inner instrument

Samkhya gives a name to the part of prakriti that does the work of the mind. The antahkarana, literally the "inner instrument", is contrasted with the ten "outer instruments", the five senses and the five organs of action. The Samkhya Karika says the inner instrument is threefold (intellect, I-maker and mind) and the outer tenfold. The Bhagavad Gita uses the same three: Krishna lists earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, intellect and ego as the eightfold division of his lower nature, prakriti. Later Vedanta added a fourth function, chitta, giving the four-part antahkarana of most modern accounts. Each function deserves a careful definition, because the four are easily confused.

The first function, manas, is the sensory mind. It receives and coordinates the impressions brought by the senses, directs attention to them, and controls the organs of action. It is also the seat of desire and of doubt: manas weighs alternatives ("is it this or that?") without being able to settle them. The second, ahamkara, is the I-maker. It appropriates experience as "mine": "I see", "I want", "this is my pen". It is the source of individuality, and in excess of egoism and pride. The third, buddhi, is the intellect. It ascertains, judges, decides and discriminates; it is the highest and most sattvic of the evolutes, the one closest to purusha and the one whose clarity makes liberation possible. The fourth, chitta, in the fourfold scheme, is the storehouse of memory, holding the latent impressions (samskaras) left by every experience. (In Patanjali's Yoga, as you will see, chitta has a wider sense: the whole mind-stuff, all three inner instruments together.) Table 2.14 summarises the four.

Push one moment through the scheme. You are writing an examination in a silent hall when a phone rings somewhere behind you. Your ears register the sound and manas takes it up: a sound, a ring, a phone, should I attend or ignore it? Chitta supplies the memory: that tune is familiar. Ahamkara claims it: "that is my ringtone!" Buddhi settles the matter: "my phone is switched off in my bag outside the hall, so it cannot be mine; keep writing". And manas sends your hand back to the answer sheet. If you compare this with the information-processing account of the batsman in Section 2.6 (sensory input, recognition from long-term memory, decision, response), the parallel is striking. The difference lies at the very end. For the Indian systems, none of these four functions is the knower. The awareness that witnessed the whole episode belongs to none of them; it belongs to purusha, or, in Vedanta, to the atman.

Exam trap

Which function is the ego, the I-maker? Ahamkara (not manas, the sensory mind; not buddhi, the intellect; not chitta, memory). The Gita's three elements of mind are manas, buddhi and ahamkara, not "prakriti, purusha, kshetra" (kshetra, the "field", is the Gita's word for the body and nature that the self knows), not the purusharthas "dharma, artha, kama", and not the gunas "sattva, rajas, tamas". Do not equate ahamkara with Freud's ego: the Freudian ego is an executive that tests reality, a job closer to buddhi; ahamkara is simply the sense of "I". Some popular study notes, following modern teachers, describe manas as memory and chitta as consciousness; the classical Vedanta assignment is the one in Table 2.14, and ahamkara is the ego in every version.

2.9 Indian Psychology II: Yoga, the Self and the Paths of TransformationHigh yield · 11 Qs

Samkhya told you what the mind is made of. The question a clinician asks next is practical: can the mind be changed, and if so, how? The Indian traditions answered with methods, and the most systematic of them is yoga. In September 1893 a young monk from Calcutta, Swami Vivekananda, addressed the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago and became famous overnight; three years later he published Raja Yoga (1896), which presented Patanjali's yoga to Western readers not as a religion or a set of exercises but as a science of the mind. This section follows his lead. It begins with yoga, moves to the Indian theories of the self and its states and sheaths, and then surveys the other great paths of transformation: Buddhist, Sufi and integral psychology, Ayurveda, and the ideas of karma and dharma.

Patanjali's Yoga Sutras: stilling the mind

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali are a collection of 195 or 196 aphorisms (the count varies slightly between editions), arranged in four chapters: on absorption (samadhi), on practice (sadhana), on the extraordinary powers that practice brings (vibhuti), and on liberation (kaivalya). A sutra is literally a thread, a compressed statement meant to be memorised and unpacked by a teacher, and the oldest surviving commentary, the Yoga Bhashya attributed to Vyasa, has guided readers ever since. Nothing reliable is known about Patanjali the man, and scholars date the compilation anywhere between about the second century BCE and the fourth century CE. Yoga adopts Samkhya's map of purusha and prakriti almost unchanged, adding one thing: Ishvara, a special purusha forever untouched by affliction and karma, who can serve as an object of devotion. For this reason Yoga is sometimes called "Samkhya with God".

The second sutra defines the whole discipline in four words: yogas chitta vritti nirodhah. Take them one at a time. Yoga is the subject. Chitta is the mind-stuff, the whole inner instrument of Section 2.8. Vritti means a whirl or fluctuation, any modification of the mind: a perception, a thought, a memory, a mood. Nirodha means restraint, stilling or cessation. So chitta-vritti-nirodha, yoga itself, is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind. The next two sutras give the reason. When the fluctuations are stilled, "the seer abides in its own nature"; at other times the seer identifies with the fluctuations. The classic image is a lake. When wind ripples its surface, you cannot see the bottom, and the reflection of the moon breaks into a thousand pieces; when the water is still, the bottom is visible and the reflection whole. The goal of the stilling is not a blank mind for its own sake but liberation, kaivalya: consciousness resting in itself, no longer confused with the mind's activity. Notice how far this is from the modern image of yoga as physical fitness. Of the 195 sutras, only three deal with posture.

What are the fluctuations? Patanjali lists five kinds of vritti, each of which can be klishta (afflicted, leading to suffering) or aklishta (unafflicted).

  1. Pramana, valid cognition, which comes from perception, inference and reliable testimony (the three pramanas of Section 2.8).
  2. Viparyaya, error: false knowledge that does not correspond to the real form of its object, like taking a coiled rope in the dusk for a snake.
  3. Vikalpa, verbal conceptualisation: an idea that follows words but has no real object, such as "the horn of a hare" or the abstractions we think with but never meet.
  4. Nidra, sleep: a fluctuation whose content is the absence of other contents.
  5. Smriti, memory: the retention of an object once experienced.

Read the list again with a modern eye: perception and reasoning, illusion, imagination and abstraction, sleep, memory. It is very nearly the table of contents of a general psychology textbook, drawn up some two thousand years ago.

Patanjali's commentator Vyasa added a description of five levels (bhumis) of the mind, which reads like a scale of attentional control. The kshipta mind is restless and scattered, driven by rajas; the mudha mind is dull and stupefied, sunk in tamas; the vikshipta mind is distracted but occasionally steady, the state of most of us most of the time; the ekagra mind is one-pointed, held on a single object; and the niruddha mind is fully restrained and still. Yoga proper begins at the one-pointed level and culminates in the restrained one.

How is the stilling achieved? Through two means that work together (sutra 1.12). The first, abhyasa, is practice: repeated effort to rest in stillness, which becomes firmly grounded only when it is kept up for a long time, without interruption and with devotion. The second, vairagya, is non-attachment or dispassion: freedom from craving for objects, whether seen or merely heard of. Practice supplies direction; non-attachment removes the pull of distraction. A modern psychologist would call the first sustained training of attention and the second a kind of acceptance, the willingness to let urges arise and pass without acting on them.

Why does the mind keep fluctuating? Because of five afflictions, the kleshas, which Patanjali lists in the second chapter.

  1. Avidya, ignorance: taking the impermanent for permanent, the impure for pure, the painful for pleasant and the not-self for the self. It is the field in which all the others grow.
  2. Asmita, "I-am-ness" or egoism: identifying the seer with the instruments of seeing, so that one says "I am this body, these thoughts".
  3. Raga, attachment: the craving that follows pleasure.
  4. Dvesha, aversion: the repulsion that follows pain.
  5. Abhinivesha, clinging to life: the fear of death, which, Patanjali notes, is present even in the wise.

The afflictions may be dormant, weakened, interrupted or fully active. They generate actions, actions leave latent impressions (samskaras) in the chitta, and the impressions give rise to new fluctuations, a self-perpetuating cycle. The cycle can be weakened by kriya yoga, the "yoga of action", which consists of discipline (tapas), self-study (svadhyaya) and surrender to Ishvara (ishvara pranidhana), and the subtler impressions can be dissolved by meditation.

Clinical link

Two sutras anticipate modern therapy. Sutra 2.33 advises that when the mind is disturbed by harmful thoughts, one should cultivate their opposites (pratipaksha bhavana), which is close in spirit to the cognitive therapist's practice of identifying an unhelpful thought and building a balanced alternative (Section 2.6). Sutra 1.33 recommends friendliness toward the happy, compassion toward the suffering, gladness toward the virtuous and equanimity toward the wrongdoer as ways to make the mind clear, attitudes that compassion-focused therapies now teach deliberately. The therapies are taught in the Clinical book.

Exam trap

The primary goal of yoga according to Patanjali is control of the mind (chitta-vritti-nirodha) leading to liberation (kaivalya): not physical fitness, not cognitive enhancement and not "emotional regulation only", although yoga may bring all three. Do not mix the two lists of five. The five vrittis (pramana, viparyaya, vikalpa, nidra, smriti) are kinds of mental activity; the five kleshas (avidya, asmita, raga, dvesha, abhinivesha) are afflictions that drive them. Abhyasa = practice; vairagya = non-attachment.

The eight limbs of yoga

The method itself is set out in sutra 2.29 as ashtanga yoga, the yoga of eight limbs (ashta, eight; anga, limb). The order matters, because each limb prepares for the next, moving from conduct toward others, through the body and breath, to the innermost work of attention. The first two limbs set the ethical foundation. The yamas are ahimsa (non-violence), satya (truthfulness), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (sexual restraint) and aparigraha (non-possessiveness); when they are kept universally, regardless of time, place and circumstance, Patanjali calls them the "great vow". The niyamas are shaucha (purity), santosha (contentment), tapas (austerity or disciplined effort), svadhyaya (self-study, including study of the scriptures) and ishvara pranidhana (surrender to Ishvara). Why begin with ethics? Because a mind full of conflict with others, guilt and greed cannot be stilled by any technique; the yamas and niyamas remove the largest sources of disturbance first. Asana and pranayama then steady the body and the breath.

The fifth limb, pratyahara, is the hinge of the system. The word means drawing back (prati, against, and ahara, food or what is taken in): the senses stop "feeding" on their objects and are drawn inward. The Gita's image is a tortoise drawing its limbs into its shell. With pratyahara, attention turns from the outer world to the inner. The last three limbs are the stages of meditation proper. The first, dharana, is concentration: fixing the mind on one point, an object, the breath, a mantra, and bringing it back each time it wanders. The second, dhyana, is meditation: the concentration becomes an unbroken flow, so that it no longer needs to be dragged back. The third, samadhi, is absorption: the sense of a separate meditator falls away and only the object remains in awareness. Patanjali calls the three together, practised on one object, samyama.

The distinctions between the last four limbs are easy to blur, so push one practice session through them. A student sits to meditate on a candle flame in a hostel room in Mysuru. She stops her attention being pulled by the traffic outside, the smell of the mess and her phone, and turns her senses inward: that is pratyahara. She fixes her gaze and attention on the flame and, each time her mind drifts to tomorrow's class, gently brings it back: dharana. After some minutes the effort drops away and attention flows steadily to the flame without breaks: dhyana. For a moment even the sense "I am meditating" disappears and there is only the flame: samadhi.

Patanjali divides the limbs into two groups. The first five are bahiranga, the outer limbs, and the last three, dharana, dhyana and samadhi, are antaranga, the inner limbs; pratyahara is the bridge between them. Samadhi itself has grades. In samprajnata samadhi the mind is absorbed in an object and still has cognitive content; in asamprajnata samadhi there is no object at all, only the latent impressions remain, and these too are finally dissolved. The end is kaivalya in its yogic sense: the gunas, their purpose fulfilled, return to their source, and purusha is established in its own nature. A simple sentence will help you remember the order of the eight: "You Need A Peaceful Place; Do Daily Sitting" (yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi).

Clinical link

Yoga has become one of India's most studied contributions to mental health, and since 2015 the International Day of Yoga has been observed every 21 June. At NIMHANS in Bengaluru, controlled trials have tested yoga as an add-on to medication for schizophrenia and depression, with encouraging effects on symptoms and functioning, and breathing practices drawn from pranayama are widely used for anxiety. The research question is always the same as for any treatment: does it work better than a credible control? Yoga is used alongside, not instead of, standard care.

Exam trap

Order of the last four limbs: pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi (not dharana before pratyahara, and not dhyana before dharana). Concentration on a single point = dharana; unbroken flow of meditation = dhyana; turning the senses inward, away from sounds, smells and sights = pratyahara; breath regulation = pranayama; personal observances = niyama. Karma is not one of the eight limbs (karma yoga is a path in the Gita), and neither is jnana (knowledge). Bahiranga = the first five limbs; antaranga = the last three.

The paths of the Gita: action, knowledge and devotion

Patanjali's way is the way of meditation. The Bhagavad Gita recognises that people differ, and offers several paths suited to different temperaments. The first, karma yoga, is the path of action: doing one's work and duty in the right spirit. Jnana yoga is the path of knowledge: discriminating the self from the not-self through study and reflection. Bhakti yoga is the path of devotion: love and surrender to God. The sixth chapter of the Gita also describes dhyana yoga, the path of meditation, close to Patanjali's. Vivekananda later presented these as four yogas, karma, jnana, bhakti and raja (his name for Patanjali's yoga). Hatha yoga, the yoga of postures, breathing and cleansing practices systematised in medieval texts such as the Hatha Yoga Pradipika of Svatmarama (fifteenth century), is the ancestor of most yoga classes today.

At the heart of the Gita's psychology of action is one famous verse (2.47): you have a right to your action alone, never to its fruits; do not let the fruits of action be your motive, and do not be attached to inaction either. This is the principle of nishkama karma, desireless or disinterested action: acting fully and well, as one's duty, without attachment to the outcome. The next verses define yoga as evenness of mind in success and failure and as "skill in action". Psychologically, nishkama karma is a cure for the anxiety that comes from fixing one's mind on results one cannot control. Sport psychologists who tell an athlete to focus on the process rather than the scoreboard, and therapists who help clients act on their values whatever the outcome, are teaching something very similar.

The Gita also offers one of the earliest analyses of how a passing thought can grow into self-destruction (2.62 to 2.63). Brooding on objects of the senses produces attachment; from attachment arises desire; from frustrated desire, anger; from anger, delusion; from delusion, the loss of memory (of what one knows to be right); from the loss of memory, the destruction of discrimination (buddhi); and with the destruction of buddhi, a person is lost. Set it beside a cognitive therapist's account of how an angry episode escalates and the resemblance is plain. Its ideal is the sthitaprajna, the person of steady wisdom described at the end of the second chapter, who is unshaken by sorrow, free from craving, fear and anger, and who can withdraw the senses from their objects at will. Elsewhere (12.12) the Gita ranks the stages of the path: knowledge is better than mere practice, meditation better than knowledge, and renunciation of the fruits of action better than meditation, because peace follows immediately on renunciation. Indian textbooks often summarise this as a progression from practice (abhyasa) through knowledge (jnana), meditation (dhyana) and renunciation (tyaga) to peace (shanti).

Exam trap

The principle of disinterestedness on which the Gita is based is nishkama karma, action without attachment to its fruits. Karma yoga is the broader path of action that applies the principle; jnana yoga (knowledge) and bhakti yoga (devotion) are different paths. If both "karma yoga" and "nishkama karma" appear, the stem's word "principle" points to nishkama karma.

The self in Vedanta and the four states of consciousness

Who, or what, is the self that all these practices are meant to free? The Upanishads, the philosophical texts at the end of the Vedas (hence Vedanta, "the end of the Veda"), answer with a story. In the Chandogya Upanishad, the gods and the demons hear that the creator Prajapati has spoken of a self that is free from evil, old age, death, grief, hunger and thirst. Each side sends a pupil: Indra for the gods and Virochana for the demons. After thirty-two years of discipline, Prajapati tells them to look at their reflections in a pan of water, dressed in their finest clothes: that, he says, is the self. Virochana goes home satisfied and teaches the demons that the body is the self. Indra, on the way home, has a doubt. If the self is the body, it is blind when the body is blind and perishes when the body perishes. He returns, studies another thirty-two years, and is told that the self is the one who moves about happily in dreams. Again he doubts: in dreams one is chased, beaten and weeps. After thirty-two more years he is told that the self is the one in deep, dreamless sleep. But in deep sleep, Indra objects, one knows neither oneself nor anything else; it is like being annihilated. After five more years, one hundred and one in all, Prajapati teaches him the self that is beyond body, dream and sleep, which only uses the body as its vehicle.

The story is a lesson in method: the self is found by examining, one after another, what it is not. The Upanishads call the true self the atman: pure, eternal, unchanging consciousness, the witness of body and mind, distinct from the physical body and from the fleeting contents of thought and feeling. They call the ultimate reality underlying the whole universe Brahman. And their boldest teaching is that the two are one. In the Chandogya, the sage Uddalaka repeats to his son Shvetaketu, "tat tvam asi", "that thou art"; in the Brihadaranyaka, the realisation is phrased "aham brahmasmi", "I am Brahman". When asked to describe the self, the Upanishadic teacher Yajnavalkya can only say "neti, neti", "not this, not this".

The most influential interpretation of these texts is Advaita Vedanta, the non-dualism systematised by Adi Shankaracharya (Shankara), who lived in the eighth century CE. For Advaita, Brahman alone is real; the world of many separate things is maya, appearance, neither simply real nor simply unreal; and the individual self (jiva) is in truth none other than atman, which is Brahman. Bondage is caused by ignorance (avidya), which superimposes the limited body-mind on the self, just as a coiled rope seen in dim light is mistaken for a snake. Liberation (moksha) comes not from action but from knowledge (jnana) that removes the error; when the light comes on, the snake is not killed, it is simply seen never to have been there. Later Vedantins disagreed: Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism) held that individual souls and the world are real parts of Brahman, and Madhva's Dvaita (dualism) that God and souls are eternally distinct. Notice the contrast with Samkhya too: Samkhya has many purushas and a real prakriti, a dualism, while Advaita has one consciousness and a world of appearance, a monism.

Indra's four stages, waking body, dreamer, deep sleeper and the self beyond, have a systematic counterpart in the Mandukya Upanishad, the shortest of the principal Upanishads (just twelve verses), which analyses the self through the four states of consciousness. In jagrat, the waking state, consciousness is turned outward and experiences gross physical objects; the self in this state is called Vaishvanara. In svapna, the dream state, consciousness is turned inward and experiences subtle objects made by the mind itself; the self here is Taijasa, "the luminous". In sushupti, deep dreamless sleep, there is neither desire nor dream; consciousness is an undivided mass, resting in bliss; the self here is called Prajna. The fourth, turiya (literally "the fourth"), is not a further state alongside the others. It is pure consciousness itself, neither inward- nor outward-turned, peaceful and non-dual, the witness present through all three states, and it is identified with the atman. The Mandukya maps the four onto the sacred syllable Om, pronounced A-U-M: A is waking, U is dreaming, M is deep sleep, and the silence that follows is turiya. Why should a psychologist care? Because the scheme makes a claim that can be argued about. When you wake from deep sleep you say "I slept well; I knew nothing". Who knew that nothing was known? Vedanta argues that some witnessing awareness must have persisted through the gap, and that this continuity across waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep is the best evidence that the self is not identical with any of its states. Exam trap

Turiya = "the fourth", pure consciousness, the witness of all three states, not a deeper stage of sleep. Sushupti = deep dreamless sleep; its self is called Prajna, so lists sometimes give the states as jagrat, svapna, prajna and turiya. Svapna = dream, not deep sleep. Texts that speak of avastha-traya ("the three states") count waking, dream and deep sleep, with turiya as what lies beyond them. Atman versus Brahman: atman is the self, Brahman ultimate reality; Advaita says they are one. Advaita Vedanta (Shankara) = non-dualism; Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja) = qualified non-dualism; Dvaita (Madhva) = dualism.

The five sheaths and the three bodies

If the self is pure consciousness, why do we experience ourselves as bodies with breath, moods and opinions? The Taittiriya Upanishad answers with a second famous story and a model that is now one of the most widely used frameworks in Indian health psychology. The young Bhrigu asks his father Varuna to teach him Brahman, and Varuna tells him only to reflect deeply. Bhrigu concludes first that food is Brahman, since beings are born from food, live on food and return to it; dissatisfied, he reflects again and decides that it is breath, then mind, then understanding, and at last bliss. Each answer goes deeper than the one before.

The panchakosha ("five sheaths") model turns Bhrigu's progression into a map of the person as five concentric layers covering the self, from the grossest and outermost to the subtlest and innermost.

  1. Annamaya kosha, the food sheath: the physical body, built and sustained by food.
  2. Pranamaya kosha, the vital sheath: the life-force (prana) that animates the body through breath, circulation and the other vital functions, together with the organs of action.
  3. Manomaya kosha, the mental sheath: manas with the five senses, the layer of perception, emotion, desire and doubt, where sense impressions are received and processed.
  4. Vijnanamaya kosha, the sheath of understanding: buddhi with the senses, the layer of discrimination, judgement, decision and the sense of being the doer.
  5. Anandamaya kosha, the bliss sheath: the subtle joy experienced in deep sleep and deep meditation, the layer closest to the self.

The atman itself is not a sheath. It is what the sheaths cover, as a scabbard covers a sword or five lampshades of increasing thickness dim a single lamp. A person who identifies with the outermost sheath says "I am fat" or "I am ill"; with the mental sheath, "I am anxious"; with the intellectual sheath, "I am the one who decides". Vedanta teaches discrimination of the self from each sheath in turn, the method Indra used.

Vedanta groups the five sheaths into three bodies (sharira-traya), and links the bodies to the states of consciousness you have just met. The sthula sharira, the gross body, is the annamaya kosha, and it is the body we experience in waking. The linga sharira (also called sukshma sharira), the subtle body, consists of the three middle sheaths, pranamaya, manomaya and vijnanamaya, and it is the body we experience in dreams; tradition holds that it is the subtle body that carries the individual from one life to the next. The karana sharira, the causal body, is the anandamaya kosha, experienced in deep sleep. Turiya is the atman, beyond all three. Figure 2.5 puts the whole scheme in one picture. (Samkhya, which does not use the kosha language, describes the subtle body by its components instead: buddhi, ahamkara, manas, the ten organs and the five subtle elements.)

The model is not only of historical interest. Yoga therapists in Bengaluru built an "integrated approach of yoga therapy" on it, prescribing practices for each sheath (postures and diet for the food sheath, breathing for the vital sheath, meditation and devotional practice for the mental sheath, study and counselling for the sheath of understanding, and practices aimed at contentment for the bliss sheath), and the panchakosha model is often used in India as a framework for holistic, multi-level assessment of well-being, much as the biopsychosocial model (Section 2.7) is used in the West.

Exam trap

Outermost sheath: annamaya (the food body); then pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya, and innermost, anandamaya. The sheath involved in perceptual processing is manomaya (manas with the senses), not vijnanamaya (intellect and discrimination), pranamaya (vital energy) or anandamaya (bliss). The subtle body (linga sharira) corresponds to the three middle sheaths, pranamaya, manomaya and vijnanamaya. A practice item keys "pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya and atman": the three sheaths are right, but the atman is the witness, never a component of any body. Ahamkara, manas, buddhi and chitta is the antahkarana, not the subtle body as the kosha scheme defines it.

Buddhist psychology

Of all the Indian traditions, the one that has entered Western clinical psychology most deeply is Buddhism. Its founder, Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha ("the awakened one"), was born a prince of the Shakya clan at Lumbini, in what is now Nepal; the traditional dates are 563 to 483 BCE, though many historians now place him somewhat later. According to the traditional story, the sight of an old man, a sick man, a corpse and a wandering ascetic led him to leave his palace in search of a way beyond suffering. Six years of extreme self-denial brought him close to death but not to freedom, and he turned instead to a "middle way" between indulgence and asceticism. Meditating under a tree at Bodh Gaya, he attained awakening, and in his first sermon, at the Deer Park in Sarnath near Varanasi, he set out the teaching on which all Buddhist psychology rests.

The Four Noble Truths have the structure of a physician's consultation: diagnosis, cause, prognosis and treatment.

  1. Dukkha: life as ordinarily lived is marked by suffering or unsatisfactoriness, in birth, ageing, illness and death, in separation from what we love, contact with what we dislike and not getting what we want.
  2. Samudaya, the origin of suffering: craving (tanha, literally "thirst"), whether for sensual pleasure, for existence or for non-existence.
  3. Nirodha, the cessation of suffering: when craving ends, suffering ends. This state is nirvana (Pali nibbana), the "blowing out" of the fires of greed, hatred and delusion.
  4. Magga, the path to the cessation of suffering: the Noble Eightfold Path.

What, according to the Buddha, is the person who suffers? Here Buddhism parts company with the Upanishads. All conditioned things, he taught, bear three marks: impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha) and non-self (anatta, Sanskrit anatman). There is no permanent, unchanging self or soul behind experience. What we call a person is a constantly changing process made of five aggregates, the skandhas (Pali khandhas): form or the body (rupa), feeling, pleasant, unpleasant or neutral (vedana), perception (samjna), mental formations such as volitions and habits (samskara), and consciousness (vijnana). In a famous dialogue from the Milindapanha, the monk Nagasena asks the Indo-Greek King Milinda what a chariot is. Is it the axle? The wheels? The pole? None of them, the king admits, yet "chariot" is not something apart from them either; it is a convenient name for parts in a certain arrangement. So too, says Nagasena, is "Nagasena". Belief in a fixed self is, for Buddhism, a root of craving and therefore of suffering. Modern cognitive science, which also describes the self as a construction assembled moment by moment by many processes, has found the Buddhist view surprisingly congenial.

Buddhism also produced one of the world's first systematic psychologies. The Pali canon is divided into three "baskets" (Tipitaka): the Vinaya (rules of monastic discipline), the Sutta (the Buddha's discourses) and the Abhidhamma ("higher teaching"). The Abhidhamma analyses experience into momentary states of consciousness (cittas) and the mental factors that accompany them (cetasikas, fifty-two in the Theravada lists), each classed as wholesome, unwholesome or neutral. The three unwholesome roots of action are greed (lobha), hatred (dosa) and delusion (moha), the "three poisons"; their wholesome opposites are generosity, loving-kindness and wisdom. In its ambition to catalogue the elements of mind it resembles Titchener's structuralism, but its purpose is therapeutic: to know which states to cultivate and which to abandon. A later Mahayana school, the Yogacara ("practice of yoga") school of Asanga and Vasubandhu (around the fourth century CE), described a "storehouse consciousness" (alaya-vijnana) holding the seeds of past actions below awareness, an idea often compared with the unconscious.

Buddhist practice divides meditation into two kinds. Samatha ("calm abiding") develops concentration on a single object, much like Patanjali's dharana and dhyana. The second, vipassana ("insight", literally "seeing clearly"), uses that stability to observe experience as it arises and passes, until its impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and lack of self are seen directly. Its central skill is sati, mindfulness: the Pali word originally meant remembering or keeping in mind, and came to mean a clear, non-reactive, present-moment awareness of whatever is happening. The Satipatthana Sutta describes four foundations of mindfulness: the body, feelings, the mind and mental objects. Alongside these, Buddhism cultivates four boundless attitudes, the brahmaviharas: loving-kindness (metta), compassion (karuna), sympathetic joy (mudita) and equanimity (upekkha), the same four Patanjali recommends in sutra 1.33. In modern India, vipassana was revived on a large scale by S. N. Goenka (1924 to 2013), a Burmese-born teacher of Indian descent who began teaching in India in 1969, founded the centre at Igatpuri near Nashik in 1976, and made the ten-day silent residential course famous, including courses held inside Tihar Jail in Delhi in the 1990s.

Clinical link

Buddhist mindfulness is the root of the third wave of cognitive-behavioural therapy (Section 2.6). Jon Kabat-Zinn (born 1944) built mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) in 1979 and defined mindfulness as paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment and non-judgementally. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT; Segal, Williams and Teasdale, 2002) uses it to prevent relapse in recurrent depression. Marsha Linehan (born 1943) developed dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) for chronically suicidal clients and borderline personality disorder; it blends cognitive-behavioural change strategies with acceptance and mindfulness practices drawn from Zen Buddhism and with dialectical philosophy, and teaches four skills modules: core mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Steven Hayes's acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) also builds on acceptance and mindful awareness. The therapies are taught in the Clinical book.

A word on Buddhism's sister tradition is useful here, because the two are easily confused. Jainism was shaped by Mahavira, an older contemporary of the Buddha whom Jains honour as the twenty-fourth and last tirthankara ("ford-maker"). Unlike Buddhism, it affirms an eternal, conscious soul (jiva), one for each living being, and treats karma as a subtle matter that clings to the soul through passions such as anger, pride, deceit and greed. Liberation, a state of perfect knowledge called kevala jnana, comes through the "three jewels" of right faith, right knowledge and right conduct, with ahimsa (non-violence) at the centre of conduct. The Jain doctrine of anekantavada, "many-sidedness", holds that reality has many aspects and every single viewpoint is partial, an ancient lesson in intellectual humility.

Exam trap

Anatta (Buddhism) is the opposite of atman (Upanishads): Buddhism denies a permanent self; Vedanta and Jainism affirm one (and Samkhya affirms many purushas). The Abhidhamma is the third basket of the Pali canon, the systematic psychology of mind-states; the Vinaya is monastic discipline and the Sutta the discourses. Mindfulness comes from Buddhist sati and vipassana; samatha is concentration. DBT's core mindfulness skills come from Zen Buddhism (not Stoicism, which influenced Ellis's REBT; not psychoanalysis or existentialism), and DBT blends CBT with mindfulness and acceptance practices and dialectical philosophy (not with free association, drugs or Bowen family systems theory). Its four modules are mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness; cognitive restructuring and relaxation are not modules. Buddhism and Jainism are nastika.

Sufi psychology

Sufism (tasawwuf) is the mystical, inward dimension of Islam, and from about the eleventh and twelfth centuries Sufi teachers made it one of the great spiritual traditions of India. The Chishti order, brought to India by Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, whose shrine at Ajmer still draws millions, and carried on by Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, became famous for its tolerance and for its use of music (sama, the ancestor of qawwali) as a spiritual practice. Like yoga and Buddhism, Sufism contains a detailed psychology of the self and its transformation. The Persian theologian al-Ghazali (1058 to 1111) analysed the "diseases of the heart" (pride, envy, greed, anger) and their cures in terms any counsellor would recognise.

Sufi psychology describes the person in terms of the nafs (the self or ego, especially the lower self of appetites and egoism), the qalb (the heart, the organ of spiritual perception), the ruh (the spirit) and the aql (the intellect). Its central teaching is that the nafs can be refined, and it describes the refinement in seven stages, most of whose names come from the Quran.

  1. Nafs al-ammara, the commanding or tyrannical self, which incites to evil and is ruled by appetite and ego.
  2. Nafs al-lawwama, the self-reproaching or blaming self, which has begun to feel remorse and to struggle against its impulses.
  3. Nafs al-mulhama, the inspired self, which begins to receive inspiration and to act from goodness.
  4. Nafs al-mutma'inna, the tranquil or serene self, at peace and secure in faith.
  5. Nafs al-radiya, the contented self, pleased with whatever comes.
  6. Nafs al-mardiyya, the self that is pleasing to God.
  7. Nafs al-kamila (also called safiya), the complete or pure self.

The journey is often described as passing through four levels, from the outer to the inner: shariat (the religious law, the outward practice), tariqat (the path of inner discipline), haqiqat (inner truth) and marifat (direct knowledge of God). Along the way the seeker passes through maqamat, "stations" reached by effort and stable once attained, such as repentance (tawba), scrupulousness (wara), renunciation (zuhd), poverty (faqr), patience (sabr), trust in God (tawakkul) and contentment (rida), and experiences ahwal, "states" such as love and longing, which come as gifts and pass. The principal practice is zikr (dhikr), remembrance through the rhythmic repetition of divine names, together with contemplation (muraqaba), under the guidance of a teacher (murshid or pir) to whom the disciple (murid) is closely bound, a relationship some writers compare with that of therapist and client. The goal is fana, the "annihilation" or passing away of the ego-self in God, followed by baqa, "subsistence", a return to life in the world with the self transformed.

Exam trap

Lowest stage of the nafs: ammara, the commanding self; next lawwama, the blaming self; the serene self is mutma'inna. Fana = annihilation of the ego in God; baqa = subsistence after it. Maqamat (stations) are earned and lasting; ahwal (states) are given and passing. Sufism is an Islamic tradition: it is neither astika nor nastika.

Sri Aurobindo's integral psychology

The last of the great Indian psychologies is modern. Sri Aurobindo (Aurobindo Ghose, 1872 to 1950) was born in Calcutta, educated in England from the age of seven and at Cambridge, and returned to India to become one of the leaders of the movement for independence. Arrested in 1908 in the Alipore bomb case, he spent a year in jail awaiting trial, and there, by his own account, had the spiritual experiences that changed the direction of his life. Acquitted in 1909, he withdrew in 1910 to Pondicherry, then a French territory, where he spent the rest of his life writing and practising yoga. His collaborator Mirra Alfassa (1878 to 1973), known as the Mother, organised the Sri Aurobindo Ashram and later founded the international township of Auroville (1968). His major works include The Life Divine, The Synthesis of Yoga and the epic poem Savitri.

Aurobindo's integral yoga differs from the classical yogas in one decisive respect. Their aim was release from the world; his was the transformation of life in the world. "All life is yoga", he wrote. He saw evolution as the progressive emergence of consciousness from matter, to life, to mind, and beyond mind to what he called the supermind, and he aimed to integrate the paths of knowledge, works and devotion into one discipline. The psychology built on his writings, which his disciple, the psychologist Indra Sen, is usually credited with naming integral psychology, describes the person along two dimensions.

The first is a concentric system, from the surface inward. On the surface is the outer being, made of three parts: the physical, the vital (Aurobindo's term for the life-energy of desires, emotions, sensations and drives) and the mental. Behind it lies a wider inner or subliminal being, with inner physical, vital and mental parts of far greater range than their surface counterparts. And at the centre is the psychic being, the innermost being: the evolving soul, a "divine spark" that grows from life to life and can, once it comes forward, guide the whole personality. The second is a vertical system. Below the waking mind lie the subconscient and the inconscient; above it rises a series of higher planes of consciousness, the higher mind, the illumined mind, intuition, the overmind and, highest, the supermind. Aurobindo described the work of yoga as a triple transformation: a psychic transformation, in which the psychic being comes to the front; a spiritual transformation, in which higher consciousness descends into the person; and a supramental transformation, the final change of the whole nature. Cornelissen's Indian Psychology Institute (Section 2.8) works largely within this framework, and transpersonal theorists such as Ken Wilber (Section 2.5) drew partly on it for their own use of the word "integral".

Exam trap

The innermost being in the concentric system of integral yoga is the psychic being, not the mind, the vital or the body, which make up the outer being. "The vital" means life-energy, desire and emotion, not the physical body. The supermind belongs to the vertical system, at its top.

Ayurveda: the doshas, the gunas and mental health

India's classical medicine, Ayurveda ("the knowledge of life"), is set out above all in two great compendia, the Charaka Samhita, on internal medicine, associated with the physician Charaka, and the Sushruta Samhita, on surgery, associated with Sushruta. Ayurveda's definition of health is strikingly psychological. Sushruta calls a person healthy (svastha) whose doshas, digestive fire, tissues and excretions are in balance, and whose self, senses and mind are prasanna, clear and content. Health is not merely the absence of disease but balance and contentment in body and mind together.

The body, in Ayurveda, is governed by three doshas, functional principles each built from two of the five elements of Samkhya. Every person is born with a characteristic balance of the three, called his or her prakriti or constitution (note that this is a different use of the word from Samkhya's cosmic prakriti), and illness is a departure from that balance. Each constitution has a recognisable psychological signature, set out in Table 2.18.

Where do the gunas fit? Ayurveda applies them to the mind. The mind (manas) is made of the three gunas; sattva is the guna of mental health and clarity, while rajas and tamas are called the two doshas of the mind (manasa doshas), the sources of mental disturbance, just as vata, pitta and kapha are the doshas of the body. Charaka described sixteen kinds of mental constitution, seven sattvic, six rajasic and three tamasic, an early typology of personality. Ayurveda also had a branch for mental illness. Bhuta vidya, one of its eight branches, dealt with disorders attributed to possession and mental causes, and the Charaka Samhita describes unmada (insanity) and apasmara (epilepsy), distinguishing disorders arising from deranged doshas within the body from those with external causes.

Most remarkable for a clinical psychologist is Charaka's threefold classification of treatment. Daivavyapashraya chikitsa is spiritual or faith-based therapy: prayer, mantras, rituals and offerings. Yuktivyapashraya chikitsa is rational therapy: diet, medicines and physical procedures, reasoned from the cause of the illness. And sattvavajaya chikitsa, "conquest of the mind", is psychotherapy: restraining the mind from harmful objects and strengthening it through knowledge, understanding, courage, memory and concentration. Long before the word existed, Ayurveda recognised a treatment that works through the mind itself.

Exam trap

Doshas are not gunas. Vata, pitta and kapha are the three doshas of the body (Ayurveda); sattva, rajas and tamas are the three gunas (Samkhya and the Gita). So vata is the wrong option in any question about the gunas. In Ayurveda, rajas and tamas are the doshas of the mind. Vata = space and air; pitta = fire and water; kapha = earth and water. Ayurveda's prakriti (a person's constitution) is not Samkhya's prakriti (primordial matter). Sattvavajaya is Charaka's psychotherapy.

Karma and dharma as psychological ideas

Two words from Indian thought have entered every language in the world, usually in a garbled form. Both are psychological ideas, and both shape how millions of people in India explain their lives.

The word karma (from the root kri, to do) means action, and by extension the law that every action has consequences for the one who acts. Each deliberate act leaves a latent impression (samskara) in the mind; impressions accumulate into tendencies (vasanas) that incline us to act the same way again; and actions bear fruit (phala), in this life or, the tradition holds, a later one. Vedanta distinguishes three kinds. Sanchita karma is the accumulated store from the past. Prarabdha karma is the portion of that store which has begun to bear fruit and shapes the circumstances of the present life. Agami (or kriyamana) karma is the new karma being made now, by present choices. Read psychologically, the doctrine of samskaras is a theory of habit: every act strengthens a disposition to repeat it, much as Hull's habit strength (Section 2.2) grows with each reinforced repetition. It is also a theory of moral responsibility and a way of finding meaning in misfortune.

That last use cuts both ways. A fatalistic reading ("it is my karma; nothing can be done") can encourage passivity and the acceptance of injustice. An agentic reading ("my present actions shape my future") encourages responsibility and hope, and the distinction between prarabdha and agami karma leaves plenty of room for choice. Indian psychologists, Ajit Dalal among them, have studied how patients explain their illnesses, and found that beliefs about karma and God often stand beside medical explanations rather than replacing them, sometimes helping people to accept what cannot be changed.

The word dharma (from the root dhr, to hold or sustain) means that which upholds: the cosmic and social order, and the duties, ethics and right conduct that sustain it. Dharma is sensitive to context. What is right depends on one's stage of life, role and situation; hence svadharma, one's own duty, of which the Gita says it is better to perform one's own dharma imperfectly than another's well. Dharma is the first of the four aims of life, the purusharthas: dharma (virtue and duty), artha (wealth and security), kama (pleasure and love) and moksha (liberation). Unlike Maslow's hierarchy (Section 2.5), the scheme does not climb from lower to higher needs; it places dharma as the regulator of the pursuit of wealth and pleasure, with liberation as the final aim. The tradition also describes four stages of life, the ashramas: the student (brahmacharya), the householder (grihastha), the retired forest-dweller (vanaprastha) and the renunciant (sannyasa), a lifespan model that Sudhir Kakar and others have compared with Erikson's psychosocial stages.

When duties collide, the result is a dharma-sankata, a dilemma of dharma: a situation in which every available action violates some obligation. Arjuna on the battlefield, bound by his duty as a warrior to fight and by his duty to his family not to kill his kinsmen, is the classic case, and his despair (the first chapter of the Gita is called "the yoga of Arjuna's despair") is the starting point of the whole poem. Krishna's answer combines several ideas you have met in this section: understand that the self is not the body (Vedanta), act according to your svadharma (dharma), and act without attachment to the fruits (nishkama karma). Indian psychologists use the concept of dharma-sankata in the study of moral reasoning and decision-making in Indian families and organisations.

Clinical link

For a clinical psychologist in India these ideas are not abstractions. Clients explain their distress in terms of karma, fate, dharma and duty to family, and a formulation that ignores those explanations will often fail. Some Indian counsellors draw explicitly on the Gita, treating the Krishna-Arjuna dialogue as a model of a helping relationship that moves from listening to the client's despair, through a change of perspective, to committed action. Whatever framework you use, the rule of the biopsychosocial model (Section 2.7) applies: understand the client's own cultural meanings first.

Step back and notice what these Indian systems share, for all their disagreements about the self. Each begins with suffering; each traces it to a mistake about who we are (avidya, aviveka, craving, the commanding nafs); each prescribes a disciplined training of ethics and attention; and each aims at a freedom that is also a transformation of experience. These are exactly the themes that the transpersonal psychologists of Section 2.5 and the third-wave therapists of Section 2.6 later went looking for in India.

Exam trap

The four purusharthas are dharma, artha, kama and moksha (not the gunas, not the koshas). The four ashramas are brahmacharya, grihastha, vanaprastha and sannyasa. Prarabdha = karma already bearing fruit in this life; sanchita = the accumulated store; agami = karma being made now. Karma as a word means action; it is not one of Patanjali's eight limbs. Dharma-sankata = a moral dilemma between duties, not Bose's theory of opposite wishes.

The case of Sultan's sticks, revisited

Go back to Tenerife, to the chimpanzee sitting on his box and idly playing with two bamboo sticks until, suddenly, he fitted one into the other and raked in the banana. You can now tell all five of the stories the opening promised. A strict behaviourist would refuse to say anything about what went on inside Sultan; she would describe the solution as a response that was emitted and then reinforced, and would want to know his history of reinforcement with sticks. Kohler, the Gestalt psychologist, saw insight: a sudden restructuring of the perceptual field, two separate sticks reorganised into one tool, a solution that appeared all at once, was carried out smoothly and was repeated without hesitation. A psychoanalyst would look past the problem to the motive it served. A cognitive psychologist would model Sultan as an information-processing system searching a problem space, holding the goal in working memory and testing operators against it. And a psychologist trained in the Indian tradition would notice what Kohler himself recorded but did not dwell on: the answer came not while Sultan was straining at the bars but after he had given up and sat quietly, when the restless, rajasic effort had subsided, much as many human solvers report that a solution arrives when they stop forcing it (incubation, Chapter 8).

The schools did not merely tell different stories; they tested one another. In 1945 Herbert Birch found that young chimpanzees with no previous experience of playing with sticks rarely solved a similar problem, but that after a few days of free play with sticks they did. Insight, it turned out, draws on a learning history, which is exactly what a behaviourist would have predicted, while the sudden reorganisation of that learning is exactly what a Gestalt psychologist would have predicted. That is the lesson of this chapter. Each school saw something real, overreached, and was corrected by its rivals; and psychology today, with its overlapping perspectives, its information-processing models, its third-wave therapies drawn from Buddhist mindfulness and its renewed interest in India's own traditions, is the sum of those corrections. Sultan's two sticks, joined end to end, are not a bad image of it.

Chapter summary

2.1 Schools, Systems and Paradigms. A school of thought is a group sharing assumptions about psychology's subject matter, method and explanation, usually with a founder, a manifesto, a home, a journal, students and a rival; Woodworth's Contemporary Schools of Psychology (1931) and Heidbreder's Seven Psychologies (1933) surveyed them. A system is an organised body of assumptions covering the whole field; a theory explains one set of facts. Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) described paradigms, normal science, anomalies, crises and paradigm shifts; a field with competing schools and no shared paradigm is a preparadigmatic science, which fits psychology's age of schools. The humanists numbered the forces: psychoanalysis first, behaviourism second, humanistic psychology third and transpersonal psychology fourth (multiculturalism is Pedersen's "fourth force" in counselling).

2.2 Behaviourism: From Watson's Manifesto to Skinner. Behaviourism was prepared by animal psychology (Darwin, Romanes, Morgan's canon, Loeb's tropisms, Thorndike's puzzle boxes of 1898), by Russian objective psychology (Sechenov, Pavlov and Bekhterev's reflexology) and by functionalism. Watson's 1913 manifesto made psychology "a purely objective experimental branch of natural science" aiming at prediction and control without introspection. He treated thought as subvocal speech (peripheralism), proposed three innate emotions (fear, rage, love), conditioned Little Albert with Rayner (1920) and made the extreme environmentalist "twelve infants" claim (1925). Behaviourism may be metaphysical, methodological or radical. The neobehaviourists drew on logical positivism and Bridgman's operationism (1927): Tolman's purposive behaviourism (molar behaviour, intervening variables, cognitive maps, latent learning), Hull's hypothetico-deductive system (drive reduction, habit strength, reaction potential = habit strength × drive), Guthrie's contiguity and one-trial learning, and Skinner's radical, anti-theoretical behaviourism (Walden Two, Verbal Behavior 1957, Beyond Freedom and Dignity 1971). Kuo abolished instinct. Dollard and Miller (1950) gave early social learning theory, Bandura and Walters (1963) the later one. Behaviourism declined under attack from Chomsky, the Brelands and Garcia but left operational definition and behaviour therapy (Jones 1924, Wolpe 1958).

2.3 Gestalt Psychology and Kurt Lewin. Wertheimer's 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon (apparent movement, with Kohler and Koffka as observers) founded Gestalt psychology, building on Ehrenfels's form qualities. Kohler's apes on Tenerife (1913 to 1920; The Mentality of Apes, 1917 and 1925) showed insight; his hens showed transposition; his theory of isomorphism was tested and rejected in its field form. Koffka's Growth of the Mind, his 1922 article and his Principles of Gestalt Psychology (1935) spread the school, and his Lake Constance story separated the geographical from the behavioural environment. The whole is different from (popularly, greater than) the sum of its parts; field theory, Pragnanz, figure-ground (Rubin, 1915) and productive thinking (Wertheimer, 1945) followed. Lewin's B = f(P, E) located behaviour in the life space (the person and the psychological environment), described with topological and hodological concepts, valences and vectors; his students found the Zeigarnik (recall) and Ovsiankina (resumption) effects; he founded group dynamics and action research. Gestalt therapy (Perls, empty chair) is not Gestalt psychology.

2.4 Psychoanalysis as a School: The First Force. Freud began as a neurologist. Charcot's demonstrations of hysteria under hypnosis (Paris, 1885 to 1886), Janet's dissociation, Bernheim's suggestion (Nancy, 1889) and above all Breuer's treatment of Anna O. (the "talking cure", the cathartic method; Studies on Hysteria, 1895) led Freud to free association and psychoanalysis. The Interpretation of Dreams (1900) called dreams the royal road to the unconscious, and the Clark University lectures (1909) brought psychoanalysis to America. The topographic model divides the mind into conscious, preconscious and unconscious; the structural model (1923) into id, ego and superego. Adler and Jung dissented; Horney, Fromm and Sullivan (the neo-Freudians), Anna Freud and Erikson (ego psychology) and Klein (object relations) followed. Popper criticised psychoanalysis as unfalsifiable.

2.5 The Third Force and Beyond. Maslow (self-actualisation) and Rogers (the actualising tendency, conditions of worth), with Buhler and May, founded humanistic psychology, the third force, which emphasises free will, growth, the whole person and phenomenological understanding; landmarks were the Journal of Humanistic Psychology (1961), the Association for Humanistic Psychology (1961 or 1962), Bugental's five postulates and the Old Saybrook conference (1964). Phenomenology (Husserl) brackets assumptions to describe experience. Existential psychology (Kierkegaard, Heidegger's Dasein, Sartre, Binswanger's Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt, Boss, Frankl's logotherapy, will to meaning and noogenic neurosis, May, Yalom's four ultimate concerns) confronts death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. Transpersonal psychology (Maslow and Sutich, 1969; Grof, Tart, Wilber, Assagioli) is the fourth force. Positive psychology was launched by Seligman (APA president for 1998) with Csikszentmihalyi (flow) in 2000, and includes PERMA, character strengths, hope and savouring. Humanistic and phenomenological approaches are criticised as vague and as missing social and cultural context.

2.6 The Cognitive Revolution. Behaviourism waned under anomalies (Tolman, Kohler, Lashley's 1948 paper on serial order), wartime problems of attention, information theory (Shannon), cybernetics (Wiener), the computer (Turing, 1950) and linguistics. In 1956 came the MIT symposium (Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist, Chomsky, Miller's magical number seven plus or minus two), the Dartmouth project on artificial intelligence and Bruner's A Study of Thinking; Broadbent's Perception and Communication (1958) was the first major information-processing theory; Chomsky's 1959 review of Verbal Behavior became a manifesto; and Neisser's Cognitive Psychology (1967) named the field. The information-processing approach treats the mind as a series of limited-capacity stages operating on representations. Bartlett (schema) and Piaget were forerunners. Cognitive science and cognitive neuroscience followed, and connectionism (Rumelhart and McClelland's Parallel Distributed Processing, 1986) modelled knowledge as weights in parallel networks. Bandura's self-efficacy (1977), Ellis and Beck brought cognition into therapy, producing CBT and later its mindfulness-based third wave.

2.7 Psychology Today: Perspectives, Eclecticism and the Enduring Issues. Schools gave way to overlapping perspectives: biological (neurobiological), evolutionary, psychodynamic, behavioural, cognitive, humanistic, sociocultural and Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977); developmental psychology is a field that uses them all. Cross-cultural psychology compares cultures, cultural psychology studies how culture and mind make each other up, and indigenous psychology builds theory from within a culture. Eclecticism selects from several schools (Lazarus's technical eclecticism versus theoretical integration, and common factors). The enduring issues, which Robert I. Watson (1967) called prescriptions, include mind-body, nature-nurture, free will-determinism, mechanism-vitalism, molar-molecular, nomothetic-idiographic (Windelband, Allport), objectivism-subjectivism and empiricism-rationalism; whether psychology has a Kuhnian paradigm is still debated.

2.8 Indian Psychology I: The Movement, Its Pioneers and Samkhya's Map of the Mind. Psychology in India is the discipline as practised in India; Indian psychology is an approach rooted in classical Indian thought, consciousness-centred, holistic, transformative, relational and open to disciplined first-person methods. Experimental psychology entered the Calcutta curriculum in 1905 (Seal); Sengupta founded the first laboratory (1915) and department (1916) and the Indian Psychological Association (1925), whose Indian Journal of Psychology began in 1926; Bose, father of Indian psychoanalysis, founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society (1922) and proposed the theory of opposite wishes; Gopalaswamy began psychology at Mysore (1924). Durganand Sinha led indigenisation (from without and from within); Uma Chowdhury adapted the TAT; J. B. P. Sinha proposed the nurturant-task leader (1980); Pareek studied role stress; Kakar applied psychoanalysis to Indian childhood. The Pondicherry Manifesto (2002), Cornelissen's Indian Psychology Institute, the Handbook of Indian Psychology (Rao, Paranjpe and Dalal, 2008) and Foundations of Indian Psychology (Cornelissen, Misra and Varma, 2011) launched the Indian Psychology movement. The darshanas are astika or nastika by their acceptance of the Vedas. Samkhya (Kapila; Ishvarakrishna's Samkhya Karika) is a dualism of passive, conscious purusha and active, unconscious prakriti, which evolves into buddhi, ahamkara, manas, the ten organs, the subtle and the gross elements (25 tattvas); liberation (kaivalya) comes from discriminating the seer from the seen. Prakriti is made of three gunas: sattva (clarity, harmony), rajas (activity, passion, restlessness) and tamas (inertia, dullness, ignorance), elaborated in the Gita. The antahkarana comprises manas (sensory mind), ahamkara (I-maker), buddhi (intellect) and, in Vedanta, chitta (memory).

2.9 Indian Psychology II: Yoga, the Self and the Paths of Transformation. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras define yoga as chitta-vritti-nirodha, aiming at kaivalya; they list five vrittis (pramana, viparyaya, vikalpa, nidra, smriti), two means (abhyasa and vairagya), five kleshas (avidya, asmita, raga, dvesha, abhinivesha) and the eight limbs: yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana and samadhi, the first five outer and the last three inner. The Gita offers the paths of action, knowledge, devotion and meditation, with nishkama karma, action without attachment to its fruits. The Upanishads identify the atman with Brahman; Shankara's Advaita Vedanta treats the world as maya. The Mandukya Upanishad describes waking (jagrat), dreaming (svapna), deep sleep (sushupti) and turiya, the fourth; the Taittiriya describes five sheaths (annamaya, pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya, anandamaya), grouped into gross, subtle (linga) and causal bodies. Buddhism teaches the Four Noble Truths, the Noble Eightfold Path, anatta and the five skandhas, systematised in the Abhidhamma, with samatha, vipassana and mindfulness (sati) as practices that now underlie MBSR, MBCT, DBT and ACT. Sufi psychology describes seven stages of the nafs and the goal of fana. Sri Aurobindo's integral psychology places the psychic being at the centre of the person. Ayurveda describes three doshas (vata, pitta, kapha), treats rajas and tamas as doshas of the mind and includes Charaka's sattvavajaya psychotherapy. Karma (with samskaras and three kinds of karma) and dharma (svadharma, the purusharthas, the ashramas and dharma-sankata) shape how Indians explain their lives.

  6. Kurt Lewin is widely titled the father of modern social psychology. Which OTHER field is he also credited with founding? (Amity recall)

    (a) Psychoanalysis (b) Action research (c) Psychophysics (d) Functionalism

    Answer: (b) Action research. Lewin founded action research, the cycle of planning, acting, observing and reflecting with the people affected. Psychoanalysis is Freud's, psychophysics Fechner's and functionalism belongs to James, Dewey and Angell.

  7. Which therapy focuses on "empty chair technique"? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) CBT (b) Gestalt therapy (c) Psychoanalysis (d) DBT

    Answer: (b) Gestalt therapy. The empty chair is Fritz Perls's technique for dialogue with an absent person or a disowned part of the self. Note that Gestalt therapy is Perls's, not the Gestalt psychology of Wertheimer, Kohler and Koffka; CBT, psychoanalysis and DBT do not use the technique as a signature.

  8. Which perspective in psychology emphasizes unconscious processes and early childhood experiences? (RML 2025)

    (a) Behavioral (b) Psychodynamic (c) Humanistic (d) Cognitive

    Answer: (b) Psychodynamic. The psychodynamic perspective, descended from Freud, explains behaviour by unconscious conflict and early experience. Behavioural stresses conditioning, humanistic free will and growth, and cognitive internal mental processes.

  9. Noogenic anxiety is described under: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Humanistic school (b) Gestalt school (c) Existential school (d) Psychodynamic school

    Answer: (c) Existential school. Frankl's noogenic neurosis (and anxiety) arises from frustrated meaning, the spiritual dimension, which places it in existential psychology. The humanistic school is a close neighbour, but the concept is Frankl's, not Maslow's or Rogers's.

  10. The concept of "Dasein," which refers to human existence as "being-in-the-world," is primarily associated with which school of psychology? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Behavioral psychology (b) Existential psychology (c) Cognitive psychology (d) Psychoanalytic psychology

    Answer: (b) Existential psychology. Dasein is Heidegger's term (Being and Time, 1927), made clinical by Binswanger and Boss in Daseinsanalysis. None of the other schools uses it.

  11. Viktor Frankl's concept of the "will to meaning," the fundamental motivational force in humans to find purpose in life, is central to which therapeutic school? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) (b) Logotherapy (c) Existential-Humanistic Therapy (d) Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

    Answer: (b) Logotherapy. Frankl's own school, the "third Viennese school", rests on the will to meaning. Existential-humanistic therapy is the broad family it belongs to, so (c) is the tempting but less specific answer; REBT is Ellis's and ACT is Hayes's.

  12. Savoring is a concept in Positive Psychology that involves: (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Avoiding negative experiences (b) Enhancing the enjoyment of positive experiences (c) Minimizing stress through relaxation (d) Reflecting on past traumas

    Answer: (b) Enhancing the enjoyment of positive experiences. Bryant and Veroff defined savouring as attending to, appreciating and intensifying positive experiences, past, present or anticipated; it is to good events what coping is to bad ones. The other options describe avoidance, relaxation and trauma work.

  13. Who is a pioneer contributor to Cognitive Psychology? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Jean Piaget (b) Kohler (c) Chomsky (d) Kohlberg

    Answer: (a) Jean Piaget. Piaget studied the growth of thinking from the 1920s, long before the cognitive revolution, and is treated as a forerunner of cognitive psychology. Chomsky was a linguist whose 1959 review fuelled the revolution, Kohler a Gestalt psychologist and Kohlberg a theorist of moral development.

  14. "Third-wave CBT" includes all EXCEPT: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (b) Dialectical Behavior Therapy (c) Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (d) Psychoanalysis

    Answer: (d) Psychoanalysis. The third wave comprises acceptance and mindfulness-based therapies such as ACT, DBT and MBCT, many drawing on Buddhist practice. Psychoanalysis is the first force and is not a wave of CBT at all.

  15. In Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), the "core mindfulness" skills are primarily derived from which tradition? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Stoicism (b) Zen Buddhism (c) Psychoanalysis (d) Existentialism

    Answer: (b) Zen Buddhism. Marsha Linehan drew DBT's mindfulness skills from Zen practice and balanced them against cognitive-behavioural change strategies. Stoicism influenced Ellis's REBT; psychoanalysis and existentialism are unrelated to DBT's mindfulness module.

  16. What is the primary focus of Indian psychology? (RML 2025)

    (a) The application of Western psychological theories exclusively (b) The promotion of meditation without psychological context (c) The study of Indian folklore and mythology (d) The integration of Indian philosophical traditions with psychological concepts

    Answer: (d) The integration of Indian philosophical traditions with psychological concepts. Indian psychology draws its concepts and methods from Samkhya, Yoga, Vedanta, Buddhism and related traditions. Option (a) describes imitative psychology in India, and Indian psychology is neither mythology nor meditation stripped of theory.

  17. Who is regarded as the pioneer of psychology in India, establishing the first Indian psychological laboratory? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) G. S. Rathi (b) S. K. Mangal (c) Girindrashekhar Bose (d) A. K. Singh

    Answer as keyed: (c) Girindrashekhar Bose. Strictly, the first laboratory was set up by N. N. Sengupta at Calcutta in 1915, and Sengupta is not among the options. Bose, Sengupta's Calcutta colleague and the father of Indian psychoanalysis, is the only pioneer listed; Mangal and A. K. Singh are textbook authors.

  18. Who is considered the father of Indian psychology? (RML 2025)

    (a) Narendra Nath Sen Gupta (b) Sudhir Kakar (c) Giriswar Misra (d) G. R. S. Rao

    Answer as keyed: (d) G. R. S. Rao. The key appears to intend a leader of the Indian Psychology movement, most plausibly K. Ramakrishna Rao, co-editor of the Handbook of Indian Psychology (2008). If the question meant modern scientific psychology in India, the answer would be (a) Sengupta; Kakar is a psychoanalyst and Misra a contemporary scholar of indigenous psychology.

  19. The concept of the three gunas comes mainly from which school of Indian philosophy, alongside the Bhagavad Gita? (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Nyaya (b) Charvaka (c) Samkhya (d) Mimamsa

    Answer: (c) Samkhya. Samkhya's prakriti is composed of sattva, rajas and tamas, and the Gita builds its psychology of personality on them. Nyaya is the school of logic, Charvaka materialism, and Mimamsa the school of Vedic ritual and duty.

  20. In Samkhya dualism, the principle of pure consciousness, the passive witness, is Purusha. The three gunas belong to its counterpart, which is: (Amity recall)

    (a) Atman (b) Prakriti (c) Brahman (d) Ahamkara

    Answer: (b) Prakriti. Prakriti, primordial matter, is made of the three gunas. Atman and Brahman are Vedantic terms for the self and ultimate reality, and ahamkara is one of prakriti's products, not its counterpart.

  21. Bavisha lost her job. Since then she is stressed, has stopped eating properly and cannot get herself to do anything. According to yogic philosophy, the main issue she is dealing with is: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Yama alone (b) Tamas alone (c) Both yama and tamas (d) Rajas alone

    Answer: (b) Tamas alone. Withdrawal, loss of appetite and inability to act are the signature of tamas, the guna of inertia and dullness. Yama is the first limb of yoga (ethical restraints), not a mental state, so (a) and (c) fail; rajas would show as restless over-activity.

  22. In Indian psychology, the 'inner instrument' (antahkarana) classically has four functions. Which one is the EGO or 'I-maker'? (Amity recall)

    (a) Manas (b) Buddhi (c) Ahamkara (d) Chitta

    Answer: (c) Ahamkara. Ahamkara refers experience to a separate "I". Manas is the sensory mind that doubts and deliberates, buddhi the intellect that decides, and chitta the store of memory impressions.

  23. According to Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, the primary goal of yoga is: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Physical fitness (b) Control of the mind (citta vritti nirodha) and liberation (kaivalya) (c) Cognitive enhancement (d) Emotional regulation only

    Answer: (b) Control of the mind and liberation. Sutra 1.2 defines yoga as the stilling of the mind's fluctuations, and its aim is kaivalya, consciousness resting in itself. Fitness, sharper cognition and calmer emotions may be by-products, but none is the goal.

  24. Which is the correct order of the last four of Patanjali's eight limbs? (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Dharana, pratyahara, dhyana, samadhi (b) Pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi (c) Pratyahara, dhyana, dharana, samadhi (d) Dhyana, dharana, pratyahara, samadhi

    Answer: (b) Pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi. The senses must first be withdrawn (pratyahara) before attention can be fixed (dharana), the fixed attention becomes an unbroken flow (dhyana) and the flow becomes absorption (samadhi). The other options swap concentration and meditation or put withdrawal after concentration.

  25. In the Pancha Kosha model, the five sheaths of the self run from gross to subtle. The OUTERMOST sheath, the physical body sustained by food, is: (Amity recall)

    (a) Annamaya kosha (b) Pranamaya kosha (c) Manomaya kosha (d) Anandamaya kosha

    Answer: (a) Annamaya kosha. Anna means food, and the food sheath is the gross body. Pranamaya is the vital sheath of breath, manomaya the mental sheath of perception and emotion, and anandamaya the innermost bliss sheath.
