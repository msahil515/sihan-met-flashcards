In 1958 a fifty-two-year-old Englishman, known in the scientific literature only by his initials, S.B., had an operation that gave him back his sight. He had been effectively blind since he was about ten months old. He had learned to get about with confidence and to use tools, and he lived a full, independent adult life by touch and hearing. Then a surgeon replaced the clouded cornea of one eye with a graft, the bandages came off, and light from the world reached a working retina for the first time in half a century.

What did he see? Not, as almost everyone expected, the world suddenly laid out before him. He heard the surgeon's voice in front of him, turned towards it, and saw a blur. Only because the voice came from it did he decide that the blur must be a face. The psychologist Richard Gregory and his colleague Jean Wallace, who studied him over the following months, recorded a string of puzzles. S.B. could read the time from a large clock almost at once, because as a blind man he had told the time by feeling the hands of a watch with no glass. He could read capital letters, which he had learned by touch at school, but not lower-case ones, which he had never felt. Looking down from his hospital window, which was several storeys up, he thought he could touch the ground with his feet if he hung from the sill by his hands.

The most famous episode came at the Science Museum in London. Gregory and Wallace showed S.B. a lathe in a glass case, a machine he had always wanted to understand. He could make nothing of it. He was then allowed to run his hands over it with his eyes shut. When he opened them again he said, "Now that I've felt it I can see."

Think about what that sentence means. The light reaching S.B.'s eyes did not change when he touched the lathe. What changed was what his brain could do with that light. This chapter is about that difference: the difference between light arriving at the eye and a person seeing a lathe, a face or a cliff edge. Keep S.B. in mind; you will meet him again at the end of the chapter.

4.1 What Perception Is: From Sensation to InterpretationMedium yield · 5 Qs

Open your eyes and the world is simply there: a cup on the table, a friend across the room, the far wall at its proper distance. It feels as if nothing could be more direct. This section shows you why that feeling is misleading, and introduces the great debate about how perception actually works.

Sensation, perception and the inverse problem

Chapter 3 followed physical energy as far as the sense organs: light focused on the retina, sound waves moving the basilar membrane, and receptors turning that energy into neural signals. That is sensation, the detection of physical energy by the sense organs and its conversion (transduction) into neural activity. Perception is what the brain then does with those signals: it selects, organises and interprets them so that they become meaningful objects and events. Sensation gives you a pattern of light; perception gives you your mother's face.

Why should perception be hard? Because the information at the eye is poorer than the world that produced it. The retina is a curved, nearly flat sheet, so the image on it has only two dimensions, yet you see a three-dimensional world. A small coin held close and a large plate held far away can throw exactly the same-sized image on the retina. A white page in shadow can send less light to your eye than a black page in sunshine. In each case many different states of the world could have produced the same retinal image. Working backwards from the image to the world that caused it is called the inverse problem, and every theory of perception is, at heart, a proposal about how the brain solves it.

Bottom-up and top-down processing

Two kinds of processing help. Bottom-up processing (also called data-driven processing) starts with the stimulus: receptors register features such as edges, colours and movements, and these are built up step by step into more complex descriptions. Information flows in one direction, from the senses upwards. Top-down processing (conceptually driven processing) runs the other way: knowledge, expectations and context, stored in the brain, shape how the incoming data are interpreted.

You can feel both at work when you read a doctor's handwriting on a prescription. Some letters are clear enough to be read from their shapes alone (bottom-up). Others are scrawls that you can read only because you know what word is likely to come next, for example that "Paracet..." is probably followed by "amol" (top-down). Almost all real perception mixes the two. The theories that follow differ in how much weight they give to each.

Perception as inference: Helmholtz, Gregory and the Bayesian brain

The oldest answer to the inverse problem came from Hermann von Helmholtz (1821 to 1894), the German physicist and physiologist who measured the speed of the nerve impulse and wrote the great Handbook of Physiological Optics (completed in 1867). Helmholtz argued that perception is the end point of unconscious inference: the brain draws rapid, automatic conclusions about the world from the sensory evidence plus past experience, much as a scientist draws conclusions from data, except that we are not aware of doing it. His likelihood principle states that we perceive the object that is most likely to have produced the pattern of stimulation we receive. A small retinal image of a person is perceived as a normal-sized person far away, not as a tiny person nearby, because that is by far the likelier cause.

A century later the British psychologist Richard Langton Gregory (1923 to 2010) turned this idea into the modern constructivist approach (also called the indirect or top-down approach) in books such as Eye and Brain (1966) and The Intelligent Eye (1970). For Gregory, a perception is a perceptual hypothesis: a best guess about what is out there, constructed from fragmentary sensory data and stored knowledge, and tested against further input. The senses supply evidence; the brain supplies the theory.

What is the evidence for this view? Gregory's favourite was the hollow-face illusion. Look at the inside of a hollow mask from a metre or so away and you see a normal face bulging towards you, not a hollow one. Your knowledge that faces stick out is so strong that it overrides the depth information from your two eyes. Illusions (Section 4.5), ambiguous figures that flip between two interpretations, and the effects of expectation (Section 4.8) all fit the idea that perception is an active construction that can be wrong.

Critics raised two problems. First, if perception is guesswork from poor data, why is it usually so accurate? Second, most illusions persist even when you know the truth; if perception were a hypothesis open to correction by knowledge, knowing that the lines of the Müller-Lyer figure are equal ought to make them look equal, and it does not.

The modern descendant of Helmholtz's idea is the Bayesian approach to perception, named after the English clergyman and mathematician Thomas Bayes (about 1701 to 1761), whose theorem shows how to update a belief in the light of new evidence. On this view the brain combines two things: prior beliefs (how likely each possible state of the world is, learned from experience or built in by evolution) and the likelihood (how well each possibility fits the current sensory evidence). The result, the posterior, is the percept. When the sensory evidence is clear it dominates; when it is weak or ambiguous the priors take over. The Indian-born neuroscientist Vilayanur S. Ramachandran (born 1951) gave a neat demonstration in 1988. Shaded discs that are lighter at the top look like bumps and discs that are lighter at the bottom look like dents, and turning the page upside down swaps them. The brain is applying a prior that light comes from above, because for the whole of our evolutionary history it has. The hollow face is the same story: the prior that faces are convex outweighs weak evidence that this one is concave.

Exam trap

The approach that "emphasises unconscious inference and probabilistic reasoning" is the constructivist approach (Helmholtz, Gregory); its Bayesian form adds that the percept combines prior beliefs and sensory evidence. Do not pick "genetic predispositions", "cultural background" or "emotional states" for the Bayesian question: they can shape priors, but the definition is priors plus evidence. And do not confuse Helmholtz's unconscious inference with Wundt's apperception: the "apperceptive mass" (a term from Johann Friedrich Herbart that Wilhelm Wundt took over) is the store of existing ideas into which a new perception is absorbed (Chapter 1).

Direct perception: Gibson's ecological approach

The fiercest critic of the inference view was the American psychologist James Jerome Gibson (1904 to 1979). During the Second World War Gibson worked on how to select and train pilots, and he noticed that the cues listed in textbooks, cues taken from flat pictures viewed by a motionless observer, were of little use in explaining how a pilot lands an aircraft. A pilot is moving, and movement creates information. Over three books, The Perception of the Visual World (1950), The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems (1966) and The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (1979), he built the theory of direct perception, also called the ecological approach because it studies perception in the animal's real environment rather than in the laboratory.

Gibson's central claim is that the light reaching a moving observer is not poor at all. The optic array, the structured pattern of light arriving at the eye from every direction, is rich in information that specifies the layout of the world without any need for inference. Three of his ideas are examined often.

  • Invariants are properties of the optic array that stay constant as the observer moves and so specify the world directly. The texture gradient (Section 4.3) is one: the elements of a surface get smaller and denser with distance in a lawful way. The horizon ratio is another: for objects standing on the same ground, the horizon cuts them at the same proportion of their height whatever their distance, so objects of equal height are seen as equal.
  • Optic flow is the pattern of movement in the optic array as you move through the world. When you walk or drive forward, the scene streams outwards from a single point, the focus of expansion, which marks exactly where you are heading. A pilot landing an aircraft keeps the focus of expansion on the start of the runway. No inference is needed; the information is in the light.
  • Affordances, a word Gibson coined in the 1960s and developed in his 1979 book, are the possibilities for action that an object or surface offers to a particular animal. A chair affords sitting, a handle affords grasping, a ledge at knee height affords climbing for an adult but not for a toddler. Gibson claimed that we perceive affordances directly, so that meaning is picked up from the environment rather than added by the brain.

Gibson described the perceiver as "resonating" to this information, as a radio resonates to a broadcast. Because perception is driven by the information in the stimulus and not by stored knowledge, Gibson's theory is the classic example of a bottom-up theory.

His approach was a healthy corrective: it put the moving, acting animal back at the centre of perception and inspired work on how we steer, catch and avoid collisions. But it has weaknesses. It has little to say about illusions, which by definition are cases where the information misleads us. It explains poorly how we recognise objects whose meaning depends on culture and learning (a letter box affords posting a letter only to someone who knows what a letter box is). And "resonance" names the process without explaining it.

Indian context

Watch a fielder at long-on under a skied hit at a cricket match in Bengaluru. He does not calculate the ball's trajectory. Studies of fielders running to catch high balls suggest that they run so as to keep the image of the ball rising smoothly in their field of view; if it starts to accelerate upwards they move back, if it starts to drop they move in. This is Gibsonian perception at its best: action guided directly by a property of the changing optic array.

Bringing the two together: Neisser's perceptual cycle and two visual systems

Must we choose between Gregory and Gibson? Ulric Neisser (1928 to 2012), whose Cognitive Psychology (1967) gave the new field its name, argued in his later book Cognition and Reality (1976) that we need not. His perceptual cycle joins both directions of processing into a loop (Figure 4.1). A schema, an organised body of knowledge about what to expect, directs exploration: where you look, what you listen for, what you reach out to touch. Exploration samples the information actually available in the object or scene. The information sampled then modifies the schema, which directs the next round of exploration. Perception is not a single event but a continuous cycle in which top-down expectation and bottom-up information keep correcting each other.

A second reconciliation came from the brain. In 1995 David Milner and Melvyn Goodale proposed that the visual system has two streams with different jobs. A ventral stream, running into the temporal lobe, serves vision-for-perception: recognising what objects are, which needs stored knowledge, just as Gregory said. A dorsal stream, running into the parietal lobe, serves vision-for-action: guiding the hand and body in real time, fast and largely unconscious, very much as Gibson described. Their patient D.F., whose ventral stream had been damaged by carbon monoxide poisoning, could not say whether a slot in a disc was horizontal or vertical, yet could turn her hand correctly and post a card through it. The two classic theories may simply be describing two different visual systems (the anatomy is covered in the Physiological book).

Two further labels appear as options in examination questions. The information-processing approach treats perception as a series of stages, each transforming the output of the one before, much as a computer program does. The computational approach, associated above all with David Marr, asks what problem each stage of vision has to solve and what calculations would solve it; you will meet it in Section 4.7. "Who proposed the bottom-up theory of perception?" The answer is James J. Gibson; Gregory and Helmholtz are the top-down (constructivist) theorists, and Wertheimer is a Gestalt psychologist. Clinical link

Several studies have found that people with schizophrenia are much less fooled by the hollow-face illusion than other people: they are more likely to see the mask as hollow, as it really is. One interpretation is that the balance between top-down expectation and bottom-up evidence is altered in the disorder. It is a reminder that "seeing correctly" in a laboratory task is not always a sign of a healthy brain.

4.2 Perceptual Organisation: The Gestalt LawsMedium yield · 8 Qs

Before you can recognise an object you must first decide which bits of the visual field belong together and which belong to something else. That sounds trivial until you try to explain how it is done. The school that took this problem most seriously, and that has contributed more to the study of perception than any other, was Gestalt psychology.

The Gestalt starting point

Gestalt psychology was founded in Germany in 1912, when Max Wertheimer (1880 to 1943) published his experiments on apparent movement, with Kurt Koffka (1886 to 1941) and Wolfgang Köhler (1887 to 1967) as his first subjects and lifelong collaborators (the history of the school is told in Chapter 2). The German word Gestalt means form, shape or configuration. The school's founding claim, aimed squarely at Wundt's and Titchener's attempt to break experience into elementary sensations, was that the whole is different from the sum of its parts. A melody is still the same melody when every note is changed by transposing it into another key; the "melodiness" lies in the relations between the notes, not in the notes themselves. In perception, said the Gestaltists, the brain spontaneously organises the visual field into wholes according to a small set of innate laws, and in 1923 Wertheimer set these out in a famous paper.

Figure and ground

The most basic act of organisation is to split the field into a figure, the object that stands out and is attended to, and a ground, the background against which it appears. The Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin (1886 to 1951) studied this figure-ground relationship in his 1915 doctoral work and drew the best-known demonstration of it, Rubin's vase: a white vase on a black background which, as you look, becomes two black faces in profile on a white background. Rubin described how figure and ground differ in experience:

  • The figure has a thing-like quality (it looks like an object) and a definite shape; the ground is shapeless and looks like substance or empty space.
  • The contour between them seems to belong to the figure, not the ground.
  • The figure appears nearer and in front; the ground appears farther away, diffuse, and seems to continue behind the figure.
  • The figure is more vivid, more easily named and better remembered.

Certain regions are more likely to become figure: smaller regions, enclosed regions, symmetrical and convex regions, and regions in the lower part of the field. Figure-ground organisation is not only visual. At a noisy wedding reception, the voice of the person talking to you is an auditory figure and the din of the hall is ground.

The vase and faces is a reversible figure: either region can be seen as figure, but never both at once, and the percept flips back and forth. Notice what this means. The stimulus never changes, yet the perception does. Organisation is something the perceiver does, not something in the picture.

Exam trap

"Which is not a characteristic of a figure?" The answer is "appears diffused and spatially distant", because that describes the ground. Smaller size, clear outlines and greater object character (thing-like quality) are all properties of the figure. Camouflage works by breaking figure-ground segregation, so that the animal's contour is lost in the ground.

The laws of grouping

Once figures are separated from ground, elements must be grouped into larger units. The Gestalt laws of perceptual organisation describe how. Look at Figure 4.2 as you read; each panel lets you watch one law at work in your own perception.

By the law of proximity, elements that are close together in space or in time are seen as belonging together. In the first panel the dots are equally spaced vertically but not horizontally, so you see three pairs of columns rather than four rows. Proximity in time works the same way: the taps of a drum heard close together form a rhythm group. By the law of similarity, elements that look alike (in colour, shape, size or orientation) are grouped together; in the second panel equal spacing would favour columns, but similarity makes you see rows of filled and open dots. By the law of closure, we tend to complete figures that have gaps, so that the broken outline in the third panel is seen as a circle, not as four separate arcs. The law of good continuation (also called the law of continuity) holds that elements that follow a smooth line or curve are grouped together, so that the fourth panel shows a straight line crossing a curve rather than two odd angular shapes meeting at a point. By the law of common fate, elements moving together in the same direction are grouped together: a flock of birds or a column of marching soldiers is seen as one unit, and in the sixth panel the arrows split the row into two sets.

The Gestaltists also described a law of symmetry (symmetrical regions are grouped together and tend to be seen as figure), and Wertheimer added a law of past experience or familiarity (elements that together form a familiar object tend to be grouped). Modern researchers have added two laws. The law of common region, proposed by Stephen Palmer in 1992, states that elements inside a common enclosed region are grouped together, even when proximity says otherwise (fifth panel, top row). The law of connectedness (element connectedness), described by Palmer and Irvin Rock in 1994, states that elements physically joined by lines are seen as a unit (fifth panel, bottom row). In the same 1994 paper Palmer and Rock proposed uniform connectedness: any connected region of uniform colour, texture or lightness is organised as a single perceptual unit. Both kinds of connectedness, and common region, are powerful enough to override proximity and similarity. Table 4.2 brings all the laws together.

Prägnanz, illusory contours and how good the laws are

Above all the others stands the law of Prägnanz, from the German for conciseness or pithiness, also called the law of good figure, the law of good form or the law of simplicity. It states that every stimulus pattern is seen in such a way that the resulting structure is as simple, regular, stable and complete as the conditions allow. Two overlapping rings could in principle be seen as three oddly shaped pieces, but you see two circles because that is the simplest description. The other laws can be seen as special cases of this one, which is why Prägnanz is called the master law of Gestalt organisation and why a question about a figure "perceived not in parts but organised in the best possible whole form" is answered by Prägnanz.

The power of these laws shows most clearly when they create something that is not there. In 1955 the Italian psychologist Gaetano Kanizsa (1913 to 1993) drew three black discs, each with a wedge cut out, arranged so that the wedges face each other. Observers see a bright white triangle lying on top of the discs, complete with sharp edges across empty paper. These illusory contours (subjective contours) show closure and Prägnanz at work: the simplest interpretation of three cut discs is one triangle covering three whole discs. A related process, amodal completion, makes us perceive an object partly hidden behind another as continuing, complete, behind it.

How good are the Gestalt laws? They are excellent descriptions; every textbook of design, typography and advertising still uses them. But they are descriptions, not explanations. They do not say why the brain groups this way, and they are vague about what happens when two laws conflict. The Gestaltists' own physiological explanation, that perceptual organisation reflects electrical fields in the brain with the same shape as the percept (Köhler's isomorphism), was not supported by later experiments in which the cortex of animals was filled with metal strips or wires to disrupt any such fields without destroying perception. And research since then has shown that grouping is partly learned: what counts as "similar" or "familiar" depends on experience, as you will see in Section 4.8.

Exam trap

The tendency to complete a figure is closure, not continuity or continuation (continuity is about smooth paths, not gaps). Items close together in space and time group by proximity. The figure "organised in the best possible whole form" is Prägnanz. The school that contributed most to perception is Gestalt psychology, not structuralism, behaviourism or psychoanalysis. And Gestalt therapy (Fritz Perls) has nothing to do with Gestalt psychology except the name.

Exam trap

Do not confuse interposition (a depth cue: a nearer object blocks a farther one, Section 4.3) with interpolation (filling in between known points, as the visual system does when it completes a contour hidden behind an occluder) or extrapolation (extending a trend beyond the known points). One past paper also used structuring for working out a rule or organising principle from incomplete evidence and then picking out the items that satisfy it; that keyed answer fits the Gestalt idea that perception imposes structure on incomplete input.

Clinical link

In 1938 the American child psychiatrist Lauretta Bender built a test from nine of Wertheimer's grouping designs. The Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test asks the person to copy the designs; distortions, rotations and failures of integration can point to visuoconstructive or organic difficulties. The test itself is covered in the Psychological Assessment book.

4.3 Depth and Distance PerceptionHigh yield · 9 Qs

Reach out and pick up the cup in front of you. Your hand opens to the right width and stops at exactly the right distance, without a moment's thought. Yet the image of the cup on each retina has no depth at all. How does a flat image give you a world in three dimensions?

The problem of the flat retina

The problem was posed clearly in 1709 by the Irish philosopher George Berkeley (1685 to 1753) in An Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision. Berkeley pointed out that a point at any distance along a line of sight falls on the same point of the retina, so distance cannot be "seen" directly. He concluded that we learn to judge distance by associating visual sensations, such as the strain of turning the eyes inwards, with the experience of reaching and walking. Modern research has replaced Berkeley's short list with a long list of depth cues, sources of information that correlate with distance and that the brain combines to give depth perception, the ability to see the world in three dimensions and to judge how far away things are. The cues fall into families according to what they need: two eyes, one eye, movement, or feedback from the eye's own muscles.

Binocular cues: disparity and convergence

Binocular cues are depth cues that require both eyes working together. The most important is retinal disparity (also called binocular disparity). Your eyes are about 6 centimetres apart, so each receives a slightly different view of the world. Hold a finger about 20 centimetres in front of your nose and close each eye in turn: the finger jumps from side to side against the background. The difference between the positions of an object's images on the two retinas is its disparity, and the nearer the object, the larger the disparity. Objects at the fixation distance fall on corresponding points of the two retinas and have zero disparity; the imaginary curved surface through all such points is called the horopter. Objects nearer than the horopter have crossed disparity and objects beyond it have uncrossed disparity, so the sign of the disparity tells the brain whether an object is in front of or behind the point being looked at. The vivid impression of solid depth produced by fusing the two images is called stereopsis.

The English physicist Charles Wheatstone (1802 to 1875) proved the point in 1838 with his stereoscope, which presented a slightly different drawing to each eye; viewers saw a single solid object. Every 3-D film works the same way, using special glasses to send a different image to each eye. In 1960 the Hungarian-American engineer Béla Julesz (1928 to 2003) went further with random-dot stereograms: two fields of random dots, identical except that a central square of dots is shifted slightly in one of them. Each image alone looks like television snow. Viewed in a stereoscope, a square floats out in depth. Because there is no square to recognise in either image alone, Julesz showed that stereopsis can work before any object is recognised, a purely bottom-up process.

The second binocular cue is convergence (vergence): the inward turning of the two eyes to fixate a near object. Look at your fingertip as you bring it towards your nose and you can feel your eyes cross. The brain registers how much the eyes are turned in, from the commands sent to the eye muscles and feedback from them, and the greater the convergence, the nearer the object. Convergence is a binocular cue, because it needs two eyes, and also an oculomotor cue, because it comes from the eye muscles; it is useful only for objects within a couple of metres.

Binocular cues can work only where the fields of the two eyes overlap. Each human eye sees roughly 160 degrees horizontally (about 60 degrees towards the nose and about 100 degrees towards the temple); the two together cover roughly 200 degrees, and the central overlap where both eyes see, the binocular field, is about 120 degrees wide. Predators such as cats and owls, and primates such as ourselves, have forward-facing eyes with a large overlap and good stereopsis; prey animals such as rabbits and deer have eyes on the sides of the head, a nearly all-round view, and little binocular overlap.

Exam trap

A past paper asked for the "approximate visual field covered by each human eye" and keyed 180 degrees. The better textbook figure for one eye is about 160 degrees horizontally; 180 to 200 degrees is the field of both eyes together, and about 120 degrees is the binocular overlap. If you meet that exact item, be aware that the paper's key was 180; if the question clearly says "both eyes", 180 to 200 is right.

Monocular cues: what a painter knows

Monocular cues are depth cues available to one eye alone. People who have lost an eye still judge depth well, and a flat painting or photograph can look deep, because of them. Most are pictorial cues, so called because artists since the Renaissance have used them to put depth on a flat canvas. Figure 4.3 shows the main ones in a single scene.

  • Relative size: if two objects are known or assumed to be the same size, the one casting the smaller retinal image is seen as farther away. A row of identical lamp posts seems to shrink into the distance.
  • Familiar size: when we know an object's real size, its retinal size tells us its distance. A tiny-looking autorickshaw is seen as a normal autorickshaw far away.
  • Interposition (also called overlap or occlusion): when one object partly blocks the view of another, the blocked object is seen as farther away. Interposition gives only order (nearer or farther), not distance.
  • Linear perspective: parallel lines, such as railway tracks or the edges of a road, appear to converge as they recede, meeting at a vanishing point on the horizon.
  • Texture gradient: a surface with an even texture (pebbles, grass, the sleepers of a railway track, bricks in a courtyard) shows its elements as larger, coarser and more widely spaced nearby, and smaller, finer and more densely packed farther away. This was Gibson's favourite cue, because it specifies distance and the slant of a surface directly.
  • Aerial perspective (atmospheric perspective): distant objects look hazier, less sharp, lower in contrast and often bluer, because light from them passes through more air, dust and moisture. On a clear day after the monsoon, the Nilgiri hills seem nearer than they do in hazy summer.
  • Height in the visual field (relative height or elevation): for objects on the ground below the horizon, those higher in the visual field, that is, closer to the horizon, are seen as farther away; for objects above the horizon, such as clouds, the reverse holds.
  • Shading (light and shadow): the pattern of light and shadow on an object reveals its three-dimensional shape, and shadows cast on the ground show where an object stands. As Section 4.1 showed, the brain assumes the light comes from above.
  • Relative brightness: of two identical objects, the brighter is usually seen as nearer.

Road engineers use these cues deliberately. Stripes painted across the approach to a toll plaza or an exit ramp, with the gaps between them getting smaller, create an exaggerated texture gradient and a heightened sense of speed, so that drivers slow down without being told.

Cues from movement and from the eye's own muscles

Gibson's complaint that textbook cues were taken from frozen pictures applies with special force to the cues that need movement. The most powerful is motion parallax: when you move, objects at different distances move across your retina at different speeds. Look out of the side window of a moving train. The electric poles beside the track flash past in the direction opposite to your travel, the trees beyond move more slowly, the distant hills crawl, and the moon seems to travel along with you. The relative speed of the images tells you their relative distance. Motion parallax is a monocular cue (one eye is enough) but not a pictorial one, because a still picture cannot show it. A related cue is deletion and accretion: as you move, a nearer surface progressively covers (deletes) and uncovers (accretes) the texture of a farther one, which is interposition in motion.

Accommodation is the change in the shape of the lens, controlled by the ciliary muscles, that focuses the image of near or far objects on the retina (Chapter 3). The brain can use the state of these muscles as a rough distance cue for objects within about two metres. Accommodation, like convergence, is an oculomotor cue, but unlike convergence it works with one eye. Is depth perception innate? The visual cliff

Berkeley thought depth perception had to be learned. Is he right? In the 1950s Eleanor Jack Gibson (1910 to 2002) and Richard Walk (1920 to 1999), at Cornell University, built an apparatus to find out.

Key study: The visual cliff (Gibson and Walk, 1960)

Question. Can infants and young animals perceive depth before they have had a chance to learn it through falls and bumps? Method. The visual cliff is a large table topped by a sheet of strong glass. Under one half a checkerboard pattern is fixed directly beneath the glass (the shallow side); under the other half the same pattern lies far below the glass (the deep side), so that the table appears to end in a sheer drop, although the glass makes it perfectly safe. A narrow centre board separates the two sides. Thirty-six infants aged 6 to 14 months were placed on the centre board and their mothers called them from the shallow side and from the deep side in turn. Results. Twenty-seven infants moved off the board. All of them crawled onto the shallow side at least once, but only three crept, hesitantly, onto the deep side. Many crawled away from their mothers when called from the deep side, or cried because they could not reach them, and some patted the glass yet still refused to cross. Newly hatched chicks, young goats and lambs placed on the apparatus within a day of birth also avoided the deep side. Conclusion. By the time human infants can crawl, they can perceive depth, and in species that walk from birth, depth perception is present from birth. Criticism. Human infants cannot be tested until they crawl, at around six months, by which time they have had months of visual experience, so the study cannot show that depth perception in humans is innate.

Later work divided the question. When Joseph Campos and his colleagues in 1970 lowered infants of about two to three and a half months directly onto the deep side, their heart rate slowed, the sign of interest and attention, not the speeding up that signals fear. Young infants therefore detect the difference in depth long before they fear it. Fear of the drop appears with experience of moving about on one's own: infants of the same age who have begun crawling, or who have been given experience in baby walkers, show fear on the deep side sooner than those who have not. The most balanced conclusion is that the ability to detect depth appears very early and may well be largely built in, while the meaning of a drop, the knowledge that it is dangerous, is learned through self-produced movement.

Exam trap

The visual cliff was devised by Eleanor Gibson and Richard Walk (1960) to study depth perception and whether it is innate. It did not study object permanence (Piaget), colour perception, imprinting (Lorenz) or language. The infants were 6 to 14 months old (crawling age), not newborns and not "3 to 6 months", as some revision notes say. The best summary of the human results is "depth perception is present by the time infants can crawl", not "fully developed at birth" and not "only after age two".

Clinical link

Children whose eyes are misaligned (strabismus) or who have a lazy eye (amblyopia) in early childhood often grow up stereoblind, because the brain never learns to combine the two eyes' images. The neurobiologist Susan Barry, cross-eyed from infancy, gained stereoscopic vision in her late forties after intensive vision therapy, a case made famous by the neurologist Oliver Sacks (1933 to 2015). Her experience, of suddenly seeing snowflakes floating in depth, shows both how much the brain depends on early experience and how much plasticity remains in adulthood.

4.4 Perceptual ConstancyLower yield · 3 Qs

As a friend walks away from you down a corridor, the image of her on your retina shrinks steadily; at twice the distance it is half the height. Yet she does not seem to shrink. When a door swings open, the image it casts changes from a rectangle to a narrower and narrower trapezoid; yet you see a rectangular door turning. This stability is called perceptual constancy: the tendency to perceive objects as having stable, unchanging properties (size, shape, lightness, colour, position) despite large changes in the sensory input they produce. Without constancy the world would be a chaos of swelling, shrinking, stretching, darkening shapes.

Size constancy and size-distance invariance

Size constancy is the perception of an object as remaining the same size even though the size of its retinal image changes with distance. Try this. Hold one hand at arm's length and the other halfway to your face. The retinal image of the far hand is only about half the size of the near one, yet both look like normal hands of the same size.

How is it done? The brain takes account of distance. The size-distance invariance hypothesis states that perceived size is proportional to retinal image size multiplied by perceived distance. If the retinal image halves while the perceived distance doubles, perceived size stays the same. It follows that size constancy should depend on depth cues, and should break down when they are removed.

Key study: Holway and Boring (1941)

Question. Does size constancy depend on information about distance? Method. Alfred Holway and Edwin Garrigues Boring (1886 to 1968) seated observers at the junction of two long corridors. Down one corridor, at distances from 10 to 120 feet, they placed test discs whose physical size was increased with distance so that every disc cast exactly the same retinal image (one degree of visual angle). In the other corridor was a comparison disc whose size the observer adjusted until it matched the test disc. Observers did this under four conditions: with both eyes, with one eye, with one eye through a small peephole, and finally through a peephole and a long black tunnel that hid almost every cue to distance. Results. With both eyes and full cues, observers matched the discs' physical sizes almost perfectly: size constancy was excellent. As cues were removed, their settings moved steadily towards the retinal size; with the peephole and tunnel, all the discs looked nearly the same size. Conclusion. Size constancy depends on information about distance, just as the size-distance invariance hypothesis predicts.

Emmert's law

You can test size-distance invariance on yourself with an afterimage. Stare for about thirty seconds at a bright lamp or a coloured square, then look at a sheet of paper in your hand, and then at a distant wall. The afterimage, which is a fixed patch of fatigued retina and therefore has a fixed retinal size, looks small on the paper and large on the wall. This is Emmert's law, described by the Swiss ophthalmologist Emil Emmert (1844 to 1911) in 1881: the perceived size of an afterimage is proportional to the perceived distance of the surface on which it is projected (Figure 4.4). Emmert's law is the size-distance relationship laid bare, because the retinal size is held perfectly constant and only perceived distance varies.

Size constancy is not perfect, and it depends on experience of distance. From the top of a tall building, people on the street look like ants, because we have little practice in judging vertical distances. In Section 4.8 you will meet a forest dweller who had never seen across open country and who took distant buffalo for insects. And when distance is misjudged, size is misjudged too; this is the key to several famous illusions (Section 4.5).

Shape constancy

Shape constancy is the perception of an object as keeping its shape even when its orientation to us changes, so that the retinal image changes shape. A round dinner plate on a table casts an elliptical image unless you look straight down on it, and an opening door casts a trapezoid, yet the plate looks round and the door rectangular. The British psychologist Robert Thouless (1894 to 1984) showed in 1931 that constancy is usually a compromise: when observers drew the shape of a tilted circle as it appeared to them, they drew an ellipse that was rounder than the retinal image but not fully round. He called this tendency for perception to lie between the retinal image and the real object phenomenal regression to the real object.

Brightness and colour constancy

A lump of coal in bright sunlight sends more light to your eye than a sheet of white paper in the shade, yet the coal still looks black and the paper white. Brightness constancy (more precisely, lightness constancy) is the perception of an object's lightness (its whiteness, greyness or blackness) as constant despite changes in the amount of light falling on it. The key, as Hans Wallach (1904 to 1998) showed in 1948, is the ratio principle: perceived lightness depends on the ratio of light reflected from an object to the light reflected from its surroundings. When the illumination changes, object and surroundings change together and the ratio stays the same. White paper reflects about 80 to 90 percent of the light falling on it and coal only a few percent, whatever the lighting.

The ratio principle also explains simultaneous contrast: a grey square on a black background looks lighter than an identical grey square on a white background, because the ratio to the surround differs. Part of the neural basis lies in lateral inhibition, the tendency of active neurons in the retina to suppress the activity of their neighbours. Lateral inhibition exaggerates differences at borders and so enhances edge perception. It explains Mach bands, the illusory light and dark stripes seen at the boundaries of a graded strip, described by the Austrian physicist Ernst Mach (1838 to 1916) in 1865, and the grey smudges seen at the intersections of the white lines of the Hermann grid, described by Ludimar Hermann (1838 to 1914) in 1870 (the retinal circuitry is in Chapter 3 and the Physiological book).

Colour constancy is the perception of an object's colour as constant under different coloured lights. A ripe banana looks yellow under the bluish light of a tube light, the orange light of a sodium street lamp and the reddish light of sunset, although the light it reflects changes greatly. The brain compares the light from the banana with the light from the rest of the scene and discounts the colour of the illuminant; adaptation of the cones to the prevailing light also helps. Edwin Land (1909 to 1991), the inventor of the Polaroid instant camera, built his retinex theory on demonstrations of this kind. Colour constancy can fail when the brain cannot tell what the illumination is. In February 2015 a photograph of a striped dress divided the internet: some people saw it as blue and black, others as white and gold. The two groups seemed to differ in whether their brains assumed the dress was lit by bluish daylight or by yellowish artificial light, and so discounted different illuminants.

Location constancy

Every time you move your eyes, the image of the world sweeps across your retina, yet the world stays still. Location constancy (position constancy) is the perception of stationary objects as remaining in the same place despite movements of the eyes, head and body. You can break it yourself with a demonstration that Helmholtz described. Close one eye and press very gently on the outer corner of the other eyelid with a fingertip: the world jumps. When you move your eyes in the normal way, the brain sends a copy of the motor command to the visual system, called a corollary discharge by Roger Sperry (1913 to 1994) and an efference copy by Erich von Holst, both in 1950, and uses it to cancel the expected movement of the retinal image. When your finger moves the eye, there is no command and nothing to cancel, so the movement of the image is attributed to the world. The same mechanism underlies the perception of real motion (Section 4.6).

How often does the brain have to make this correction? Several times every second. When you read or scan a scene your eyes do not glide smoothly; they jump from point to point in rapid movements called saccades, separated by brief pauses called fixations, during which information is taken in. Even during a fixation the eyes are never perfectly still: they make a fine tremor, slow drifts and tiny rapid flicks called microsaccades. These small movements are not a flaw. In the 1950s researchers devised ways of keeping an image fixed on the same receptors however the eye moved (a stabilised retinal image), and the image faded from view within seconds, because receptors stimulated without change adapt (sensory adaptation, Chapter 3). By constantly refreshing the image on the retina, saccades and microsaccades ensure constant perception, while the corollary discharge keeps the world from seeming to jump with every one of them. Your outstretched hand looks the same size as your near hand because of size constancy, not shape constancy, motion parallax or "depth perception" in general (depth perception is what size constancy uses, not the name of the effect). Constancy is a property of perception, not of sensation: the sensory input changes; the percept does not.

4.5 Illusions: What Perception's Mistakes RevealHigh yield · 7 Qs

Everything in this chapter so far has been a success story: a flat retina yields a deep world, a shrinking image yields a friend of constant size, a scatter of dots yields tidy groups. But a system that works by inference, as Helmholtz and Gregory argued, must sometimes infer wrongly. Those failures are far more than curiosities for puzzle books. A slip of the tongue tells you something about how speech is planned; in the same way, a visual illusion tells you which rules the visual system is following, because it catches the system applying a sensible rule in a place where the rule does not fit.

What an illusion is

An illusion is a perception that does not correspond to the physical properties of the stimulus: a misinterpretation of a stimulus that really exists in the outside world. Three features mark an illusion in the psychologist's sense. First, there is a real external stimulus; the lines of the Müller-Lyer figure are really there on the page. Second, the error is systematic. Nearly everyone who looks makes the same mistake, in the same direction and by roughly the same amount, so illusions can be measured with the psychophysical methods of Chapter 3. Third, most illusions are stubborn. You can measure the lines with a ruler, satisfy yourself that they are equal, look again, and still see them as unequal. This was the second objection to Gregory's hypothesis theory that you met in Section 4.1, and it tells you that much of the machinery behind illusions runs automatically, below the level that knowledge can reach.

Where exactly does the error happen? Not in the eye. Photograph the Müller-Lyer figure and the two lines in the photograph are equal; the image on your retina is just as faithful. The receptors register the light correctly, so sensation is not distorted. What goes wrong is the interpretation of that light, the organising and inferring that turn a retinal image into a seen world. Optical illusions are therefore distortions of perception, not of sensation, which is why textbooks reach for them whenever they want to make the difference between sensation and perception concrete.

Illusions are not only visual. Cross your index and middle fingers, close your eyes and roll a small marble between the two fingertips: you feel two marbles (Aristotle's illusion, described more than two thousand years ago). Lift a small box and a large box of exactly the same weight and the small one feels clearly heavier; this size-weight illusion was measured by the French physician Augustin Charpentier in 1891. Listen to a sequence of Shepard tones and the pitch seems to climb for ever without ever getting higher. In each case a real stimulus is correctly registered by the receptors and then misread.

Many Indian textbooks of general psychology sort illusions into two classes, and examiners still use the terms. Personal illusions (individual illusions) are private and variable. They depend on the observer's state, expectations and surroundings, so they differ from person to person and from one occasion to the next. The standard example is the coil of rope seen as a snake at dusk: one person sees a snake, the next sees a rope, and the same person may not be fooled the following evening. Universal illusions, also called permanent or general illusions, are experienced by practically everyone, every time, because they arise from the normal working of the perceptual system: railway tracks that seem to converge in the distance, the moon that looks larger at the horizon, the unequal-looking lines of the Müller-Lyer figure. Most of the illusions studied in perception laboratories are universal illusions, and that is exactly what makes them useful. A universal error must come from a universal rule.

Indian context

The rope mistaken for a snake (rajju-sarpa) is one of the oldest illusions in Indian thought. In the Advaita Vedanta of Adi Shankaracharya (eighth century CE) it illustrates adhyasa, superimposition: the mind projects a snake onto the rope, just as, on that view, ignorance projects the world of appearances onto Brahman. Psychologically it is a model personal illusion. It needs dim light (weak bottom-up evidence), a setting in which snakes are expected, and a little fear (top-down influences, Section 4.8); and it vanishes the moment someone shines a torch on the rope.

Exam trap

"Rail tracks appearing to converge" was keyed as a permanent illusion, with "personal illusion", "visual illusion" and "Müller-Lyer illusion" as the other options. The item is testing the personal versus permanent classification: everyone sees the rails converge, every time, so the illusion is permanent (universal). It is of course also a visual illusion in the broad sense, but when "permanent" is offered alongside "personal", choose permanent. It is not the Müller-Lyer illusion. Strictly, what you are seeing is the depth cue of linear perspective (Section 4.3): the retinal images of the rails really do converge, and the illusion lies only in the impression that the rails themselves draw together. Finally, illusions result from a distortion in perception, not in sensation, adaptation or "translation" (a loose word for transduction, the receptor's conversion of energy into nerve signals, which works perfectly well when you look at an illusion).

The Müller-Lyer illusion and the carpentered world

The most studied illusion of all was published in 1889 by the German psychiatrist and sociologist Franz Carl Müller-Lyer (1857 to 1916). In the Müller-Lyer illusion, two lines (the shafts) are of equal length. One ends in arrowheads that point outwards, away from the shaft; the other ends in fins that flare outwards like the tail of an arrow. The shaft with the fins looks clearly longer than the shaft with the arrowheads (Figure 4.5, first panel). Look at the figure, then measure it.

Why should a few short strokes at the ends of a line change its apparent length? The best-known answer came from Richard Gregory in 1963, and he called it inappropriate constancy scaling (it is also called the misapplied size constancy theory). The shafts in Figure 4.5 are drawn vertically; take the arrowhead version first. It resembles the outside corner of a building seen from the street: the vertical edge is nearest to you and the walls slope away on either side. The finned version resembles the inside corner of a room: the vertical edge where two walls meet is the farthest point from you, and the edges of the ceiling and floor come towards you. Gregory argued that the visual system reads these depth cues automatically, even in a flat drawing, and then applies size constancy to them (Section 4.4). The shaft that is coded as farther away (the inside corner) is scaled up and looks longer; the shaft coded as nearer (the outside corner) is scaled down and looks shorter. Two lines that cast the same retinal image, one of which is judged to be farther away, must be of different sizes; that is the size-distance invariance hypothesis doing its normal job in a place where it does not belong, a flat page on which both lines are really at the same distance.

Gregory reported a striking piece of support: when Müller-Lyer figures are made luminous and shown in a dark room, where the flat surface of the paper cannot be seen, observers see them as three-dimensional corners, receding or approaching in just the way the theory predicts. But the theory has met real criticism. First, the illusion survives when the arrowheads and fins are replaced by small circles or squares at the ends of the lines, shapes that suggest no corner and no depth at all. Second, the figure does not look deep on paper, yet the theory needs depth to be registered. Third, a rival account, defended by the Australian psychologist Ross Day among others, holds that the visual system cannot completely separate the length of the shaft from the length of the whole figure, fins included, and the finned figure as a whole is longer. A fourth finding links back to Section 4.1: in a meta-analysis of 33 studies, Nicola Bruno and colleagues (2008) found that fast pointing movements to the ends of the shafts are much less affected by the illusion than verbal judgements are, as Milner and Goodale's separation of vision-for-action from vision-for-perception would predict.

If Gregory is even partly right, the illusion should depend on experience with corners, and therefore on culture. In the late 1950s and early 1960s the psychologists Marshall Segall and Donald Thomas Campbell (1916 to 1996) and the anthropologist Melville Jean Herskovits (1895 to 1963) tested this idea with nearly two thousand children and adults from more than a dozen societies in Africa and the Philippines, and from the city of Evanston in the United States. Their reports (1963, and the book The Influence of Culture on Visual Perception, 1966) set out the carpentered-world hypothesis: people who grow up in a "carpentered" environment, full of rectangular rooms, buildings, windows and furniture made with saws and set squares, learn to read acute and obtuse angles in a drawing as right angles extending in depth, and so are more susceptible to the Müller-Lyer illusion. People raised in a non-carpentered environment, with round huts and few straight edges, should be less susceptible.

That is broadly what they found. The urban American sample was among the most susceptible to the Müller-Lyer illusion, and some African samples, such as the San foragers of the Kalahari, were among the least. The rural Zulu, who traditionally lived in round huts with round doorways, became the textbook example of a non-carpentered culture. The same team also found the reverse pattern for the horizontal-vertical illusion (a vertical line looks longer than an equal horizontal line): it was strongest in people who live on open plains, where long stretches of ground recede horizontally into the distance, and weakest in people who live in dense forest, which is just what an experience-based account predicts. The hypothesis is not settled. A rival explanation proposed that people with more densely pigmented retinas (pigmentation tends to go with darker skin) detect contours slightly less sharply and are therefore less affected by such figures; schooling and experience with pictures differ between groups too; and later studies gave mixed results. But the work established the main point that matters for this chapter: susceptibility to illusions is partly learned, so illusions are not purely the product of innate machinery.

Exam trap

The Müller-Lyer illusion is strongest in cultures with buildings with corners (carpentered environments), not in cultures with "a lot of trees", not in those with "structures without many corners and angles" (that is the low-susceptibility group), and "more men than women" is pure decoration. The carpentered-world hypothesis is Segall, Campbell and Herskovits (1963, 1966). The illusion is "best explained by" misapplied size constancy (Gregory's inappropriate constancy scaling). Binocular disparity cannot be the cause, because the illusion works with one eye shut and on a flat page; lateral inhibition explains Mach bands and the Hermann grid (Section 4.4), not line length; figure-ground reversal is Rubin's vase (Section 4.2).

Ponzo, the Ames room and the moon

The same size-distance logic explains the second panel of Figure 4.5. The Italian psychologist Mario Ponzo (1882 to 1960) described it in 1911. In the Ponzo illusion, two identical horizontal bars are drawn between two lines that converge towards the top, like railway tracks receding into the distance, and the upper bar looks longer. Linear perspective (Section 4.3) tells the visual system that the upper bar is farther away; it casts the same retinal image as the lower bar; so, by size-distance invariance, it must be bigger. The illusion is strong in photographs of real roads and railway lines, where the depth cues are richer, which is what a depth-scaling account predicts.

The most dramatic size illusion is a whole room. The Ames room was first built in 1946 (the commonly cited date; some textbooks cite Ames's account of 1952) by the American scientist Adelbert Ames Jr. (1880 to 1955), a lawyer turned painter turned researcher in physiological optics, developing an idea that Helmholtz had described. Seen through a peephole with one eye, it looks like an ordinary rectangular room. In fact, as Figure 4.6 shows, its back wall slopes away from the viewer, so that one back corner is about twice as far from the peephole as the other, and the walls, floor, ceiling and windows are distorted in exactly the way needed to make the room cast the same retinal image as a rectangular one. Two people of the same height standing in the two back corners now look absurdly different: the one in the near corner looks like a giant and the one in the far corner like a child. A person walking from one corner to the other seems to grow or shrink as they walk.

Why does the room win? Two assumptions are in conflict: that rooms are rectangular, and that people stay the same size. The brain has met far more rectangular rooms than people who change size, yet it keeps the room and sacrifices the person. Ames, whose view of perception became known as transactionalism, argued that every percept is a kind of bet based on our past dealings with the world, and that in a peephole view the bet on rectangular rooms is the safest one available. Familiarity can shift the bet. In 1952 Warren Wittreich reported the "Honi phenomenon", named after a woman nicknamed Honi who saw strangers change size in the room but saw her own husband almost undistorted; in a story often retold in textbooks, a researcher's wife arrived at the laboratory, saw her husband inside the room and said at once that the room was distorted. Ames also built a trapezoidal window that, when rotated steadily, seems to swing back and forth instead. Gordon Willard Allport (1897 to 1967) and Thomas Pettigrew found in 1957 that rural Zulu children, who had grown up with few rectangular windows, were less likely than urban children to see this illusion when the viewing conditions were poor: another sign that the "bet" is learned.

The oldest illusion of all hangs in the sky. A full moon rising over the horizon looks enormous; the same moon high overhead a few hours later looks much smaller. This is the moon illusion, and it is purely perceptual. The moon's retinal image is the same size in both positions (about half a degree of visual angle, roughly the size of a pea held at arm's length); photographs show identical discs; and the horizon moon is in fact very slightly farther from you than the overhead moon, by about the radius of the Earth. Contrary to a popular belief, the atmosphere does not magnify it.

The leading explanation, the apparent distance hypothesis, goes back about a thousand years to the Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham (about 965 to about 1040), known in Europe as Alhazen. The sky is not perceived as a hemisphere but as a flattened dome, whose horizon seems much farther away than its zenith, because at the horizon we see the moon across a great stretch of terrain filled with depth cues (fields, trees, buildings) while overhead there is only empty sky. A moon of constant retinal size that is registered as farther away must be larger; size-distance invariance again. Lloyd Kaufman and Irvin Rock supported this in 1962 by showing observers the moon through optical arrangements that removed or added the terrain: the horizon moon lost much of its enlargement when the terrain was hidden, and an elevated moon looked larger when it was seen across terrain. There is a puzzle, though. Asked which moon looks nearer, most people say the horizon moon, the opposite of what the theory seems to need. One resolution is that the registered distance that drives size scaling is unconscious, while the conscious judgement of distance is made afterwards from the size that has been seen ("it looks big, so it must be close"). A second explanation, angular size contrast, adds that the horizon moon is seen next to small-looking objects such as trees and roofs, while the overhead moon is dwarfed by the vast expanse of sky, a contrast effect like the Ebbinghaus illusion that follows.

Exam trap

The Ponzo, Müller-Lyer, Ames room and moon illusions are all explained by size-distance scaling going wrong: something registered as farther away is seen as bigger. The Ames room depends on the assumption that rooms are rectangular and must be viewed with one eye through a peephole, which removes binocular and motion cues. The moon illusion is not caused by the moon being nearer at the horizon (it is slightly farther), nor by atmospheric magnification or refraction; the retinal image is the same. "Apparent distance hypothesis" is the answer for the moon; Kaufman and Rock (1962) are its classic experimenters.

Size contrast and bent lines

Not every illusion is about depth. In the Ebbinghaus illusion (third panel of Figure 4.5), a circle surrounded by large circles looks smaller than an identical circle surrounded by small ones. It is named after Hermann Ebbinghaus (1850 to 1909), the pioneer of memory research (Chapter 7), and was made widely known by Edward Bradford Titchener (1867 to 1927) in his laboratory manual of 1901, which is why it is also called the Titchener circles. The usual explanation is size contrast: the visual system judges size relative to the neighbours, just as it judges lightness relative to the surround in simultaneous contrast (Section 4.4). In 1995 Salvatore Aglioti, Joseph DeSouza and Melvyn Goodale used poker-chip versions of the figure and found that when people reached out to pick up the central disc, the opening of their finger and thumb fitted the disc's real size even though they saw it as larger or smaller; they summed this up as illusions that "deceive the eye but not the hand". Later studies have argued over how complete this immunity is, but the finding remains a classic piece of evidence for a separate vision-for-action system.

A family of nineteenth-century illusions bends or breaks straight lines. In the Zöllner illusion, described in 1860 by the German astrophysicist Johann Karl Friedrich Zöllner (1834 to 1882), long parallel lines crossed by short oblique strokes, slanting in opposite directions on alternate lines, no longer look parallel; they seem to tilt towards and away from each other. When Zöllner sent his figure to the physicist Johann Christian Poggendorff (1796 to 1877), editor of the journal in which it appeared, Poggendorff noticed another distortion in it. In the Poggendorff illusion (1860), a straight diagonal line that passes behind a vertical strip seems to come out at the wrong place, so the two visible parts look misaligned. In the Hering illusion, described in 1861 by the German physiologist Ewald Hering (1834 to 1918), the same man who proposed the opponent-process theory of colour vision (Chapter 3), two straight parallel lines drawn across a pattern of lines radiating from a central point appear to bow outwards; Wilhelm Wundt drew a variant in which the background lines converge the other way and the parallels bow inwards.

No single explanation covers this family. One idea is that the visual system tends to overestimate acute angles, perhaps because neurons tuned to neighbouring orientations inhibit one another (an orientation version of the lateral inhibition you met in Section 4.4), so each crossing line pushes the perceived orientation of the long line slightly away from itself. Another is that the figures are again read as perspective drawings: the radiating background of the Hering figure looks like a tunnel or a road stretching away. What these illusions share is the lesson that the visual system does not measure lines one at a time; it codes every element in relation to its context.

Ambiguous and impossible figures

A different kind of trick produces not a wrong percept but two percepts that take turns. An ambiguous figure (reversible figure) is a single unchanging stimulus that can be seen in two or more different ways, with the percept flipping from one to the other. You have already met Rubin's vase, in which figure and ground swap (Section 4.2). In 1832 the Swiss crystallographer Louis Albert Necker (1786 to 1861) noticed that his drawings of crystals seemed to jump in depth, and the Necker cube, a line drawing of a transparent wire cube, now carries his name: look at it for a few seconds and the face that was in front suddenly lies behind, then jumps forward again. Other famous examples are the duck-rabbit, used by the American psychologist Joseph Jastrow in 1899, and "My wife and my mother-in-law", a cartoon by W. E. Hill published in 1915 and brought into psychology by Edwin Boring in 1930, in which a young woman looking away and an old woman in profile share the same lines.

Ambiguous figures are the strongest single argument for Gregory's view of perception as hypothesis testing. The stimulus does not change, so the flipping must come from the perceiver: the brain entertains one hypothesis, then the other, because the evidence fits both equally well. Why does it not settle on one? A popular explanation is that the neural representation of the current interpretation adapts or tires, letting the rival take over; another is that shifts of attention and fixation tip the balance (fixating a particular corner of the Necker cube tends to bring that corner forward). Knowledge matters too. Irvin Rock and his colleagues found in 1994 that young children rarely reverse ambiguous figures spontaneously unless they have been told that there are two interpretations to look for.

An impossible figure is a drawing that the visual system reads as a solid object which could not exist in three dimensions. The best known is the Penrose triangle (tribar), whose three beams each look perfectly sensible but join at right angles to make a closed triangle. It was published, together with the never-ending Penrose staircase, in a short paper of 1958 by the British psychiatrist and geneticist Lionel Penrose (1898 to 1972) and his son, the mathematician Roger Penrose (born 1931), later a Nobel laureate in physics; the Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd had drawn a version in 1934, and the Dutch artist M. C. Escher built his lithographs Ascending and Descending (1960) and Waterfall (1961) around the Penroses' figures. Impossible figures work because each part is interpreted locally: every corner, taken by itself, is a legitimate piece of a three-dimensional object, and the visual system does not check that the local interpretations add up to a consistent whole. Notice the contrast with ambiguous figures. An ambiguous figure gives one stimulus two possible objects; an impossible figure gives one stimulus a single object that cannot be. Illusions, hallucinations and delusions

For a clinical psychologist the most important thing about illusions is what they are not. Three terms are confused constantly, in examinations and in casual speech, and the distinctions between them are part of every mental status examination.

An illusion, as you have seen, is a misinterpretation of a real external stimulus. A hallucination is a perception without an adequate external stimulus: the person hears a voice when no one is speaking or sees a figure in an empty room, and the experience has the full vividness of a real perception, is located in outer space and is not under voluntary control. The French psychiatrist Jean-Étienne Esquirol, in the early nineteenth century, was the first to separate the two clearly: in an illusion something is there and is misperceived; in a hallucination nothing is there at all. A delusion is not a perception at all. It is a false belief, held with unshakeable conviction, not open to correction by argument or evidence and out of keeping with the person's cultural and educational background: for example, the conviction that one's neighbours are broadcasting one's thoughts. Illusions and hallucinations are disorders of perception; delusions are disorders of the content of thought.

The British psychiatrist Andrew Sims, in his textbook of descriptive psychopathology Symptoms in the Mind, adds a finer distinction that one past paper used as its options. Sensory distortions are changes in the perception of a real object that is perceived as it is but altered in intensity, quality or size: sounds that seem unbearably loud, colours that seem changed, objects that look too small (micropsia) or too large (macropsia). Sensory deceptions are new perceptions that are false, and they include both illusions and hallucinations. So "a real perceptual object perceived in a distorted way" describes a sensory distortion; "misinterpretation of stimuli arising from an external object" is the standard psychopathology definition of an illusion; "perception without an adequate external stimulus" is a hallucination; and "vivid mental images" describes imagery, which is experienced in the mind's eye rather than in outer space and which a person can usually call up or dismiss at will.

Psychopathology recognises three kinds of illusion. A completion illusion depends on inattention: you read a misprinted word as the word you expected, and the error disappears as soon as you pay attention. An affect illusion is driven by emotion: a frightened child alone at night sees the coat hanging on the door as a man, and a grieving widow briefly "sees" her husband in a crowd. Pareidolia is the effortless perception of vivid, meaningful images in vague stimuli, such as faces in clouds, animals in the flames of a fire or a face on the front of a car; unlike the other two, it persists, and even grows richer, when you attend to it, and it coexists with knowing perfectly well what the stimulus really is.

A last group of "illusions" belongs to memory rather than perception. In déjà vu ("already seen") a new situation feels as if it has been experienced before; in déjà entendu ("already heard") something heard for the first time feels as if it has been heard before; in déjà vécu ("already lived") a whole experience feels relived; and in jamais vu ("never seen") a familiar situation suddenly feels strange, as if it had never been seen. These are illusions of familiarity, occasional in healthy people and frequent in some forms of temporal lobe epilepsy. Do not confuse them with hypermnesia, an unusually vivid and detailed memory for past events, or with disorders of speech such as palilalia, the involuntary repetition of one's own words (Disorders book). 4.6 Motion PerceptionLower yield · not asked yet

For most animals, movement matters more than anything else in the visual field: it means food, danger or a mate. A hungry frog will ignore dead, motionless flies yet snap at a moving speck, and a rabbit freezes because a predator's eye is drawn to motion. Human beings are no different. A flicker at the edge of your vision pulls your eyes towards it before you know what it is. Yet the perception of motion turns out to be as constructed as the perception of depth, and the illusions of motion are as revealing as the illusions of size.

Real motion: two systems and a motion area

Real motion is the perception of an object that is physically moving. You might think that seeing it is simply a matter of an image moving across the retina, and sometimes it is. When you hold your eyes still and a car crosses your field of view, its image sweeps across the retina, stimulating one receptor after another. Richard Gregory called this the image-retina system. But now follow a flying kite with your eyes. Its image stays more or less still on your fovea, while the image of the background streams across your retina in the opposite direction; yet you see the kite moving and the background standing still. Here the information comes from what Gregory called the eye-head system: the brain knows the eyes are turning because it issued the commands to turn them (the corollary discharge of Section 4.4), and it attributes movement to whatever the eyes are tracking. This is why the world stays still when you move your eyes normally, and jumps when you push your eyeball with a finger.

The image-retina system relies on neurons that respond to movement in a particular direction and not in the opposite one. Such direction-selective cells were found in the retinas of frogs and rabbits and in the visual cortex of cats and monkeys (Section 4.7 describes the work of Hubel and Wiesel). In primates, an area near the junction of the occipital and temporal lobes, the middle temporal area (MT, also called V5), is specialised for motion: nearly all its neurons are direction-selective, and damaging it in monkeys impairs the perception of motion while leaving other aspects of vision intact.

Clinical link

In 1983 Josef Zihl and his colleagues in Munich described L.M., a woman who had suffered damage to this region on both sides of the brain. She could see shapes, colours and positions but not movement, a rare condition called akinetopsia (motion blindness). Pouring tea was difficult because the stream of liquid looked frozen, like a glacier, and she could not see the level rising in the cup, so it overflowed. Crossing the road was frightening: a car that seemed far away a moment ago was suddenly close, with no movement in between. Conversation was strange because she could not see the speaker's lips move. Her case shows that motion is not inferred from a series of positions; it is computed by its own neural machinery, which can be lost while position perception survives.

Motion is also a powerful source of information about what things are. In 1973 the Swedish psychologist Gunnar Johansson (1911 to 1998) attached small lights to the shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees and ankles of a person dressed in black and filmed them walking in the dark. Any single frame of the film looks like a meaningless scatter of a dozen dots. The moment the film runs, every observer sees a person walking, and later studies showed that people can tell from such point-light displays whether the walker is a man or a woman, happy or sad, and even, sometimes, which of their friends it is. This perception of biological motion is an extreme case of the Gestalt law of common fate (Section 4.2): dots that move together in the pattern of a body are grouped into a body.

Movement also tells an animal when something will hit it. As an object approaches at a steady speed its retinal image expands, and the Gibsonian psychologist David Lee showed in 1976 that the ratio of the image's size to its rate of expansion, which he called tau, specifies the time remaining before contact, without any need to know the object's distance or speed. Diving seabirds appear to fold their wings at a constant value of tau, and drivers use the expanding image of the car ahead when they brake. Gibson's idea that the optic array carries information directly (Section 4.1) is at its most persuasive here.

Apparent motion: phi, beta and the cinema

Can you see movement when nothing moves? You do every time you watch a film. Apparent motion is the perception of movement where there is no physical movement at all, produced by stimuli that appear in different places at different times. Its scientific study founded a school of psychology. In 1912 Max Wertheimer, working in Frankfurt with Koffka and Köhler as his observers, flashed two lines one after the other in slightly different places (the history is told in Chapter 2). When the interval between them was very short, about 30 milliseconds, observers saw two lines at once; when it was long, about 200 milliseconds, they saw one line and then the other; at intervals in between, around 60 milliseconds, they saw a single line moving from the first position to the second. The impression of movement was not in either stimulus; it arose from the relation between them, and Wertheimer took this as proof that a perceptual whole can contain something its parts do not. He called the phenomenon phi phenomenon.

A subtlety lies behind the name. Wertheimer distinguished two experiences. At some intervals observers saw a smooth, object-like movement of the line from one place to the other; later writers named this beta movement, or optimal movement. At other intervals they reported a pure sensation of movement in the gap between the lines without seeing any object travel across it, which Wertheimer called pure phi. Most introductory textbooks, and most examination papers, now use "phi phenomenon" loosely for any apparent movement of flashing lights. Strictly, the film-like movement of an object is beta movement, and phi is the objectless motion; in 2000 Robert Steinman and his colleagues wrote a paper whose title says it all, "Phi is not beta". In 1915 Adolf Korte showed that apparent motion is lawful: for example, the farther apart the two lights, the longer the interval needed between them for the best movement (Korte's laws).

Apparent motion is everywhere once you look for it. The strings of lights that seem to chase each other round a balcony at Diwali, the arrows that seem to slide along a shop sign and the running headlines of a news ticker are all rows of stationary bulbs switching on and off in sequence. Stroboscopic motion is the apparent motion produced when a series of still pictures, each slightly different from the last, is presented in rapid succession. A cinema film shows 24 still frames each second (the projector flashes each frame two or three times, so that the light flickers faster than the eye can follow, Chapter 3), a flip book does the same in your hands, and an animated cartoon is drawn frame by frame. The visual system's motion detectors respond to such sequences much as they respond to real motion, and brain imaging shows that apparent motion activates area MT just as real motion does. Early researchers gave Greek letters to other varieties of apparent movement as well; the one you are most likely to meet is gamma movement, the apparent expansion of a figure when it is suddenly lit and its contraction when the light goes off.

Exam trap

The phi phenomenon was described by Max Wertheimer in 1912, and that paper is usually taken as the founding of Gestalt psychology. In loose usage phi means any apparent movement from lights flashed in sequence; strictly, smooth movement of an object is beta movement and objectless "pure" motion is phi. Motion pictures produce stroboscopic motion. None of these involves anything physically moving, which is what separates apparent motion from real motion. Motion parallax (Section 4.3) is a depth cue produced by real movement of the observer; it is not an illusion of motion.

Induced motion and the autokinetic effect

On a windy night, look at the moon through scattered clouds. The moon seems to race across the sky while the clouds stand still. In reality the clouds are moving and the moon, for the purposes of your perception, is not. This is induced motion: a stationary object appears to move because the frame that surrounds it moves. The German Gestalt psychologist Karl Duncker (1903 to 1940), whom you will meet again in Chapter 8 for his work on problem solving, studied it in 1929. In a dark room he showed observers a luminous dot inside a luminous rectangle and moved the rectangle slowly to one side. Observers saw the rectangle as still and the dot as moving the opposite way. The visual system treats the larger, surrounding frame as the stable background against which smaller things move, which is usually a safe assumption, since rooms, landscapes and skies rarely move.

The same rule applies to your own body. Sit in a stationary train at a busy station such as Mumbai Central, and when the train on the next platform pulls out, you feel your own train begin to move, perhaps even sway, until you glance at the platform. The illusion of self-motion produced by a large moving visual field is called vection. It is induced motion with the observer as the "dot" and the neighbouring train as the "frame", and it is the principle behind flight simulators and some forms of motion sickness.

Now remove the frame altogether. Sit in a completely dark room and look steadily at a single small, stationary point of light. Within a few seconds the light begins to drift, wander or swing, sometimes by what seems a large distance, even though it is perfectly still. This is the autokinetic effect (autokinesis), long noticed by astronomers watching single stars and by night pilots who have mistaken a solitary star or distant lamp for another aircraft. Its cause is the absence of any frame of reference. In darkness the eyes drift slightly, and the small commands needed to hold fixation steady are not fully accounted for, so the brain cannot tell whether the image or the eye has moved; with no visible background against which to check, it attributes some of the uncertainty to the light. The amount and direction of movement seen differ from person to person and from moment to moment, which made the effect perfect for one of the most famous experiments in social psychology.

Key study: Sherif's autokinetic norms (1935)

Question. How do social norms form when a situation is ambiguous and there is no objectively correct answer? Method. The Turkish-American social psychologist Muzafer Sherif (1906 to 1988) seated participants in a completely dark room and showed them a stationary pinpoint of light, asking them on each of many trials to say how far it had moved. Some first made their judgements alone and then in groups of two or three, calling out their estimates aloud; others started in groups and were later tested alone. Results. Alone, each person soon settled on a personal range and a typical estimate, and these differed widely from one person to another. In groups, the estimates converged over trials on a common group norm. When people who had formed a norm in a group were later tested alone, they kept to the group's norm. Conclusion. When reality is ambiguous, people use one another as a frame of reference, and the norms they build together become their own private standards (Sherif reported the work in 1935 and in his book The Psychology of Social Norms, 1936). In 1961 Robert Jacobs and Donald Campbell showed that an arbitrary, exaggerated norm planted by a confederate survived for several "generations" of a laboratory group after the confederate had left and members had been replaced one by one. Criticism. Because the movement is wholly illusory, there is no right answer to resist, so the study shows informational influence (using others as a source of information), not the yielding to group pressure on an obvious question that Solomon Asch tested in 1951 with judgements of line length (Social book).

Exam trap

Sherif used the autokinetic effect to study norm formation; Asch used line-length judgements to study conformity. The autokinetic effect is a stationary light seen moving in darkness, with no frame of reference; induced motion is a stationary object seen moving because its frame moves (the moon "racing" behind clouds, Duncker 1929); vection is illusory self-motion (the train beside yours pulls out).

The motion aftereffect

In 1834 a visitor to the Scottish Highlands, Robert Addams, stared for some time at the waterfall at the Falls of Foyers, near Loch Ness, and then turned his eyes to the rocks beside it. The rocks seemed to drift slowly upwards, in the direction opposite to the falling water. This motion aftereffect, often called the waterfall illusion, is the illusory movement of a stationary scene in the direction opposite to a movement that has been watched for some time. You can produce it after staring out of the window of a moving train: when the train stops, the platform seems to creep the other way.

The explanation is adaptation in direction-selective neurons, and it parallels the colour afterimages of Chapter 3. A stationary scene normally produces roughly equal, low activity in cells tuned to downward and to upward motion, and the brain reads the balance as "no movement". Prolonged watching of downward motion fatigues the downward-tuned cells. When you then look at the still rocks, the upward-tuned cells fire at their normal resting rate while the tired downward cells fire below theirs, the balance tips towards "up", and you see upward drift. Two further observations make the aftereffect especially informative. First, the rocks seem to move without getting anywhere: they drift upwards yet stay in the same place. That paradox shows that the brain codes motion and position separately, just as L.M.'s akinetopsia suggests. Second, if you adapt one eye and then test with the other, a weaker aftereffect is still seen, so at least part of the adaptation must take place in the visual cortex, where cells first receive input from both eyes. A rotating spiral gives a vivid version: after watching a spiral that seems to expand, a friend's face looks as if it is shrinking. 4.7 Pattern and Object RecognitionLower yield · 2 Qs

You recognise your mother's voice on a crackling phone line, a friend's face across a crowded platform at Bengaluru City station, and the letter A whether it is printed in a newspaper, scrawled on a blackboard or carved in stone. You do it in a fraction of a second and you cannot say how. Pattern recognition is the process of identifying a sensory pattern by matching it with a representation stored in long-term memory; when the pattern is a three-dimensional thing in the world, psychologists speak of object recognition. The difficulty is variability. The same letter or object almost never produces the same retinal image twice: it changes with size, distance, angle, lighting, partial hiding and, for letters, the hand or font that made it. Any theory of recognition has to explain how a single stored representation can match all of these. This section follows the main attempts, roughly in the order in which they were proposed.

Template matching

The simplest idea is that memory holds a template for every known pattern: an exact, miniature copy, like a stencil. In template matching, the incoming pattern is compared with the stored templates point by point, and it is recognised as whichever template it overlaps best. Machines work this way when the input is standardised. The numbers printed along the bottom of an Indian bank cheque are in a special magnetic ink and a fixed typeface so that a reader in the clearing house can match them against templates; barcode scanners work on the same principle.

For human recognition the theory fails quickly. A template for A matches only an A of the same size, shape, slant and position. To recognise every A you would need a separate template for every variant, an impossible number, and you would still be stuck the first time you met a new handwriting. One could add a preliminary stage that rotates, shrinks or centres the input before matching (normalisation), but to know how to rotate it the system would already have to know what it was. And templates cannot explain why the same shape is read differently in different contexts, as you will see at the end of this section. Template matching survives as a component of some modern theories, but no one now believes it is the whole story.

Feature analysis and the Pandemonium model

A more flexible idea is that patterns are recognised from their parts. According to feature analysis (feature theories), every pattern can be described as a set of distinctive features, elementary components such as vertical lines, horizontal lines, oblique lines, curves, angles and intersections. An A is two obliques meeting at the top plus a horizontal bar; an H is two verticals plus a horizontal. The perceiver detects the features present in the input and compares this list with the feature lists stored for each pattern. Because features stay the same when a letter changes size or style, the theory handles variability far better than templates do.

Two kinds of evidence supported it. When people are shown letters very briefly and asked to identify them, the letters they confuse are those that share many features: P with R, E with F, C with G, while O and X are almost never confused. Eleanor Gibson and her colleagues used such confusions in the 1960s to work out a set of distinctive features for the letters of the alphabet. Ulric Neisser showed in 1964 that it is much faster to find a Z hidden in a list of rounded letters (O, Q, D, C) than in a list of angular ones (X, V, M, W), because in the first list the Z is the only item with straight-line features, while in the second every item shares them.

The best-known feature model was proposed in 1959 by the computer scientist Oliver Selfridge (1926 to 2008), one of the founders of artificial intelligence. He called it Pandemonium, after the capital of hell in John Milton's Paradise Lost, because it imagined recognition as the work of crowds of shrieking "demons", each a simple processing unit (Figure 4.7). Image demons record the incoming pattern. Feature demons, each responsible for one feature, inspect the image and shout when they find their feature. Cognitive demons, one for each letter, listen to the feature demons and shout in proportion to how many of their own features are present. A decision demon listens to the cognitive demons and picks the loudest. Presented with R, the demons for P and B shout fairly loudly, because they share many features with R, but the R demon shouts loudest of all. Two features of the model are important. All the demons at each level work at the same time (parallel processing), and the model could learn by adjusting how much weight each cognitive demon gave to each feature, which made it an ancestor of today's neural network models. Neisser's Cognitive Psychology (1967) made it famous among psychologists.

Feature theories received spectacular support from the brain. In the late 1950s, at Johns Hopkins University, the neurophysiologists David Hubel (1926 to 2013) and Torsten Wiesel (born 1924) began recording from single neurons in the visual cortex of cats.

Key study: Hubel and Wiesel's feature detectors (1959 onwards)

Question. What kinds of stimuli make individual neurons in the primary visual cortex fire? Method. Hubel and Wiesel lowered fine microelectrodes into the striate (primary visual) cortex of anaesthetised cats and recorded the activity of one neuron at a time while projecting simple patterns of light onto a screen in front of the animal. Results. For hours, the spots of light that worked so well for retinal cells produced almost nothing. Then, as they slid a glass slide into the projector, one cell began to fire furiously: the faint shadow of the slide's straight edge, sweeping across the screen at a particular angle, was the stimulus it wanted. Systematic work over the next years distinguished simple cells, which respond best to a line or edge of a particular orientation at a particular place in the visual field; complex cells, which respond to a line of a particular orientation anywhere within a larger field, often only when it moves in a particular direction; and hypercomplex (end-stopped) cells, which respond best to lines of a particular length or to corners. Conclusion. The visual cortex contains feature detectors, neurons that respond selectively to particular features of a stimulus, arranged so that simple features are combined into more complex ones as information moves through the system. Hubel and Wiesel shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Roger Sperry. Criticism. Later work showed that such cells are better described as filters tuned to patterns of light and dark at different scales than as detectors of "lines" in the everyday sense, and that their responses are shaped by context and attention. The anatomy is covered in the Physiological book.

In the same year, 1959, Jerome Lettvin and his colleagues published "What the frog's eye tells the frog's brain", describing cells in the frog's visual system that responded best to small dark moving spots, nicknamed bug detectors; this is the reason a frog snaps at a moving speck but ignores a dead fly. Further along the primate visual system, cells in the inferior temporal cortex respond to complex shapes such as hands and faces, and in 2005 Rodrigo Quian Quiroga and his colleagues found single neurons in the human medial temporal lobe that fired to many different photographs of one particular film star and not to other people. The brain seems to build complex detectors out of simple ones, much as Pandemonium proposed.

Feature theories nevertheless have serious limits. A list of features says nothing about how the features are arranged, yet T, L and + are all one vertical and one horizontal line: the relations between features matter as much as the features themselves, so the stored description must be a structural description, not a list. Features also struggle with three-dimensional objects seen from different angles, and they cannot explain the effects of context that you will meet below. And there is evidence that the whole is often processed before its parts. In 1977 David Navon showed people large letters made up of small letters (a big H made of small S's): they identified the large letter faster than the small ones, and the large letter interfered with naming the small letters more than the reverse. This global precedence is just what the Gestalt psychologists would have expected.

Prototypes

A third approach replaces exact copies with averages. According to prototype theory, memory stores for each category a prototype: an idealised, abstract representation of the typical member, the central tendency of all the examples one has met, which need not correspond to any example actually seen. An incoming pattern is recognised as belonging to the category whose prototype it most resembles; it does not need to match exactly, only closely enough. Your prototype of a dog is a sort of average dog, and you recognise a new breed as a dog because it is reasonably similar to it. The idea is most closely associated with Eleanor Rosch (born 1938), whose work in the 1970s on natural categories showed that some members of a category are more typical than others (a robin is a better example of a bird than a penguin) and are recognised faster (concepts and categories are treated fully in Chapter 8).

The most elegant evidence came from Michael Posner (born 1936) and Steven Keele in 1968. They created random patterns of dots as prototypes, then made distorted versions of each by moving the dots slightly. Participants learned to sort the distortions into categories but never saw the prototypes themselves. In a later test, the never-seen prototypes were classified as accurately as the distortions that had actually been studied, and more accurately than new distortions, and the advantage held up better over time. People had abstracted the prototype from its variants. Prototype theory thus explains recognition of new examples and of typicality, which templates cannot. Its weakness is that it throws away information that people do seem to keep: we know that dogs vary enormously in size and that some birds cannot fly. A rival, exemplar theory, holds that we store many individual examples and compare new items with all of them.

Exam trap

"An idealised representative object of a category" is a prototype. A template is an exact stored copy that must match point for point; a feature is an elementary part such as a line or curve; geons are Biederman's three-dimensional building-block shapes. Pandemonium is Selfridge's 1959 feature model with image, feature, cognitive and decision demons. Hubel and Wiesel's feature detectors were found first in the cat; the frog's "bug detectors" are Lettvin's.

Recognition-by-components: Biederman's geons

Templates, features and prototypes were mostly developed for flat patterns such as letters. How do we recognise solid objects seen from any angle? The most influential answer is the recognition-by-components theory (RBC) proposed in 1987 by the American psychologist Irving Biederman. Its central claim is that every object can be described as an arrangement of a small number of basic three-dimensional shapes, which Biederman called geons (short for geometric ions): blocks, cylinders, spheres, cones, wedges, arcs and so on. He estimated that about 36 geons are enough. That sounds far too few, until you remember that about 44 phonemes are enough to build every word of spoken English. What matters is the combination and arrangement. A cup is an arc attached to the side of a cylinder; a bucket is the same two geons with the arc attached to the top.

How are geons found in the image? Biederman proposed that the visual system first extracts the edges of the object and then divides it into parts at regions of deep concavity, the places where the contour bends sharply inwards, such as where the handle of a cup meets its body. Each part is then identified as a geon from non-accidental properties, properties of edges in the image that are very unlikely to arise by accident from a particular viewpoint and that therefore stay the same from almost any angle. He listed five: curvature, parallel lines, cotermination (edges ending at a common point), symmetry and collinearity (points lying along a straight line). A straight edge in the image almost always means a straight edge in the object; parallel edges almost always mean parallel edges. Because geons can be identified from almost any viewpoint, the theory predicts that recognition should be largely viewpoint-invariant: an object should be recognised equally easily from most angles, unless a key geon is hidden.

The theory made a striking prediction about damaged pictures. Biederman deleted the same amount of contour from line drawings of objects in two ways. When the deletions were in the middle of line segments, people still recognised the objects easily, because the concavities and non-accidental properties survived. When the deletions removed the vertices and concavities, the objects became almost impossible to identify. Biederman and Peter Gerhardstein (1993) also found that seeing an object primed later naming of the same object almost as well when it was rotated by 135 degrees as when the view was identical, which fits viewpoint invariance.

The evaluation is mixed. RBC explains how we tell a cup from a bucket or a dog from a chair, but not how we tell one breed of dog, or one face, from another, because those discriminations depend on fine differences in shape and surface that geons ignore. Recognition is often viewpoint-dependent after all: Michael Tarr and Heinrich Bülthoff (1995), using novel objects learned from particular views, found that recognition was faster and more accurate from the familiar views. Colour and surface texture help recognition more than a pure edge theory allows. And like Marr's theory, below, RBC is almost entirely bottom-up, with little role for expectation and context.

Marr's computational theory

Biederman built on the work of the British neuroscientist David Marr (1945 to 1980), who worked at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and died of leukaemia at the age of 35; his book Vision was published after his death, in 1982. Marr made two lasting contributions. The first was a way of thinking. Any information-processing system, he argued, must be understood at three levels: the computational level (what problem is being solved, and why), the algorithmic or representational level (what representations and procedures solve it) and the implementational level (how the procedures are carried out in neurons or silicon). This is the computational approach you met by name in Section 4.1.

The second was a theory of how vision proceeds, through a series of increasingly rich representations:

  • The primal sketch is a two-dimensional description of the main changes in light intensity in the image: edges, bars, blobs and line endings. Marr divided it into a raw primal sketch (the intensity changes themselves) and a full primal sketch (in which these are grouped, by Gestalt-like principles, into contours and regions).
  • The 2.5-D sketch (two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch) describes the depth and orientation of every visible surface, worked out from cues such as binocular disparity, motion, shading and texture (Section 4.3). It is viewer-centred: it describes the surfaces as they face this observer from this viewpoint, and it says nothing about the hidden back of the object.
  • The 3-D model representation describes the object's three-dimensional shape and the arrangement of its parts in an object-centred frame, independent of the observer's viewpoint, typically as a hierarchy of cylinder-like components arranged along axes. Only at this stage can the object be matched with stored descriptions and recognised from any angle.

Marr's achievement was to show how much computation recognition requires, and to inspire a generation of computational models. His theory, however, is overwhelmingly bottom-up (Marr himself admitted that top-down processing is "sometimes used and necessary" but largely left it out), and no one has yet shown how the fully viewpoint-independent 3-D models he proposed could actually be computed. Faces: a special case

Faces pose the hardest recognition problem of all. Every face has the same parts in the same arrangement, two eyes above a nose above a mouth, so faces cannot be told apart by listing their features or geons. What distinguishes one face from another is the fine spacing and shape of the parts, their configuration, and there is good evidence that faces are processed holistically, as wholes, more than other objects are. In 1969 Robert Yin showed that turning photographs upside down impairs the recognition of faces much more than the recognition of houses or aeroplanes, the face inversion effect; inversion seems to disrupt configural processing. In the Thatcher illusion, described by Peter Thompson in 1980, a photograph of Margaret Thatcher with the eyes and mouth cut out and turned upside down looks grotesque when the face is upright but almost normal when the whole picture is inverted. Brain imaging by Nancy Kanwisher and her colleagues (1997) identified a region in the fusiform gyrus of the temporal lobe, the fusiform face area, that responds much more strongly to faces than to other objects.

The clearest evidence that face recognition is special comes from people who have lost it. Prosopagnosia (face blindness), named in 1947 by the German neurologist Joachim Bodamer (1910 to 1985) from the Greek for "face" and "not knowing", is a severe difficulty in recognising familiar faces, including one's own in a mirror, with much less impairment in recognising other objects. Acquired prosopagnosia follows damage to the occipitotemporal region, including the fusiform gyrus. Developmental prosopagnosia occurs without any obvious brain damage and may affect up to about 2 percent of people; Oliver Sacks, whom you met in Section 4.3, had it, and the American writer Heather Sellers did not realise until well into adulthood why she sometimes failed to recognise her own mother and husband. People with prosopagnosia recognise others by their voices, hairstyles, clothes and walk. Some of them show covert recognition: their skin conductance rises when they see a familiar face that they insist they do not know, a dissociation between recognition and awareness that anticipates Section 4.9.

Clinical link

In Capgras delusion, a patient insists that a spouse or parent has been replaced by an identical impostor. One influential explanation, proposed by Hadyn Ellis and Andrew Young in 1990, is that it is the mirror image of covert recognition in prosopagnosia: conscious recognition of the face is intact, but the emotional response that normally accompanies a familiar face is missing, and the patient explains the strange lack of feeling with a delusion. The disorders of face perception show how recognition, emotional familiarity and belief can come apart (Disorders and Physiological books).

Top-down help: the word superiority effect and context

Every theory so far has been mainly bottom-up, building recognition from the stimulus. Yet recognition is also driven by what we know. The clearest demonstration is with letters and words. As long ago as 1886, James McKeen Cattell (1860 to 1944) found that in a very brief exposure readers could report more letters when the letters formed a word than when they were a random string. The modern version was devised by Gerald Reicher in 1969 and refined by Daniel Wheeler in 1970. A string of letters, such as WORD, a single letter such as D, or a non-word such as ORWD, is flashed for a fraction of a second and then covered by a pattern mask. The participant must say which of two letters appeared in a given position, for example D or K in the fourth position. Both choices are chosen so that they make a word (WORD or WORK), so guessing from knowledge of words cannot help. Even so, the letter is identified more accurately when it is part of a word than when it is part of a non-word, or even when it is shown alone. This is the word superiority effect: a letter is perceived more accurately in a word than in isolation or in a meaningless string.

How can a whole word be easier than one of its letters? In 1981 James McClelland and David Rumelhart explained it with their interactive activation model, in which information flows both ways between levels: features activate letters, letters activate words, and activated words send activation back down to their own letters, strengthening them. The letter D in WORD gets support from above; the D in ORWD gets none. The model is a modern, more precise descendant of Pandemonium, with the crucial addition of top-down connections.

Context works the same way beyond the page. Selfridge himself drew attention to the phrase THE CAT written with an identical, ambiguous character in the middle of both words, half H and half A: everyone reads it as THE CAT, without noticing that the same shape has been read as two different letters. Stephen Palmer showed in 1975 that after looking at a kitchen scene, people identified a briefly flashed loaf of bread more accurately than a mailbox or a drum of similar shape. In 1970 Richard Warren played listeners a recorded sentence in which one sound in the word "legislatures" had been replaced by a cough; nearly all of them heard the missing sound clearly and could not say where the cough had occurred (phonemic restoration). In all of these, bottom-up information from the stimulus and top-down information from knowledge and context are combined, just as Neisser's perceptual cycle in Section 4.1 proposed. The next section looks more closely at the top-down side: at how expectations, needs, feelings, experience and culture shape what we see.

Exam trap

The word superiority effect (Reicher, 1969) is better identification of a letter when it appears in a word than when it appears alone or in a non-word; it is evidence for top-down processing. Prosopagnosia is face blindness, linked to the fusiform region; it is not a loss of all object recognition (that is visual object agnosia) and not a memory disorder. The face inversion effect is Yin (1969); the Thatcher illusion is Thompson (1980).

4.8 Perceptual Set, Learning and CultureLower yield · 1 Q

If perception were simply a matter of recording the stimulus, everyone would see the same thing in the same picture. They do not. A hungry person, a frightened person, a person who has just been looking at pictures of animals and a person who grew up in a forest may all look at the same figure and see different things. This section examines the perceiver's contribution to perception, which Section 4.1 described in general terms as top-down processing.

Perceptual set: seeing what we expect

A perceptual set is a readiness to perceive a stimulus in a particular way: in the definition given in 1955 by the American psychologist Floyd Henry Allport (1890 to 1979), "a perceptual bias or predisposition or readiness to perceive particular features of a stimulus". A set can be created by expectation, context, instructions, recent experience, needs, emotions, values and culture. It works in two ways: it makes us notice some things rather than others (selection, closely linked to selective attention, Chapter 5), and it makes us interpret ambiguous input one way rather than another (interpretation).

Look at Figure 4.8. The shape in the middle is identical whichever way you read it, yet read across it is the letter B and read downwards it is the number 13. Jerome Seymour Bruner (1915 to 2016) and Leigh Minturn used exactly this ambiguous figure in 1955: participants who had just seen a series of letters drew it as a B, and those who had just seen numbers drew it as 13. This is the context effect, the influence of surrounding stimuli and of the situation on the perception of a target.

Recent experience creates sets just as powerfully. In 1961 B. R. Bugelski and Delia Alampay showed participants an ambiguous drawing that can be seen either as a rat or as a bald man wearing glasses. Those who had first seen a series of pictures of animals overwhelmingly saw a rat; those who had seen no such series, or pictures of people, mostly saw a man. As early as 1935 Robert Leeper had shown that a brief look at an unambiguous version of the "wife and mother-in-law" figure (Section 4.5) determined which woman people saw in the ambiguous one. The same set makes proofreading so hard: read "Paris in the the spring" quickly and most people miss the repeated word, because they see what they expect a sentence to say.

Sets can cross the senses. In 1976 Harry McGurk and John MacDonald dubbed a recording of a voice saying "ba" onto a film of a face saying "ga". Viewers heard "da", a fusion of the two, and went on hearing it even when they knew the trick; with their eyes shut they heard "ba". The McGurk effect shows that speech perception integrates sight and sound automatically, and that what we see changes what we hear.

Clinical link

Clinicians have perceptual sets too. In 1973 David Rosenhan reported that eight healthy "pseudopatients", admitted to psychiatric hospitals after claiming to hear voices, behaved normally from then on, yet staff interpreted their ordinary behaviour through the diagnosis: one nurse recorded that a patient "engages in writing behaviour" when he was taking notes. Later investigators have questioned how the study was carried out, but the lesson stands. Once a label is applied, it creates a set that shapes what the clinician notices, which is why structured assessment and deliberately considering alternative explanations matter. The same danger, called diagnostic overshadowing, occurs when a physical illness in a person with a psychiatric diagnosis is wrongly put down to the psychiatric condition.

Needs, values and emotions: the New Look

In the late 1940s Bruner and his colleagues launched what became known as the New Look in perception, the claim that perception is shaped not only by expectation but by the perceiver's needs, values and emotions. The evidence came from a run of ingenious experiments. In 1948 David McClelland and John Atkinson showed dim or even blank slides to sailors at a naval training base who had gone one, four or sixteen hours without food and asked them what they could see; the longer they had gone hungry, the more often they reported food-related objects. Values had similar effects. In 1948 Leo Postman, Bruner and Elliott McGinnies measured the values of their participants with the Allport-Vernon Study of Values and found that words related to a person's strongest values (for example, religious words for people with strong religious values) were recognised at shorter exposure times than words related to weak values.

Key study: Bruner and Goodman's coins (1947)

Question. Do needs and values change the perceived size of objects? Method. Bruner and Cecile Goodman tested ten-year-old children in Boston. The experimental group, made up of children from a well-off progressive school and children from a settlement house in a poor district, adjusted the size of a circular patch of light until it matched the size of coins (1, 5, 10, 25 and 50 cent pieces) held in the palm of the hand. A control group made the same judgements for cardboard discs of the same sizes. Results. Coins were judged larger than they really were, and on the whole the more valuable the coin, the greater the overestimation; cardboard discs were judged much more accurately. Poor children overestimated the coins considerably more than rich children did. Conclusion. The value of an object and the perceiver's need for it can influence its perceived size: economic deprivation affects perception. Criticism. The samples were small, and the rich and poor children differed in many ways besides income, including familiarity with handling coins. In 1949 Launor Carter and Kermit Schooler found the rich-poor difference when coins were judged from memory but not when they were present, which suggests that the effect may lie partly in memory and judgement rather than in perception itself.

The most controversial New Look claim was perceptual defence, the idea that we resist perceiving stimuli that are threatening or emotionally disturbing. In 1949 Elliott McGinnies flashed words in a tachistoscope (a device for presenting visual stimuli for precisely controlled, very short times) at gradually longer exposures until participants could read them. Emotionally loaded taboo words needed longer exposures to be recognised than neutral words. More intriguingly, the galvanic skin response, a measure of emotional arousal, rose for the taboo words before participants reported seeing them, as if some part of the mind had read the word and decided to keep it out of awareness. Critics were quick to respond. Davis Howes and Richard Solomon (1950) pointed out that taboo words are less frequent in print, and less frequent words need longer exposures anyway; and participants, especially students reporting to a stranger in 1949, may simply have hesitated to say such words aloud until they were certain. The opposite tendency, faster recognition of threatening stimuli, is called perceptual vigilance, and it is now the better supported of the two. In 1951 Richard Lazarus (1922 to 2002) and Robert McCleary paired some nonsense syllables with electric shocks; later, when the syllables were flashed too briefly to be identified correctly, the shock-paired syllables still produced larger skin-conductance responses. They called this discrimination without awareness subception, a bridge to the subliminal perception of Section 4.9.

Emotions and bodily states shape perception in more everyday ways too. People who are anxious are more likely to interpret ambiguous stimuli as threatening: asked to write down spoken words that sound the same but have different meanings, such as die and dye, anxious people more often write the threatening spelling. Some studies have reported that hills look steeper to people who are tired or carrying a heavy backpack, although other researchers argue that such effects reflect what participants think the experimenter wants rather than a change in what they see. The safest conclusion is that needs, values and emotions certainly influence attention, interpretation and judgement, and probably influence perception itself, but less dramatically than the New Look first claimed.

Perceptual learning

Expectation and need change perception from moment to moment. Experience changes it for good. Perceptual learning is a relatively lasting improvement in the ability to perceive, especially to discriminate between stimuli, as a result of experience. A tea taster in an Assam garden distinguishes grades of leaf that taste identical to a visitor; a radiologist sees a small tumour in a chest X-ray that a medical student cannot find even when it is pointed out; a cricket umpire judges in an instant whether the ball pitched in line with the stumps; a student of Hindustani music gradually comes to hear the fine differences of pitch between the same note in two different ragas.

What changes? In 1955 James and Eleanor Gibson posed the question in a paper titled "Perceptual learning: differentiation or enrichment?" On the enrichment view, held by most psychologists since Berkeley, learning adds associations and memories to an impoverished sensation, so that we come to see more than is there. The Gibsons argued for differentiation: learning makes perception more specific, so that we come to detect more of what is there, picking up distinctive features and variables of the stimulus that we had not noticed before. In their experiment participants were shown a meaningless "scribble", a curly line, and then a long series of similar scribbles that varied in the number of coils, their compression and their orientation, with copies of the original mixed in; their task was to say which were identical to the first. At first they accepted many of the variants as the same; with repeated runs, and without any feedback about which answers were right, they came to pick out exactly the right items, and adults learned faster than children. Eleanor Gibson developed the idea in her book Principles of Perceptual Learning and Development (1969): perceptual learning is the learning of distinctive features, of invariants and of higher-order structure, and it makes attention more economical, so that the expert looks at less but sees more. It is also why, as Section 4.2 warned, what counts as "similar" or "familiar" for the Gestalt laws depends partly on experience.

Perceptual adaptation: Stratton's upside-down world

How flexible is the adult visual system? In 1896 the American psychologist George Malcolm Stratton (1865 to 1957), who had trained in Wundt's laboratory, put a lens system over one eye that turned the visual world upside down and reversed left and right, and covered the other eye. In a first experiment he wore it for about three days; in 1897 he wore it for eight days, removing it only in darkness or with his eyes closed. At first everything was chaos. He reached for things in the wrong direction, stumbled, felt nauseated, and found that the world swung wildly whenever he moved his head. Day by day his movements became more accurate and the scene less disturbing, and by the end there were periods when things seemed to be in their proper places and he could move about with some ease. When he finally removed the lenses, the normal world looked strange for a while, and his movements were briefly wrong in the opposite direction.

Stratton had demonstrated perceptual adaptation: the ability of the perceptual system to adjust to an artificially displaced, distorted or inverted visual input. Later researchers, notably Ivo Kohler at Innsbruck in the 1950s, had participants wear inverting or distorting goggles for weeks; they came to perform complex skilled activities, and the distortions became less noticeable. With prisms that shift the visual world a few degrees to one side, adaptation is much faster: people's reaching errors shrink within minutes, and when the prisms are removed they show a negative aftereffect, reaching too far the other way, which proves that something in the system really changed. How complete the visual change is remains debated. Much of the adaptation seems to lie in the coordination between vision and movement, the felt position of the body, rather than in the picture itself turning the right way up.

Movement, it turns out, is essential to that coordination. Richard Held (1922 to 2016) and Alan Hein demonstrated this in a classic experiment of 1963.

Key study: Held and Hein's kitten carousel (1963)

Question. Is visual experience enough for normal visually guided behaviour to develop, or must the animal produce its own movement? Method. Kittens were reared in darkness and then spent a few hours a day in pairs in a "kitten carousel": a cylinder with vertical stripes on its walls. One kitten walked around freely and actively, pulling a central arm; the other rode passively in a gondola attached to the other end of the arm, so that it received the same visual stimulation but did not produce its own movement. Results. When tested, the active kittens behaved normally: they put out their paws when carried towards a surface, blinked at approaching objects and avoided the deep side of a visual cliff (Section 4.3). The passive kittens did not, until they were allowed to move about freely for some days. Conclusion. The development of visually guided behaviour requires self-produced movement; visual input that is not linked to one's own actions is not enough. Criticism. The passive kittens' movements were restricted in ways other than self-production, and some later studies found a smaller difference; but the result fits the visual cliff finding that fear of heights appears with self-produced locomotion.

Experience matters most early in life. When Austin Riesen reared chimpanzees in darkness in the 1940s, they later had great difficulty learning simple visual discriminations, although part of the problem turned out to be damage to the retina from lack of light. When Colin Blakemore and Grahame Cooper (1970) raised kittens for their first months in a cylinder painted only with vertical stripes, the cats later behaved as if blind to horizontal edges, and neurons in their visual cortex responded almost only to vertical orientations. Hubel and Wiesel showed that closing one eye of a kitten during the first few months of life leaves the cortex almost unresponsive to that eye, while the same closure in an adult cat has little effect. There is a sensitive period early in life when visual experience shapes the developing visual brain (Developmental and Physiological books).

Exam trap

Stratton (1896, 1897) wore inverting lenses: perceptual adaptation. Held and Hein (1963) used the kitten carousel: active versus passive movement. Blakemore and Cooper (1970) reared kittens with vertical stripes only. Gibson and Gibson (1955) argued that perceptual learning is differentiation, not enrichment. Do not confuse perceptual adaptation (adjusting to distorted input) with sensory adaptation (receptors becoming less responsive to an unchanging stimulus, Chapter 3).

Restored sight: S.B. and his successors

The most direct test of what experience contributes comes from people who gain sight after a lifetime of blindness. The question was put in 1688 by the Irish scientist William Molyneux (1656 to 1698) in a letter to the philosopher John Locke, who printed it in the second edition of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1694). Molyneux's question asks: if a man born blind, who has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, were suddenly made to see, could he tell which was which by sight alone, before touching them? Molyneux and Locke both answered no. In 1932 Marius von Senden collected the published reports of people operated on for congenital cataracts and concluded that most could at first see colours and tell figure from ground, but could not recognise even simple shapes by sight, and learned to do so only slowly and with great effort.

The case you met at the start of this chapter was reported in 1963 by Richard Gregory and Jean Wallace in their monograph Recovery from Early Blindness. S.B.'s story fits von Senden's conclusion and adds a twist: whatever he had known by touch transferred to vision quickly. He read the time from a clock because he had felt the hands of his watch; he read capital letters but not lower-case ones because he had learned only capitals by touch; he understood the lathe only when he had felt it. His perception of distance was poor, as his belief that he could touch the ground from his high window showed. And Gregory reported that he was less affected than normally sighted people by some of the classic illusions, and that the Necker cube did not reverse in depth for him, as if he lacked the stored depth interpretations on which those effects depend.

Modern cases tell a similar story. Mike May, an American blinded in a chemical accident at the age of three and a half, had his sight restored in one eye at 46. When Ione Fine and her colleagues tested him in 2003, he could see colour and motion well, but even years later he struggled to recognise faces and objects from their shape alone and to see depth in flat drawings. Three-dimensional shape, faces and pictorial depth, the most learned parts of perception, were the parts that did not come back.

Indian context

India has made Molyneux's question testable on a large scale. Project Prakash ("light" in Sanskrit), founded in the mid-2000s by the Indian-born MIT neuroscientist Pawan Sinha, has provided cataract surgery to many blind children and young people in India, most of them from poor rural families, and studied how their vision develops. In 2011 Richard Held, Sinha and their colleagues reported that newly sighted participants could not at first match a shape they had felt with the same shape seen, the answer "no" that Molyneux and Locke had predicted, but that within a few days they learned to do so. Other Prakash studies found that the newly sighted at first had great difficulty separating overlapping shapes, and that motion helped them most to bind parts into objects: common fate (Section 4.2) seems to be one of the first grouping principles available.

Culture and perception

If experience shapes perception, people who grow up in different physical and cultural environments should perceive differently. You have already met the carpentered-world evidence for illusions (Section 4.5) and the Zulu children who were less fooled by the Ames window. Several other lines of evidence point the same way.

The first concerns distance. The British anthropologist Colin Turnbull (1924 to 1994), in The Forest People (1961), described a journey out of the dense Ituri rainforest of the Congo with Kenge, a young BaMbuti man who had spent his whole life in the forest, where one can rarely see more than a few dozen metres. On the open plains Kenge saw a herd of buffalo several kilometres away and asked what kind of insects they were. As they drove towards the herd, the "insects" grew larger, and Kenge, frightened and convinced that witchcraft was at work, could not understand how. Lacking experience of judging great distances, he had no basis for size constancy at that range (Section 4.4).

The second concerns pictures. In 1960 the South African psychologist William Hudson showed people a set of simple line drawings in which a hunter aims his spear, with an antelope in the middle distance and an elephant drawn small on a hill far behind it. Most people who had been to school, and who were used to pictures, saw the hunter aiming at the nearby antelope; many unschooled participants said he was aiming at the elephant, interpreting the drawing as flat, without reading its pictorial depth cues. Later, Jan Deregowski found that some African participants preferred "split" drawings of an elephant showing it as if flattened out from above, with all four legs visible, to the perspective drawings that Western viewers prefer. Reading depth in pictures, it seems, is a skill learned through exposure to a pictorial culture.

The third concerns what people attend to. In 2001 Takahiko Masuda and Richard Nisbett showed Japanese and American students short animated underwater scenes. Asked to describe them, the Americans tended to begin with the large, fast-moving focal fish; the Japanese more often began with the background ("it looked like a pond") and mentioned the relations between objects and their surroundings. When the focal fish was later shown against a new background, Japanese participants' recognition suffered more. Nisbett argued that East Asian cultures encourage a more holistic, context-sensitive way of seeing and Western cultures a more analytic, object-focused one.

A related individual difference, with cultural roots, is field dependence. In a series of studies beginning in the 1940s, the American psychologist Herman Witkin (1916 to 1979) seated people in a dark room and asked them to set a luminous rod to the true vertical while it was surrounded by a tilted luminous frame (the rod-and-frame test), or to find a simple shape hidden within a complex pattern (the embedded figures test). Some people's judgements were strongly pulled by the surrounding frame or pattern; others ignored it and relied on the gravitational feel of their own bodies or on the parts themselves. Witkin called this cognitive style field dependence-independence: field-dependent people perceive in a more global way, strongly influenced by the surrounding field, while field-independent people perceive analytically, separating items from their context. The Canadian cross-cultural psychologist John Berry (born 1939) found in 1966 that Inuit hunters of Baffin Island, whose survival depended on picking out small details in a featureless snowy landscape, were on average more field-independent than Temne farmers of Sierra Leone, whose society stressed conformity; his ecocultural approach holds that each group's ecology and economy shape the perceptual skills it encourages.

Indian context

Berry's ecocultural approach has been tested extensively in India. Ramesh C. Mishra, Durganand Sinha and John Berry studied Adivasi communities in Bihar, comparing the Birhor, traditionally nomadic hunters and gatherers, with neighbouring groups who had settled into agriculture, and reported their findings in 1996 in the book Ecology, Acculturation and Psychological Adaptation. Their broad conclusion was that a group's ecology and way of life, its degree of acculturation (contact with town life) and schooling were all related to performance on perceptual and cognitive tasks, as the ecocultural model predicts.

Exam trap

Field dependence-independence is Witkin's perceptual or cognitive style (rod-and-frame test, embedded figures test), not a procedure for studying subliminal perception; that is semantic priming (Section 4.9). Locus of control is Julian Rotter's personality variable (Personality book), and signal detection ability is measured with the methods of Chapter 3. Kenge and the buffalo is Turnbull (1961); the hunter, antelope and elephant pictures are Hudson (1960); the underwater scenes are Masuda and Nisbett (2001).

4.9 Perception Without Awareness, and Extrasensory PerceptionLower yield · 2 Qs

Throughout this chapter perception has meant conscious seeing and hearing: the vase you see, the moon that looks large, the letter you read. This last section takes up two claims that stretch that idea in opposite directions. The first is that a stimulus can be perceived, and can influence what you think and do, without your ever being aware of it. The second is that information can be perceived without reaching any sense organ at all. The first claim, after a shaky start, is now well supported. The second, after nearly a century of research, is not.

Subliminal perception and the Vicary hoax

Chapter 3 introduced the absolute threshold, or limen, and the word subliminal for a stimulus below it. Subliminal perception is the registration and processing of a stimulus that is presented too briefly or too weakly to be consciously perceived, shown by its effects on later behaviour, judgements or physiological responses. Its opposite, a stimulus strong enough to be consciously seen or heard, is supraliminal.

The idea burst into public view in 1957. A market researcher named James Vicary (1915 to 1977) announced that during screenings of the film Picnic at a cinema in Fort Lee, New Jersey, he had flashed the messages "Eat Popcorn" and "Drink Coca-Cola" on the screen every few seconds, each for a tiny fraction of a second, far too briefly to be noticed. He claimed that over six weeks the cinema's sales of Coca-Cola had risen by about 18 percent and of popcorn by nearly 58 percent. The announcement caused a sensation and a moral panic about "hidden persuaders"; broadcasters in several countries banned subliminal advertising. But no one could replicate the result, Vicary never published any details, and in 1962 he admitted in a magazine interview that the study had been a gimmick and that he had collected too little data, if any, to mean anything. The Vicary study is now regarded as a hoax.

The hoax did not settle whether subliminal perception exists; it only showed that this particular claim was false. Answering the real question turned out to require care about what "unaware" means. In 1984 Jim Cheesman and Philip Merikle distinguished two thresholds. A stimulus is below the subjective threshold when the person says they saw nothing, and below the objective threshold when they cannot even guess its identity better than chance in a forced-choice test. Many early demonstrations used stimuli that were below the subjective threshold only; participants who said they saw nothing might simply have been cautious about saying so, a matter of response criterion in the sense of signal detection theory (Chapter 3).

The most influential evidence used priming, the effect of an earlier stimulus (the prime) on the processing of a later one (the target). In semantic priming, a prime makes it easier to recognise a target that is related to it in meaning: in 1971 David Meyer and Roger Schvaneveldt found that people decide that BUTTER is a word faster if they have just seen BREAD than if they have just seen NURSE. Semantic priming became the standard procedure for studying subliminal perception when the British psychologist Anthony Marcel reported in 1983 that primes flashed so briefly and so heavily masked that participants could not tell whether any word had been presented still speeded responses to related target words. A word that was never consciously seen had been processed for its meaning. Later work with stricter tests of awareness has confirmed that masked primes can affect responses, although the effects are small and short-lived.

Other studies showed effects on feelings and choices. In 1980 William Kunst-Wilson and Robert Zajonc flashed irregular octagons for one millisecond each. Later, shown pairs of octagons, participants could not tell which ones they had seen (recognition was at chance), yet they reliably said that they liked the previously shown ones better: the mere exposure effect without awareness. In 2006 Johan Karremans and his colleagues flashed the brand name of a soft drink, Lipton Ice, below awareness; afterwards, participants who were thirsty were more likely to choose that drink, while participants who were not thirsty were unaffected. Subliminal influence, in other words, can nudge a goal the person already has; it cannot create one. And the subliminal self-help tapes sold to improve memory or self-esteem fail completely. In 1991 Anthony Greenwald and his colleagues gave volunteers tapes labelled "memory" or "self-esteem", but secretly swapped the labels on half of them. After a month, people believed they had improved in whatever the label said, but objective tests showed no improvement of either kind: a placebo effect of the label, not of the hidden message.

Exam trap

The procedure called semantic priming (masked priming) is used to study subliminal perception. It is not a method for studying signal detection ability (Chapter 3), field dependence-independence (Witkin, Section 4.8) or locus of control (Rotter, Personality book). Vicary's 1957 popcorn and Coca-Cola study was a hoax, admitted in 1962; genuine subliminal effects are real but small, brief and dependent on existing goals. Subliminal means below the threshold of awareness; supraliminal means above it.

Blindsight and other dissociations

The most dramatic evidence for perception without awareness comes from patients. In the early 1970s the British neuropsychologist Lawrence Weiskrantz (1926 to 2018) and his colleagues at Oxford studied D.B., a man who had had part of the primary visual cortex of his right hemisphere removed in an operation to cure severe migraines caused by an abnormal tangle of blood vessels. As expected, he was blind in much of his left visual field: shown a stimulus there, he said he saw nothing at all. But when the researchers insisted that he guess, pointing to where a light might be, saying whether a line was vertical or horizontal, or whether something was moving, he was correct far more often than chance would allow. When he was shown a film of his own accurate performance, he was openly astonished. Weiskrantz named the condition blindsight in 1974: the ability of people with damage to the primary visual cortex to respond accurately to visual stimuli in the blind part of the field while denying any conscious experience of seeing them.

How is it possible? Not all visual information goes through the primary visual cortex. Smaller pathways run from the eye to the superior colliculus in the midbrain and onwards to the cortex, and some fibres from the thalamus reach motion-sensitive areas such as MT directly. These routes can support detection, localisation and the sensing of movement, and they feed the dorsal, vision-for-action stream of Section 4.1, which operates largely outside awareness. Some patients even show "affective blindsight", responding differently to happy and fearful faces they cannot see. Sceptics first suggested that blindsight patients were really responding to stray light scattered onto the intact part of the retina, but D.B. could not detect stimuli presented to his natural blind spot, where stray light would have worked just as well. A more serious doubt is that some patients have faint, degraded visual experiences in the "blind" field and are simply very cautious about reporting them; this is the subjective threshold problem again. Even so, blindsight is accepted as a real dissociation between visual processing and visual awareness.

You have now met several such dissociations in this chapter: D.F., who could not see the orientation of a slot but could post a card through it (Section 4.1); the hand that is not fooled by the Ebbinghaus illusion (Section 4.5); prosopagnosic patients whose skin conductance recognises faces they cannot name (Section 4.7). Attention produces dissociations of its own in healthy people. In inattentional blindness, an unexpected object in plain view goes unseen because attention is elsewhere; in change blindness, large changes to a scene go unnoticed when they coincide with a blink, a cut or a flicker. These are covered fully in Chapter 5, together with the wider question of what consciousness is.

Exam trap

Blindsight (Weiskrantz, patient D.B., 1974) follows damage to the primary visual cortex; the patient denies seeing yet performs above chance on forced-choice tests. Failing to notice significant changes in a scene, even obvious ones, is change blindness; failing to see an unexpected object while attending to something else is inattentional blindness; selective attention is the focusing of processing on some stimuli at the expense of others; perceptual constancy (Section 4.4) is a success of perception, not a failure to notice (Chapter 5).

Extrasensory perception

Extrasensory perception (ESP) is the claimed perception of information without the use of any known sensory channel. It is quite different from subliminal perception: a subliminal stimulus does reach the senses, only too weakly to be noticed, whereas ESP claims that information arrives with no sensory input at all. Three forms of ESP are traditionally distinguished, and a fourth phenomenon is usually studied alongside them (Table 4.9 collects the vocabulary of the field).

  • Telepathy is the direct transfer of thoughts or feelings from one mind to another, mind reading without speech, gesture or any other sensory signal.
  • Clairvoyance is the perception of objects or events that are hidden from the senses or far away, without any other mind knowing them: naming a card lying face down in a sealed envelope, or "seeing" a fire in a distant city.
  • Precognition is the perception of events before they happen; its rarely mentioned partner, retrocognition, is paranormal knowledge of past events.
  • Psychokinesis (PK) is the claimed ability to influence physical objects or events by mental effort alone, such as influencing the fall of dice. It is a claimed power of action, not of perception, so strictly it is not a form of ESP.

ESP and psychokinesis together are called psi phenomena, and parapsychology is the field that studies them. Its scientific history begins with Joseph Banks Rhine (1895 to 1980), a botanist by training who came to Duke University in North Carolina in 1927, with his wife and colleague Louisa Rhine, to work with the psychologist William McDougall. Rhine's book Extra-Sensory Perception (1934) made the term famous, and the Duke Parapsychology Laboratory became the centre of the field. Rhine's chief tool was a pack of Zener cards, designed by his Duke colleague, the perception psychologist Karl Zener (1903 to 1964). A pack has 25 cards, five each of five symbols: a circle, a cross, three wavy lines, a square and a star. In a clairvoyance test the participant guesses the order of a shuffled pack; in a telepathy test a "sender" looks at each card in turn; in a precognition test the guesses are made before the pack is shuffled. Pure chance gives an average of 5 correct guesses in 25 (20 percent), and Rhine reported participants who averaged well above that over thousands of trials.

Critics found problems with almost every aspect of the early work. The symbols could sometimes be seen faintly through the backs of cheap cards, or reflected in the sender's glasses or cornea; senders could give unintentional cues by their breathing or timing; shuffling was sometimes inadequate; recording errors tended to favour hits; and successful series were more likely to be reported than unsuccessful ones. Laboratories elsewhere generally failed to replicate Rhine's results. The British researcher S. G. Soal produced some of the strongest card-guessing data of the 1940s, but statistical analysis by Betty Markwick in 1978 showed that his records had been manipulated.

Modern parapsychologists turned to the Ganzfeld procedure, developed in the 1970s by Charles Honorton (1946 to 1992) and others. Ganzfeld is German for "whole field". The receiver relaxes in a reclining chair with halved table-tennis balls taped over the eyes, a soft red light shining on them and steady hissing noise in headphones, which produces a uniform, featureless sensory field; the idea is that reducing ordinary sensory input makes any faint psi signal easier to notice. In another room, a sender concentrates on a randomly chosen target picture or video clip. Afterwards the receiver is shown four pictures, the target and three decoys, and picks the one that best matches their impressions; chance success is 25 percent. In 1994 the social psychologist Daryl Bem (born 1938) and Honorton published, in a leading journal, an analysis of computer-controlled Ganzfeld studies reporting a hit rate of about 32 percent. But in 1999 Julie Milton and Richard Wiseman analysed 30 newer Ganzfeld studies from several laboratories and found no significant effect, and the debate has swung back and forth since. In 2011 Bem reported nine experiments that appeared to show precognition, for example that people could guess, better than chance, behind which of two curtains an erotic picture would appear before the computer had chosen its position. Several independent teams repeated his procedures and found nothing, and the episode became one of the triggers of psychology's "replication crisis", the realisation that many published effects in psychology were not reproducible (Research Methods book).

Why do most scientists remain sceptical?

  • The effects do not replicate reliably. Positive results in one laboratory are followed by null results in others, and the effects tend to shrink as experimental controls become stricter.
  • There is no plausible mechanism. No known form of energy carries information from mind to mind or from the future to the present, and claimed psi effects do not weaken with distance or with time as physical signals do.
  • The history of the field contains sensory leakage, poor randomisation, selective reporting (the file-drawer problem, in which unsuccessful studies are never published) and outright fraud.
  • Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. A claim that would overturn much of established physics needs far stronger and more consistent evidence than a claim about, say, the effect of hunger on perception. A large prize offered for decades by the stage magician and sceptic James Randi for any demonstration of paranormal ability under agreed test conditions was never won.
  • Belief can be explained without psi. People remember the one dream that came true and forget the thousands that did not; they underestimate how often coincidences occur by chance (in a group of just 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday is better than even); and they notice evidence that confirms what they already believe.

Indian context

Claims of extraordinary perception have a long history in India. The third chapter of Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, the Vibhuti Pada, describes siddhis, powers said to arise from advanced meditation, including knowledge of other minds and of the past and future. Modern parapsychology also put down roots here: a department of psychology and parapsychology was established at Andhra University in Visakhapatnam, associated with K. The scientific standard is the same everywhere, however: a claimed ability must be demonstrated under controlled conditions that rule out sensory cues and chance, and replicated by independent investigators.

Clinical link

Belief in telepathy, clairvoyance or a "sixth sense" is common in the general population and, on its own, says nothing about mental health; cultural context always matters. But odd beliefs and magical thinking that go well beyond the person's culture, such as a conviction that one can read other people's minds or that one's thoughts can cause distant events, are part of the description of schizotypal personality disorder, and magical thinking also appears in obsessive-compulsive disorder. Telling a culturally shared belief from a clinically significant one is a matter of careful assessment (Disorders book).

Telepathy is mind to mind; clairvoyance is perceiving objects or events directly, with no other mind involved; precognition is perceiving the future; psychokinesis is mind over matter and is not a form of ESP, although it belongs to psi. Perception, then, extends below awareness in ways that are now well established, but not, on present evidence, beyond the senses. Everything you perceive arrives through the sense organs described in Chapter 3 and is organised and interpreted by the processes described in this chapter; what you then select from it, and how much of it reaches consciousness, is the subject of Chapter 5.

The case of S.B., revisited

You can now see why S.B.'s first months of sight were so puzzling, and why they mattered so much to Richard Gregory. The bottom-up machinery of vision worked from the start: S.B. saw light, colour and movement, and he could pick out a figure from its ground. What he lacked was everything this chapter has shown to depend on stored knowledge. With no experience of seeing distance, he had no size constancy across the space below his window (Section 4.4); with no visual templates, features or prototypes for most objects, he could not recognise them (Section 4.7); with no stored depth interpretations of flat drawings, some illusions and the reversing Necker cube had little hold on him (Section 4.5). Where knowledge existed, it transferred at once, even from another sense. He read a clock because he had felt a watch, and he saw the lathe once his hands had built the schema that Neisser's perceptual cycle says must direct looking (Section 4.1). "Now that I've felt it I can see" is as clear a statement of top-down processing as the literature contains.

The story did not end happily. Gregory and Wallace reported that as the months passed S.B. became increasingly disappointed and depressed. The visual world he had imagined turned out to be drab, the flaking paint and blemishes of things troubled him, and he often preferred to sit in the evenings without turning on the light. He died in 1960, less than two years after the operation. Later cases such as Mike May's and the children of Project Prakash show both sides of the lesson: perception in the adult brain is slow to rebuild after a lifetime without sight, yet young brains given sight can learn to see with remarkable speed. Perception, in short, is not a window on the world but a skill, built from the meeting of what arrives at the senses and what the brain already knows.

Chapter summary

4.1 What Perception Is. Sensation is the detection and transduction of physical energy; perception is the selection, organisation and interpretation of the resulting signals. Because many states of the world can produce the same retinal image (the inverse problem), perception must combine bottom-up (data-driven) and top-down (conceptually driven) processing. Constructivist theories (Helmholtz's unconscious inference and likelihood principle, 1867; Gregory's perceptual hypotheses, 1966 and 1970, illustrated by the hollow-face illusion) treat perception as inference, and the Bayesian approach treats it as the combination of prior beliefs with sensory evidence (Ramachandran's light-from-above prior, 1988). J. J. Gibson's direct, ecological theory (1950, 1966, 1979) holds that the optic array, with its invariants, optic flow and affordances, specifies the world without inference; it is the classic bottom-up theory. Neisser's perceptual cycle (1976) links schema, exploration and object in a loop, and Milner and Goodale's two visual streams (1995) suggest that the constructivist and direct theories describe vision-for-perception and vision-for-action respectively.

4.2 Perceptual Organisation: The Gestalt Laws. Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler, from 1912) held that the whole is different from the sum of its parts. The field is first split into figure and ground (Rubin, 1915): the figure is thing-like, bounded and in front; the ground is shapeless and behind, and reversible figures such as Rubin's vase show that organisation is done by the perceiver. Elements are grouped by proximity, similarity, closure, good continuation, common fate and symmetry, and by the modern laws of common region (Palmer, 1992) and connectedness (Palmer and Rock, 1994), all governed by the master law of Prägnanz, the tendency to see the simplest, most stable organisation. Kanizsa's illusory contours (1955) show the laws creating edges that are not there. The laws describe rather than explain, and grouping is partly learned.

4.3 Depth and Distance Perception. Berkeley (1709) posed the problem of how a flat retina yields depth. The binocular cues are retinal disparity, which produces stereopsis (Wheatstone's stereoscope, 1838; Julesz's random-dot stereograms, 1960), and convergence. The monocular pictorial cues are relative and familiar size, interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, aerial perspective, height in the visual field and shading; motion parallax is a monocular cue that needs movement; accommodation and convergence are oculomotor cues. The visual cliff (Gibson and Walk, 1960) showed that infants of 6 to 14 months avoid an apparent drop and that newborn animals of walking species do too; Campos (1970) found that younger infants detect depth before they fear it, and fear grows with self-produced locomotion.

4.4 Perceptual Constancy. Perceptual constancy is the perception of stable object properties despite changing sensory input. Size constancy depends on taking distance into account (size-distance invariance), as Holway and Boring (1941) showed by removing distance cues, and Emmert's law (1881) shows the same relation for afterimages. Shape constancy is a compromise between retinal and real shape (Thouless's phenomenal regression, 1931); lightness constancy follows Wallach's ratio principle (1948), with lateral inhibition producing simultaneous contrast, Mach bands and the Hermann grid; colour constancy discounts the illuminant (Land's retinex theory); and location constancy uses the corollary discharge (Sperry; von Holst, 1950).

4.5 Illusions. An illusion is a systematic misinterpretation of a real stimulus, a distortion of perception rather than sensation; personal illusions vary between people, while universal (permanent) illusions are shared. The Müller-Lyer illusion (1889) is best explained by Gregory's inappropriate constancy scaling (1963), and the carpentered-world hypothesis (Segall, Campbell and Herskovits, 1963, 1966) links susceptibility to experience with rectangular buildings. The Ponzo illusion (1911), the Ames room (1946) and the moon illusion (apparent distance hypothesis; Kaufman and Rock, 1962) are further cases of misapplied size-distance scaling, while the Ebbinghaus (Titchener) illusion reflects size contrast and the Zöllner, Poggendorff and Hering illusions distort orientation. The Necker cube (1832) and other ambiguous figures alternate between interpretations; the Penrose triangle (1958) is an impossible figure. Illusions must be distinguished from sensory distortions, hallucinations (perceptions without an adequate stimulus), imagery and delusions (false fixed beliefs), and from memory illusions such as déjà vu and déjà entendu.

4.6 Motion Perception. Real motion is detected by the image-retina and eye-head systems, using direction-selective neurons and area MT (V5), whose damage causes akinetopsia (patient L.M., 1983); Johansson's point-light displays (1973) reveal biological motion. Apparent motion includes Wertheimer's phi phenomenon (1912), strictly distinguished from beta movement, and the stroboscopic motion of films. Induced motion (Duncker, 1929) makes a stationary object appear to move within a moving frame, and vection is illusory self-motion. The autokinetic effect, apparent movement of a stationary light in darkness, was used by Sherif (1935) to study norm formation. The motion aftereffect, or waterfall illusion (Addams, 1834), results from adaptation of direction-selective neurons.

4.7 Pattern and Object Recognition. Template matching cannot cope with variability. Feature analysis, supported by letter confusions, visual search and Hubel and Wiesel's simple and complex cells (from 1959), is embodied in Selfridge's Pandemonium (1959). Prototype theory (Posner and Keele, 1968; Rosch) stores idealised averages. Biederman's recognition-by-components theory (1987) describes objects as arrangements of about 36 geons identified from non-accidental properties, and Marr's computational theory (1982) proceeds from the primal sketch through the viewer-centred 2.5-D sketch to the object-centred 3-D model. Faces are processed holistically (face inversion effect, fusiform face area), and their recognition can be lost in prosopagnosia (Bodamer, 1947). The word superiority effect (Reicher, 1969) and other context effects show top-down processing.

4.8 Perceptual Set, Learning and Culture. A perceptual set is a readiness to perceive in a particular way (Allport, 1955), created by context (Bruner and Minturn's B or 13, 1955), recent experience (Bugelski and Alampay, 1961) and other senses (the McGurk effect, 1976). The New Look showed effects of need and value: hungry sailors saw food (McClelland and Atkinson, 1948), and poor children overestimated coin sizes more than rich children (Bruner and Goodman, 1947). Perceptual defence (McGinnies, 1949) was challenged on grounds of word frequency and response suppression; subception (Lazarus and McCleary, 1951) showed discrimination without awareness. Perceptual learning is differentiation of distinctive features (Gibson and Gibson, 1955). Stratton's inverting lenses (1896, 1897) demonstrated perceptual adaptation, Held and Hein (1963) showed the need for self-produced movement, and restored-sight cases (Molyneux's question; S.B.; Project Prakash) show which aspects of perception depend on experience. Culture shapes perception of distance (Turnbull's Kenge), pictorial depth (Hudson, 1960), attention to context (Masuda and Nisbett, 2001) and field dependence-independence (Witkin; Berry, 1966).

4.9 Perception Without Awareness, and Extrasensory Perception. Subliminal perception is the processing of stimuli below the threshold of awareness. Vicary's 1957 popcorn study was a hoax, but masked semantic priming (Marcel, 1983), mere exposure without recognition (Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc, 1980) and goal-dependent brand effects (Karremans and colleagues, 2006) show small, short-lived genuine effects, while subliminal self-help tapes are placebos. Blindsight (Weiskrantz, patient D.B., 1974) shows visual processing without awareness after damage to the primary visual cortex. Extrasensory perception comprises telepathy, clairvoyance and precognition, studied with psychokinesis under the name parapsychology; Rhine's Zener-card studies at Duke and the Ganzfeld procedure (Honorton; Bem and Honorton, 1994) have not produced replicable evidence, and most scientists remain sceptical because of failed replications, the absence of a mechanism and the history of methodological flaws.

From the past papers

  1. Which approach to perception emphasises the role of unconscious inference and probabilistic reasoning in interpreting sensory information? (RML 2023)

    (a) Computational approach (b) Constructivist approach (c) Direct perception approach (d) Information processing approach

    Answer: (b) Constructivist approach. Unconscious inference is Helmholtz's idea and the core of Gregory's constructivism; its probabilistic, Bayesian form treats the percept as the most likely interpretation. The computational approach (Marr) asks what each stage of vision computes, direct perception (Gibson) denies that inference is needed, and the information-processing approach describes perception as a series of stages without stressing inference.

  2. According to the Bayesian approach to perception, our perceptual experiences are influenced by: (RML 2023)

    (a) Genetic predispositions (b) Cultural background (c) Prior beliefs and sensory evidence (d) Emotional states

    Answer: (c) Prior beliefs and sensory evidence. Bayes's theorem combines the prior (what is likely, from experience or evolution) with the likelihood (how well each possibility fits the current evidence). Genes, culture and emotion can shape the priors, but none of them is the definition.

  3. Which psychologist is associated with the concept of "affordances", which refers to the opportunities for action provided by the environment? (RML 2023)

    (a) Hermann von Helmholtz (b) James J. Gibson (c) Richard Gregory (d) Ulric Neisser

    Answer: (b) James J. Gibson. Affordances are central to Gibson's ecological theory (1979): a chair affords sitting, a handle affords grasping. Helmholtz and Gregory are the inference (top-down) theorists, and Neisser proposed the perceptual cycle.

  4. According to Wundt, "apperceptive mass" constitutes ___________. (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Compound of elemental processes bound by association (b) Sensation and pure perception (c) Pure perception and preexisting ideas (d) Sensation plus unconscious inference

    Answer: (c) Pure perception and preexisting ideas. The apperceptive mass (Herbart's term, taken over by Wundt) is the body of ideas a person already has, into which a new perception is absorbed and so made meaningful. Option (a) describes associationist compounds of elements, (b) leaves out the existing ideas, and (d) mixes in Helmholtz's unconscious inference.

  5. The most important school of psychology which has contributed a lot towards perception is: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Psychoanalysis (b) Behaviour school (c) Structural school (d) Gestalt psychology

    Answer: (d) Gestalt psychology. Wertheimer, Koffka and Köhler began with the phi phenomenon (1912) and gave perception figure-ground organisation, the grouping laws and Prägnanz. Structuralism analysed sensations into elements, behaviourism set perception aside as unobservable, and psychoanalysis dealt with the unconscious, not perception.

  6. According to the law of ______, items which are close together in space and time tend to be perceived as belonging together or forming an organised group. (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Proximity (b) Continuity (c) Similarity (d) Closure

    Answer: (a) Proximity. Nearness in space or time is proximity. Continuity concerns elements on a smooth path, similarity concerns elements that look alike, and closure is the completion of gaps.

  7. The tendency to complete a figure is called: (RML 2024)

    (a) Continuation (b) Continuity (c) Closure (d) Similarity

    Answer: (c) Closure. A circle drawn with gaps is seen as a complete circle. Continuation and continuity are two names for the same law of good continuation, which is about smooth paths, not gaps; similarity groups look-alike elements.

  8. Any figure or form not perceived in parts but organised in perception in the best possible whole form is known as: (RML 2020)

    (a) Law of proximity (b) Law of Prägnanz (c) Law of figure and background (d) Law of continuity

    Answer: (b) Law of Prägnanz. Prägnanz, the law of good figure or simplicity, says we see the simplest, most stable and complete organisation possible; it is the master law of which the others are special cases. Figure-ground and the other laws describe particular kinds of organisation.

  9. Determining a rule of structure from incomplete evidence and then identifying items that fulfil the rule would be a demonstration of: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Interpolation (b) Extrapolation (c) Interposition (d) Structuring

    Answer: (d) Structuring. Imposing an organising rule on incomplete input and then picking out what fits it is structuring. Interpolation fills in between known points, extrapolation extends a trend beyond them, and interposition is a monocular depth cue (overlap).

  10. Among monocular cues for depth perception is: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Convergence (b) Retinal disparity (c) Assimilation (d) Interposition

    Answer: (d) Interposition. An object that partly hides another is seen as nearer, and one eye is enough to see it. Convergence and retinal disparity are the two binocular cues; assimilation is not a depth cue at all (it is Piaget's term, or the opposite of contrast).

  11. Retinal disparity, which enables three-dimensional depth perception, is classified as which type of visual depth cue? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Monocular static cue (b) Binocular cue (c) Motion parallax cue (d) Pictorial depth cue

    Answer: (b) Binocular cue. Disparity is the difference between the two eyes' images, so it needs two eyes by definition and gives stereopsis. Pictorial cues are monocular and static; motion parallax is monocular and needs movement.

  12. Highway engineers sometimes paint stripes on freeway off-ramps to enhance the perception of depth. This uses the cue of: (RML 2024)

    (a) Aerial perspective (b) Parallax (c) Overlap (d) Texture gradients

    Answer: (d) Texture gradients. Stripes that get closer together exaggerate the normal crowding of texture with distance and heighten the sense of speed. Aerial perspective is haze, parallax needs relative movement of objects at different distances, and overlap is interposition.

  13. The density of stones increases as we look farther away. This phenomenon is due to: (RML 2024)

    (a) Convergence (b) Aerial perspective (c) Interposition (d) Texture gradient

    Answer: (d) Texture gradient. Texture elements look smaller, finer and more densely packed with distance. Convergence is a binocular eye-muscle cue, aerial perspective is haziness of distant objects, and interposition is one object blocking another.

  14. What is the approximate visual field covered by each human eye? (RML 2024)

    (a) 130 degrees (b) 160 degrees (c) 180 degrees (d) 220 degrees

    Answer: (c) 180 degrees, as keyed. Be careful: the better textbook figure for one eye is about 160 degrees horizontally (about 60 degrees towards the nose and 100 towards the temple); 180 to 200 degrees is the field of both eyes together, with about 120 degrees of binocular overlap. Choose 180 for this exact item, but recognise 160 if a question is precise about a single eye.

  15. When you stretch your right hand farther away from your body, you still perceive it to be of the same size as your left hand. This is due to: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Size constancy (b) Shape constancy (c) Motion parallax (d) Depth perception

    Answer: (a) Size constancy. The far hand's retinal image is smaller, but the brain scales it by distance (size-distance invariance). Shape constancy concerns changes of orientation, motion parallax is a depth cue from movement, and depth perception is what size constancy uses, not the name of the effect.

  16. Illusions are: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) misinterpretation of stimuli arising from an external object (b) real perceptual object perceived in a distorted way (c) vivid mental images (d) perception without an adequate external stimulus

    Answer: (a) Misinterpretation of stimuli arising from an external object. This is the standard psychopathology definition of an illusion. Option (b) describes what Sims calls a sensory distortion (such as micropsia), (c) describes imagery, and (d) is the definition of a hallucination.

  17. Illusions are: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Distortions of existing stimuli (b) The same as hallucinations (c) The result of innate mechanisms (d) Not based on external reality

    Answer: (a) Distortions of existing stimuli. In an illusion a real stimulus is misperceived. A hallucination has no adequate external stimulus, so (b) and (d) describe hallucinations; (c) fails because culture and learning shape susceptibility, as the carpentered-world studies show.

  18. Optical illusions result from distortion in: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Translation (b) Sensation (c) Perception (d) Adaptation

    Answer: (c) Perception. The retinal image of the Müller-Lyer figure is accurate and the receptors transduce it correctly; the error lies in the interpretation. Sensation, transduction ("translation") and sensory adaptation work normally.

  19. The rail tracks appearing to converge to all of us is an example of: (RML 2024)

    (a) Permanent illusion (b) Personal illusion (c) Visual illusion (d) Müller-Lyer illusion

    Answer: (a) Permanent illusion, as keyed. In the personal versus permanent (universal) classification, an illusion that everyone experiences every time is permanent; a personal illusion, such as a rope seen as a snake, varies between people and occasions. It is also, broadly, a visual illusion, but the item is testing the classification; it is not the Müller-Lyer illusion, and the convergence itself is the depth cue of linear perspective.

  20. It has been found that the Müller-Lyer illusion exists in certain cultures in which there are: (RML 2022)

    (a) Lot of trees (b) Buildings with corners (c) Structures without many corners and angles (d) More men than women

    Answer: (b) Buildings with corners. Segall, Campbell and Herskovits's carpentered-world hypothesis (1963, 1966) holds that experience of rectangular buildings teaches people to read angles as corners in depth, increasing susceptibility. People living among round huts and few straight edges, option (c), were less susceptible; trees and the sex ratio are irrelevant.

  21. The Müller-Lyer illusion is best explained by: (Recall set)

    (a) Misapplied size constancy (inappropriate constancy scaling) (b) Binocular disparity between the two eyes' images (c) Lateral inhibition between neighbouring retinal cells (d) Figure-ground reversal

    Answer: (a) Misapplied size constancy. Gregory (1963) argued that the fins and arrowheads are read as inside and outside corners, so one shaft is coded as farther away and scaled up. The illusion works with one eye and on a flat page, ruling out disparity; lateral inhibition explains Mach bands and the Hermann grid; figure-ground reversal is Rubin's vase.

  22. Déjà entendu refers to: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Illusion of having seen something before (b) Memory of past events with clarity (c) Illusion of having heard something previously (d) Repetitive involuntary speech

    Answer: (c) Illusion of having heard something previously. Entendu means "heard". Having seen something before is déjà vu, (a); an unusually clear memory of past events is hypermnesia, (b); involuntary repetition of speech, (d), is a speech disorder such as palilalia.

  23. Who demonstrated that economic deprivation affects perception? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Broota and Ganguli (b) Bruner and Goodman (c) Segall (d) Herskovits

    Answer: (b) Bruner and Goodman. In their 1947 coin study, poor ten-year-olds overestimated the sizes of coins more than rich children did. Segall and Herskovits are the carpentered-world researchers, who studied culture and illusions, and Broota and Ganguli are Indian psychologists who are not associated with this classic finding.

  24. The procedure called "semantic priming" is used to study which of the following phenomena? (RML 2022)

    (a) Signal detection ability (b) Subliminal perception (c) Field dependence-independence (d) Locus of control

    Answer: (b) Subliminal perception. Masked primes too brief to be consciously seen still speed responses to related words (Marcel, 1983), showing processing without awareness. Signal detection ability is measured by d-prime in yes/no detection tasks, field dependence-independence by Witkin's rod-and-frame and embedded figures tests, and locus of control (Rotter) by personality questionnaires.

  25. What term describes the phenomenon where individuals fail to notice significant changes in their environment, even when those changes are obvious? (RML 2023)

    (a) Change blindness (b) Inattentional blindness (c) Perceptual constancy (d) Selective attention

    Answer: (a) Change blindness. The stem stresses failing to notice changes, typically when they coincide with a blink, cut or flicker (Chapter 5). Inattentional blindness is failing to see an unexpected object while attending to something else; perceptual constancy is the stable perception of objects despite changing input; selective attention is the focusing of processing on some stimuli rather than others.
