1 · Terms to Know

  • Random assignment: giving every participant an equal chance of being in an experimental or control group.
  • Internal validity: extent to which an experiment investigates what it is supposed to.
  • External validity: generalisability.
  • Demand characteristics: factors that influence a participant's behaviour based on what they think the purpose of the experiment is.
  • Observer expectancy effect: experimenter communicates expectations unintentionally (Pygmalion / Rosenthal effect).
  • Descriptive statistics: the branch of statistics which deals with descriptions of obtained data.
  • Inferential statistics: deals with drawing conclusions about a large group of individuals on the basis of observations of a few participants.
  • Parameter: a measurable characteristic of a population.
  • Confidence interval: the mean of your estimate plus and minus the variation in that estimate.
  • Null hypothesis: the claim that the effect being studied does not exist.
  • Alternate hypothesis: statement that suggests there is a relationship between the variables being studied.
  • Significance level: the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true, i.e., the probability of making a Type I error.
  • Effect size: a quantitative measure of the magnitude of the experimental effect. The larger the effect size, the stronger the relationship between the two variables.
  • Skewness: a lopsided and asymmetrical curve because of extreme data.
  • Kurtosis: the term used to identify the degree of steepness or shallowness of a distribution.
  • Post hoc test: "like a post mortem." Requires two conditions: a directional hypothesis and a significant F ratio.
Trap: Type I error = null hypothesis is true but we reject it (alternate is false but we accept it) → "false alarm." Type II error = null hypothesis is false but we accept it (alternate is true but we reject it) → "miss." The significance level is the probability of making a Type I error.
Key: Test power is the probability that the test will reject the null hypothesis when the alternate hypothesis is true, i.e., it will not make a Type II error.

2 · What is Research (Steps & Types)

Research is a systematic, controlled, empirical, critical investigation.

Steps

  1. Identify a research problem
  2. Review of literature
  3. Formulate a hypothesis
  4. Research design
  5. Collect data
  6. Analyse data
  7. Interpret data
  8. Report findings

4 · Research Designs

Experiment

  • Experiment (lab): complete control on secondary (error) variance, e.g. lab experiment.
  • Field experiment: secondary variance just greater than lab experiment; happens in a real setting; difficult to control extraneous variables.
  • Quasi experiment: secondary variance greatest; no random assignment; non-equivalent groups, e.g. ex post facto studies.

Correlation

  • Not causation established; only helps us understand association.
  • Gives us the direction of the relationship.
  • Can't control extraneous variables.
  • Generalization is possible.
  • Types: positive, negative, zero.
Trap: Correlation is not causation. It shows association and direction but cannot control extraneous variables.

Observation

  • Can be naturalistic or controlled.
  • Can be participant or non-participant.

Special Designs

Longitudinal design

  • Involves taking a single group and studying it for a long period; the group is called a cohort.
  • Founder note: Lewis Terman — eugenics.
  • Prospective study: looking at the future.
  • Retrospective study: looking at the past.

Cross-sectional design

Various age groups are compared at the same time.

Cohort-sequential (cross-sequential)

Cross-sectional + longitudinal; also known as cross-sequential.

Survey

Uses questionnaires and observation; usually used to check attitude towards something. Tools used: survey, interviews; can be participant or non-participant.

Who → what: Lewis Terman — associated with eugenics and long-term longitudinal cohort study.

Key: Empirical relationship: Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean.

Frequency is measured by: Ogive curve, Histogram, Bar graph.

Variance / Dispersion

How the scores are spread around the mean.

  • Range = highest frequency − lowest frequency.
  • Standard deviation: spread of scores around the mean.
  • Variance: standard deviation squared.
  • Average deviation: mean of absolute deviations.
  • Quartile deviation (for median values): QD = (Q3 − Q1) / 2.

7 · Factor Analysis & PCA

Who → what: Factor analysis was given by Charles Spearman while creating his theory of intelligence. It uses a correlational design.

Types

TypePurposeDetails / Methods
ExploratoryFor creating a new theory.Unique variance: one factor is enough to explain variance; method = principal component method (unrotated factors given). Common variance: gives factors that explain variance together but not individually; methods = principal axis method, maximum likelihood estimation, generalized least squares.
ConfirmatoryFor verifying another theory.Changes cannot be made.

PCA (Principal Component Analysis)

  • Uses Cattell's scree plot (for a large number of factors) and the Kaiser–Guttman criterion (for a small number of factors, < 20).
  • Eigenvalues indicate how much variance we expect each factor to explain; an eigenvalue of 1 is the minimum expectation from each factor.
  • In a scree plot: x-axis = order of factors; y-axis = unreduced eigenvalues.
  • Identity matrix: all the factors have 0 correlation with each other except with themselves.

Factor Rotation

Allows us to decide if a second round of FA is required.

RotationResultAngleTypes

Orthogonal rotationGives uncorrelated factors.90°equimax, quartimax, varimax

8 · Regression & Correlation

  • Simple (bivariate) regression: Y = a + bx + E.
  • Multiple regression: Y = a + b1x1 + b2x2 + b3x3 + E, where b = partial slope.
  • The regression coefficient is indicated by β.
  • R = geometric mean of partial correlation (range 0–1).
  • Variance = R × R × 100%.

Partial correlation

Studying a single correlation in a multiple correlation matrix by removing the impact of other correlations.

9 · Reliability

Reliability checks if our test is consistent. It can be external and internal.

MethodDescriptionNotes / Formula
Test–retestGive the test to the same group after some time gap, and correlate the two tests (2 administrations).Source of error: practice effect, time sampling error, carryover effects.
Parallel / equivalent formsCorrelate the new test with a previous version of the test or a similar test.Can be done with or without a time gap.
Split halfComparing the first half of the score with the 2nd half; odd-even method may be used.Formula: r = 2r / (1 + r). Similar to Cronbach's alpha but can only be used for dichotomous items.
Internal consistencyCorrelating performance on each item with overall performance across participants.—
Kuder–Richardson methodInternal consistency for dichotomous items.—
Inter-raterAgreement of raters with regards to performance of participants.Cohen's kappa is used (r should be > .8).
Key — Spearman–Brown correction: used to correct for the effect of the length of the test. Formula: Kr / (1 + (K − 1)r), where K = test length; r = correlation.
Key — Cronbach's alpha: average of all possible split halves; applicable to dichotomous and polytomous items.

10 · Validity & Threats

ValidityMeaning
Face validityWhat a judge or expert thinks about the test; how much a test appears to be accurate (MMPI has poor face validity).
Content validityItems are relevant and representative of the target construct.
Construct validityUsed to validate a theory. Comparing a new test with another test that correlates to the same construct. Refers to the accuracy of the test.
Criterion validityThe extent to which the test fulfills the criteria made by another source.

Sub-types

  • Concurrent validity (criterion): the extent that a measure simultaneously relates to another measure that it is supposed to relate to, e.g. comparing a new personality questionnaire with the 16PF.
  • Predictive validity (criterion): the extent that a measure predicts another measure, e.g. predicting a real exam through mocks.
  • Convergent validity (construct): the extent that different measures of the same construct converge or strongly correlate with one another.
  • Divergent validity (construct): the extent that measures of different constructs diverge or minimally correlate with one another.

Threats to Validity

12 · Research Ethics

Research ethics involve a subjective evaluation of the risk-benefit ratio.

  • Informed consent: a person's explicitly expressed willingness to participate in a research project based on a clear understanding of the nature of the research.
  • Privacy: the rights of individuals to decide how information about them is to be communicated to others.
  • Confidentiality: information obtained from a research participant will be kept secret and private.
  • Minimizing deception.
  • Debriefing: explaining to participants the need for deception, addressing misconceptions, and removing harmful effects resulting from deception.
