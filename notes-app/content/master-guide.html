1. Exam pattern analysis: what actually gets tested

Admission into CIP Ranchi, IHBAS, RML and NIMHANS (for MA/MPhil, PGDCP/PDCP, PsyD and PhD Clinical Psychology) is competitive, but the entrances recur in shape. The structure across institutes is similar: MCQs dominate, covering theoretical concepts, psychological tools, research methods, psychotherapies and clinical diagnosis.

1.2 Commonly asked topics, with real stems

A. General & Clinical Psychology. Foundational theories of Freud, Jung, Adler, Skinner, Bandura come up constantly.

  • "Psychoanalytic theory about human personality was proposed by?" (CIP)
  • "Insight learning is studied by?" (CIP)

B. Psychometric Testing & Intelligence. Binet, Wechsler, Bhatia Battery / Performance tests — components and Indian-context applications.

  • "The prototype of the modern intelligence test was developed by?" (CIP)
  • "Bhatia Battery of Performance Test includes which test?" (CET)
Key tip

Go deep on test theory and application; drill test-construction concepts (reliability, validity) — they're commonly tested.

C. Research Methodology & Statistics. Design, hypothesis testing, and stats. Know ANOVA, chi-square, correlation, factor analysis cold.

  • "Degrees of freedom in a chi-square 3×4 table?" (CIP)
  • "What is Cronbach's Alpha used for?" (RML)

D. Clinical Diagnosis & Disorders. DSM-5 criteria, especially schizophrenia, OCD, PTSD, depression; defense mechanisms; therapy approaches.

  • "Which defense mechanism is most common in Borderline Personality Disorder?" (RINPAS)
  • "Which therapy focuses on changing negative thought patterns?" — CBT (CIP)

E. Neuropsychology & Biopsychology. Brain structures — hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, hypothalamus — and their functions.

  • "Which structure allows communication between the hemispheres?" (RINPAS)
  • "What is the role of the hippocampus in memory?" (NIMHANS)

F. Therapies & Treatment Approaches. CBT, DBT, psychodynamic, client-centred — and applying them.

  • "Functional Analysis is considered under which therapy?" (RINPAS)

1.4 Strategy for cracking the MPhil entrances

  • Focus on core areas: master psychoanalytic, behavioural and cognitive theories; DSM-5 categories, criteria and treatments for common disorders; brain anatomy tied to cognition and memory.
  • Practise previous-year papers: they expose recurring themes and question patterns — drill MCQs on the Bhatia Battery, CBT techniques and brain functions.
  • Master psychometrics & research: intelligence tests, methodology and statistical tools are tested heavily; command of them is a real edge.
  • Stay current: DSM-5 revisions and emerging trends in psychotherapy and neuropsychology.
  • Time management: front-load high-yield topics (research methods, clinical disorders, brain functions); revise and take timed mocks to build speed and accuracy.

2. Why revising UGC-NET JRF papers pays off

One of the strongest levers for these entrances: revise UGC-NET JRF papers. The overlap with MPhil Clinical Psychology entrances is large and well-documented.

  1. High overlap of topics & questions. Clinical and abnormal psychology (schizophrenia, OCD, personality disorders and their DSM-5 criteria), psychoanalytic theory and research methodology all recur in both. "What are the key diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia?" turns up across UGC-NET, GATE and MPhil papers. Theorist items — e.g. "Which theorist proposed the collective unconscious?" — appear in MPhil exams after first showing in UGC-NET JRF.
  2. Strong focus on research methods & statistics. ANOVA, t-tests, chi-square, validity and reliability are repeated. "Degrees of freedom in a chi-square 3×4 table?" appears across UGC-NET and GATE with slight variations in MPhil papers.
  3. Psychometric & intelligence tests. WAIS, Binet, and the Bhatia Battery are frequent. "Bhatia Battery of Performance Test includes which test?" has appeared across CIP Ranchi, UGC-NET and GATE — high repetition.
  4. Direct repeats in MPhil exams. Items on cognitive distortions, memory systems and CBT techniques carry over from UGC-NET into IHBAS, CIP and NIMHANS papers (e.g. shortlisting rounds).
  5. High-utility shared topics. Neuropsychology (hippocampus, amygdala, neurotransmitters; serotonin in depression), cognitive psychology and clinical diagnostics overlap heavily across UGC-NET JRF, GATE and MPhil.
Bottom line

UGC-NET JRF papers cover the same foundational topics and carry repeated questions and terminology. Folding them into your practice strengthens concepts, drills the repeats, and lifts your odds across the board.

3. A–Z terminology glossary

Quick definitions for the key terms the guide flags. Searchable — type any term in the library search and this entry surfaces.

A

AgraphiaThe partial or complete inability to write, usually caused by brain damage.
AphasiaPartial or complete loss of the ability to speak or comprehend language, typically caused by brain injury or neurological conditions.
AphoniaThe inability to produce vocal sounds due to damage to the larynx or related structures.
ApraxiaA disorder where an individual struggles to produce normal movement or speech despite intact muscles and comprehension.
Anorexia NervosaAn eating disorder marked by an intense fear of gaining weight and a distorted body image, leading to extreme restriction of food intake.
AmygdalaA structure in the brain's limbic system involved in emotion regulation, particularly fear and aggression.
Anterograde AmnesiaThe inability to form new memories following brain damage, with preserved recall of past events.
Attachment TheoryDeveloped by John Bowlby; explains how early relationships with caregivers shape emotional bonds, with types such as secure, anxious and avoidant.
Attention SpanThe length of time an individual can concentrate on a task without becoming distracted.
AutosomeAny chromosome that is not a sex chromosome, carrying genetic information unrelated to sex determination.

B

Behavioural TheoriesTheories such as classical and operant conditioning that explain learning through associations, reinforcements and punishments (Pavlov, Skinner).
Borderline Personality DisorderA disorder of unstable moods, relationships and behaviour, often marked by impulsive actions and intense fears of abandonment.
Broca's AreaA frontal-lobe region responsible for speech production; damage results in Broca's aphasia.
Brute ForceA problem-solving strategy that tries all possible solutions until the correct one is found.

C

CatharsisThe release of pent-up emotions and psychological tension through expression, resulting in emotional relief.
Chi-Square TestA statistical test used to examine the association between two categorical variables.
ChunkingA memory strategy where information is grouped into larger, meaningful units, making it easier to recall.
Classical ConditioningLearning where a neutral stimulus becomes associated with a meaningful one, eliciting a conditioned response (Pavlov).
Client-Centred TherapyA humanistic approach by Carl Rogers emphasising unconditional positive regard, empathy and client autonomy.
Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)A widely used therapy focused on altering negative thought patterns and behaviours to treat psychological disorders.
Cognitive DissonanceThe discomfort of holding conflicting beliefs or behaviours, motivating people to reduce the inconsistency (Festinger).
Cognitive DistortionsIrrational thought patterns (all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophising) that lead to emotional distress (CBT framework).
Cognitive PsychologyThe study of mental processes like memory, problem-solving, perception and decision-making.
ConformityThe tendency to change behaviour or beliefs to align with group norms, often under social pressure.

D

Defense MechanismsUnconscious strategies that protect the ego — e.g. repression, displacement, projection (Freud).
Degrees of Freedom (df)The number of values free to vary when calculating a statistic.
Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)An offshoot of CBT by Marsha Linehan, aimed at borderline personality disorder via mindfulness and emotion regulation.
DisplacementA defense mechanism where negative emotions are redirected from the original source to a less threatening target.
Down's Syndrome (Trisomy 21)A congenital condition caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21, leading to developmental and intellectual delays.

E

EEG (Electroencephalogram)A method for recording electrical activity in the brain using electrodes on the scalp.
ExtinctionIn classical conditioning, the weakening and disappearance of a conditioned response when the CS is no longer paired with the US.
Eclectic TherapyAn approach combining elements from different therapeutic techniques to best meet the client's needs.
EmpiricismThe belief that knowledge comes primarily from sensory experience and observable evidence.
EpigeneticsThe study of how environmental factors influence gene expression without changing the DNA sequence.
EthnocentrismViewing one's own culture as superior and judging other cultures by its standards.

F

Factor AnalysisA statistical method to identify underlying relationships between variables, often used in psychometrics.
Flashbulb MemoryVivid, detailed memories of significant, emotionally charged events, often resistant to decay.

G

Gate Control TheoryA theory of pain perception: a neural "gate" in the spinal cord modulates pain by controlling signal flow to the brain.
GeneA unit of heredity composed of DNA carrying genetic information that influences traits and characteristics.
GenotypeThe genetic makeup of an individual, including dominant and recessive genes.
GroupthinkA phenomenon where group members prioritise consensus over critical thinking, leading to poor decisions.

H

Halo EffectA general impression of a person (positive or negative) influencing judgements about their other, unrelated traits.
HallucinationsSensory perceptions occurring without external stimuli, common in psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia.
HippocampusA brain structure crucial for forming new memories, particularly long-term memory processing.
House-Tree-Person TestA projective test in which individuals draw a house, tree and person to assess personality traits.
HypothesisA testable prediction made by researchers, usually formed before an experiment.

I

IllusionA misperception or distortion of a sensory experience where perception does not match reality.
Implicit MemoryMemory that influences behaviour unconsciously, without intentional retrieval (e.g. procedural memory, riding a bike).
Insight LearningA problem-solving process where understanding arises suddenly (Köhler's chimpanzee experiments).
Insight-Oriented TherapyPsychotherapy aimed at helping clients gain awareness of unconscious thoughts, emotions and behaviours.
Internal Locus of ControlThe belief that one controls one's own life events and outcomes, vs. external factors like luck or fate.

J

Just Noticeable Difference (JND)The smallest change in sensory stimulation a person can detect 50% of the time.
Just-World HypothesisA bias where people believe the world is fair, assuming individuals get what they deserve even in misfortune.

K

Klüver-Bucy SyndromeA disorder from temporal-lobe and amygdala damage, causing hypersexuality, visual agnosia and reduced fear responses.
Klinefelter Syndrome (XXY)A genetic condition in males caused by an extra X chromosome, leading to developmental delays and specific physical traits.

L

Lateralisation of Brain FunctionCertain mental processes are specialised to the left or right hemisphere (e.g. language is typically left-hemisphere).
Learned HelplessnessFeeling unable to control or change a situation after repeated failure, leading to passivity and depression.
Limbic SystemBrain structures regulating emotion, memory and motivation — includes hippocampus, amygdala and hypothalamus.
Linguistic Relativity Hypothesis (Sapir-Whorf)Language influences thought and perception; speakers of different languages may experience the world differently.

M

Memory SystemsThe categorisation of memory into short-term, long-term and working memory, with subtypes like episodic and semantic.
Null HypothesisA statistical hypothesis stating there is no effect or difference; the baseline assumption in hypothesis testing.

N

NarcolepsyA sleep disorder of excessive daytime sleepiness and sudden sleep attacks, often with muscle weakness (cataplexy).
NeurotransmittersChemical messengers across synapses — e.g. serotonin (mood), dopamine (reward), GABA (inhibitory).
NeuroplasticityThe brain's ability to reorganise and form new neural connections, especially after injury or during learning.
Nomothetic ApproachSeeking general laws that apply to all individuals (vs. the idiographic focus on the unique individual).
Nurture AssumptionThe hypothesis that development is largely shaped by environment (nurture) rather than genetics (nature).

O

Object PermanenceUnderstanding that objects continue to exist when unseen — Piaget's sensorimotor stage.
Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)Intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviours (compulsions) performed to reduce anxiety.
Operant ConditioningSkinner's theory of how behaviour is shaped by consequences, through reinforcement and punishment.
OvergeneralisationA cognitive distortion: applying a single event or experience to an entire range of situations.

P

PhenotypeThe observable traits of an individual, influenced by both genetics and environment.
Premack PrincipleIn operant conditioning, high-probability behaviours reinforce low-probability ones ("games after homework").
PrimingExposure to one stimulus influences the response to a later stimulus, often outside conscious awareness.
ProjectionA defense mechanism: attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or motives to another person.
Prosocial BehaviourVoluntary behaviour aimed at helping others, motivated by empathy or social norms.
PsychoanalysisFreud's approach exploring unconscious thoughts and desires via free association and dream analysis.

R

ReinforcementIn operant conditioning, increasing a behaviour by adding a positive outcome (positive) or removing an aversive stimulus (negative).
Reciprocal DeterminismBandura's view that behaviour, environment and cognition all influence one another bidirectionally.
Regression to the MeanExtreme data points tend to move closer to the average on subsequent measurements.
Regression AnalysisA statistical method to predict the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables.
ReliabilityThe consistency of a test or measure in producing stable, repeatable results (e.g. test-retest reliability).
Retrograde AmnesiaThe inability to recall memories formed before the onset of amnesia.

S

ScaffoldingIn Vygotsky's theory, support from more knowledgeable others that helps a learner reach a higher level of understanding.
SchemasMental frameworks used to organise and interpret information, shaping perception and memory.
SchizophreniaA severe disorder with hallucinations, delusions, disorganised speech and impaired functioning.
Short-Term Memory (STM)Temporary storage lasting ~15–30 seconds, with limited capacity.
Social Learning TheoryBandura's view that learning occurs through observation, imitation and modelling, with reinforcement and punishment.
Somatoform DisordersDisorders with physical symptoms lacking a medical cause — e.g. conversion disorder, hypochondriasis.
StandardisationEstablishing uniform procedures for administering and scoring tests to ensure consistency across situations.
StereotypesOversimplified, generalised beliefs about groups of people, often based on race, gender or ethnicity.
Stroop TestNaming the ink colour of a word, hardest when the word names a different colour (e.g. "red" printed in blue).

T

TemperamentBiologically based tendencies to feel or act a certain way, present from early childhood; the foundation of personality.
TransferenceIn psychotherapy, clients projecting feelings and attitudes from past relationships onto the therapist.
Transactional AnalysisEric Berne's theory examining interactions (transactions) based on Parent, Adult and Child ego states.
T-TestA test comparing the means of two groups to see whether the difference is statistically significant.
Turner Syndrome (XO)A condition in females from partial or complete absence of one X chromosome, with delayed development and infertility.

U

Unconditional Positive RegardRogers' term for a therapist's non-judgemental acceptance and support of a client, regardless of what they say or do.
Unconscious MotivationDesires, urges or thoughts that influence behaviour but exist outside conscious awareness (Freudian).
Universal EmotionsPer Paul Ekman, basic emotions such as happiness, sadness, anger and fear are recognised and expressed similarly across all human cultures.

V

ValidityThe degree to which a test measures what it is supposed to. Types: construct, criterion and content validity.
Variable Ratio ScheduleA reinforcement schedule rewarding behaviour after an unpredictable number of responses, giving high response rates (e.g. gambling).
Visual AgnosiaA disorder where a person can see but cannot recognise or interpret visual information, due to brain damage.

W

Working MemoryShort-term memory that actively holds and manipulates information; crucial for reasoning, learning and comprehension.
Wernicke's AreaA region involved in language comprehension; damage causes Wernicke's aphasia (fluent but nonsensical speech).

X

XenophobiaAn irrational fear or dislike of people from other countries or cultures.
X-Linked DisordersGenetic disorders from mutations on the X chromosome, often affecting males more severely (e.g. colour blindness, haemophilia).

Y

Yerkes-Dodson LawPerformance improves with arousal up to a point, after which further arousal hinders performance.
Youth BulgeA demographic phenomenon where a large share of a population is young, often linked to social instability.

Z

Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)Vygotsky's gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can achieve with guidance or collaboration.
Zeigarnik EffectThe tendency to remember uncompleted or interrupted tasks better than completed ones — tension aids memory.

4. 24 important therapies

Founder · key concepts · techniques for each. NIMHANS/MET love "Therapy X was founded by?" and "Technique Y belongs to which therapy?" — drill both directions.

TherapyFounderKey conceptsTechniques
1. Animal-Assisted Therapy (AAT)No single founder (various psychologists & animal therapists)Uses animals (dogs, horses) to improve emotional well-being, enhance cognition and reduce stress; used for PTSD, anxiety, depression.Structured interaction with animals to build rapport and reduce anxiety.
2. Attachment TherapyBased on John Bowlby's attachment theoryBuilding and repairing attachment bonds, often in children with trauma, neglect or inconsistent caregiving.Activities to build trust and strengthen the caregiver–child relationship.
3. BibliotherapyNo specific individual (used as an adjunct)Reading material that relates to the person's struggle to support healing and growth.Reading and discussing books, poetry or essays that give insight.
4. Cognitive Analytic Therapy (CAT)Anthony Ryle (1980s)Integrates cognitive and analytic approaches to understand problematic patterns of thinking, feeling and relating.Mapping behaviour patterns, identifying maladaptive thinking, reformulation letters to build insight.
5. Coherence TherapyBruce Ecker & Laurel HulleySymptoms arise from learned emotional responses; therapy uncovers and resolves them.Emotional uncovering, experiential work, creating moments of change.
6. Dance Movement Therapy (DMT)Marian Chace and other dance therapists (1940s)Movement as a form of expression to enhance emotional, cognitive and physical well-being.Movement exploration, body mirroring, expressive dance.
7. Developmental Needs Meeting Strategy (DNMS)Shirley Jean SchmidtUnmet childhood developmental needs drive adult emotional and psychological issues.Healing attachment wounds by meeting unmet developmental needs.
8. DramatherapyInfluenced by Jacob L. Moreno & Peter SladeDrama and theatre techniques to facilitate emotional expression, self-awareness and conflict resolution.Role-play, improvisation, storytelling, guided imagery.
9. EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation & Reprocessing)Francine Shapiro (1987)Treats trauma by processing distressing memories and reducing their impact; client recalls trauma while focusing on bilateral stimuli.Eye movements, bilateral tapping, cognitive restructuring of traumatic memories.
10. Functional Analytic Psychotherapy (FAP)Robert Kohlenberg & Mavis TsaiUses the therapeutic relationship to change problematic interpersonal behaviours as they emerge in session.Reinforcing adaptive behaviour in the relationship, in-vivo exposure, emotional validation.
11. HypnotherapyModern form attributed to Milton Erickson (roots in Franz Mesmer, 18th c.)Hypnosis to create focused attention, heightened suggestibility and deep relaxation; used for phobias, addictions, chronic pain.Trance induction, suggestion therapy.
12. Integral TherapyKen WilberIntegral theory integrating multiple perspectives — internal/external, individual/collective — into a holistic whole.Working across four quadrants to create a holistic approach to therapy and development.
13. Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy (ISTDP)Habib DavanlooActive therapy to overcome resistance and access repressed emotions; tackles deep-rooted problems in a short time.Emotionally focused interventions, unlocking the unconscious, working through defenses.
14. LogotherapyViktor FranklExistential therapy centred on finding meaning in life, even amid suffering; humans are driven by a "will to meaning."Socratic dialogue, dereflection, paradoxical intention.
15. Metacognitive TherapyAdrian WellsModifies dysfunctional metacognitive beliefs that maintain anxiety and depression.Detachment from thought processes, attention training, challenging metacognitive beliefs.
16. Multimodal Therapy (MMT)Arnold LazarusEvaluates seven dimensions of personality — the BASIC ID: Behaviour, Affect, Sensation, Imagery, Cognition, Interpersonal relationships, Drugs/biology.Tailored interventions for each modality based on the person's specific needs.
17. Narrative TherapyMichael White & David EpstonSees people as separate from their problems; clients "re-author" their lives with empowering narratives.Externalising problems, identifying unique outcomes, re-authoring personal stories.
18. Play TherapyVirginia Axline & Carl Rogers (influences)Play as a medium for children to express emotions, resolve conflicts and process difficult experiences.Sand tray, role-playing, art-based activities, storytelling.
19. Positive PsychotherapyNossrat PeseschkianEmphasises inherent capacities for growth and healing; focuses on strengths rather than pathology.Balancing techniques, active goal-setting, identifying personal strengths.
20. Rational Living Therapy (RLT)Aldo R. PucciA cognitive-behavioural approach building on REBT to replace irrational beliefs with more logical ones.Cognitive restructuring, disputing irrational beliefs, a rational-living plan.
21. Reality TherapyWilliam GlasserBased on choice theory: clients take control of their lives by making better choices; emphasises personal responsibility and the present.Action planning, focusing on current behaviour, exploring new choices.
22. Sexual Identity TherapyNo single founderAddresses sexual orientation, identity and gender identity in a supportive, affirming way.Exploratory discussion, psychoeducation, supportive counselling.
23. Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)Steve de Shazer & Insoo Kim BergShort-term, goal-oriented; finds solutions in the present rather than dwelling on past problems.Miracle question, scaling questions, identifying exceptions.
24. Structural Family TherapySalvador MinuchinLooks at family hierarchies, boundaries and subsystems and how they shape individual behaviour and mental health.Joining, restructuring family interactions, boundary setting.

5. 25 landmark experiments

The classic studies, with who ran them, when, where and what they showed. The entrances ask these as "Experiment X was conducted by?" and "Which study demonstrated effect Y?".

ExperimentConducted byYearWhereWhat it showed
1. Hawthorne StudiesElton Mayo1924–1932Western Electric, Hawthorne Works, ChicagoProductivity rose when workers knew they were observed — the Hawthorne effect.
2. Little Albert ExperimentJohn B. Watson & Rosalie Rayner1920Johns Hopkins UniversityA baby was classically conditioned to fear a white rat; fear generalised to similar stimuli. Now a landmark in research ethics.
3. Halo Effect ExperimentEdward Thorndike1920Case Western UniversityA positive impression of one quality (e.g. attractiveness) led people to overestimate other qualities.
4. The Monster StudyWendell Johnson1939University of IowaAttempted to induce stuttering in orphan children through negative speech therapy — one of the most unethical studies in psychology.
5. Robbers Cave ExperimentMuzafer Sherif1954Robbers Cave State Park, OklahomaIntergroup conflict arises between groups and is resolved through superordinate goals.
6. Asch Conformity ExperimentSolomon Asch1951Swarthmore CollegeParticipants conformed to clearly wrong answers given by a group — the power of social pressure.
7. Cognitive Dissonance ExperimentLeon Festinger1957Stanford UniversityDiscomfort when behaviour conflicts with belief; participants justified their behaviour by changing their beliefs.
8. Harlow's Monkey ExperimentHarry Harlow1961University of Wisconsin-MadisonInfant monkeys preferred a cloth "mother" (comfort) over a wire one (food) — contact comfort drives attachment.
9. Fantz Looking Chamber ExperimentRobert Fantz1961Case Western Reserve UniversityInfants preferred looking at human faces over patterns — innate visual preferences.
10. Bobo Doll ExperimentAlbert Bandura1961Stanford UniversityChildren who watched an adult act aggressively toward a doll imitated it — observational / social learning.
11. Milgram Obedience ExperimentStanley Milgram1961Yale UniversityParticipants administered what they believed were painful shocks when told to by an authority — obedience over morality.
12. Schachter & Singer Experiment on EmotionStanley Schachter & Jerome Singer1962Columbia UniversitySupported the two-factor theory of emotion (arousal + cognitive labelling); participants misattributed arousal based on others' behaviour.
13. Ainsworth Strange SituationMary Ainsworth1969Johns Hopkins UniversityAssessed infant attachment via reaction to a caregiver's absence/return; three styles — secure, insecure-avoidant, insecure-resistant.
14. Split-Brain ExperimentRoger Sperry1968California Institute of TechnologyStudied severed-corpus-callosum patients; showed the two hemispheres have specialised functions (Nobel Prize).
15. The Good Samaritan ExperimentJohn Darley & C. Daniel Batson1973Princeton UniversitySituational factors (being in a hurry) drove helping more than values — even among theology students.
16. Stanford Prison ExperimentPhilip Zimbardo1971Stanford UniversityA simulated prison showed the extreme psychological effects of perceived power; guards turned abusive, prisoners traumatised — ended early.
17. Marshmallow TestWalter Mischel1972Stanford UniversityA longitudinal study of delayed gratification in children; those who waited for a larger reward had better later outcomes.
18. Bystander Effect (Good Samaritan study)John Darley & Bibb Latané1968Columbia UniversityPeople are less likely to help in distress when others are present — responsibility is diffused.
19. Ross's False Consensus Effect StudyLee Ross1977Stanford UniversityPeople overestimate how much others share their beliefs, attitudes and behaviours — the false consensus effect.
20. Selective Attention (The Invisible Gorilla)Daniel Simons & Christopher Chabris1999Harvard UniversityCounting basketball passes, many viewers missed a gorilla walking through — inattentional blindness.
21. Car Crash ExperimentElizabeth Loftus & John Palmer1974University of California, IrvineThe misinformation effect: question wording ("smashed" vs "hit") altered recalled speed of the cars.
22. A Class DividedJane Elliott1968Riceville, IowaAfter MLK's assassination, Elliott split her class by eye colour, treating one group as superior then reversing it — showing prejudice is arbitrary and damaging.
23. Learned Helplessness ExperimentMartin Seligman1967University of PennsylvaniaDogs given unavoidable shocks stopped trying to escape even when they could — learned helplessness, a model for depression.
24. Surrogate Mother Experiment (Harlow's monkeys)Harry Harlow1961University of Wisconsin-MadisonMonkeys offered a wire mother (food) and a cloth mother (comfort) chose comfort — contact comfort over feeding.
25. Visual Cliff ExperimentEleanor Gibson & Richard Walk1960Cornell UniversityInfants placed on a glass surface over an apparent drop avoided the "cliff" — depth perception develops early.
