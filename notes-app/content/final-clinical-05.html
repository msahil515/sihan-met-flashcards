In the late 1950s Aaron Beck was a young psychiatrist at the University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia and, like most ambitious American psychiatrists of his generation, a fully trained psychoanalyst. His patients lay on the couch and free-associated: they said whatever came into their minds, and he listened for the hidden wishes and conflicts that psychoanalytic theory told him were there. One day a patient was doing exactly what the method asked of him. He was angrily criticising his analyst. When the outburst ran down, Beck asked him what he was feeling. "I feel very guilty," the patient said.

That answer did not fit. A man who has just vented his anger should, by the theory Beck had been taught, feel relieved, not guilty. So Beck asked a further question, and the patient explained. While he had been voicing his anger, a second stream of thoughts had been running alongside it, thoughts he had not mentioned because he had barely noticed them himself: "I said the wrong thing... I shouldn't have said that... He won't like me... I'm bad." It was this quiet, rapid, self-critical commentary, and not the anger, that was producing the guilt.

Beck began asking his other patients about thoughts of this kind, and nearly all of them had them. The thoughts were fast, they felt completely believable, they arrived without being summoned, and they were followed by feelings the patients were very much aware of. Beck called them automatic thoughts. At about the same time he was running studies that were meant to confirm the psychoanalytic account of depression as anger turned inward against the self. He studied the dreams of depressed patients, expecting to find disguised hostility, and found instead dreams full of loss, defeat and rejection: the same themes his patients voiced when they were awake. In laboratory tasks, depressed patients who were given an experience of success did not sabotage themselves, as a "need to suffer" would predict; their mood and their performance improved.

The evidence was pointing somewhere new. Depressed people were not secretly seeking suffering; they were thinking about themselves, their world and their future in a consistently negative and distorted way. Out of that second stream of thought grew cognitive therapy, and out of cognitive therapy grew the large family of treatments now called cognitive-behavioural therapy, the most researched form of psychotherapy in the world. Keep Beck's guilty patient in mind. You will meet him again at the end of this chapter, after you have seen what became of his second stream.

5.1 From behaviour to belief: the cognitive turn in therapyMedium yield · 3 Qs

Imagine two students who both score 48 out of 90 on a mock entrance test. The first feels disappointed, studies the questions she got wrong, and books another mock for the weekend. The second feels crushed, tells himself he was never good enough for a clinical programme, and stops opening his books. The event was the same; the reactions were opposite. What made the difference? Not the score, but what each student made of it. That simple observation is the foundation of every therapy in this chapter.

cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) is best understood as a family name rather than a single treatment. It covers dozens of therapies that share three propositions, set out by the Canadian psychologist Keith S. Dobson: that cognitive activity affects behaviour and emotion; that cognitive activity can be monitored and altered; and that desired changes in behaviour and emotion can be brought about by changing cognition. The first proposition is often called the cognitive mediation hypothesis: between the event and the reaction stands an interpretation, and the interpretation, not the event alone, shapes how we feel and what we do. Notice that the claim is that thinking mediates, not that thinking is all there is. Biology, learning history and real circumstances matter too; CBT simply chooses thought and behaviour as the most accessible levers for change.

Ancient and modern roots

The idea is very old. The Greek Stoic philosopher Epictetus (about 50 to 135 CE), a freed slave, taught, in the Enchiridion (the handbook of his sayings compiled by his pupil Arrian), that "men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them." Albert Ellis quoted this line so often that it became the motto of his therapy. Indian and Buddhist traditions said something similar many centuries earlier: the opening verses of the Dhammapada teach that what we are is shaped by our thoughts, and Buddhist meditation was, among other things, a training in watching thoughts arise and pass without being swept away by them. As you will see later in the chapter, this second idea (watching thoughts rather than arguing with them) returns in the third wave.

Within modern psychology, several people prepared the ground. Alfred Adler (1870 to 1937) argued that people act on a "private logic" and that neurosis rests on "basic mistakes" in a person's beliefs about self and life. Karen Horney (1885 to 1952) wrote of the "tyranny of the shoulds" (in Neurosis and Human Growth, 1950), the inner dictates that drive neurotic people to impossible standards; Ellis's "musts" are close cousins. George Alexander Kelly (1905 to 1967) published The Psychology of Personal Constructs in 1955, describing every person as a scientist who builds and tests theories about the world (you will meet his fixed-role therapy in Section 5.5). And in academic psychology, the "cognitive revolution" of the 1950s and 1960s made it respectable again to study what goes on inside the head.

Why behaviour therapy turned cognitive

By the 1960s, behaviour therapy (Chapter 4) had shown that conditioning-based methods could treat phobias and many habits. But it struggled with problems that seemed to live in thought itself: depression, obsessional rumination, worry, self-criticism. Behaviour therapists began to treat thoughts as "covert behaviours" that could be learned and unlearned, and during the 1970s a series of books announced the merger: Michael Mahoney's Cognition and Behavior Modification (1974) and Donald Meichenbaum's Cognitive-Behavior Modification (1977) gave the hybrid its name.

The most influential bridge came from Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021). In 1977 he proposed the concept of self-efficacy: a person's belief in his or her capacity to organise and carry out the actions needed to manage a particular situation. Self-efficacy is not skill; it is confidence in one's skill, and it is specific to a task (you can feel highly efficacious at cricket and hopeless at statistics). Bandura identified four sources of self-efficacy: mastery experiences (actually succeeding), vicarious experiences (watching someone like you succeed), verbal persuasion (being told you can), and physiological and emotional states (reading a calm body as a sign of competence). His bold claim was that all forms of psychological treatment, whatever their theory, work largely by raising self-efficacy. That is why self-efficacy is so often described as a cognitive-behavioural explanation of how people change. Compare this with Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990), whose radical behaviourism explained behaviour entirely by its consequences and treated inner "beliefs" as unnecessary explanations.

Exam trap

"Which theorist developed self-efficacy, used as a cognitive-behavioural explanation of how people change?" The answer is Albert Bandura (1977), not Beck or Ellis, who built therapies around beliefs, and certainly not Skinner, who rejected cognitive explanations. A related one-liner: pharmacotherapy (treatment with medicines) is not a psychotherapy; cognitive, behavioural and psychodynamic therapies all are, because they work through a psychological relationship and psychological techniques.

Three waves

In 2004 the American psychologist Steven C. Hayes described the history of the field as three waves, and the metaphor stuck. The first wave was behaviour therapy (1950s onward: Wolpe, Eysenck, Skinner and Lazarus), which applied learning principles directly to observable behaviour. The second wave was cognitive therapy (from the mid-1950s with Ellis, and the 1960s and 1970s with Beck and Meichenbaum), which argued that changing the content of thoughts (from irrational or distorted to rational or realistic) would change emotion and behaviour. The third wave (from the 1990s: dialectical behaviour therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and others) shifted the target from the content of thoughts to a person's relationship with them: noticing thoughts, accepting feelings, and acting on values even while unpleasant thoughts and feelings are present. Figure 5.1 shows the timeline. Notice that each wave added to the field rather than replacing what came before; all three are practised today, often in the same clinic.

All CBTs, whatever their wave, share a recognisable style. They are usually time-limited (often 8 to 20 sessions), focused on present problems rather than the distant past, structured, collaborative, educational (clients learn a model of their problem), and they rely on homework: the real work happens between sessions. They also measure: symptoms are rated at the start and tracked throughout, which is one reason CBT has accumulated more research than any other therapy.

Exam trap

"Third-wave CBT includes all EXCEPT..." The third wave includes acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), compassion-focused therapy, metacognitive therapy and functional analytic psychotherapy. Psychoanalysis belongs to none of the waves; it is a separate, older psychodynamic tradition (Chapter 3). REBT and Beck's cognitive therapy are second wave; systematic desensitisation and token economies are first wave.

5.2 Albert Ellis and rational emotive behaviour therapyHigh yield · 14 Qs

As a young man in New York, Albert Ellis (1913 to 2007) was painfully shy with women. So he set himself an assignment. Over one month in the Bronx Botanical Garden he sat down beside about a hundred women, one after another, and made himself start a conversation within a minute. Almost none of the conversations led anywhere (the one woman who agreed to a date did not turn up), but something more important happened: his fear of rejection faded, because he had proved to himself that rejection was unpleasant but not terrible. Ellis later said he had invented his therapy on himself before he ever practised it on anyone else. The episode already contains its main ingredients: a belief ("being rejected would be awful"), a deliberate behavioural experiment against that belief, and a new conclusion ("I don't like rejection, but I can stand it").

Ellis earned his doctorate in clinical psychology at Columbia University in 1947 and practised psychoanalysis from about 1947 to 1953. He grew frustrated. Patients gained insight, he said, but rarely got better; knowing why you are disturbed did not stop you disturbing yourself. Returning to the philosophy he had read since his teens (the Stoics above all), he began in the mid-1950s to practise a far more active approach. He first called it rational therapy, presenting it at the American Psychological Association convention in 1956; the approach is usually dated to 1955. Because critics heard "rational" as "unemotional", he renamed it rational-emotive therapy (RET) in 1961, and in 1993 he renamed it again as rational emotive behaviour therapy (REBT), to acknowledge how much behavioural homework it had always used. In 1959 he founded the Institute for Rational Living in New York (later the Albert Ellis Institute), and his 1962 book Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy became the classic statement of the approach. Because it predates Beck's work, REBT is often called the first cognitive-behavioural therapy.

The ABC model

The heart of REBT is the ABC model. A is the activating event (or adversity): something that happens, or that a person notices, remembers or anticipates. C is the consequence: the emotional and behavioural reaction. Common sense assumes that A causes C ("the exam result made me depressed"). Ellis's central claim is that A contributes to C but does not cause it directly; the main cause is B, the beliefs the person holds about A. Return to the two students who scored 48 out of 90. Same A. The first believes, "I really wanted a better score, and it's disappointing, but one mock doesn't decide my future." Her C is disappointment and more study. The second believes, "I absolutely must score well, and since I didn't, I'm a failure who will never make it." His C is depression and avoidance. Different B, different C. Figure 5.2 lays out the full model.

Ellis distinguished two kinds of belief. A rational belief is flexible and phrased as a preference ("I want to pass, but there is no law of the universe that says I must"). It is consistent with reality, it follows logically, and it helps the person reach his or her goals. An irrational belief is rigid and absolute ("I must pass"). It is inconsistent with reality (nothing guarantees that one must pass), illogical (a strong wish does not create a necessity) and self-defeating. Rational beliefs produce healthy emotions even when events are bad; irrational beliefs produce disturbance.

Exam trap

In Ellis's model, A = activating event, B = belief about the event, C = consequence of the belief (emotional and behavioural). Wrong options to watch for: "B = behaviour", "B = baseline", "B = bias", "B = biological predisposition", "B = beliefs about oneself" (too narrow: the belief is about the event), "C = critical analysis", "A = anticipatory event", "A = antecedent", and invented strings such as "attention, bonding, change" or "affect, behaviour, coping". "Affect, behaviour, cognition" is a real ABC, but a different one: the three components of an attitude in social psychology (Social book). The behavioural "ABC" of antecedent, behaviour, consequence is a different model altogether: it is the functional analysis of the behaviour therapists (Chapter 4), in which C means the reinforcing or punishing outcome of a behaviour. Same letters, different meanings.

The irrational beliefs

What makes a belief irrational? Ellis's answer narrowed over the years to four processes. The first and most basic is demandingness: turning preferences into absolute "musts", "shoulds", "oughts" and "have-tos". Ellis, who loved provocative language, called this musturbation (you will also see it spelled "musterbation"). From a demand, three other irrational conclusions tend to follow when the demand is not met. awfulising (or awfulizing, close to what Beck calls catastrophising) is rating an event as "awful", that is, more than 100 percent bad, the worst thing that could happen. low frustration tolerance is the belief that one cannot stand discomfort or frustration ("I can't bear it"; Ellis's joke name was "I-can't-stand-it-itis"). And global rating of self, others or life is judging a whole person by a single act ("I failed, so I am a failure"; "he lied, so he is a rotten person"). Global rating of the self is also called self-downing; of others, damnation. Table 5.1 sets each of the four beside its rational alternative.

Ellis grouped the endless variety of musts into three major musts, one each about the self, other people and the world. The first is "I must do well and win the approval of others, or else I am no good": it underlies anxiety, depression, shame and guilt. The second is "Other people must treat me kindly and fairly, or else they are rotten and deserve to be punished": it underlies anger, rage and violence. The third is "The conditions under which I live must give me what I want, quickly and easily, or else life is awful and I can't stand it": it underlies self-pity, procrastination and addictions. In Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy (1962) he had listed his "irrational ideas" at greater length (eleven in the version most often quoted; some sources count twelve), among them the ideas that one must be loved and approved of by virtually everyone, that one must be thoroughly competent and achieving in every respect to be worthwhile, that some people are wicked and should be severely blamed and punished, that unhappiness is caused by outside events and cannot be controlled, and that one's past must go on determining one's present.

Ellis also described two kinds of anxiety. Ego anxiety arises when one's sense of worth is threatened ("I must perform well or I am worthless"). Discomfort anxiety arises when one's comfort is threatened ("I must not feel uncomfortable"). And he pointed out that people frequently disturb themselves about their disturbance. A student who is anxious before a viva and then tells herself "I mustn't be anxious; it's terrible that I'm anxious" has added a second layer of anxiety to the first. This secondary disturbance (anxiety about anxiety, depression about depression, shame about being depressed) is often tackled first in REBT, because it keeps the primary problem going.

Healthy and unhealthy negative emotions

A common misunderstanding is that REBT tries to make people calm or cheerful about bad events. It does not. Ellis drew a sharp line between an unhealthy negative emotion, which comes from an irrational belief and blocks a person's goals, and a healthy negative emotion, which comes from a rational belief about the same bad event and helps the person respond to it. If you fail an exam you care about, feeling nothing would be odd; feeling sad and concerned is healthy. Table 5.2 sets out the usual pairs.

D, E and F: disputing and its results

Identifying the irrational belief is only the start. The active ingredient of REBT is disputing (D): challenging the irrational belief until the client no longer believes it. Disputing has been described as three steps: detecting the irrational belief, discriminating it from rational beliefs, and debating it. Three kinds of argument are used, and exam papers like to ask for them:

  • Logical disputes ask whether the belief follows from the facts or from the person's wishes. "You would very much like to pass. How does it follow that you must pass?"
  • Empirical disputes ask for evidence. "Where is the evidence that you are a total failure? Have you failed at everything you have ever tried?"
  • Pragmatic (or functional) disputes ask whether the belief helps. "Where does believing 'I must pass or I'm worthless' get you? Does it help you study, or does it keep you away from your books?"

Some writers add philosophical disputes ("Even if you fail this exam, can you still find meaning and satisfaction in your life?"). Ellis delivered disputes in several styles: short didactic explanations, Socratic questions, humour and exaggeration (he wrote comic "rational songs" for clients to sing), metaphors and occasional self-disclosure.

When disputing succeeds, the client arrives at E, an effective new philosophy (also described as an effective new belief, or simply the new effect): a rational belief that can be used again in future. The result is F, new healthier feelings and behaviours (sadness instead of depression, renewed study instead of avoidance). Some versions of the model also add G, the person's goals, at the very beginning (the "GABCDE" model), because whether a belief is self-defeating can only be judged against what the person is trying to achieve.

Ellis also distinguished two levels of change. The inelegant solution relieves the immediate symptom (the client stops being anxious about this particular exam). The elegant solution is a deep philosophical shift: the client gives up demandingness in general and accepts self, others and life unconditionally, so that future adversities disturb him far less. REBT aims for the elegant solution where it can.

Exam trap

"In the A-B-C-D-E model, D refers to..." Disputing (of irrational beliefs). Not desensitisation (Wolpe's technique, Chapter 4), not "discuss", not "drama". E is the effective new philosophy (some books write "effect" or "effective new belief"), and F is new feelings. Also remember the order of names: rational therapy (about 1955 to 1956), rational-emotive therapy (1961), rational emotive behaviour therapy (1993).

Unconditional acceptance

If global rating is the poison, unconditional acceptance is the antidote. Ellis urged clients toward unconditional self-acceptance (USA): accepting oneself as a fallible human being whose acts can be rated as good or bad, but whose whole self cannot be given a single rating. "I behaved badly" is a rational evaluation of an act; "I am bad" is an irrational evaluation of a person. He extended the same idea to unconditional other-acceptance (people who act badly are fallible humans, not rotten people) and unconditional life-acceptance (life contains real adversity and is still worth living). The idea resembles Carl Rogers's unconditional positive regard (Chapter 6), but the two men used it differently. Rogers believed the therapist's warmth and acceptance were themselves curative; Ellis accepted his clients fully but thought warmth was neither necessary nor sufficient, and warned that too much of it could feed a client's dire need for approval. His style was famously blunt.

Techniques: cognitive, emotive and behavioural

REBT is deliberately multimodal, combining three kinds of technique. The cognitive techniques include disputing, REBT self-help forms on which clients write out their own A, B, C, D and E, rational coping statements, reading (bibliotherapy), and listing the real costs and benefits of a habit. Ellis also insisted on precise language: "I can't speak in a group" becomes "I haven't yet spoken in a group and I'm afraid to."

The emotive techniques work on beliefs through feeling and vivid experience. In rational emotive imagery, a technique developed by the psychiatrist Maxie C. Maultsby Jr. and adopted by Ellis, the client vividly imagines the activating event, lets himself feel the unhealthy emotion fully, then changes the feeling to its healthy counterpart (from depression to sadness, say), and finally notices what he said to himself to make the change; that new self-statement is the rational belief, discovered from the inside. In a shame-attacking exercise, the client deliberately does something harmless but "shameful" in public, so as to feel the shame, survive it, and learn that other people's disapproval is unpleasant rather than unbearable. Ellis's own favourite example was calling out the names of the stations aloud in a New York subway train. A Mysuru student might ask loudly at a chai stall for "one tea without tea", or wear mismatched slippers to class. Forceful self-statements and role-play are also used.

The behavioural techniques include homework (almost every session ends with an assignment), in vivo exposure, "stay in there" exercises (remaining in an uncomfortable situation to build frustration tolerance), skills training, and self-management with rewards and penalties (the client allows himself a favourite activity only after doing the dreaded task, and imposes a penalty if he avoids it). Ellis's own month in the Bronx Botanical Garden was, in effect, a homework assignment of this kind.

Clinical link

REBT does not say that activating events are unimportant. When the A is real and ongoing (domestic violence, harassment, caste discrimination, poverty), the first job is safety and practical help, and the belief work comes alongside it. An REBT therapist would never tell an abused woman that her distress is "only her beliefs". What REBT targets is the extra, self-made layer of disturbance (self-blame, "I can't survive this") that stops people acting on their situation.

Evaluating REBT

REBT's strengths are its clarity, brevity and teachability. The ABC model can be explained in one session, used in groups, in schools and in workplaces, and practised as self-help; Ellis's books sold millions of copies. It has been applied to anxiety, depression, anger, addictions, sexual problems and performance anxiety. Its evidence base, though, is thinner and older than Beck's. Many early studies were small and methodologically weak. A meta-analysis by Lyons and Woods (1991) of some 70 outcome studies found REBT more effective than no treatment and placebo conditions, and later trials (for example, by Daniel David and colleagues in Romania in 2008) found REBT broadly comparable with Beck's cognitive therapy and with antidepressant medication for major depression.

The criticisms are worth knowing because they turn up as exam options. First, REBT can be direct and confrontational, and a client may experience vigorous disputing as intimidating, invalidating or humiliating, especially early in therapy or if he is fragile. Second, the therapist's idea of what is "rational" may carry the therapist's own values; in cultures where duty to family is a moral ideal, a blanket attack on "shoulds" can feel like an attack on the client's ethics. Third, its philosophical claims (for example, that demandingness is the core of all disturbance) are hard to test. Notice what is not a fair criticism: REBT does not focus on sexuality (that is a criticism of Freud), and it does have evidence for depression.

Indian context

Many Indian clients carry a powerful version of Ellis's first must, often voiced as "log kya kahenge?" ("what will people say?"). Indian therapists using REBT usually dispute the absolutist form of the belief ("everyone must approve of me or I am nothing") rather than the value of family and community itself, and they work gently, since a frontal attack on a client's sense of duty tends to end the therapy rather than the belief.

Exam trap

Any stem mentioning musts, musturbation, awfulising, irrational beliefs, disputing or shame attacking points to Albert Ellis and REBT, not to Beck's cognitive therapy, DBT, or William Glasser's reality therapy (Chapter 6). "Rational Emotive Therapy was given by..." Ellis, not Freud, not William James (a founder of functionalism and of the James-Lange theory of emotion), and not Aaron Beck. Ellis was influenced by Adler and the Stoics, but REBT is his own.

5.3 Aaron Beck's cognitive modelHigh yield · 12 Qs

Ellis reached his therapy through philosophy and through his own shyness. The man you met in the opening pages reached his through data. Aaron Temkin Beck (1921 to 2021) grew up in Providence, Rhode Island, studied English and political science at Brown University and qualified in medicine at Yale in 1946. Like Ellis, he had treated himself before he treated anyone else: a dangerous infection in childhood had left him with a fear of blood and surgery, and during his medical training he overcame it by exposing himself gradually to the sights and sounds of the operating theatre until the fear faded. He trained in psychiatry, joined the University of Pennsylvania in 1954, and graduated from the Philadelphia Psychoanalytic Institute in 1956. In other words, he set out to prove psychoanalysis, not to replace it.

The research described in the opening story changed his mind. His dream studies and his success experiments did not support the idea that depressed people secretly need to suffer, and his patients' "second stream" of thought explained their feelings better than any hidden wish. Beck published his new account in two papers called "Thinking and Depression" (1963 and 1964), then in the book Depression: Clinical, Experimental, and Theoretical Aspects (1967). In Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders (1976) he extended the model to anxiety, phobias and other conditions, and in Cognitive Therapy of Depression (1979), written with A. John Rush, Brian Shaw and Gary Emery, he set out the treatment session by session. That 1979 book was one of the first psychotherapy manuals, and manuals made controlled trials possible. Beck kept working almost to the end of a very long life; with his daughter, the psychologist Judith S. Beck, he founded the Beck Institute for Cognitive Behavior Therapy near Philadelphia in 1994, and he received the Lasker Award, one of medicine's highest honours, in 2006.

Key study: cognitive therapy versus imipramine (Rush, Beck, Kovacs and Hollon, 1977)

Could a talking treatment match a drug for depression? In 1977 A. John Rush, Beck, Maria Kovacs and Steven Hollon randomly assigned 41 depressed outpatients to about 12 weeks of either cognitive therapy or the tricyclic antidepressant imipramine. Both groups improved, but the cognitive therapy group improved more, and far fewer of them dropped out. It was the first trial in which any psychotherapy had beaten an antidepressant, and it launched a generation of research. Critics noted that the drug dose had been tapered before the final assessment, and later, larger trials usually found cognitive therapy and medication roughly equal during treatment. The lasting finding, as you will see in Section 5.11, was a different one: cognitive therapy protects against relapse after treatment ends.

Automatic thoughts

Beck's starting point was the second stream itself. automatic thoughts are the quick, evaluative thoughts or images that pop into the mind in a situation, without deliberate reasoning, and shape how a person feels and acts. Beck compared them with Freud's "preconscious": not hidden by repression, but so habitual that people notice the feeling and miss the thought that produced it. Automatic thoughts have a recognisable signature. They are specific and brief, often telegraphic (a single word such as "Idiot!" or a fleeting picture of being laughed at). They feel completely plausible, so they are accepted without question. They are idiosyncratic: two people in the same situation have different ones. And they are hard to switch off. The automatic thought that carries the most emotion is often called the hot thought.

Return to the student who scored 48 out of 90 in Section 5.1. A cognitive therapist would not ask him what he believes about life in general. She would ask a much simpler question: "When you saw the score, what went through your mind?" The answer ("I'll never get into a clinical programme; everyone else is better than me") is the automatic thought, and it is the first thing cognitive therapy learns to catch. Notice the difference from Ellis. Ellis went straight for the deep philosophical "must"; Beck starts with the concrete thought in the concrete situation and works downward.

The cognitive triad

What do depressed people's automatic thoughts have in common? Beck's answer, first set out in 1967, is the most examined idea in this chapter. The cognitive triad is the pattern of negative thinking about three things: the self, the world (or ongoing experience) and the future.

  • A negative view of the self: the person sees himself as defective, inadequate, worthless or unlovable, and attributes unpleasant experiences to these supposed defects ("I failed because I'm stupid").
  • A negative view of the world, or of current experience: the world seems to make impossible demands, to block every goal and to offer only defeat and deprivation ("Nothing ever works out for me; everyone is against me").
  • A negative view of the future: present hardships are expected to continue indefinitely ("It will always be like this; things will never get better"). This is the root of hopelessness.

Beck argued that the other symptoms of depression follow from the triad. If you expect every effort to fail, it makes sense to stop trying, and the result looks like a loss of motivation or "paralysis of the will". If the future is hopeless, death can begin to look like the only escape, which is why Beck regarded hopelessness, rather than sadness, as the key to suicide. If you believe you cannot cope, you lean on others, and the result is increased dependency. Depression, on this view, is not primarily a disorder of mood. It is a disorder of thinking whose mood is the natural consequence.

Exam trap

The cognitive triad is negative views of self, world (experience) and future. It is not "past, present and future" (the past is not one of the three), not "self, others and environment", not "self, mother and father", not "mood, behaviour and somatic state", not "body, mind and spirit", and not "id, ego and superego" (Freud's structural model, Chapter 3). Nor is it "thoughts, feelings and behaviours", the three linked parts of the general CBT model. When a stem asks what maintains depression in Beck's model, the answer is the triad and the distortions that feed it; repressed sexual conflict is Freud's account, a "deficiency of reinforcement" is Peter Lewinsohn's behavioural account (Section 5.10), and insecure attachment is John Bowlby's (Developmental book). And if a stem describes "the pattern of negative thoughts about self, world and future", the name is the cognitive triad, not automatic thoughts, cognitive distortions or dysfunctional assumptions, which are the other parts of the model described next.

Three levels of cognition

Why do some people react to a poor mark with the triad and others with mild disappointment? Beck's answer was that automatic thoughts grow out of deeper and more lasting structures. His daughter Judith Beck later organised these into three levels, set out in her textbook Cognitive Therapy: Basics and Beyond (1995). Figure 5.3 shows how they fit together.

At the deepest level are core beliefs: global, rigid, absolute beliefs about oneself, other people and the world, usually formed in childhood ("I am incompetent", "I am unlovable", "People can't be trusted"). Beck often used the word schema (plural schemas or schemata) for the mental structure that holds a core belief and filters incoming information: a schema works like a pair of tinted glasses, so that information that fits it is noticed and remembered and information that does not fit is ignored or explained away. Judith Beck grouped negative core beliefs about the self into three families: helpless beliefs ("I'm weak, I'm a failure"), unlovable beliefs ("I'm unwanted, I'll be rejected") and worthless beliefs ("I'm bad, I don't deserve to live").

At the middle level are intermediate beliefs: the attitudes, rules and assumptions that grow out of a core belief and tell the person how to live with it. Consider a student whose core belief is "I am incompetent". An attitude might be "It's terrible to be seen as stupid." A rule might be "I must top every test." An assumption, usually in if-then form, might be "If I study harder than everyone else, I can hide my incompetence; if I don't, everyone will see it." These conditional rules are also called dysfunctional assumptions, and they can be measured with the Dysfunctional Attitude Scale (Arlene Weissman and Beck, 1978). They are often the source of compensatory strategies: the student copes by overworking, which works until the day it doesn't.

At the surface level are the automatic thoughts. When a situation touches the rule (a mock score of 48), the core belief is activated and the automatic thoughts follow ("I'm useless; I'll never get in"), and these in turn produce the person's reactions: an emotion (despair), a behaviour (closing the books) and a physiological response (heaviness, fatigue).

The model is a diathesis-stress model. A dysfunctional schema can lie dormant for years, like a landmine, until a matching stress steps on it. Beck (1983) suggested that people differ in which landmines they carry. A sociotropic person invests his worth in being liked and close to others, so he is most vulnerable to rejection or loss; an autonomous person invests his worth in achievement and independence, so he is most vulnerable to failure or loss of control. The same broken engagement may devastate the first and barely touch the second, while a failed examination does the reverse. Notice the practical consequence: when the depression lifts, the schema does not simply vanish. It goes quiet and can be reawakened by the next matching stress, or even by an ordinary low mood. That observation would later become the starting point for mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (Section 5.9).

The cognitive distortions

If schemas are tinted glasses, how exactly do they bend what a person sees? Beck noticed that depressed patients made the same errors of reasoning again and again, and he called them cognitive distortions (also cognitive errors or thinking errors): systematic, biased ways of processing information that turn ordinary events into evidence for the negative schema. In 1979 Beck listed six: arbitrary inference, selective abstraction, overgeneralisation, magnification and minimisation, personalisation, and absolutistic dichotomous thinking. The psychiatrist David D. Burns, who had trained with Beck in Philadelphia, rewrote the list in plain language for his best-selling self-help book Feeling Good (1980) and added several more; later writers added others still. arbitrary inference is jumping to a conclusion that the evidence does not support. In a classic example, a patient sees a friend in the street who fails to acknowledge him, and concludes that the friend no longer likes him. Perhaps the friend did not see him, or was preoccupied, or was short-sighted; nothing in the situation points to dislike, yet the conclusion feels certain. Burns later split arbitrary inference into two narrower kinds of "jumping to conclusions": mind reading (concluding what someone else thinks, without checking) and fortune telling (predicting a bad outcome as if it were already fact). When an option offers "arbitrary inference" and no narrower name, that is the one to choose.

selective abstraction is focusing on a detail taken out of context, ignoring other, more important features of the situation, and judging the whole experience by that one fragment. Burns called it the mental filter, and some Indian textbooks and papers call it selective attention. A teacher lecturing to forty students notices two who look bored and concludes that his class is not liked by the students: thirty-eight attentive faces have been filtered out. A cricketer who scored a century but dropped one catch, and spends the night replaying the catch, is doing the same thing. Its close cousin is disqualifying the positive: the filtered-out good news is noticed, but then dismissed as a fluke or as politeness.

overgeneralisation is different. Here the person takes one or a few events and turns them into a general rule that is then applied across the board, to related and unrelated situations alike: "I couldn't answer one question in the viva, so I'll never be good at interviews," or "Ravi let me down, so nobody can be trusted." The signal words are "always", "never", "everyone" and "nothing". So the difference between the two is the direction of the error. Selective abstraction narrows the view within a situation (one detail stands for the whole). Overgeneralisation spreads a conclusion across situations (one event stands for all events). labelling is overgeneralisation applied to a whole person: instead of "I made a mistake", "I'm a failure"; instead of "she was late", "she's useless". You met its REBT twin, global rating, in Section 5.2.

magnification and minimisation is an error of size: one's mistakes, other people's strengths and the importance of bad events are enlarged, while one's own strengths and good events are shrunk. Burns compared it to looking through binoculars, the right way round at one's faults and the wrong way round at one's virtues. catastrophising is the extreme form of magnification applied to the future: worrying that the worst will happen and that it will be unbearable when it does ("If my heart races in the exam hall, I'll pass out, fail, and my family will never forgive me"). It is common in every anxiety disorder, and Beck's term overlaps closely with Ellis's awfulising. Some lists treat catastrophising as a kind of magnification, others as a kind of fortune telling; either way, its defining mark is the expectation of the worst.

personalisation is relating external events to oneself when there is no basis for doing so: taking things personally, or taking responsibility for events one did not cause. A mother whose son fails his board examination thinks, "It's my fault; I'm a bad mother," though the boy barely studied. A man who thinks "it always rains when I plan a picnic" has personalised the weather. dichotomous thinking (all-or-nothing or black-and-white thinking) sorts every experience into one of two extreme categories, perfect or worthless, success or failure, saint or sinner, with nothing in between. It is the distortion behind perfectionism, and you will meet it again in borderline personality disorder (Section 5.8), where other people are experienced as wholly good or wholly bad.

The remaining three are easily recognised once named. emotional reasoning treats a feeling as evidence ("I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong"; "I feel anxious on aeroplanes, so flying must be dangerous"). should statements are rigid rules aimed at oneself (producing guilt) or at others (producing anger and resentment); they are cognitive therapy's version (the name is Burns's) of Ellis's musts and Karen Horney's "tyranny of the shoulds". Labelling, already described, completes the list.

Exam trap

Three classic stems. (1) "A patient sees a friend in the street who fails to acknowledge him; he thinks his friend does not like him any more." This is arbitrary inference: a conclusion without evidence. It is not selective abstraction (no detail is being picked out of a larger picture), not personalisation (he is not blaming himself for an event he did not cause) and not magnification (nothing is being blown up in size). (2) "A teacher sees two students bored and feels his class is not liked by all the students." This is selective abstraction, which the paper called selective attention. A good student may be tempted by overgeneralisation, because the teacher moves from two students to "all the students"; but overgeneralisation means carrying a conclusion from one situation to other, unrelated situations, whereas the teacher has filtered one detail out of a single situation and ignored the rest of it. (3) "Catastrophic thinking describes..." worrying that the worst will always happen. Categorising all experiences as either extremely positive or extremely negative is dichotomous thinking; focusing on negative events while ignoring positive ones is the mental filter (selective abstraction); taking things very personally is personalisation.

Exam trap

"Catastrophic thinking" belongs to the cognitive model of anxiety, not to the diagnostic criteria of schizophrenia. If a question asks which of delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech and catastrophic thinking is not an ICD or DSM criterion for schizophrenia, the answer is catastrophic thinking (the criteria themselves are covered in the Disorders book).

5.4 Cognitive therapy in practiceHigh yield · 12 Qs

Suppose the student with the 48 out of 90 decides to see a cognitive therapist. What will actually happen in the room? The short answer is that he will be treated as a scientist with a hypothesis. His automatic thoughts and beliefs will not be interpreted, as a psychoanalyst would interpret them, and they will not be argued down, as Ellis might argue them down. They will be examined, the way a researcher examines a claim: what is the evidence, how else could the data be explained, and what experiment would settle the matter? This section describes the method, the techniques that carry it out, and the way it differs from REBT.

Collaborative empiricism

Beck called the working relationship collaborative empiricism. "Collaborative" means that therapist and client work as a team, a pair of co-investigators who set goals and agendas together and share responsibility for the work. "Empiricism" means that the client's beliefs are treated as hypotheses to be tested against evidence, not as facts to be accepted and not as errors to be corrected by authority. The core activity of the therapy is therefore making objective assessments of beliefs: gathering data from the client's own life, weighing it, and running real-world tests. A depressed client who says "I can't do anything right" is not told he is wrong; he and the therapist go looking for evidence, and the client draws the conclusion himself.

The relationship matters more in Beck's therapy than it did in Ellis's. Beck and his co-authors wrote in 1979 that warmth, accurate empathy and genuineness, the qualities Carl Rogers had emphasised (Chapter 6), were necessary for cognitive therapy but not sufficient: a good relationship is the ground on which the techniques work, not the treatment itself. The therapist is also openly educational. In the first sessions the client learns the cognitive model, using examples from his own week, because the long-term aim is for him to become his own therapist.

Notice what is absent. There is no couch, no free association (saying whatever comes to mind so that the analyst can detect unconscious material), no dream analysis, no interpretation of transference and no attempt to uncover repressed childhood memories. Those are the tools of psychoanalysis (Chapter 3). Nor does the therapist administer medication; a cognitive therapist may work alongside a psychiatrist who prescribes, but prescribing is no part of cognitive therapy.

Exam trap

"Which of the following is NOT a fundamental concept of cognitive therapy: collaborative empiricism, Socratic dialogue, free association, guided discovery?" Free association, which is psychoanalytic. The other three are the core of Beck's method. Similarly, "Which method is used in Beck's cognitive therapy for depression?" is answered by making objective assessments of beliefs, not by encouraging dream analysis or free association (Freud) and not by administering psychoactive medication (pharmacotherapy, which is not a psychotherapy at all).

Socratic questioning and guided discovery

How does a therapist help a client test a belief without simply telling him it is wrong? By asking questions. The Greek philosopher Socrates (about 470 to 399 BCE) taught not by lecturing but by asking a series of questions that led the other person to examine his own assumptions and discover their weaknesses for himself. Beck borrowed the method. Socratic questioning (or Socratic dialogue) is the use of carefully chosen, open-ended questions to help the client examine his own thoughts, notice evidence he has overlooked, consider other explanations and reach a more balanced conclusion of his own. guided discovery is the broader process the questions serve: the therapist guides, but the client discovers.

The American psychologist Christine A. Padesky, in a well-known 1993 address titled "Socratic Questioning: Changing Minds or Guiding Discovery?", gave the definition that examiners now quote. Socratic questioning, she said, asks the client questions which he has the knowledge to answer and which draw his attention to information that is relevant to the issue but outside his current focus. In the words of one past paper, it is a form of dialogue that helps a person discover and attend to previously overlooked information and that guides the discovery of the individual rather than interpretation from the therapist. Padesky argued that the aim is not to change the client's mind for him (the therapist does not know the answer in advance) but to guide a genuine discovery, and she described four steps: informational questions, empathic listening, summarising, and synthesising questions that ask the client to apply what he has found to his original belief. Three questions do much of the work, and together they are sometimes called the three-question technique: What is the evidence for this thought? How else could you look at the situation? If it were true, what would it mean, and how would you cope?

Here is how it sounds. Kavya, a first-year MA student, has been asked to present a case at a departmental seminar.

Therapist: You said that when your supervisor frowned during your presentation, you thought, "She thinks I'm hopeless." How much did you believe that at the time?
Kavya: Completely. Ninety percent.
Therapist: What did you see that fitted the thought?
Kavya: The frown. And she wrote something down.
Therapist: Anything that didn't quite fit?
Kavya: Well... afterwards she asked me to send her my slides. And she frowns when she concentrates; she frowned all through Arjun's talk too.
Therapist: So, putting all of that together, what do you make of the frown now?
Kavya: Maybe she was thinking hard about the case. Maybe forty percent that she thought it was bad.

The therapist never said "You're wrong" or "That's mind reading." Every piece of new evidence came from Kavya's own memory, and so did the new conclusion. That is the difference between guided discovery and persuasion.

Exam trap

Socratic questioning is the therapist guiding the client to discover his own maladaptive thoughts and generate alternatives through open-ended questions. It is not the therapist interpreting unconscious conflicts, not the therapist giving direct advice or telling the client what to think, not direct confrontation of beliefs, not free association while the therapist listens passively, and not hypnotic suggestion. In the long "definition" options, check two details: the information is previously overlooked (not "previously attended"), and the discovery belongs to the individual (not to the therapist), "rather than interpretation from the therapist". Two relatives cause confusion. Viktor Frankl's logotherapy also uses a Socratic dialogue, but to help the client find meaning; logotherapy is an existential therapy (Chapter 6), not a "cognitive therapy emphasising irrational beliefs" (that description fits REBT). And Ellis's disputing can be Socratic in form but is far more persuasive and didactic.

The structure of a session and the course of therapy

Cognitive therapy is the most structured of the talking therapies. A typical course for depression runs 12 to 20 weekly sessions of about 50 minutes, often followed by a few "booster" sessions spaced weeks or months apart. The early sessions assess the problem, build the relationship, set goals, teach the cognitive model and build a case conceptualisation (the therapist's working map of how the client's core beliefs, rules, automatic thoughts and behaviours fit together; Judith Beck's cognitive conceptualisation diagram is a one-page version, and formulation in general is described in Chapter 2). Termination is planned from the first session. Each session follows a recognisable order, which Judith Beck set out as follows:

  1. Mood check, usually with a brief questionnaire such as the Beck Depression Inventory, plus a quick update on the week (and on medication, if any).
  2. Bridge from the previous session: what the client learned last time and what he remembers of it.
  3. Agenda setting: client and therapist agree on the two or three problems the session will tackle.
  4. Homework review: what the client tried, what happened and what he concluded.
  5. Discussion of agenda items, using the techniques described below, with frequent summaries.
  6. New homework, agreed together and arising directly from the session's work.
  7. Final summary and feedback: the client summarises the main points and says what was helpful or unhelpful, including anything the therapist said that upset him.

The structure is not bureaucracy. Depressed people find it hard to concentrate and easy to feel overwhelmed, and a predictable structure makes the hour productive. Asking for feedback at the end protects the alliance, and homework carries the work into the 167 hours of the week that the client spends outside the room.

Progress is measured throughout, usually with Beck's own scales, which are described fully in the Assessment book. The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI, 1961; revised as the BDI-II in 1996) has 21 items, each with four statements of increasing severity. The Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI, 1988) also has 21 items. The Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS, 1974) has 20 true-or-false items about the future. All three are self-report measures: the client reads the items and rates himself. They contrast with clinician-rated (observer-rated) scales such as the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (1960) and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (1959) of the British psychiatrist Max Hamilton (1912 to 1988), which a trained clinician completes after an interview.

Exam trap

"Which is a clinician-rated questionnaire: BDI, BAI, Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale, both BAI and Hamilton?" Only the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale. The BDI and BAI are self-report, so "both" is wrong. A useful memory hook: Beck's scales are filled in by the patient; Hamilton's by the clinician.

Clinical link

Beck's emphasis on the future part of the triad had a practical payoff. In a ten-year follow-up of patients hospitalised with suicidal thoughts, Beck and his colleagues (1985) found that hopelessness, measured with the BHS, predicted who would later die by suicide better than the severity of depression did. Hopelessness is therefore something a cognitive therapist asks about directly and early. In any risk assessment, risk is judged highest when several markers converge: frequent suicidal ideas, a specific plan, a highly lethal method, previous attempts and a family history of suicide (suicide risk assessment and crisis work are covered in Chapter 9).

Working with automatic thoughts

The first skill clients learn is to catch automatic thoughts. Because people notice their feelings more than their thoughts, the therapist uses the feeling as the signal. Whenever the client's mood shifts in the session (his face falls, his voice tightens), the therapist asks the key question of cognitive therapy: "What was going through your mind just then?" Between sessions the client does the same thing on paper.

The paper is the dysfunctional thought record (DTR), introduced by Beck and his colleagues in 1979 as the "Daily Record of Dysfunctional Thoughts". It is a sheet of columns. The client writes down the situation (what happened, where, when), the emotion and its intensity (0 to 100), the automatic thought and how strongly he believed it (0 to 100 percent), then, with practice, a rational (or balanced) response and finally the outcome, re-rating the original belief and emotion. Later versions, such as the seven-column record in Dennis Greenberger and Christine Padesky's self-help book Mind Over Mood (1995), split the middle into "evidence for the hot thought" and "evidence against it". Its purpose, in a phrase examiners use, is to identify, examine and restructure automatic negative thoughts. Table 5.4 shows a completed example.

A thought record is used to identify, examine and restructure automatic negative thoughts. It is not used to record dreams for symbolic interpretation (psychoanalysis), not to measure galvanic skin response (the change in the skin's electrical conductance with arousal, used in biofeedback and lie detection), and not to track medication side effects.

Once a thought is on paper, several techniques help the client examine it. Examining the evidence lists the facts for and against the thought, separating facts from interpretations. Generating alternative explanations asks what else could account for the event (the friend who did not greet you may not have seen you). The double-standard technique asks what the client would say to a close friend in the same situation, since most people are far kinder to friends than to themselves. Identifying the distortion by name ("That sounds like fortune telling") gives the client a quick way to step back. And two techniques have names of their own.

decatastrophising, sometimes called the "what if" technique, is used when the client fears an outcome. The therapist asks, in effect: What is the worst that could realistically happen? How likely is it? What is the best that could happen, and the most likely? And if the worst did happen, how would you cope, and would it still matter in five years? Asked properly, the questions shrink a catastrophe to a problem. A student terrified of failing the entrance examination may discover that the worst realistic outcome is a year's delay and a second attempt, which is painful but survivable.

reattribution is used when the client takes too much of the blame for an event. The therapist helps him list everyone and everything that contributed and assign each a fair share, often by drawing a responsibility pie. A man who blames himself entirely for his son's failing marks may end up with slices for the son's own effort, a change of school, a long illness, a strict new teacher and, yes, a modest slice for himself. Reattribution targets personalisation; it does not try to make the client blameless, only fairly blamed.

For dichotomous thinking, the continuum technique replaces the two boxes with a line from 0 to 100. A perfectionist who thinks she is "a complete failure" is asked who would sit at 0 (someone who never tried at all?) and who at 100, and then where she herself belongs. She rarely puts herself at 0. Finally, balanced responses that work are often written on small coping cards that the client carries and reads when the old thought returns.

Behavioural techniques in cognitive therapy

Cognitive therapy is not only talk. Beck used behavioural methods from the start, especially with severely depressed patients who were too slowed down to do much thinking work. The difference from pure behaviour therapy (Chapter 4) is the purpose: in cognitive therapy, behaviour change is used above all to produce evidence that tests a belief.

activity scheduling begins with monitoring: the client records what he does each hour for a week. Each activity is then rated for mastery (the sense of accomplishment, 0 to 10) and pleasure (enjoyment, 0 to 10). The record usually shows two things the depressed client did not believe: that he does do things, and that some activities lift his mood more than others. The next step is to plan the coming week, scheduling more of the activities that bring mastery or pleasure. A client who believes "I can't do anything" and "nothing helps" now holds a sheet of paper that says otherwise.

graded task assignment breaks a daunting task into small, manageable steps, arranged from easiest to hardest, so that the client succeeds early and often. A student who cannot face "revising for the exam" may start with "open the clinical psychology notes and read one page", then "read one section and do five questions", and so on. Each completed step is evidence against "I can't", and the growing sense of mastery is exactly what Bandura called a mastery experience (Section 5.1).

behavioural experiments are planned activities designed to test a specific belief in real life. The client states the prediction and how strongly he believes it ("If I ask a question in class, people will laugh at me: 80 percent"), designs a test, carries it out, records what actually happened, and re-rates the belief. Behavioural experiments are often more powerful than any amount of discussion, because the new evidence is lived rather than argued. They differ from exposure (Chapter 4) in their rationale: exposure aims mainly at fear reduction through repeated contact, whereas an experiment aims at disconfirming a prediction, and a single well-designed experiment can be enough. Role-play (rehearsing a difficult conversation in the session) and cognitive rehearsal (imagining each step of a task in detail before doing it, so that obstacles can be anticipated) complete the behavioural toolkit.

Reaching the deeper beliefs

Automatic thoughts are the way in, but lasting change usually requires work on the intermediate and core beliefs underneath. The standard way down is the downward arrow technique, described by David Burns (1980), who called it the vertical arrow technique. Instead of disputing an automatic thought, the therapist provisionally accepts it and asks, again and again: "Suppose that were true. What would it mean to you? Why would that be so upsetting?" Each answer is written below the last, with an arrow pointing down, until the client reaches a belief that cannot be pushed further. That is usually the core belief. Push Kavya's thought through it:

"She thinks my presentation was poor." And if she did, what would that mean? "That she'll think I'm not good enough for clinical work." And if she thought that? "That she'd be right. I'm not good enough." And if you're not good enough? "Then I'm a fraud. Sooner or later everyone will find out I'm incompetent."

Four steps have taken her from a frown at a seminar to "I am incompetent": a helpless core belief, and the real target of therapy.

Exam trap

The downward arrow technique is used to uncover the underlying core belief by repeatedly asking "If that were true, what would it mean?" It is not used to identify behavioural antecedents (that is functional analysis, Chapter 4), not to rate symptom intensity from 0 to 10 (that is the SUDS, subjective units of distress, used in exposure), and not to construct an exposure hierarchy (Wolpe's systematic desensitisation, Chapter 4).

Core beliefs change slowly, because the schema filters out disconfirming evidence. Therapists therefore work on them deliberately over many weeks. With a positive data log, the client records every day the small pieces of evidence that do not fit the old belief, which the schema would otherwise let slip. With a historical test of the schema, he reviews his life period by period, looking for evidence for and against "I am incompetent" at each age. He may be asked to act "as if" he held a new, more balanced belief, and to see what happens. Beck and his colleagues described three degrees of success. The fullest is schema restructuring, in which the old belief is replaced. More common is schema modification, in which it is softened and made more flexible. The most limited is schema reinterpretation, in which the belief stays but the person learns to live with it in a less harmful way (a perfectionist who keeps her standards but finds a job, such as quality inspection, where they are an asset).

Beck and Ellis compared

You are now in a position to compare the two founders, and examiners love the comparison. Begin with what they share. Both Beck and Ellis hold that emotional disturbance arises largely from the way people think about events, rather than from the events themselves; both trace the idea to the Stoics; both are active, directive, present-focused and brief; both use homework and behavioural assignments; and both are therefore counted as founders of the cognitive, or cognitive-behavioural, tradition. Neither holds that behaviour is controlled only by reinforcement (Skinner), that unconscious sexual drives determine behaviour (Freud), or that the therapeutic relationship alone produces change (Rogers).

The differences are of emphasis, but they are real. Ellis, a psychologist with a philosopher's temperament, believed that one root process, demandingness, underlies nearly all disturbance, and he set out to persuade clients out of it, vigorously and sometimes bluntly. Beck, a psychiatrist with a researcher's temperament, believed that each disorder has its own characteristic content (loss in depression, danger in anxiety; Section 5.6) and set out to help clients test their thoughts like hypotheses. Ellis calls the problematic beliefs irrational: absolutist and illogical. Beck calls them distorted or dysfunctional: inaccurate and unhelpful. In a published exchange, Padesky and Beck (2003) described the difference as one between science and philosophy: cognitive therapy tests beliefs empirically, whereas REBT disputes them philosophically. Ellis replied that REBT was empirical too. Table 5.5 sets out the contrasts.

Beck's therapy was developed primarily for depression, and only later extended to anxiety disorders, personality disorders, substance use, OCD and schizophrenia.

5.5 Coping skills, problem solving and personal constructsLower yield · 1 Q

Ellis and Beck both started from the same diagnosis: the client believes something false or rigid, and the belief must be changed. But think of a person who is not so much mistaken as unequipped. A child who rushes every sum and makes careless errors does not hold an irrational belief about arithmetic; he has never learned to slow himself down. A woman whose husband has lost his job, whose mother-in-law is ill and whose loan instalments are due is not distorting anything; she has more problems than she knows how to handle. For people like these, a second group of cognitive-behavioural therapists asked a more practical question. What skills does this person lack, and how can they be taught?

Keith Dobson, whose definition of CBT you met in Section 5.1, follows Michael Mahoney and Diane Arnkoff (1978) in sorting the cognitive-behavioural therapies into three families. The cognitive restructuring therapies (Ellis's REBT and Beck's cognitive therapy) replace faulty beliefs with better ones. The coping skills therapies (Donald Meichenbaum's self-instructional training and stress inoculation training) teach people what to do and say to themselves in a difficult situation. The problem-solving therapies (D'Zurilla and Goldfried's problem-solving therapy) teach a general method for working through problems of living. This section covers the second and third families, then two approaches that fit no family neatly: Arnold Lazarus's multimodal therapy, which sets out to leave no part of a person's life unexamined, and George Kelly's much older fixed-role therapy, which asks a client to spend two weeks living as somebody else.

Meichenbaum and self-instructional training

In the 1960s a graduate student at the University of Illinois named Donald Meichenbaum (born 1940) was running an operant conditioning study with hospitalised patients with schizophrenia. He reinforced them for "healthy talk": coherent, relevant answers in an interview. The method worked. But, as Meichenbaum later told the story, something in the follow-up interviews caught his attention. Some of the patients were quietly repeating the experimenter's instruction to themselves: "Give healthy talk; be coherent and relevant." The reinforcement had come from outside, but these patients had turned it into an instruction that came from inside, and they seemed to do best. What if, Meichenbaum wondered, people could be taught directly what to say to themselves?

The idea had a respectable pedigree. The Soviet psychologists Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (1896 to 1934) and Alexander Romanovich Luria (1902 to 1977) had described how children gain voluntary control over their behaviour through speech: first the speech of adults directs the child ("Put the red block on top"), then the child directs himself aloud, as any four-year-old building a tower does, and finally the speech goes underground as silent inner speech. Meichenbaum reasoned that in impulsive children this inner guide had never properly formed, and that it could be built deliberately. The result was self-instructional training: a method in which a therapist models a task while talking himself through it aloud, and the client then learns, in stages, to guide his own performance first with overt and finally with covert self-instructions.

Key study: teaching impulsive children to talk to themselves (Meichenbaum and Goodman, 1971)

Question. Can impulsive children learn to slow down and work carefully by being taught what to say to themselves? Method. Donald Meichenbaum and Joseph Goodman worked with second-grade children who had been placed in a special class because of hyperactivity and poor self-control. Some of the children received a few short sessions of self-instructional training on tasks such as copying line patterns and colouring figures within boundaries; others spent the same time on the same tasks without the self-instructions, or were simply assessed. Result. The trained children improved more than the controls on tests of planning and impulsivity, including the Porteus Maze and Jerome Kagan's Matching Familiar Figures Test, on which they began to take longer before answering (a sign of reflection rather than impulsive guessing), and the gains were still present a month later. Conclusion. Self-talk can be taught, and taught self-talk can regulate behaviour. Criticism. The samples were small, and the gains on laboratory tasks did not show up clearly in the children's classroom behaviour. Generalisation from the training task to everyday life has remained the method's weak point, which is why later versions train self-instructions across many different tasks and settings.

Meichenbaum and Goodman's training ran through five steps, and examiners like to ask for them in order:

  1. Cognitive modelling. The adult performs the task while talking to himself aloud, and the child watches and listens.
  2. Overt external guidance. The child performs the same task while the adult speaks the instructions aloud.
  3. Overt self-guidance. The child performs the task while speaking the instructions aloud to himself.
  4. Faded overt self-guidance. The child whispers the instructions while performing the task.
  5. Covert self-instruction. The child performs the task while guiding himself with silent inner speech.

Notice that the five steps recreate, in a few sessions, Vygotsky and Luria's sequence from the speech of others, to one's own speech aloud, to inner speech. The content of the self-talk matters as much as its form. Meichenbaum's model monologue for a line-copying task ran roughly: "Okay, what is it I have to do? Copy the picture. Go slowly and be careful. Draw the line down, good; now to the right. I'm doing fine so far. Oops, I was supposed to go down. That's okay; just erase it carefully. Finished. I did it." Inside that small speech are five skills: defining the problem, focusing attention and guiding the response, self-reinforcement ("good, I'm doing fine"), coping with errors ("that's okay") and self-evaluation at the end.

The error-coping step was deliberate. Meichenbaum found that a coping model, who makes mistakes and talks himself through them, teaches more than a mastery model, who performs flawlessly; his 1971 study of modelling found that coping models reduced avoidance more than mastery models did. A model who never stumbles shows the learner what success looks like, but not what to do on the way there when things go wrong.

Push a case through the method. Rohan, eight, at a school in Mysuru, races through his arithmetic worksheets, makes careless errors and crumples the page. His therapist works the first sum aloud ("What do I have to do? Add these. Slowly. First the ones: six and seven, thirteen; write three, carry one..."). Rohan does the next sum while she says the words, then says them himself aloud, then in a whisper, then silently. She has already modelled what to do with a mistake: "Oops. That's okay. I can rub it out and go slowly." Because self-talk learned on one worksheet tends to stay on that worksheet, the same instructions are then practised on reading, a board game and getting ready for school.

Self-instructional training was soon applied to test-anxious students (Meichenbaum, 1972), to patients with schizophrenia, whom Meichenbaum and Roy Cameron (1973) trained to instruct themselves to "be relevant and coherent", and to anger, pain and attention problems. Meichenbaum, who spent most of his career at the University of Waterloo in Ontario, Canada, drew the work together in Cognitive-Behavior Modification: An Integrative Approach (1977), the book that, as Section 5.1 noted, helped give the field its name. Its message was that what people say to themselves, their internal dialogue, is a behaviour that can be observed, modelled, rehearsed and changed.

Exam trap

Meichenbaum developed two methods, and some books abbreviate both as "SIT". Self-instructional training (Meichenbaum and Goodman, 1971) teaches a person what to say to himself to guide a task; it began with impulsive children. Stress inoculation training (described next) is a broader three-phase package for coping with stress. The five steps of self-instructional training run from the adult's speech to the child's own overt, then whispered, then covert speech: cognitive modelling, overt external guidance, overt self-guidance, faded overt self-guidance, covert self-instruction. The theory behind it, the internalisation of speech, comes from Vygotsky and Luria, not Piaget. And self-instruction is not "positive thinking" or thought stopping (Section 4.9): the statements guide action and cope with errors rather than praising oneself or blocking thoughts.

Stress inoculation training

How does a vaccine work? It exposes the body to a weakened form of a disease, a dose too small to make you ill but large enough to make the immune system build defences, so that the real disease is resisted when it comes. In the 1970s Meichenbaum borrowed the metaphor for psychology. (The American psychologist Irving Janis had earlier written of the "emotional inoculation" of surgical patients who were prepared in advance for the discomforts of their operations.) stress inoculation training (SIT) is a structured, three-phase cognitive-behavioural programme in which a person first learns to understand his stress, then acquires and rehearses a set of coping skills, and finally applies them to gradually increasing, manageable doses of the real stressor, so as to build resistance to future stress. Meichenbaum set it out fully in his book Stress Inoculation Training (1985).

SIT rests on a particular view of stress. Following the American psychologist Richard Stanley Lazarus (1922 to 2002), whose transactional model is described in Section 9.3, Meichenbaum treats stress as a transaction between person and situation: we feel stressed when we appraise the demands of a situation as exceeding our resources for coping. Change the appraisal, or strengthen the resources, and the stress changes too. That is why SIT is a package rather than a single technique. Figure 5.4 shows its three phases.

Phase 1: conceptualisation. The first phase is educational and collaborative. The therapist builds a relationship, gathers a detailed picture of the stress (often with a stress diary) and helps the client reconceptualise it: stress stops being a mysterious, overwhelming force and becomes a sequence of reactions that can be anticipated and a set of problems that can be worked on. A key step is to break a global stressor ("my job is killing me") into parts and sort them into those that can be changed (unrealistic deadlines, answering emails at midnight) and those that cannot (the company's restructuring). Changeable parts call for problem-focused coping, acting on the situation; unchangeable ones call for emotion-focused coping, managing one's reactions. Both terms come from Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman.

Phase 2: skills acquisition and rehearsal. In the second phase the client learns and practises the coping skills his particular stress requires. Typical skills include relaxation and slow breathing (Section 4.8), cognitive restructuring of stress-producing thoughts, problem solving (described below), assertiveness, and the use of coping self-statements: brief, task-focused instructions a person gives himself at each stage of a stressful encounter, developed from self-instructional training. Meichenbaum divided a stressful encounter into four stages and suggested statements for each (Table 5.6). The skills are rehearsed first in the safety of the session, in imagery and in role play.

The client imagines coping, then role-plays it, then meets the stressor in real life in graded steps. Because setbacks are certain, relapse prevention (G. Crediting improvement to his own efforts raises his self-efficacy (Section 5.1), and follow-up and booster sessions complete the programme.

Because it is a framework rather than a fixed protocol, SIT has been applied very widely. Raymond W. Novaco adapted it for anger in 1975, rewriting the four stages as preparing for a provocation, meeting it, coping with arousal and reflecting afterwards. It has been used to prepare patients for surgery, to treat test anxiety and chronic pain, to support carers, and to prepare soldiers, police officers, nurses and athletes for high-stress work before the stress arrives (prevention as well as treatment); in the late 1970s and 1980s it was among the first structured treatments offered to survivors of rape. A meta-analysis of 37 studies by Saunders, Driskell, Johnston and Salas (1996) found that it reduced performance anxiety and state anxiety and improved performance under stress.

Key study: stress inoculation versus prolonged exposure after rape (Foa, Rothbaum, Riggs and Murdock, 1991)

Which helps survivors of rape with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) more: learning to cope with the fear, or confronting the memory? Edna B. Foa and her colleagues in Philadelphia randomly assigned 45 women to nine sessions of stress inoculation training, prolonged exposure (repeatedly reliving the assault in imagination, Section 4.3), supportive counselling or a waiting list. At the end of treatment SIT had produced the largest improvement; at follow-up some three months later prolonged exposure had pulled ahead. Results like these explain why exposure-based and cognitive trauma therapies (Section 5.6) became the first-line treatments for PTSD, while SIT's coping skills survive as components inside them.

Exam trap

The three phases of stress inoculation training, in order, are conceptualisation (education), skills acquisition and rehearsal, and application and follow-through. A distractor may offer "relaxation, desensitisation, flooding" or Selye's "alarm, resistance, exhaustion" (the general adaptation syndrome, a description of the stress response, not a therapy). The metaphor is vaccination: small, manageable doses of stress, after skills have been learned. SIT is therefore not flooding, which begins at the top of the fear hierarchy (Section 4.3). Above all, do not confuse the two Lazaruses. Richard Lazarus gave SIT its theory of stress (appraisal and coping); Arnold Lazarus created multimodal therapy (described below).

Problem-solving therapy

Many people who seek help are not distorted thinkers; they are overwhelmed by debts, a failing marriage, a sick parent. In 1971 Thomas J. D'Zurilla and Marvin R. Goldfried, at the State University of New York at Stony Brook, published "Problem solving and behavior modification" in the Journal of Abnormal Psychology. Much abnormal behaviour, they argued, is better understood as ineffective behaviour, the result of failing to solve the problems life presents, and teaching a general problem-solving strategy might help people with problems they had not yet met. That is the basis of problem-solving therapy (PST): a cognitive-behavioural treatment that trains clients in a systematic method for identifying problems, generating and choosing among solutions and carrying them out, so as to reduce distress and improve functioning. A problem, in this approach, is any situation that demands a response for which no effective response is immediately apparent; a solution is a coping response that changes the situation, one's emotional reaction to it, or both.

D'Zurilla and Goldfried's original model had five stages: a general orientation to problems; problem definition and formulation; generation of alternatives; decision making; and verification. Over the following decades D'Zurilla worked with Arthur M. Nezu and Christine Maguth Nezu, who tested the therapy with depressed adults and refined the model. In its mature form it separates two partly independent processes.

The first is problem orientation: a person's general attitude to problems and to his own ability to solve them. A positive orientation sees a problem as a challenge, believes it can be solved, trusts one's ability and accepts that solving it takes time and effort. A negative orientation sees problems as threats, doubts one's ability and reacts with frustration. A negative orientation is strongly associated with depression, anxiety and suicidal thinking, so modern PST begins here: before a client can solve problems, he must stop treating every problem as a catastrophe and himself as helpless.

The second process is the person's problem-solving style. The effective style is rational problem solving, which is deliberate and systematic and uses four skills (the remaining four stages of the original model). Two styles are ineffective: the impulsive or careless style, which grabs the first solution that comes to mind, and the avoidant style, which procrastinates, waits for the problem to go away or hands it to someone else. The four skills are taught as steps:

  1. Problem definition and formulation. Gather the facts, separate them from assumptions, state the problem concretely and set a realistic goal. "My life is a mess" cannot be solved; "I have no free time on weekday evenings" can.
  2. Generation of alternative solutions. Produce as many possible solutions as possible, using the rules of brainstorming, popularised by the American advertising executive Alex Osborn and adapted by D'Zurilla and Nezu: the quantity principle (the more ideas, the more good ones), the deferment-of-judgement principle (no criticism while ideas are being produced) and the variety principle (ideas of many different kinds, not twenty versions of one).
  3. Decision making. Weigh the likely consequences of each option, short term and long term, for oneself and for others, and choose the best one or a combination.
  4. Solution implementation and verification. Carry the solution out, monitor what happens and compare the result with the goal. If it matches, the client rewards himself; if it does not, he returns to an earlier step.

Here is the method at work. Lakshmi, a 34-year-old schoolteacher in Mysuru, has become low and tearful: her husband lost his job three months ago, her mother-in-law has had a stroke and needs help each evening, and the family is behind on a loan. Her therapist first works on orientation (problems are a normal part of life and can be tackled one at a time; feeling overwhelmed is a signal to start, not a sign of weakness). Lakshmi chooses one problem, no time in the evenings to prepare her lessons, and defines the goal concretely: "two free hours on at least four evenings a week". She brainstorms without judging (her husband could take over the evening care while he is between jobs; she could prepare lessons in the school's free period; a paid attendant; help from her sister-in-law; a transfer nearer home), weighs the options and chooses the first two together. Two weeks later three evenings are free, so she returns to her list for the fourth. Nothing in this work disputes a belief or names a distortion; her mood lifts because her life is becoming manageable, and because she is regaining the sense that she can manage it.

The evidence for PST is solid, particularly for depression. Nezu (1986) found it more effective than a waiting list and than a less structured problem-focused therapy. In Oxford, Laurence Mynors-Wallis and his colleagues (1995) found six sessions of PST, delivered in primary care by a psychiatrist and trained general practitioners, as effective for major depression as the antidepressant amitriptyline and more effective than a placebo pill. Because its steps are simple, PST also travels well. In Zimbabwe the Friendship Bench programme led by the psychiatrist Dixon Chibanda trained lay health workers, many of them older women, to deliver it on benches outside clinics, and a randomised trial published in 2016 found that it reduced depressive symptoms more than enhanced usual care. Problem solving also appears inside many other treatments: stress inoculation training, behavioural family management for schizophrenia (Section 7.2) and dialectical behaviour therapy (Section 5.8).

Indian context

India has far too few clinical psychologists for its population, so interventions that trained lay counsellors can deliver matter a great deal, and problem solving is one of the main candidates. The Goa-based organisation Sangath, in its PRIDE programme for adolescents, has developed and tested a brief problem-solving intervention delivered by lay counsellors to students in low-income urban schools in New Delhi.

Exam trap

Problem-solving therapy was introduced by D'Zurilla and Goldfried (1971) and developed with Arthur and Christine Nezu. Its two components are problem orientation (attitude) and problem-solving style or skills; the rational style has four steps: definition and formulation, generation of alternatives, decision making, implementation and verification. In brainstorming, judgement is deferred, not exercised. Do not confuse PST with solution-focused brief therapy (Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg, Section 6.8), which deliberately spends little time analysing the problem and instead builds on exceptions, using devices such as the miracle question. A third relative, George Spivack and Myrna Shure's interpersonal cognitive problem-solving programme for children ("I Can Problem Solve"), teaches young children to think of alternative solutions and consequences in conflicts with others.

Arnold Lazarus and multimodal therapy

You met Arnold Allan Lazarus (1932 to 2013) in Chapter 4 as one of Joseph Wolpe's students in Johannesburg and one of the first people to use the term "behaviour therapy" in print (1958). After moving to the United States, where he spent most of his career at Rutgers University, Lazarus did something therapists rarely do: he followed up his own clients. Many who had done well with behaviour therapy had relapsed, and he concluded that the reason was narrowness: a treatment that dealt with a client's avoidance but not his self-critical thoughts, or his anxiety but not his marriage, left gaps through which the problem could return. He argued for a "broad-spectrum" behaviour therapy in Behavior Therapy and Beyond (1971), and turned it into a system in Multimodal Behavior Therapy (1976) and The Practice of Multimodal Therapy (1981).

multimodal therapy is a systematic, comprehensive approach in which the therapist assesses a client's problems across seven interacting modalities of personality and then selects, for each problem in each modality, the techniques with the best evidence, whatever their theoretical origin. The seven modalities form the acronym BASIC ID (often written BASIC I.D.):

  • B, behaviour: observable acts, habits and reactions, including doing too much or too little of something.
  • A, affect: emotions and moods, such as anxiety, anger, guilt and depression.
  • S, sensation: what is seen, heard, touched, tasted and smelled, including unpleasant bodily sensations such as tension, pain, dizziness and palpitations.
  • I, imagery: mental pictures, fantasies, dreams and memories, including one's self-image and body image.
  • C, cognition: thoughts, beliefs, values, attitudes and self-statements.
  • I, interpersonal relationships: how the person relates to family, friends, colleagues and partners.
  • D, drugs and biology: physical health, sleep, diet, exercise, substance use and medication. Lazarus used "D" rather than "B" so that the acronym would read as "basic identity", but he stressed that the category covers the whole biological side of the person, of which drugs are only one part.

Notice that affect is the one modality that cannot be changed directly. Nobody can simply decide to feel less anxious; but one can change the behaviours, sensations, images, thoughts, relationships and biology that produce the anxiety. Lazarus called himself an "authentic chameleon", changing his style to suit each client while remaining genuine.

Multimodal therapy is the best-known example of technical eclecticism, a term Lazarus introduced in 1967: borrowing techniques from any school when evidence shows they work, without adopting that school's theory. Lazarus would borrow the empty chair from Gestalt therapy (Section 6.3) without accepting Gestalt theory; his own theory remained social and cognitive learning theory, much influenced by Bandura. He was critical of theoretical integration, which tries to blend the theories themselves (as Paul Wachtel combined psychodynamic and behavioural ideas), warning that mixing incompatible theories breeds confusion. The wider psychotherapy integration movement, in which Lazarus was a founding figure, is described in Section 8.11.

Multimodal therapy's strengths are thoroughness and flexibility: a multimodal assessment rarely misses a problem, and treatment is tailored to the person rather than to the diagnosis. The weakness is the other side of the coin. Because every treatment is different, multimodal therapy is hard to test as such in a controlled trial, and its evidence rests largely on the evidence for the techniques it borrows.

Exam trap

BASIC ID = Behaviour, Affect, Sensation, Imagery, Cognition, Interpersonal relationships, Drugs and biology. Common wrong expansions: S as "self" or "social", the first I as "insight", D as "defences", "dreams" or "desensitisation". Multimodal therapy belongs to Arnold Lazarus, not to Richard Lazarus (stress and appraisal), not to Wolpe (systematic desensitisation and SUDS) and not to Jon Kabat-Zinn (mindfulness-based stress reduction, Section 5.9). Technical eclecticism borrows techniques but not theories; theoretical integration combines theories. One last trap: the American "Multimodal Treatment Study of Children with ADHD" (the MTA study of the 1990s) compared medication and behavioural treatment for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and has nothing to do with Lazarus.

George Kelly and fixed-role therapy

The oldest approach in this section is also the most unusual. George Kelly, whom you met in Section 5.1, trained in physiological psychology but began his career at Fort Hays Kansas State College during the depression of the 1930s, when there was no money for laboratory research. So he set up travelling clinics for schools across rural Kansas and taught himself therapy, largely by assuming that each client was as much a scientist as himself. He later taught at Ohio State University for nineteen years (he replaced Carl Rogers there), published the two volumes of The Psychology of Personal Constructs in 1955, and died in 1967, shortly after moving to Brandeis University. His personality theory belongs to the Personality book; what matters here is the therapy it produced.

Kelly's starting point was the person as a scientist, who makes sense of the world by building theories and testing them against experience. The units of these theories are personal constructs: bipolar dimensions, such as kind versus selfish or clever versus dull, through which a person interprets events and predicts what will happen next. His fundamental postulate states that "a person's processes are psychologically channelized by the ways in which he anticipates events". Because constructs are tools rather than truths, Kelly insisted on constructive alternativism: any event can be construed in more than one way, so people are prisoners not of their circumstances but only of their current interpretations. Psychological trouble arises when a person keeps using a construct system that keeps failing to predict events, instead of revising it.

How do you find out what a person's constructs are? Kelly's answer was disarmingly simple: "If you don't know what is going on in a person's mind, ask him; he may tell you!" Besides this credulous attitude to the client's own account, he used the Role Construct Repertory Test (the Rep test, described in the Assessment book) and the self-characterisation sketch, in which the client writes about himself in the third person: "I want you to write a character sketch of [client's name] just as if he were the principal character in a play. Write it as it might be written by a friend who knew him very intimately and very sympathetically, perhaps better than anyone ever really could know him."

The self-characterisation is the first step of fixed-role therapy: Kelly's method in which the therapist writes a sketch of a new, fictional character, built around constructs different from the client's own, and the client lives as that character for about two weeks, in order to discover that he is free to construe himself, and to act, differently. Kelly is said to have taken the idea from watching a friend who acted in a college play and gradually began to behave offstage like the character he played onstage (Kelly himself had coached drama at a junior college in Iowa). The procedure has four steps.

  1. The self-characterisation. The client writes his sketch, and the therapist studies it for the constructs that are trapping him.
  2. The fixed-role sketch. The therapist (Kelly often worked with a panel of colleagues) writes a sketch of a new character with a new name. The sketch is not an ideal self and not simply the client's opposite. Turning a shy man into a bold one would only move him from one end of the same construct to the other, a shift that personal construct therapists call "slot rattling". Instead the sketch introduces a new theme, a different dimension along which the character makes sense of people.
  3. The acceptance check. The client reads the sketch and is asked whether this sounds like a person he would like to know and could imagine being. If not, it is revised.
  4. The enactment. For about two weeks the client is asked to act, think and talk as the character would, as though his old self had gone on holiday. He meets the therapist frequently, often several times a week, to rehearse scenes with his employer, friends and family and to review what happened. At the end the role is dropped, and the client decides what, if anything, to keep.

The genius of the method lies in the word "role". Because the client is only playing a part, a failure belongs to the character, not to him, so he can risk behaviour his anxiety would otherwise forbid; and because the experiment is real, other people's responses are real data. Suresh, a 24-year-old engineering graduate in Chennai, describes himself as "a quiet, dutiful boy who never troubles anybody", and his central construct, "troubling others versus being good", keeps him silent and lonely. His therapist does not write him a bold, assertive character, which would only flip the construct. She writes "Vikram", a man fascinated by what makes other people tick, who asks questions out of curiosity and listens to the answers. As Vikram, Suresh asks a colleague how she chose engineering and the tea-stall owner about his village, and he discovers that people enjoy being asked. The aim was never to turn Suresh into Vikram, but to show him that the person he took himself to be was a construction, and constructions can be revised.

Fixed-role therapy has little controlled evidence and is rarely practised in its original form, but its influence is large. Kelly anticipated the central claim of cognitive therapy, his constructs closely resemble Beck's schemas, and his client-as-scientist leads straight to collaborative empiricism and behavioural experiments (Section 5.4). 5.6 Disorder-specific cognitive models: panic, social anxiety, trauma and psychosisHigh yield · 7 Qs

Section 5.4 noted a deep difference between Beck and Ellis: Ellis traced almost all disturbance to one process, demandingness, whereas Beck believed that each disorder has its own characteristic content. Beck's idea proved one of the most productive in the history of psychotherapy. From the 1980s it led a group of researchers, most of them at the University of Oxford, to build a precise cognitive model for each anxiety disorder, test it in the laboratory, and design a therapy aimed at exactly the processes the model said were keeping the disorder alive. This section shows how, using four examples: panic, social anxiety, trauma and psychosis.

Cognitive specificity and the logic of disorder-specific models

In Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders (1976), Beck proposed the cognitive specificity hypothesis (or content-specificity hypothesis): each emotional disorder is marked by its own typical theme of thought. Depression is about loss and failure, anxiety about danger, hypomania about gain. Table 5.9 gives Beck's "cognitive profiles". When Beck and his colleagues (1987) gave patients a checklist of thoughts, depressed patients reported more thoughts of loss and failure and anxious patients more thoughts of danger; the separation is not perfect, since many patients are both anxious and depressed, but it is real.

Knowing the theme is not enough to treat the disorder, however. Early cognitive therapies for anxiety applied Beck's general method and got moderate results. The Oxford group, led by David M. Clark and Paul M. Salkovskis and later joined by Anke Ehlers, Adrian Wells (whose metacognitive therapy is in Section 5.10) and others, asked a sharper question. A man has had three hundred panic attacks; in every one of them he was sure he was about to have a heart attack, and he has never had one. Why has being wrong three hundred times not corrected his belief? Something must be stopping the evidence from getting in.

That question gives disorder-specific models their shape. Each model identifies the key appraisal (the specific catastrophe feared: a heart attack in panic, humiliation in social anxiety, current danger in PTSD), then the maintaining processes that stop the appraisal from being disconfirmed, and then designs a therapy, usually built on behavioural experiments, to reverse each maintaining process in turn. Notice the emphasis on maintenance rather than origin. How a disorder began matters, but the past cannot be changed, whereas the vicious circles that keep it going today can be broken. The maintaining process found in almost every model is the subject of the next subsection.

Safety behaviours: why fears survive

There is an old joke that CBT trainers like to tell. A man on a train keeps snapping his fingers. Asked why, he says, "To keep the tigers away." "But there are no tigers on this train." "Exactly. It works." The joke captures the central idea of Salkovskis's influential 1991 paper on the maintenance of anxiety. safety behaviours (or safety-seeking behaviours) are actions a person takes in, or in anticipation of, a feared situation in order to prevent the feared catastrophe. A man who fears fainting sits down or grips something solid when dizzy; a woman who fears heart failure carries her medicines everywhere and keeps near the exit; a student who fears sounding stupid rehearses every sentence before speaking and avoids eye contact.

Safety behaviours are not the same as avoidance. The avoider stays away from the situation altogether; the person using safety behaviours enters it, but with protection. That is exactly why they are so insidious. They keep anxiety alive in three ways:

  • They prevent disconfirmation. When the catastrophe does not happen, the person credits the safety behaviour rather than the harmlessness of the situation: "I didn't faint, but only because I held on to the trolley." Each "narrow escape" strengthens the belief instead of weakening it. This is the misattribution in the tiger joke.
  • They can produce the very symptoms that are feared. Gripping a trolley tightly makes the arms shake; holding a glass with rigid fingers to stop it trembling makes it tremble; breathing deeply to "get enough air" leads to over-breathing, which produces dizziness and tingling.
  • They can make the feared outcome more likely. The student who avoids eye contact and rehearses his sentences comes across as cold and distracted, so the other students respond less warmly, which he takes as proof that they dislike him.

Safety behaviours also keep attention fixed on the threat, since a person cannot protect himself from a danger without monitoring it. And what counts as a safety behaviour depends on its function, not its form. Slow breathing used as a general way of calming down is a coping skill; slow breathing used in the belief that it is the only thing standing between the person and a heart attack is a safety behaviour.

Key study: dropping safety behaviours (Salkovskis, Clark, Hackmann, Wells and Gelder, 1999)

If safety behaviours prevent disconfirmation, exposure should work better when they are dropped. Salkovskis and his colleagues gave patients with panic disorder and agoraphobia brief exposure to feared situations such as shops and crowded streets. One group received the standard rationale of the time (stay until the anxiety fades); the other was told to treat the situation as a test of whether the catastrophe would really happen, and to drop its safety behaviours (stop holding on, walk without a companion, stand rather than sit). With the same amount of exposure, the second group showed much greater reductions in anxiety and in belief in the feared catastrophe. Wells, Clark, Salkovskis and colleagues (1995) found the same in social phobia after a single session. Stanley Rachman and his colleagues (2008) later argued that judicious safety behaviours can help a frightened client to start exposure at all, but for lasting change the protection has to go.

Exam trap

"A client engages in safety behaviours, such as gripping a shopping cart tightly when feeling dizzy in a store. In CBT for panic disorder, the therapist would likely..." work with the client to gradually drop these behaviours to test catastrophic beliefs (Real PYQ 2024). Not prescribe them to prevent fainting: the behaviour is what keeps the belief alive, and people having a panic attack almost never faint (see below). Not use them as a measure of insight: they are a maintaining process to be changed, not an index to be scored. And not explore the childhood origins of the need for safety, which is a psychodynamic aim; CBT works on what maintains the problem now. Remember also that safety behaviours differ from avoidance: the person is in the feared situation, but protected.

Panic: Clark's catastrophic misinterpretation model

Ramesh, a 38-year-old software engineer in Hyderabad, has been rushed to emergency departments four times in six months with a racing heart and the certainty that he was dying. Each time the electrocardiogram was normal. He no longer takes the metro, goes to the supermarket only with his wife, and grips the trolley with both hands when he feels light-headed. He has panic disorder (recurrent, unexpected panic attacks with persistent worry about further attacks) and is developing agoraphobia: fear and avoidance of places such as crowds, shops and public transport, from which escape might be difficult or help unavailable if panic struck (both are described in the Psychiatric Disorders book). Why does a man with a healthy heart keep believing it is failing?

In 1986 David Clark published a short paper, "A cognitive approach to panic", in the journal Behaviour Research and Therapy. Its central claim fits in one sentence: panic attacks result from the catastrophic misinterpretation of certain bodily sensations, that is, perceiving harmless sensations, mostly the ordinary sensations of anxiety, as signs of an immediate disaster. Palpitations are read as an impending heart attack, breathlessness as suffocation, dizziness as fainting or a stroke, and feelings of unreality (derealisation) or of being detached from oneself (depersonalisation) as the onset of madness or loss of control. Figure 5.5 shows how such a misinterpretation produces a vicious circle.

Follow Ramesh round the circle. In a crowded mall (an external trigger) he feels slightly apprehensive, and apprehension, like any anxiety, speeds up his heart and breathing. He notices his heart pounding and thinks, "My heart is going to fail." The threat now seems far greater, so his anxiety rises, his heart pounds harder and the interpretation seems confirmed; within a minute he is in a full panic attack. The trigger can also be internal: a skipped heartbeat, the light-headedness of standing up quickly, too much coffee, even a thought. That explains attacks that seem to come "out of the blue" (the trigger was a small sensation, misread before the person was aware of noticing it), and it explains attacks that wake people from sleep.

Between attacks, two processes keep the misinterpretation alive. The first is selective attention to the body: having been frightened by his heart, Ramesh monitors it constantly, and anyone who monitors his own heartbeat notices changes that others never register. The second is safety behaviours: each time the heart attack does not happen, he credits the trolley, his wife or his quick exit.

Clark's model sits beside two related ideas. In 1978 Alan Goldstein and Dianne Chambless described agoraphobia as, at heart, a fear of fear: a learned fear of the bodily sensations of anxiety. In 1985 Steven Reiss and Richard J. McNally introduced anxiety sensitivity, a lasting tendency to believe that the sensations of anxiety are harmful; people who score high on the Anxiety Sensitivity Index are more likely to develop panic attacks later. Anxiety sensitivity is the trait; catastrophic misinterpretation is what happens in the moment. David H. Barlow and Michelle Craske's panic control treatment, with its interoceptive exposure (Section 4.3), attacks the same circle from the behavioural side.

Key study: the illusion of control (Sanderson, Rapee and Barlow, 1989)

If panic depends on how sensations are interpreted, then changing the interpretation should change the panic, even when the sensations stay the same. William Sanderson, Ronald Rapee and David Barlow asked patients with panic disorder to breathe air enriched with 5.5 percent carbon dioxide, which reliably produces breathlessness, a racing heart and dizziness. All the patients were told that when a light came on they could reduce the carbon dioxide by turning a dial. For half of them the light came on; for the other half it never did. In fact the dial did nothing at all. The physical dose was identical, yet the patients who had no "control" reported more panic symptoms, more catastrophic thoughts and far more panic attacks than those who believed they could control the gas (the figures usually quoted are about 80 percent against about 20 percent, in groups of only ten patients each). The perception of control, a purely cognitive variable, made the difference between panicking and not panicking.

Clark's cognitive therapy for panic disorder, developed with Salkovskis and colleagues, follows directly from the model. It usually takes about 12 sessions, and it has four parts.

  1. Building the vicious circle. A recent attack is reviewed in slow motion ("What did you notice first? What went through your mind? What happened to the sensation then?") and the client's own version of Figure 5.5 is drawn. Seeing the circle on paper often reduces fear by itself.
  2. Verbal reattribution. The evidence is examined. Ramesh's heart has been tested four times; his attacks come when he is sitting still but never when he climbs the stairs, though a weak heart would be most likely to fail under exertion; and the sensations fade when he is distracted. Information helps too: fainting is caused by a sudden drop in blood pressure, whereas panic raises blood pressure, so a panicking person is one of the people least likely to faint (the exception is blood-injection-injury phobia, in which a drop in blood pressure really can cause fainting).
  3. Behavioural experiments. The client deliberately produces the feared sensations to discover their harmless cause: over-breathing for a minute or two, focusing on the body, even reading word pairs such as "palpitations: dying", which can bring on a mild attack in the clinic and shows him how powerful his interpretations are. Then, in the feared situations, he drops his safety behaviours: Ramesh lets go of the trolley when he feels dizzy, walks briskly round the shop instead, and does not collapse.
  4. Relapse prevention: planning how to respond to future sensations and setbacks.

The therapy works remarkably well. In a trial by Clark and his colleagues (1994), patients with panic disorder were randomly assigned to cognitive therapy, to applied relaxation (Lars-Göran Öst's method; Section 4.8), to the antidepressant imipramine or to a waiting list. At three months, when cognitive therapy ended, 86 percent of the cognitive therapy patients were free of panic (counting drop-outs as failures; close to 90 percent of those who completed it), compared with roughly half of those in the other two active treatments. Imipramine caught up while patients were still taking it, but at follow-up fifteen months after the start cognitive therapy was again ahead (76 percent panic-free, against under 50 percent for the others).

Exam trap

The cognitive model of panic belongs to David M. Clark (1986): panic is the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations. Do not confuse it with Barlow and Craske's panic control treatment (interoceptive exposure; Section 4.3), with Reiss and McNally's anxiety sensitivity (a lasting trait) or with Goldstein and Chambless's fear of fear. Treatment drops safety behaviours rather than prescribing them. And agoraphobia, when asked about, is fear of public or hard-to-escape places (crowds, shops, public transport, being outside the home alone), because escape might be difficult or help unavailable if panic struck. Nervousness in a lift is claustrophobia and fear of tall trees or buildings is a specific phobia (both are phobias of particular situations or objects), and hearing voices is a psychotic symptom, not a phobia at all.

Social anxiety: the Clark and Wells model

Social anxiety disorder (social phobia) is an intense, persistent fear of situations in which one might be scrutinised and judged, such as speaking in a group, eating in front of others or meeting strangers (the disorder itself is in the Psychiatric Disorders book). It poses a puzzle like the one in panic. A socially anxious person meets other people every day, and all that exposure should, by the logic of Chapter 4, extinguish the fear. Why doesn't it?

In 1995 David Clark and Adrian Wells answered with what has become the standard model. Entering a social situation activates three kinds of long-standing belief: excessively high standards ("I must always sound fluent and intelligent"; "I must never show anxiety"), conditional beliefs ("If I blush, people will think I'm weird") and unconditional negative beliefs about the self ("I'm boring", "I'm odd"). These make the situation look dangerous, and the moment social danger is perceived, attention turns inward. Figure 5.6 shows what happens next.

The centre of the model is processing of the self as a social object. The person shifts into self-focused attention, monitoring his own sensations, thoughts and behaviour at the expense of the conversation, and he uses this internal information to judge how he looks. He feels hot, so he "sees" himself bright red; he feels shaky, so he imagines his hands visibly trembling. Many socially anxious people report a vivid image of themselves as seen from outside (an observer perspective), and Ann Hackmann, Clark and Freda McManus (2000) found that such images are often linked to early memories of humiliation. The image feels like the truth, but it is built from feelings, not facts: emotional reasoning (Section 5.3) turned on the self.

Three other processes lock the circle. Safety behaviours (rehearsing sentences, speaking little, avoiding eye contact, holding a cup with both hands, covering a blush) prevent disconfirmation, increase self-focus and make the person seem aloof, so that others respond coolly and appear to confirm his fears. The symptoms of anxiety (blushing, trembling, the mind going blank) become further evidence of danger. And the processing extends in time: in anticipatory processing the person rehearses beforehand everything that might go wrong, and in post-event processing, the "post-mortem", he replays the encounter afterwards through his anxious feelings, so that even a conversation that went well is remembered as a failure. Here is the answer to the puzzle: socially anxious people meet others daily, but with attention turned inward and behaviour protected, they never collect the evidence that they were doing better than they thought. Ronald M. Rapee and Richard G. Heimberg (1997) proposed a similar model, stressing the person's mental representation of how he appears to an audience.

Clark's cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder targets each of these processes, over about 14 sessions:

  • An individual version of the model, drawn from a recent social situation.
  • A self-focus and safety behaviours experiment. Ananya, a 23-year-old trainee chartered accountant in Kolkata, has two short conversations with a stranger, once using her safety behaviours and focusing on herself, and once dropping them and attending to the other person. In the second she feels less anxious, believes she looks less anxious and, by the stranger's account, seems warmer: her "protection" was making things worse.
  • Video feedback. Ananya predicts in detail how she will look on the recording ("bright red, voice shaking"), then watches it as if she were watching a stranger. Most clients look far less anxious than they felt, which corrects the observer image directly.
  • Training in external attention, so that the client listens and responds instead of monitoring herself.
  • Behavioural experiments in real situations, including deliberate small "mistakes" (a long pause, spilling a little water) to test whether rejection really follows.
  • Work on anticipatory and post-event processing, and imagery rescripting of the early memories that feed the self-image.

The results are among the best for any anxiety disorder. Clark and his colleagues found the therapy more effective than fluoxetine combined with self-exposure (2003) and than exposure combined with applied relaxation (2006); in the 2006 trial 84 percent of the cognitive therapy patients no longer met the diagnostic criteria for social phobia at the end of treatment, against 42 percent of those given exposure and applied relaxation, and the difference held a year later. A trial by Ulrich Stangier, Clark and colleagues (2011) found it more effective than interpersonal psychotherapy, a network meta-analysis by Evan Mayo-Wilson and colleagues (2014) found individual CBT the most effective psychological treatment for social anxiety in adults, and the British National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends individual CBT based on the Clark and Wells or the Heimberg model as the first-line treatment.

Heimberg's own approach, cognitive-behavioural group therapy, treats about six clients together, combining exposure to simulated feared situations (a mock interview, a party conversation) with cognitive restructuring before and after, and homework; the group provides a ready-made audience. Earlier treatments relied on exposure alone, or on social skills training (Section 4.7), on the assumption that socially anxious people lack conversational skills. Most, it turned out, have adequate skills that anxiety and safety behaviours stop them using, so skills training is now kept for genuine deficits. A complete CBT package for social anxiety may therefore contain all three elements: exposure, social skills retraining where needed, and cognitive restructuring.

Exam trap

"Successful CBT treatments of social phobia include elements of exposure therapy, social skills retraining and cognitive restructuring" calls for all of the above (IHBAS 2020). The model to name is Clark and Wells (1995); its distinctive ideas are self-focused attention, the observer-perspective self-image, safety behaviours and anticipatory and post-event processing. Its distinctive technique is video feedback. Heimberg's version is the group treatment. Do not credit Clark and Wells to Beck, and do not confuse Adrian Wells's work here with his later metacognitive therapy for worry (Section 5.10).

Trauma: cognitive models of post-traumatic stress

Most people who live through a terrible event (a road accident, an assault, a flood, a bomb blast) have intrusive memories, nightmares and jumpiness for some weeks, and most of them recover. A minority develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), in which re-experiencing, avoidance and hyperarousal persist for months or years (the disorder itself is in the Psychiatric Disorders book). What keeps them ill?

Anke Ehlers and David Clark's answer (2000) begins with a paradox: PTSD is an anxiety-related disorder, and anxiety concerns future threat, yet the trauma is in the past. PTSD becomes persistent, they proposed, when the trauma is processed in a way that produces a sense of serious current threat. Two things create it. The first is excessively negative appraisals of the trauma or its consequences: "Nowhere is safe"; "It was my fault"; "I'm going mad, because I can't stop the flashbacks"; "My life is ruined." The second is the nature of the trauma memory: poorly elaborated and poorly integrated into ordinary autobiographical memory, stored as vivid sensory fragments without the context of time, place and outcome ("and then the ambulance came, and I survived"). Such a memory is easily triggered by cues that merely resemble the trauma (a smell, a sound) and is re-experienced with a sense of "nowness", as though the event were happening again. The disorder is maintained by the person's ways of coping (suppressing thoughts of the trauma, ruminating, avoiding reminders, staying on guard, drinking and other safety behaviours), each of which stops the memory from being elaborated and the appraisals from being corrected.

The resulting cognitive therapy for PTSD has one aim for each part of the model. It changes the negative appraisals with Socratic questioning and behavioural experiments. It makes the memory less intrusive by using brief imaginal reliving to find the hot spots (the moments of greatest distress and their meanings) and then updating each with what the person now knows ("At that moment I thought I would die; I know now that I survived and the driver was arrested"), together with discrimination of triggers ("then" versus "now") and sometimes a visit to the site. And it removes the maintaining strategies, asking the client to drop safety behaviours, stop suppressing and ruminating, and reclaim the parts of life he has given up.

Key study: early cognitive therapy after road accidents (Ehlers and colleagues, 2003)

Can cognitive therapy given soon after a trauma stop PTSD from becoming chronic? Ehlers and her colleagues randomly assigned people who had developed PTSD within six months of a road accident to up to 12 weekly sessions of cognitive therapy, to a detailed cognitive-behavioural self-help booklet, or to repeated assessments only. At follow-up only 11 percent of the cognitive therapy group still had PTSD, against 61 percent of the self-help group and 55 percent of those only assessed: good information on paper did no better than nothing. By contrast, putting every survivor through a single "debriefing" session soon after a trauma has been found unhelpful and sometimes harmful (Section 9.7).

An older and equally successful approach was developed in the late 1980s by Patricia A. Resick and her colleagues in St Louis, first for survivors of rape. cognitive processing therapy (CPT) is a 12-session cognitive therapy for PTSD that focuses on how the trauma has changed the survivor's beliefs. When a trauma conflicts with prior beliefs, the conflict can be resolved in three ways (the terms are borrowed from Piaget). In assimilation the person alters his memory of the event to fit his old beliefs: someone who believes the world is just concludes, "It must have been my fault." In over-accommodation he changes his beliefs too far: "No one can be trusted"; "I am never safe." In accommodation, the healthy outcome, he changes them just enough: "Some people are dangerous, and I can be careful whom I trust." The beliefs that block recovery are called stuck points, and CPT looks for them in five areas: safety, trust, power and control, esteem, and intimacy.

Therapy begins with an impact statement, a short essay on why the client thinks the trauma happened and how it has changed her beliefs about herself, other people and the world. She then works through a series of worksheets (linking events, thoughts and feelings; challenging questions; patterns of problematic thinking; challenging beliefs) to examine her stuck points Socratically. A detailed written account of the trauma was part of the original therapy, but Resick and her colleagues (2008) found that CPT works about as well without it, and it is now optional. Meera, assaulted by a colleague after agreeing to stay late at the office, holds the stuck points "It happened because I stayed late" (assimilation) and "No man can be trusted" (over-accommodation), and arrives at "He chose to assault me; staying late is not an invitation" and "Some men are dangerous, and I can let trust build slowly." Compared with prolonged exposure for survivors of rape (Resick and colleagues, 2002), CPT was similarly and highly effective, with somewhat greater reductions in guilt.

Children need a different approach, with the parent in the room. trauma-focused cognitive-behavioural therapy (TF-CBT), developed in the 1990s by Judith A. Cohen, Anthony P. Mannarino and Esther Deblinger, first for sexually abused children, is now the best-supported treatment for traumatised children and adolescents. It usually runs for 12 to 16 sessions, with parallel sessions for a caregiver who is not the abuser, and its components are remembered by the acronym PRACTICE: psychoeducation and parenting skills, relaxation, affective expression and modulation, cognitive coping, trauma narrative and processing, in vivo mastery of trauma reminders, conjoint child-parent sessions and enhancing future safety. The early components build coping skills. The centrepiece is the trauma narrative, in which the child gradually tells, writes or draws the story of what happened, so that the memory loses its power and distorted beliefs ("it was my fault") can be corrected, before sharing it with the parent in a joint session. Cohen and her colleagues (2004) found TF-CBT more effective than child-centred therapy for sexually abused children.

Two other trauma treatments are described in Chapter 4: Edna Foa's prolonged exposure (Section 4.3) and Francine Shapiro's eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), in which the trauma is recalled while the eyes follow the therapist's moving finger, a method whose mechanism is still disputed (Section 4.4). Psychosis: cognitive behavioural therapy for delusions and voices

For most of the twentieth century psychotherapists left delusions and hallucinations alone. The psychiatrist and philosopher Karl Jaspers had called primary delusions "un-understandable", and clinical folklore held that arguing with a delusion was pointless or dangerous. Yet in 1952, as a young psychiatrist years before he broke with psychoanalysis, Aaron Beck published a case report describing how he had helped a man with a long-standing persecutory delusion to examine, piece by piece, the evidence that he was being followed, and the belief weakened. The modern revival came from Britain in the late 1980s and 1990s, from David Kingdon and Douglas Turkington (Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy of Schizophrenia, 1994), Philippa Garety, Elizabeth Kuipers and David Fowler, Max Birchwood and Paul Chadwick, and Nicholas Tarrier. cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis (CBTp) applies the cognitive model to psychotic experiences. It aims not to argue a person out of his delusions or voices but to reduce the distress and disability they cause, by helping him understand them and test the beliefs he holds about them.

CBTp rests on two ideas. The first is the continuum view: psychotic experiences differ in degree, not in kind, from ordinary ones. Many people hear a voice at some point without becoming ill, suspicious thoughts are common, and anyone deprived of sleep, isolated or held hostage can begin to hallucinate. Kingdon and Turkington built this into a normalising rationale, explaining early in therapy that the client's experiences are understandable reactions to stress, isolation and sleeplessness to which anyone is vulnerable, which reduces fear and the sense of being uniquely abnormal. The second idea is that distress depends on appraisal. Chadwick and Birchwood (1994) found that what made voices distressing was less what they said than what the hearer believed about their identity, power and intent: voices believed to be all-powerful and malevolent were feared, resisted and often obeyed, while the same words from a voice believed to be weak were far less frightening. This is Ellis's ABC applied to voices (A, the voice; B, beliefs about it; C, distress and the urge to comply), and it makes the beliefs, not the voice, the first target.

Garety, Kuipers, Fowler, Freeman and Bebbington (2001) combined these ideas in a cognitive model of the positive symptoms. In vulnerable people, stress triggers anomalous experiences (heightened perceptions, thoughts that seem to come from outside, events that feel strangely significant). The person searches for an explanation, and anxiety, low self-esteem and biases in reasoning push him towards a threatening, external one, especially the jumping-to-conclusions bias. In the "beads task" (Huq, Garety and Hemsley, 1988), beads are drawn one at a time from one of two jars with opposite proportions of two colours, and people with delusions typically decide which jar they come from after only one or two beads. Once formed, the belief is maintained like any anxious belief: by selective attention, by worry and, as Daniel Freeman and his colleagues have shown, by safety behaviours. A man who believes his neighbours plan to attack him stays indoors and avoids eye contact, and credits these precautions for his survival, just as Ramesh credits the trolley.

CBTp is usually delivered one to one, in at least 16 sessions and often over many months, and it moves slowly. Its main steps are:

  • Engagement: building trust with a person who may suspect everyone, often starting with what he wants to change (sleep, fear, isolation) rather than with the symptoms.
  • Normalising and collaborative formulation: a shared account of how the experiences arose and what keeps them going.
  • Gentle examination of beliefs: Socratic exploration of the evidence for a delusion and of other possible explanations, without confrontation, often beginning with peripheral questions rather than the central belief.
  • Behavioural experiments, such as recording the room on a phone during a voice-hearing episode to see whether the voice is captured, or dropping a safety behaviour to see whether the feared attack follows.
  • Coping strategy enhancement (Tarrier's term): strengthening strategies the client already uses with some success, such as humming, music, talking to someone or going for a walk.
  • Relapse prevention: recognising early warning signs (sleeplessness, irritability, growing suspicion) and planning a response.

How well does it work? NICE recommends that CBTp be offered to everyone with psychosis or schizophrenia, alongside family intervention (Section 7.2). Meta-analyses, such as that of Til Wykes and her colleagues (2008), find real but modest effects on positive symptoms, smaller in the most rigorous trials with blinded assessors. The COMMAND trial led by Birchwood (2014) found that cognitive therapy reduced harmful compliance with command hallucinations, voices that order the hearer to harm himself or others. Above all, CBTp is an addition to medication, not a replacement for it; a realistic success is that the person still hears voices but is no longer terrified or ruled by them.

Exam trap

"The front-line treatment for schizophrenia is..." antipsychotic medication (RML 2021). CBT for psychosis, social skills training (Section 4.7 and Chapter 9), stress management and family psychoeducation (Section 7.2) are valuable adjuncts that reduce distress, improve functioning and prevent relapse, but none of them replaces medication in acute schizophrenia. The ABC analysis of voices is Chadwick and Birchwood's; the jumping-to-conclusions bias and the beads task come from Garety's group; the normalising rationale is Kingdon and Turkington's; coping strategy enhancement is Tarrier's.

Indian context

Many Indian families first explain voices and strange beliefs as possession, black magic or the evil eye, and consult a faith healer or visit a temple or dargah before, or alongside, a psychiatrist. A cognitive therapist need not demolish that explanatory model in order to help: exploring what the client and family believe and fear, and offering the stress-vulnerability and normalising accounts as additional ways of making sense of the experience, keeps the family engaged. In neighbouring Pakistan, Farooq Naeem and his colleagues have culturally adapted CBT for psychosis for South Asian clients, involving family members closely and working respectfully with beliefs about jinn and magic.

The wider family of disorder-specific models

Panic, social anxiety, trauma and psychosis are only the best-known examples. The same method (find the key appraisal, find what maintains it, reverse each maintaining process) has produced a model for almost every common disorder. For obsessive-compulsive disorder, Salkovskis (1985) argued that intrusive thoughts are nearly universal and become obsessions only when they are appraised as meaning that one is responsible for causing or preventing harm; checking, washing, neutralising and reassurance seeking then work as safety behaviours. That is one reason the behavioural treatment of OCD, exposure and response prevention (introduced by Victor Meyer in 1966 and turned into a standard treatment above all by Edna Foa; Section 4.3), works: blocking the ritual lets the person discover that the feared harm does not follow. For health anxiety, Salkovskis and Hilary Warwick (1986) described the misreading of harmless bodily signs as serious illness, kept alive by body checking and reassurance seeking. For eating disorders, Christopher G. 5.7 Going deeper: schema therapy and cognitive analytic therapyLower yield · 2 Qs

By the early 1980s cognitive therapy had proved itself with depression and was fast proving itself with the anxiety disorders (Sections 5.3 to 5.6). Yet therapists at Beck's centre in Philadelphia kept meeting a different kind of client. She did her thought records faithfully for a fortnight, then missed three sessions in a row. She could list the evidence against "I am unlovable" and then say quietly, "I know all that. I just don't feel it." Her difficulty was not an episode of illness but the shape of her whole life, above all of her relationships, including the one with her therapist. Clients like her were often diagnosed with a personality disorder (described in the Psychiatric Disorders book), and short-term cognitive therapy failed them more often than anyone else.

Beck's own answer was Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders (1990), written with Arthur Freeman and colleagues, which described the typical core beliefs of each personality disorder (the avoidant person's "If people knew the real me, they would reject me"). Two other therapists went further and built new therapies, one from inside the cognitive tradition, the other by translating psychoanalysis into the collaborative language of cognitive therapy. Both "go deeper" than standard CBT in the same sense: they work on patterns laid down in childhood and repeated in adult relationships.

Why standard cognitive therapy struggles with personality problems

Jeffrey E. Young trained with Aaron Beck at the Center for Cognitive Therapy at the University of Pennsylvania, and he was struck by how often the clients who failed in standard cognitive therapy were the ones with lifelong, characterological problems. In Cognitive Therapy for Personality Disorders: A Schema-Focused Approach (1990) he set out what he first called schema-focused cognitive therapy, now simply schema therapy. Its fullest statement is Schema Therapy: A Practitioner's Guide (2003), written with Janet Klosko and Marjorie Weishaar; Young and Klosko had already written a popular self-help version, Reinventing Your Life, in 1993.

Young's diagnosis of the problem tells you what schema therapy adds. Standard cognitive therapy, he pointed out, quietly assumes that clients can, after brief training, report their thoughts and feelings; that they have specific problems to work on and will do homework; that they can form a collaborative relationship within a few sessions, so that the relationship itself need not be a focus; and that their beliefs will yield to evidence, logic, experiments and practice. Clients with personality disorders break nearly every one of these assumptions. Their problems are vague and pervasive ("my whole life doesn't work"). They avoid painful thoughts and feelings, so they cannot report what they will not let themselves notice. Their beliefs feel like the truth about who they are, so evidence slides off them. And the relationship with the therapist is often not the background to the problem but the problem itself, re-enacted in the room through mistrust, clinging, compliance or hostility.

The client who says "I know it, but I don't feel it" states the central difficulty. Cognitive therapy is good at changing intellectual conviction (what the client knows in her head) and much less good at changing emotional conviction (what she feels in her gut). Schema therapy therefore adds methods aimed at the gut: imagery, chair dialogues and an unusually warm, active therapy relationship. It is an integrative therapy, combining cognitive-behavioural methods with ideas from attachment theory, Gestalt therapy, object relations theory and constructivism, around one goal: helping clients get their core emotional needs met by healing the schemas that formed when those needs went unmet in childhood.

Exam trap

Jeffrey Young, not Aaron Beck (although Young trained with Beck and the therapy grew out of Beck's), not Marsha Linehan (DBT, Section 5.8) and not Steven Hayes (ACT, Section 5.9). "Schema therapy was originally designed for patients who..." had failed to respond to standard CBT, particularly those with personality disorders. It was not designed for patients hospitalised with acute psychosis (who need medication and the CBT for psychosis of Section 5.6), not for simple specific phobias (exposure therapy works well for them, Chapter 4), and not as a brief solution-focused intervention (solution-focused brief therapy is de Shazer and Berg's, Chapter 6). Schema therapy is long, often a year or more.

Early maladaptive schemas

You met Beck's use of the word schema in Section 5.3: the mental structure that holds a core belief and filters incoming information, like a pair of tinted glasses. Young's concept is larger. He defined an early maladaptive schema as a broad, pervasive theme or pattern, made up of memories, emotions, thoughts and bodily sensations, about oneself and one's relationships with others, developed during childhood or adolescence, elaborated throughout life, and dysfunctional to a significant degree. Notice four things in that definition. An early maladaptive schema is more than a belief: it includes memories, feelings and body sensations, which is why it can be "known" to be false and still be felt as true. It is about relationships, not just about the self. It begins early. And it is self-perpetuating: a schema fights for its own survival, because however painful it is, it is familiar, and people are drawn back to the situations that trigger it.

Where do such schemas come from? Young's answer begins with needs. Every child, he proposed, has five core emotional needs: secure attachment to others (safety, stability, nurturance and acceptance); autonomy, competence and a sense of identity; freedom to express valid needs and emotions; spontaneity and play; and realistic limits and self-control. Schemas form when these needs are not met, through four kinds of early experience: toxic frustration of needs, too little of a good thing (cold or absent parents breed emotional deprivation); traumatisation or victimisation (abuse, humiliation or bullying breed mistrust and defectiveness); too much of a good thing (an overprotected child may grow up dependent, an overindulged one entitled); and selective internalisation or identification with a significant other (the child absorbs a parent's pessimism, harsh standards or fear of the world). Temperament matters too: an anxious, sensitive child and a robust one can grow up in the same family with different schemas, a point you will meet again in Linehan's biosocial theory (Section 5.8).

Push a case through the model. Meera is 29, a software engineer in Bengaluru. Her father was warm and playful one week and absent, drinking, the next; her mother, overwhelmed, told her that a good daughter does not make demands. At work Meera is excellent. In love she is in torment. She is drawn, again and again, to men who are charming and unavailable, and when a boyfriend does not answer a message for an hour she is flooded with panic and certain that he has gone for good. Standard cognitive therapy taught her to write, "He is probably in a meeting; he replied within two hours every other time." She believed the balanced response at 80 percent and still felt the panic at 90. A schema therapist would see two early maladaptive schemas at work, abandonment/instability (from the unpredictable father) and subjugation (from "good daughters do not make demands"), and would set out to work on them where they live: in memory, emotion and relationships, not only in thought.

Exam trap

Know the numbers: Young described 18 early maladaptive schemas in five domains (disconnection and rejection; impaired autonomy and performance; impaired limits; other-directedness; overvigilance and inhibition). Do not confuse an early maladaptive schema with Beck's core belief or with Beck's three families of core beliefs (helpless, unlovable, worthless; Section 5.3). Beck's schema is mainly cognitive; Young's includes memories, emotions and bodily sensations, is rooted in an unmet childhood need, and is about relationships with others as much as about the self.

Coping styles: how schemas keep themselves alive

A schema by itself does not make a person act. What a person does when a schema is triggered is governed by his schema coping style. Faced with the pain of an activated schema, people respond in one of three ways, which map neatly onto the three basic animal responses to threat. In schema surrender (the freeze response) the person gives in to the schema and behaves as if it were true, choosing partners and situations that confirm it. In schema avoidance (the flight response) the person arranges life so that the schema is never triggered, avoiding situations, closeness and even thoughts and feelings that might activate it, often with the help of alcohol, overwork or compulsive busyness. In schema overcompensation (the fight response) the person behaves as if the opposite of the schema were true, fighting it so hard that he goes too far the other way. The cruelty of coping styles is that each one, in the long run, keeps the schema alive. Meera, with her abandonment schema, could surrender (cling to unreliable men and accept being left as her fate), avoid (refuse all close relationships and live for her work), or overcompensate (end every relationship herself, at the first sign of distance, so that no one can leave her first). Whichever she chooses, she never collects the evidence that a reliable person might stay. Young called this process schema perpetuation and described three ways it happens: through the cognitive distortions you met in Section 5.3 (selective abstraction notices every late reply and filters out every loyal act), through self-defeating life patterns (the choice of partners and jobs), and through the coping styles themselves. schema chemistry is the intense attraction people often feel towards partners who trigger their core schemas: the unavailable man feels exciting to Meera, and the kind, steady one feels dull, because the first one feels like home. The opposite of perpetuation is schema healing, the goal of the therapy: weakening the schema's memories, emotional charge, bodily sensations and beliefs, and replacing maladaptive coping styles with behaviour that actually meets the person's needs.

Exam trap

The three schema coping styles are surrender, avoidance and overcompensation (freeze, flight and fight). Overcompensation is the one students miss, because it can look healthy: the confident, high-achieving, dominating person who never shows weakness may be fighting a defectiveness or failure schema. The test is rigidity and cost. Healthy confidence can bend; overcompensation cannot, and it usually damages relationships.

Schema modes

Working schema by schema went well with moderate problems. With severely disturbed clients, especially those with borderline and narcissistic personality disorders, it ran into trouble: they often had a dozen schemas active at once, and they shifted, sometimes within minutes, from one intense state to another: tearful and childlike, then furious, then cold and empty, then savagely self-critical. To describe these shifts, Young introduced the concept of schema modes: the moment-to-moment emotional states and coping responses, adaptive and maladaptive, that a person experiences. A mode is the set of schemas and coping responses that is active in a person right now. The distinction is simple and examinable: a schema is a trait, an enduring pattern; a mode is a state, the part of the self that is in charge at this moment.

Everyone has modes, but in a healthy person the shifts are smooth and a sensible adult part stays in charge; in severe personality disorders the modes are extreme and walled off from one another. Young described ten modes in four groups. The child modes are innate and universal: the Vulnerable Child (lonely, frightened, carrying the core schemas), the Angry Child (enraged that core needs are not met), the Impulsive or Undisciplined Child (acting on desires without regard to limits) and the Happy Child (content because needs are met). The dysfunctional coping modes correspond to the three coping styles: the Compliant Surrenderer, the Detached Protector (who shuts off needs and feelings, leaving the person numb and empty) and the Overcompensator. The dysfunctional parent modes are internalised versions of the parents at their worst: the Punitive Parent, who punishes the child for having needs or making mistakes, and the Demanding Parent, who pushes ceaselessly for higher standards. Finally, the Healthy Adult nurtures the Vulnerable Child, sets limits on the Angry and Impulsive Child and stands up to the parent modes; it is the mode therapy sets out to strengthen. Later research by the Dutch group around Arnoud Arntz, using the Schema Mode Inventory, extended the list, but the ten-mode version is the one taught.

Young's model of borderline personality disorder uses just five modes, shown in Figure 5.7: the Abandoned or Abused Child, the Angry or Impulsive Child, the Punitive Parent, the Detached Protector and a weak Healthy Adult. Imagine Sana, 24, with a borderline pattern, in a single session. She begins flat and distant: "I'm fine. Nothing really matters." (Detached Protector.) The therapist mentions that she will be on leave next month. Sana goes quiet, then tearful: "Everyone leaves." (Abandoned Child.) Within minutes she is shouting: "You're like all the others; you never cared." (Angry Child.) That night she cuts her arm while a voice in her head says, "You're disgusting. You deserve this." (Punitive Parent.) To an onlooker this is the instability of borderline personality disorder; to a schema therapist it is a sequence of mode flips, each of which can be named and worked with.

Each mode calls for a different response. The therapist bypasses the Detached Protector gently, without attacking it, because it once protected a child who had no other way to survive; she protects and comforts the Abandoned Child; she sets limits on the Angry Child while validating the need underneath the anger; and she fights and banishes the Punitive Parent, the one mode with which there is no negotiation. Throughout, she acts as the Healthy Adult the client does not yet have, so that the client can gradually take that role over for herself.

Exam trap

A schema is an enduring trait-like pattern; a mode is the state that is active at the moment. Young's dysfunctional parent modes are the Punitive Parent and the Demanding Parent; the healthy, nurturing role belongs to the Healthy Adult mode. Some books write "Critical Parent" or "Nurturing Parent", but those are the ego-state names of Eric Berne's transactional analysis, a different therapy. And the five-mode model of borderline personality disorder is Young's, not Linehan's: DBT does not use modes.

Limited reparenting and the techniques of change

Schema therapy runs in two phases. In the assessment and education phase the therapist and client identify the schemas, coping styles and modes, trace them back to their childhood origins, and link them to the client's present problems. In the change phase the therapist uses four groups of techniques: the therapy relationship itself, experiential techniques, cognitive techniques and behavioural pattern-breaking.

The relationship comes first, and it is where schema therapy differs most from Beck's. You saw in Section 5.4 that Beck regarded warmth and empathy as necessary but not sufficient: the ground on which the techniques work. In schema therapy the relationship is itself an active ingredient, through limited reparenting. Within the bounds of a professional relationship, the therapist partly meets the core emotional needs that the client's parents did not meet: she offers warmth, safety, stability, praise, validation of feelings and encouragement of autonomy, and also the firm, kind limits that an impulsive child needs. With borderline personality disorder the reparenting goes further: some contact between sessions, or a card or recorded message to hold on to when the Abandoned Child is frightened. The word limited matters: the therapist is not the client's parent and stays within professional ethics (Chapter 1).

The partner of limited reparenting is empathic confrontation (or empathic reality testing): the therapist shows that she understands why the schema developed and why it feels true, and at the same time confronts the client, clearly and repeatedly, with the need to change the behaviour that keeps it alive. With Meera: "When you were small, your father really did disappear without warning, and you learned to be ready for everyone to leave. That made sense then. And I notice that when your boyfriend is a little late in replying, you end the relationship before he can. That protects you from being left, and it also keeps you alone. Shall we look at that together?" Empathy without confrontation lets the schema stay in charge; confrontation without empathy feels like one more critical parent.

The experiential techniques aim at the gut, where the schema's emotional conviction lives. The best known is imagery rescripting, set out for work with childhood memories by Arnoud Arntz and Anoek Weertman (1999) and now central to schema therapy. The client closes her eyes and goes back, in imagery, to a childhood scene in which a core need was not met: little Meera waiting by the window for a father who did not come home. Once the scene is vivid and the feeling is present, the therapist enters the image (or the client's adult self does), and the scene is changed: the adult comforts the child, tells her that it is not her fault, and perhaps speaks firmly to the father. Nothing changes the historical facts, but the emotional meaning of the memory changes, and with it the intensity of the schema. The second experiential method is chair work, borrowed from Fritz Perls's Gestalt therapy (Chapter 6). The client sits in one chair and speaks as her schema ("You'll always be left; you're too much trouble") and then moves to another chair and answers it from her Healthy Adult; or the Punitive Parent is placed in an empty chair and the client, with the therapist's help, tells it to leave. Clients may also write letters, which are not sent, to the parents who hurt them.

The cognitive techniques are the familiar tools of Sections 5.3 and 5.4 turned on the schema: reviewing the evidence for and against the schema across the whole of the client's life, reattributing childhood events ("I was not left because I was too much trouble; my father had a drinking problem"), writing dialogues between the "schema side" and the "healthy side", keeping a schema diary, and carrying schema flashcards, coping cards that the client reads when a schema is triggered. Finally, behavioural pattern-breaking, usually the longest phase, replaces schema-driven coping with healthier choices: Meera practises asking for reassurance instead of ending relationships, notices schema chemistry when she meets an unavailable man, and deliberately gives a steady man more than one evening.

Key study: schema therapy versus transference-focused psychotherapy (Giesen-Bloo and colleagues, 2006)

Could schema therapy hold its own against a respected psychodynamic treatment for borderline personality disorder? Josephine Giesen-Bloo, Arnoud Arntz and their colleagues in the Netherlands randomly assigned outpatients with borderline personality disorder to three years of either schema therapy or Otto Kernberg's transference-focused psychotherapy (Chapter 3), both delivered twice a week. Both groups improved, but by the end of the three years about 46 percent of the schema therapy patients had recovered, against about 24 percent of the transference-focused patients, and fewer schema therapy patients dropped out. Critics pointed out that the transference-focused arm may not have been delivered as skilfully as its developers would wish, and that a trial run by the developers of one treatment risks allegiance effects (Chapter 8). Later trials of group and individual schema therapy have also been encouraging, and schema therapy is now counted among the evidence-based treatments for borderline personality disorder.

Clinical link

Schema therapy is now used well beyond borderline personality disorder: for other personality disorders, chronic and recurrent depression, eating disorders, couples, and offenders with personality disorders in forensic hospitals. A clinician meeting a client with a long history of failed short treatments, and relationships that keep collapsing in the same way, should think of it. It is demanding to deliver: limited reparenting asks the therapist to care openly and to hold firm limits at the same time, and good supervision is essential.

Exam trap

An option reading "schema analysis, mode work, limited reparenting" describes schema therapy. It is the classic distractor when the question asks for DBT's skills modules (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness; Section 5.8). The empty chair technique is used in schema therapy but belongs to Gestalt therapy; if asked which therapy "focuses on" the empty chair, answer Gestalt.

Cognitive analytic therapy

Schema therapy started from cognitive therapy and reached towards childhood and relationships. The second therapy in this section travelled the other way. Anthony Ryle (1927 to 2016), a nephew of the Oxford philosopher Gilbert Ryle, spent years as a general practitioner in London and then as head of a university student health service before becoming a consultant psychotherapist at St Thomas' Hospital in London. Two things troubled him: long-term psychoanalytic therapy could be offered to very few patients in Britain's National Health Service, and its ideas were rarely put in a form that patients could understand and researchers could test. Ryle had already used George Kelly's repertory grid, a method of mapping a person's personal constructs (Kelly appears in Sections 5.1 and 5.5), to measure how patients changed in therapy. Through the 1980s he built a brief, structured, collaborative therapy that put psychoanalytic insights into cognitive terms, and in 1990 he published Cognitive-Analytic Therapy: Active Participation in Change. cognitive analytic therapy (CAT) integrates three sources: cognitive psychology and Kelly's personal construct theory; psychoanalytic object relations theory (Chapter 3); and, from the 1990s, the ideas of the Russian psychologist Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (1896 to 1934; Section 5.5) and the Russian literary theorist Mikhail Mikhailovich Bakhtin (1895 to 1975), introduced into CAT by the Finnish psychologist Mikael Leiman. "Analytic" in the name means psychoanalytic, not statistical.

Ryle's first model was the procedural sequence model. Any purposeful action, he argued, runs through a repeated sequence: we perceive a situation and an aim, appraise it, predict what will happen, act, and evaluate the result, confirming or revising the aim or the means. Neurotic problems persist because certain problem procedures are not revised even when they repeatedly fail. Ryle then joined this to object relations theory. The procedures that matter most are learned in early relationships, and they come in pairs. A child who grows up with a critical, contemptuous parent does not learn only how to be a crushed, humiliated child; he learns the whole relationship, both its poles. These internalised pairs are reciprocal roles, written as a pair linked by an arrow: "critical, contemptuous" to "crushed, worthless". As an adult, the person can play either pole: he may provoke criticism from others, treat a junior colleague with contempt, or, most often, play both roles inside his own head, criticising himself in his father's voice. This self-to-self relationship is CAT's translation of the internalised critic.

CAT is time-limited, usually 16 sessions (eight for milder problems, and often 24 for borderline personality disorder), and it moves through three phases often summarised as the three Rs: reformulation, recognition and revision. In the reformulation phase (roughly the first four sessions) the therapist takes a careful history, agrees with the client on a short list of target problems and the procedures that maintain them, and then writes the reformulation letter, which she reads aloud to the client, usually around the fourth session. The letter tells the client's story back to her with compassion: what happened to her, how her ways of coping made sense as survival strategies at the time, how the same procedures now cause her problems, and how they are likely to show up in the therapy itself. Clients are often deeply moved by it. The prose letter is followed by a sequential diagrammatic reformulation (SDR), a diagram that places the core reciprocal roles at the centre and traces each problem procedure as a loop that leads back to the same pain. Figure 5.8 shows a simple one.

In the recognition phase the client learns to spot her procedures as they happen, often by carrying a copy of the diagram and noting each day when the trap or dilemma appeared. In the revision phase client and therapist work out "exits", new procedures drawn onto the diagram as ways out of each loop. Here the analytic side of CAT comes into its own. Because reciprocal roles are relationships, they will be enacted with the therapist: a client whose core role is "critical, contemptuous to crushed, worthless" may feel criticised by the therapist's every question, or may provoke criticism, and the therapist will feel the pull to reciprocate. Instead of interpreting this transference in the psychoanalytic manner (Chapter 3), the CAT therapist points to the diagram: "I think this is the trap happening between us right now." Ryle drew here on Vygotsky's idea that new mental capacities are first exercised jointly with a more skilled helper, using shared tools and signs, and only later internalised (the zone of proximal development, described in the Developmental book): the letters and diagrams are tools the client first uses with the therapist and then carries inside herself.

Ending is planned from the start and worked on openly, because for many clients an ending is itself a trigger for their core roles (abandonment, rejection). In the last session therapist and client exchange goodbye letters, each summarising honestly what has changed, what has not, and what remains to be done, and a follow-up meeting is held a few months later. For borderline personality disorder Ryle developed the multiple self-states model (1997), which describes the disorder as switching between a small number of partly dissociated self-states, each organised around its own reciprocal role, with a weakened capacity to reflect on the switches; the diagram maps the states and the switches between them (compare the schema modes of Figure 5.7). The evidence base for CAT is smaller than that for CBT, DBT or schema therapy. Its best-known trial, by Andrew Chanen and colleagues in Melbourne (2008), treated adolescents with borderline features and found CAT at least as effective as good clinical care, with faster improvement. CAT is widely used in the United Kingdom.

Exam trap

Cognitive analytic therapy is Anthony Ryle's, published in book form in 1990. Its signatures are reciprocal roles, traps, dilemmas and snags, the reformulation letter, the sequential diagrammatic reformulation and goodbye letters, in a course of about 16 sessions. "Analytic" means psychoanalytic object relations. Do not confuse CAT with Eric Berne's transactional analysis (parent, adult and child ego states), with Kelly's fixed-role therapy (Section 5.5), or with Lazarus's multimodal therapy (Section 5.5). A dilemma in CAT is a false either-or choice; a snag is a goal abandoned because of guilt or others' imagined reactions; a trap is a self-confirming vicious circle.

5.8 The third wave I: dialectical behaviour therapyHigh yield · 13 Qs

In 1961 a 17-year-old girl from Tulsa, Oklahoma, was admitted to the Institute of Living, a psychiatric hospital in Hartford, Connecticut. She burned her wrists with cigarettes, cut her arms and legs, and banged her head against the walls, and she spent long stretches of her stay locked alone in a seclusion room. She was diagnosed with schizophrenia and given the treatments of the day, strong tranquillising drugs and repeated electroconvulsive therapy. None of it helped. When she was discharged, more than two years later in 1963, her discharge summary recorded that for much of her stay she had been one of the most disturbed patients in the hospital. Before she left she made a private vow: that she would get herself out of hell, and that when she was out she would come back and get others out too.

For almost fifty years she told hardly anyone. Then, in June 2011, she stood up in front of an audience at the same hospital and told the whole story, and the New York Times carried it around the world. By then she was one of the most influential clinical psychologists alive, and the treatment she had built for people very like her younger self was being used on every continent. She said she now believed she would have been diagnosed not with schizophrenia but with borderline personality disorder. Her name was Marsha M. Linehan (1943 to 2026), and her treatment is dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT).

Why a new therapy was needed

Linehan took her PhD at Loyola University Chicago in 1971 and in 1977 joined the University of Washington in Seattle, where she set out to treat the clients most therapists avoided: chronically suicidal women who harmed themselves again and again, many of whom met the criteria for borderline personality disorder. She began with the best-tested tool she had, standard behaviour therapy (Chapter 4): define the problem behaviours, analyse what maintains them, teach new skills and change the contingencies. It failed, and the way it failed was instructive.

When Linehan pushed for change, her clients heard criticism: to a woman who already felt that she was the problem, every request to monitor her behaviour and practise skills said "you are doing it wrong". She became angry, withdrew or stopped coming. When Linehan switched to warm acceptance and support, in the manner of client-centred therapy (Chapter 6), clients felt that nobody was taking their unbearable misery seriously or helping them change a life that was not worth living. Meanwhile weekly crises swallowed the sessions, and the therapists were exhausted. Neither change alone nor acceptance alone worked.

The way out was to do both at once, and to make the tension between them the engine of the therapy. For acceptance Linehan turned to Zen, which she had practised herself (including at Shasta Abbey, a Zen monastery in California, and with the German Zen teacher Willigis Jäger), and translated its mindful awareness and radical acceptance into behavioural skills without religious content. To hold acceptance and change together she borrowed a philosophical framework, dialectics. In 1993 she published the treatment in two manuals, Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder and the Skills Training Manual for Treating Borderline Personality Disorder (revised as the DBT Skills Training Manual in 2015). DBT was the first third-wave therapy (Section 5.1) to gain wide acceptance, yet it remains at heart cognitive-behavioural: change still comes through behavioural analysis, skills training, exposure and contingency management, always balanced with acceptance.

Key study: the first DBT trial (Linehan, Armstrong, Suarez, Allmon and Heard, 1991)

Could any outpatient treatment reduce repeated self-harm in chronically suicidal women with borderline personality disorder, a group that most trials had excluded? Linehan and her colleagues randomly assigned a small sample of such women (about 45; reports of the trial give slightly different totals) to one year of DBT or to treatment as usual in the community. Over the year, the DBT group made fewer and less medically serious acts of self-harm (then called parasuicide), spent far fewer days as psychiatric inpatients, and were much more likely to stay in therapy: only about one in six dropped out of individual therapy, against about half of the comparison clients. The trial had limits. It was small, it compared DBT with a loosely defined "usual" treatment rather than with another structured therapy, and the two groups did not differ in depression, hopelessness or suicidal thinking. But it was the first randomised trial to show that a psychological treatment could reduce self-harm in this population, and it put DBT on the map.

Exam trap

DBT was developed by Marsha Linehan primarily for borderline personality disorder with chronic suicidality (her first clients were chronically suicidal, self-harming women). It was not designed for schizophrenia, acute and transient psychotic disorder, specific phobias, panic disorder, OCD, anxiety disorders in general, autism, ADHD or anorexia nervosa. Adapted versions are now used for some of these and for PTSD and substance use (see the end of this section), so read the stem: "originally designed for" or "primarily indicated for" means borderline personality disorder. The other names in such questions belong elsewhere: Aaron Beck (cognitive therapy), Albert Bandura (self-efficacy), Virginia Satir (family therapy, Chapter 7).

The biosocial theory

Every therapy rests on a theory of what has gone wrong. Linehan's is the biosocial theory of borderline personality disorder, and its central claim is simple to state: borderline personality disorder is primarily a disorder of the emotion regulation system. It arises from a transaction, over many years, between a child who is biologically vulnerable to intense emotion and an environment that invalidates her emotional experience. (The diagnostic criteria for the disorder are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.)

Linehan first regrouped the diagnostic criteria into five areas of dysregulation (Table 5.16), and each skills module was later built to repair one of them. The disorder, on this view, is not simply moodiness but a pervasive difficulty in managing emotion that spills over into relationships, behaviour, thinking and the sense of self.

The biological half of the theory is emotional vulnerability, which has three parts: high sensitivity (a low threshold, so that small events such as a delayed reply or a slightly cool tone set off an emotion others would hardly notice); high reactivity (once triggered, the emotion is extremely intense, narrowing attention and disorganising thought and action); and a slow return to baseline (the emotion lasts, so the next trigger arrives before the last has faded, and emotions pile up). Linehan's own image is famous: people with the disorder are like patients with third-degree burns over most of their bodies; they have, so to speak, no emotional skin, and the slightest touch causes immense suffering. The vulnerability is a matter of temperament, partly inherited, not of character or will.

The social half is the invalidating environment: a family or setting that responds to a child's communication of her private experience (feelings, thoughts, sensations) in ways that are erratic, inappropriate or extreme, telling her in effect that her experience is wrong or unimportant. It has three features. It dismisses or punishes emotional expression ("Stop crying or I'll give you something to cry about"; "You're not hurt, you're just tired"). It intermittently reinforces emotional escalation: ordinary distress is ignored but extreme distress finally gets attention, so the child learns that only extremes work, and intermittent reinforcement (Chapter 4) makes the lesson hard to unlearn. And it oversimplifies the ease of solving problems ("If you really wanted to, you'd just stop"), so the child never learns the patient skills real problems need, and blames herself for failing. Linehan described chaotic families (parents preoccupied with their own troubles), perfect families (parents who cannot tolerate negative emotion) and typical families (in cultures that prize self-control and achievement). Abuse is a severe form of invalidation, but it is not required: a poor fit between a highly sensitive child and calm, well-meaning parents can be enough.

Such a child does not learn to label her emotions, to trust them as valid responses, or to tolerate and soothe distress. She learns instead to invalidate herself as her environment did, and to swing between suppressing emotion and showing it in extreme form. The result is emotion dysregulation: a pervasive inability to modulate emotion, to stay organised under strong feeling and to act for long-term goals rather than short-term relief. Many dramatic behaviours of the disorder are attempts to regulate unbearable emotion. Self-harm often brings rapid relief, so it is negatively reinforced (Chapter 4) every time it works. To the client, cutting is a solution; to the therapist, it is a problem behaviour to be replaced by better solutions. That double view runs through the whole of DBT.

The word transaction is the key, and Figure 5.9 shows why. Biology and environment do not simply add together; each changes the other over time. A highly reactive child is hard to parent; exhausted parents become more dismissive; their invalidation makes her more dysregulated; her escalating behaviour provokes still more invalidation. The theory is therefore a no-blame theory, and families are treated as partners rather than culprits. Sheila Crowell, Theodore Beauchaine and Linehan (2009) later added early impulsivity to emotional sensitivity as an inherited trait.

The theory also explains three dialectical dilemmas, in which the client swings between opposite extremes, one shaped mainly by biology and the other by environment: emotional vulnerability (feeling overwhelmed) versus self-invalidation (telling herself her feelings are stupid); active passivity (helplessly waiting for others to solve her problems) versus apparent competence (seeming far more capable than she is, so that others expect too much); and unrelenting crises versus inhibited grieving (avoiding the painful emotions of loss and trauma, whose avoidance keeps the crises coming). The therapist helps her find the middle path in each pair, which leads to the idea at the centre of DBT.

Indian context

Invalidation does not require cruelty. In many Indian homes it arrives in loving, ordinary sentences: "Don't cry, people will see", "What is there to be sad about? You have everything", "Just adjust", "Log kya kahenge?" For most children these do no harm. For a highly sensitive child they can add up to a daily experience of having her feelings declared wrong. A clinician adapting DBT for an Indian family can therefore teach parents to validate, much as the adolescent version of DBT brings parents into the skills group (described at the end of this section), and can present emotion regulation as serving family harmony as well as personal well-being, which makes the skills easier for the whole family to accept.

Exam trap

Borderline personality disorder is characterised primarily by emotional instability and impulsivity: a pervasive pattern of instability in relationships, self-image and emotion, with marked impulsivity (ICD-10 called it emotionally unstable personality disorder, with impulsive and borderline types). The distractors describe other personality disorders: detachment and restricted emotion (schizoid), eccentric beliefs and odd speech (schizotypal), pervasive grandiosity and lack of empathy (narcissistic), obsessive perfectionism (obsessive-compulsive) and suspicious, paranoid ideation (paranoid). The defence mechanism most characteristic of the disorder is splitting, the defence Otto Kernberg placed at the centre of his account of it (Chapter 3), not repression, sublimation or rationalisation. In Linehan's theory the core problem is emotion dysregulation, arising from the transaction of emotional vulnerability with an invalidating environment.

Dialectics: acceptance and change

What makes DBT "dialectical"? dialectics, a way of thinking developed by the German philosopher Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770 to 1831) and later by Karl Marx, holds that reality is made of opposing forces. A position (popularly called the thesis, though Hegel rarely used the word) contains its opposite (the antithesis), and their tension is resolved not by one side defeating the other but by a synthesis that keeps what is true in both; the synthesis then meets its own opposite, so change never stops. Linehan drew three working principles from this: interrelatedness and wholeness (behaviour must be understood within the client's whole system of relationships and history), polarity (every position contains its opposite, and truth is found by looking at both) and continuous change.

The central dialectic of DBT is between acceptance and change. The therapist accepts the client completely as she is and pushes steadily for change: "You are doing the best you can, and you need to do better." Too much acceptance abandons the client to an unbearable life; too much change makes the therapist one more invalidating voice. Think of a seesaw: when the client moves to one extreme, the therapist moves to the other, and both slowly work towards the middle.

Dialectical thinking is also something the client is taught, because it is the antidote to one of the most destructive thinking habits of the disorder. You met dichotomous (all-or-nothing) thinking in Section 5.3. In borderline personality disorder it extends to people, who are experienced as wholly good or wholly bad, idealised one week and devalued the next. Psychodynamic writers call this splitting: the failure to hold good and bad images of the same person (or of oneself) together, a concept Melanie Klein introduced and Otto Kernberg made central to his account of the disorder (Chapter 3). DBT does not interpret splitting; it teaches the client to replace "either-or" with "both-and". "My mother loves me and she hurt me." "I am angry with my therapist and I still want to come back next week." "I have made progress and I still have a long way to go."

On the acceptance side the core strategy is validation: communicating to the client that her responses make sense and are understandable in her current situation or in the light of her history. Validation is not the same as agreement or praise. The DBT rule is to validate the valid: the therapist validates the pain, the wish for relief and the fact that self-harm "works" in the short term, without validating self-harm as a wise choice. Linehan described six levels of validation, each deeper than the last:

  1. Listening and observing: full, alert attention to what the client says and feels.
  2. Accurate reflection: saying back, without judgement, what the client has communicated.
  3. Articulating the unverbalised: putting into words feelings or thoughts the client has not expressed ("I wonder if you felt humiliated as well as angry").
  4. Validating in terms of past learning or biology: "Given what happened every time you cried as a child, it makes sense that you hide your feelings."
  5. Validating in terms of the present context: "Anyone would feel hurt if a friend forgot their birthday"; the response is normal, not just understandable given a disordered past.
  6. Radical genuineness: treating the client as an equal and a capable person, not a fragile patient.

On the change side the core strategy is problem solving, beginning with behavioural chain analysis: a moment-by-moment reconstruction of one episode of problem behaviour, link by link. The chain runs from vulnerability factors (poor sleep, illness, alcohol, an earlier quarrel) to the prompting event, through every link of thought, emotion, sensation and action, to the problem behaviour and its short- and long-term consequences, which show what is reinforcing it. Solution analysis then asks at which links a skill could have been used. Clients learn that every crisis can be taken apart, and that each link is a place where a different choice can be made. Other change strategies come straight from behaviour therapy and CBT: skills training, contingency management (the therapist's warmth is used as a reinforcer, and care is taken not to reward self-harm with extra attention), exposure to avoided emotions, and cognitive modification.

Two further pairs keep the balance. In style, the therapist moves between reciprocal communication (warm, responsive, genuine) and irreverent communication (matter-of-fact, offbeat, used to jolt a client out of a rigid position). Linehan's best-known example: a client announces, "I'm going to kill myself," and the therapist replies, "But I thought you'd agreed not to drop out of therapy." The reply is not callous; it treats the client as capable and refuses the familiar crisis script. The therapist also uses dialectical strategies such as playing devil's advocate (arguing against change so that the client argues for it), metaphor, and "making lemonade out of lemons". Behind all of this sit Linehan's assumptions about clients: they are doing the best they can; they want to improve; they need to do better and try harder; they may not have caused all their problems but must solve them anyway; and they cannot fail in DBT (if a client does not improve, the treatment has failed).

Exam trap

"DBT's core mindfulness skills are primarily derived from..." Zen Buddhism (with some Western contemplative practice). Not Stoicism (the root of Ellis's REBT, Section 5.2), not psychoanalysis and not existentialism (Chapter 6). "DBT blends CBT with..." mindfulness and acceptance practices drawn from Zen, held together by dialectical philosophy; not psychoanalytic free association, not pharmacological augmentation, and not Bowen's family systems theory (Chapter 7). The "dialectic" is above all the balance of acceptance and change. Note also that validation in DBT means validating what is valid, not approving every behaviour.

The structure of DBT: functions, modes and stages

Standard DBT is a whole treatment programme, built to serve five functions: to enhance capabilities (teach the skills the client lacks); to improve motivation to use them; to ensure generalisation, so that skills learned in a clinic are used at home at midnight; to structure the environment so that it reinforces progress rather than crisis; and to enhance the therapists' capabilities and motivation, because chronically suicidal clients burn therapists out. Each function is carried by a mode of treatment, and standard DBT has four (Table 5.17).

In individual therapy client and primary therapist work on her targets with chain analyses and the full range of strategies. Each session begins with the diary card, on which she has recorded every day her urges to self-harm or die, any self-harm, drug or alcohol use, her strongest emotions and the skills she used; the card sets the agenda. Group skills training runs more like a class than a therapy group, with handouts and homework, and is usually led by someone other than the individual therapist; a full cycle of the modules takes about six months, so clients usually go through it twice in a year. telephone coaching lets the client call between sessions for brief, practical help in using a skill before she acts on an urge; it is not long telephone therapy. Under the 24-hour rule, a client who has harmed herself may not call for coaching for the next 24 hours (medical emergencies apart), so that contact reinforces reaching out before self-harm, not after it. The consultation team, a weekly meeting of all the programme's DBT therapists, is often called therapy for the therapists: members apply DBT to one another, catch colleagues who have become rigid or burnt out, and agree, among other things, to accept that they are fallible. Linehan insisted that a therapist without a team is not doing DBT. Medication, case management and hospital admission are treated as ancillary.

A pretreatment stage of orientation and commitment comes first. The client learns the biosocial theory and the structure of DBT, and she and the therapist agree to work together for a set period (usually a year), to attend, and to work on reducing suicidal and self-harming behaviour. Commitment is sought with named strategies such as weighing pros and cons, the foot-in-the-door and devil's advocate. One rule is famous: a client who misses four consecutive weeks of scheduled skills training or individual therapy is out of the programme for the rest of the contract, though she may apply to return.

Treatment then proceeds in stages, defined by how severe the client's problems are. In Stage 1 the aim is to move from severe behavioural dyscontrol to behavioural control, and the work is organised by a strict target hierarchy:

  1. Life-threatening behaviours: suicide attempts, suicidal threats and urges, non-suicidal self-injury and behaviour that endangers others. If any occurred during the week, they come first in the session, every time.
  2. Therapy-interfering behaviours: anything, by the client or the therapist, that gets in the way of therapy, such as missed sessions, undone homework, hostility, or a therapist who is unreliable or rigid.
  3. Quality-of-life-interfering behaviours: substance misuse, high-risk sexual behaviour, problems with money, housing or work, and co-occurring disorders such as depression or eating disorders.
  4. Increasing behavioural skills: acquiring and strengthening the skills that will replace the problem behaviours.

In Stage 2 the aim is to move from "quiet desperation" to full emotional experiencing, which usually means treating post-traumatic stress and the painful emotions that the client has avoided, often with exposure-based methods. In Stage 3 the aim is ordinary happiness and unhappiness: building self-respect, solving the ordinary problems of living and pursuing personal goals. Linehan later added a Stage 4, moving from a sense of incompleteness towards a capacity for sustained joy and freedom. The stages explain the phrase that Linehan uses for DBT's ultimate goal: not just staying alive, but building a life worth living.

Exam trap

The four modes of standard DBT are individual psychotherapy, group skills training, telephone coaching (consultation) and the therapist consultation team. "Family genogram construction" is not one of them; the genogram belongs to Murray Bowen's family systems therapy (Chapter 7). A second trap: some textbooks and coaching notes list DBT's "stages" as (1) life-threatening behaviours, (2) therapy-interfering behaviours, (3) quality of life, (4) skills or growth. That list is really the target hierarchy within Stage 1. Linehan's stages are pretreatment, then Stage 1 (behavioural control), Stage 2 (emotional experiencing, often trauma work), Stage 3 (ordinary happiness and unhappiness) and Stage 4 (capacity for joy). If a question asks what comes first in a DBT session, the answer is life-threatening behaviour.

The four skills modules

DBT's skills are its best-known export; many clinics that do not offer full DBT run DBT skills groups. Linehan organised them into four modules, and the dialectic of acceptance and change is built into the very arrangement. Two modules are acceptance skills: core mindfulness and distress tolerance. Two are change skills: emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Mindfulness is the "core" because every other skill depends on it, and it is revisited for a week or two at the start of each other module. Table 5.18 summarises the four.

core mindfulness skills are psychological and behavioural versions of Zen meditation practice, taught without religious content. They start from three states of mind. In emotion mind thinking and behaviour are controlled by the current emotion, and facts are bent to fit the feeling. In reasonable mind the person is cool and logical but cut off from feeling. wise mind is the integration of the two, to which it adds intuitive knowing: the quiet sense of what is true and what is needed. Clients reach wise mind through two sets of skills. The three "what" skills say what to do: observe (notice an experience, without words), describe (put words to it: "tightness in my chest", "the thought that she hates me") and participate (throw oneself fully into the activity of the moment). The three "how" skills say how to do it: non-judgementally (describing facts without labelling them good or bad), one-mindfully (one thing at a time, with full attention) and effectively (doing what works, rather than what is "right" or "fair"). Notice the link to the biosocial theory: a client invalidated for years has learned to judge her feelings harshly, and the non-judgemental stance teaches her to observe them instead. (Mindfulness in the wider sense, and the mindfulness-based therapies, are the subject of Section 5.9.)

distress tolerance skills are for the moments when emotion is at its peak and nothing can be changed right now: getting through a crisis without making it worse, above all without self-harm. The crisis survival skills include STOP (stop, take a step back, observe, proceed mindfully); TIP, added in 2015, which changes body chemistry fast (cold temperature, such as holding the face in cold water, which triggers the dive reflex and slows the heart; intense exercise; paced breathing; paired muscle relaxation); distraction, remembered as "wise mind ACCEPTS" (activities, contributing, comparisons, opposite emotions, pushing away, thoughts, sensations); self-soothing through the five senses; "IMPROVE the moment" (imagery, meaning, prayer, relaxation, one thing in the moment, a brief vacation, encouragement); and weighing the pros and cons of acting on the urge. Distress tolerance also contains the reality acceptance skills, which go to the heart of DBT. radical acceptance is complete acceptance, with mind, body and heart, of facts that cannot be changed, such as a past trauma or a relationship that has ended. It is not approval and not resignation; it is the end of fighting reality. In Linehan's formula, pain is part of life, but pain plus non-acceptance equals suffering. Related skills are turning the mind (choosing acceptance again each time the mind drifts back to "this should not have happened") and willingness (doing what the situation needs, as opposed to wilfulness, refusing to tolerate the moment).

Emotion regulation skills are for changing emotions that can and should be changed. Clients first learn what emotions are for (they communicate to others, motivate action and signal that something matters), how they unfold (prompting event, interpretation, body changes, action urge, expression, after-effects) and how to name them accurately. They then learn to change them. Check the facts asks whether the emotion and its intensity fit the actual situation. When they do not, opposite action means deliberately acting opposite to the emotion's action urge: approaching what one unjustifiably fears, staying engaged instead of hiding when shame is not warranted, and, with unjustified anger, gently avoiding the person or doing something a little kind rather than attacking. When the emotion does fit the facts, problem solving changes the situation causing it. Finally, clients reduce their vulnerability to emotion mind by building positive experiences and mastery, coping ahead with difficult situations, and caring for the body with the PLEASE skills: treat physical illness, balance eating, avoid mood-altering substances, balance sleep and get exercise. Because emotional vulnerability is biological, looking after the body counts as a real regulation skill.

Interpersonal effectiveness skills are for asking for what one wants, refusing unreasonable requests and handling conflict, while protecting both the relationship and one's self-respect. Linehan distinguishes three goals, which the client learns to rank in each situation: objectives effectiveness (getting what one wants), relationship effectiveness and self-respect effectiveness. Each has its own acronym. DEAR MAN, for getting what one wants, stands for describe the situation, express feelings, assert (ask or say no clearly), reinforce (explain what the other person gains), stay mindful (keep to the point, like a "broken record"), appear confident and negotiate. GIVE, for keeping the relationship, means be gentle, act interested, validate and use an easy manner. FAST, for keeping self-respect, means be fair, no excessive apologies, stick to one's values and be truthful. The 2015 manual added "walking the middle path" skills (dialectics, validation and behaviour change), first developed for adolescents and their families.

Watch the skills work together in one night. Priya is 22, a final-year BA student in Pune, eight months into DBT. At 11 p.m. her mother says sharply that she is "wasting her life like always". Her diary card already shows poor sleep and a missed lunch that day (vulnerability factors). Shame and rage surge to 90 out of 100, with the old urge to cut. She uses STOP; then TIP, splashing cold water on her face until her heart slows; then distraction, a playlist and a text to a friend. When the urge has dropped to 40, she calls her therapist for two minutes of coaching. The next morning she uses DEAR MAN with her mother: "Last night you said I was wasting my life (describe). I felt hurt and very angry (express). I'd like you to tell me when you are worried, instead of calling me names (assert). Then I can actually tell you what's going on with my studies (reinforce)." In her next session a chain analysis of the night finds the link where the missed lunch and poor sleep made everything harder, and that week's homework is the PLEASE skills. Her diary card shows no self-harm that week, the first such week in a month.

Exam trap

The four DBT skills modules are mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Any option that includes cognitive restructuring or relaxation as a module is wrong (relaxation appears only inside TIP, and Beck-style cognitive restructuring is not a DBT module). "Distress tolerance is a major treatment modality in which therapy?" DBT, not behaviour therapy, CBT in general or psychodynamic therapy. An ACT question may offer "mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness, problem-solving, behavioural activation" as a distractor for the hexaflex; the first four are DBT modules, the list as a whole is nobody's model, and the hexaflex is described in Section 5.9. Mindfulness and distress tolerance are the acceptance modules; emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness are the change modules.

Evidence, adaptations and DBT among the treatments for borderline personality disorder

DBT has more randomised trials behind it than any other psychotherapy for borderline personality disorder. After 1991 the critical question was whether it worked for specific reasons or simply because it was an organised, enthusiastic treatment.

Key study: DBT versus treatment by community experts (Linehan and colleagues, 2006)

Linehan's group recruited 101 women with borderline personality disorder and recent suicidal or self-injurious behaviour, and randomly assigned them to a year of DBT or of therapy with community therapists who had been nominated by local mental health leaders as experts with difficult clients. The comparison therapists were experienced, believed in their own methods and received supervision, which controlled for therapist enthusiasm and attention. Over the treatment year and a further year of follow-up, about 23 percent of the DBT clients made a suicide attempt, against about 46 percent of the expert-treated clients; the DBT group also needed fewer hospital admissions for suicidal thinking, made fewer emergency visits, and dropped out of therapy less often. Both groups improved in depression and suicidal thinking. The conclusion: DBT's advantage was specific to suicidal behaviour and to keeping clients in treatment, and it could not be explained by general therapist expertise alone.

Not every trial has favoured DBT. In 2009 Shelley McMain and colleagues in Toronto assigned 180 people with borderline personality disorder to a year of DBT or of general psychiatric management, a structured but less intensive approach developed by John Gunderson. Both groups improved substantially, with no differences in suicidal behaviour, self-harm or other outcomes. The lesson is not that DBT is weak but that any well-organised, committed treatment does far better than the disorganised care these clients usually receive (Chapter 8 develops this theme). Cochrane reviews published in 2012 and 2020 found DBT the most-studied psychotherapy for the disorder, with benefits for self-harm and overall severity, but judged the certainty of the evidence low because many trials were small. In Britain, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommended in 2009 that a comprehensive DBT programme be considered for women with borderline personality disorder for whom reducing recurrent self-harm is a priority. And the skills appear to be an active ingredient: Andrada Neacsiu, Shireen Rizvi and Linehan (2010) found that clients' increasing use of skills partly accounted for their reductions in suicide attempts and depression and their better control of anger.

DBT has spread through adaptation. DBT for adolescents (Alec Miller, Jill Rathus and Linehan, 2007) is shorter, brings parents into a multifamily skills group and adds the "walking the middle path" module; a multisite trial by Elizabeth McCauley and colleagues (2018) found that it reduced suicide attempts and self-harm in suicidal adolescents during treatment more than supportive therapy did. There are versions for substance use (aiming at "dialectical abstinence": total commitment to abstinence together with a non-judgemental plan for recovering quickly from a lapse), for binge eating and bulimia, and for PTSD, where Melanie Harned's DBT prolonged exposure protocol adds Edna Foa's prolonged exposure (Chapter 4) once behavioural control is achieved, Linehan's Stage 2 made concrete. Thomas Lynch's radically open DBT turns the model around for people who are over-controlled rather than under-controlled (chronic depression, anorexia nervosa), teaching openness rather than restraint.

Clinical link

Even if you never work in a full DBT programme, three of its ideas will change how you work with any client in crisis. First, validate before you try to change anything: a client who feels understood can listen; one who feels judged cannot. Second, treat self-harm as a behaviour with functions to be analysed, not as manipulation or "attention-seeking", a phrase DBT therapists avoid because it invalidates real distress and blocks curiosity about what the behaviour is doing for the person. Third, look after yourself and use your team: Linehan built the consultation team into the treatment because working with chronic suicidality is hard, and burnt-out therapists become rigid and invalidating without noticing.

5.9 The third wave II: acceptance and commitment therapy and the mindfulness-based therapiesMedium yield · 5 Qs

In 1978 a young assistant professor at the University of North Carolina at Greensboro was arguing a point in a faculty meeting when his heart began to pound, his throat tightened, and he found that he could not finish his sentence. It was his first panic attack. Over the next few years the panic spread, as panic does: to lectures, to telephone calls, to lifts, to restaurants, until there were whole parts of his life he could no longer enter. The irony was painful. He was a behaviour therapist who knew every technique for reducing anxiety, he tried them all, and the harder he fought the fear the more of his life it took. Then one night, a few years in, he woke in the grip of one of the worst attacks he had ever had. Lying on the carpet of his flat, he made a decision that felt more like surrender than strategy. He would stop running from his own experience. Whatever his mind and body served up, he would let it be there, and he would still move towards the life he wanted.

The man on the carpet was Steven C. Hayes (born 1948), whom you met in Section 5.1 as the psychologist who named the third wave. His panic did not vanish that night, but his war with it ended, and out of that decision, and out of two decades of laboratory research on how human language works, grew acceptance and commitment therapy. Section 5.8 showed how Marsha Linehan brought acceptance into behaviour therapy for clients overwhelmed by emotion. This section follows the two other main roads into the third wave: Hayes's therapy, built on a behavioural theory of language, and the mindfulness-based programmes of Jon Kabat-Zinn and of Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale, built on meditation. Their starting points were very different, but they arrived at the idea that Section 5.1 promised: you can change your relationship with a thought without changing the thought itself.

Steven Hayes and the origins of ACT

Hayes had trained as a behaviour analyst in the tradition of B. F. Skinner, and his early research concerned rules. Animals learn from the consequences of what they do; human beings can also learn from verbal rules ("study hard and you will get in"), which lets us avoid dangers we have never met. But behaviour analysts found in the laboratory that people following a rule become strangely insensitive to what is actually happening, and go on obeying the rule after the real consequences have changed. A depressed client who lives by "I must never let anyone see that I'm struggling" may go on hiding long after hiding has cost him his friends.

In the early 1980s Hayes and his student Robert D. Zettle turned these ideas into a treatment, which they first called comprehensive distancing, and Zettle compared it with Beck's cognitive therapy for depression in small studies during that decade. The approach was rethought over the following years and emerged, renamed, in the 1999 book Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: An Experiential Approach to Behavior Change, by Hayes, Kirk D. Strosahl and Kelly G. Wilson. By then Hayes had moved to the University of Nevada, Reno, which became the centre of the movement. acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) is a behaviour therapy that combines acceptance and mindfulness processes with commitment and behaviour-change processes in order to increase a person's psychological flexibility: the capacity to stay in contact with the present moment, unwanted thoughts and feelings included, and to act in the service of chosen values. The name is pronounced as the single word "act", not as three letters, and the choice is deliberate, because the therapy is about action. Hayes's own short summary is the acronym itself: Accept your reactions and be present, Choose a valued direction, and Take action.

Notice the family tree. ACT is a behaviour therapy, a grandchild of Skinner's radical behaviourism, even though it speaks of values, meaning and the self in a way that can sound humanistic or even spiritual. Its philosophy of science is called functional contextualism, and two ideas define it. First, the thing to be studied is always an act in its context: a thought, a feeling or a behaviour cannot be understood apart from the situation and the history in which it occurs. Second, the test of an idea is not whether it is "true" in the abstract but whether it works in achieving a stated goal; ACT therapists call this its workability. Here ACT parts company with Beck. Beck's cognitive therapy asks of a thought, "Is it accurate? What is the evidence?" ACT asks, "If you hold on to this thought and let it steer you, does that work? Does it take you towards the life you want?" A thought can be perfectly true ("I might stammer in my viva") and still be unworkable, if it keeps you away from the viva altogether.

Put another way, second-wave therapy tries to change the form of a thought (its content) or its frequency. ACT leaves the form alone and changes the thought's function, the effect it has on what the person does, by changing the context in which it occurs. "I can't cope, so stay home" stops being a command once it is experienced as "there's my mind telling me I can't cope again", and the person is free to go out anyway.

Exam trap

"Acceptance and commitment therapy was developed by..." Steven Hayes and colleagues (Kirk Strosahl and Kelly Wilson; the key book is from 1999). Not Marsha Linehan, who developed DBT (Section 5.8); not Aaron Beck or Albert Ellis, the second-wave founders. ACT is a third-wave behaviour therapy rooted in Skinner's radical behaviourism and in functional contextualism, not a psychoanalytic or humanistic therapy, however much it talks about values. Its earlier name was comprehensive distancing.

Relational frame theory: why language makes us suffer

Here is the puzzle that drove Hayes's research. A dog that is safe, fed and warm is, as far as anyone can tell, at peace. A human being who is safe, fed and warm can be in agony. She can sit in a comfortable hall at a family wedding and be overwhelmed by grief because the song reminds her of her late father; she can lie awake in a soft bed, tormented by a future that has not happened. Why are human beings the only animals that suffer in this way? Hayes's answer is language, and his account of how language works is relational frame theory (RFT), set out in full in 2001 by Hayes, Dermot Barnes-Holmes and Bryan Roche in Relational Frame Theory: A Post-Skinnerian Account of Human Language and Cognition.

The theory grew out of a striking discovery by the American behaviour analyst Murray Sidman (1923 to 2019). In 1971 Sidman worked with a young man with severe intellectual disability who could match spoken words to pictures but could not read. Sidman taught him one new thing: to match spoken words to printed words. Without any further teaching, the young man could now match printed words to pictures, which is reading with understanding. Relations he had never been taught had appeared by themselves. Sidman called the phenomenon stimulus equivalence. Nothing in classical or operant conditioning predicted it, and attempts to demonstrate it convincingly in non-human animals have largely failed.

RFT generalises Sidman's finding. Its central claim is that the core of human language and thought is derived relational responding. From early childhood we learn to relate things to one another not only by their physical properties but by social convention, and once we have learned a relation in one direction we derive others that were never taught. A small child offered a choice between five one-rupee coins and a single hundred-rupee note will take the coins, because five things are more than one thing. An older child takes the note, because she has learned a relation ("worth more than") that has nothing to do with size or number. RFT calls such learned patterns of relating relational frames, and three properties define them:

  • Mutual entailment. If A is related to B, a relation from B back to A is derived. Teach a child that the English word "dog" goes with the Hindi word "kutta", and she will, untaught, say "dog" when asked what "kutta" means. Teach her that A is bigger than B, and she knows without being told that B is smaller than A.
  • Combinatorial entailment. If A is related to B and B is related to C, a relation between A and C is derived. If Rahul is taller than Sita and Sita is taller than Ravi, nobody needs to tell you that Rahul is taller than Ravi.
  • Transformation of stimulus functions. The psychological effects of one thing are transferred to the things it is related to. A child bitten by a dog may come to fear not only dogs but the word "dog", the word "kutta" and a picture in a storybook, none of which ever bit anyone.

Humans learn many families of frames: coordination (same as), distinction (different from), opposition (opposite of), comparison (more than, better than), hierarchy (part of, a kind of), temporal frames (before, after), causal frames (if, then) and deictic frames (I and you, here and there, now and then), which underlie perspective taking and, as you will see, the sense of self.

Now look at what this machinery does to suffering. Because of the transformation of functions, a word, a memory or an image can carry much of the pain of the event it is related to, so the past can be brought into the present at any moment. Because of comparison frames, we can rank ourselves against anyone ("my cousin got into NIMHANS; I am the failure of the family") and against ideals no one could meet. Because of temporal and causal frames, we can live through disasters that have not happened and may never happen. And because language gives us problem solving, we develop a rule that serves us brilliantly in the world outside the skin: if something is unpleasant, work out how to get rid of it, and get rid of it. That rule fixes a leaking tap or a weak exam strategy. Applied to the world inside the skin, to anxiety, grief, unwanted memories and painful thoughts, it backfires, because a private experience is not a leaking tap. The very thought "I must stop feeling anxious" is related to anxiety, and so it brings anxiety with it.

From this analysis come the two central ideas of ACT's account of psychological problems. The first is cognitive fusion: the state in which a person's behaviour is dominated by the literal content of his thoughts, so that he responds to words as if they were the things they describe. Fused with the thought "I'm worthless", a person does not experience himself as having a thought about worthlessness; he simply is worthless, and he acts accordingly. In a phrase from the ACT literature, he looks at the world from his thoughts instead of looking at them. The second is experiential avoidance, defined by Hayes and his colleagues in 1996 as the unwillingness to remain in contact with particular private experiences (bodily sensations, emotions, thoughts, memories and urges), together with attempts to change their form or frequency or to escape the situations that bring them on, even when those attempts cause harm. Drinking to blunt grief, avoiding the metro because of panic, pushing intrusive thoughts away, seeking reassurance again and again, sleeping through the afternoon to escape low mood: each brings short-term relief, and each, practised for months, makes the person's life smaller. ACT's slogan for this is blunt: control is the problem, not the solution.

Key study: "Don't think of a white bear" (Wegner, Schneider, Carter and White, 1987)

In 1863 the Russian novelist Fyodor Dostoevsky challenged his readers to try not to think of a polar bear, predicting that "the cursed thing will come to mind every minute." More than a century later the social psychologist Daniel M. Wegner (1948 to 2013) and his colleagues at Trinity University in Texas put the challenge to the test. Students sat alone and spoke their thoughts aloud for five minutes. Those told to try not to think of a white bear, and to ring a bell whenever they did, rang it on average more than once a minute. Then everyone was invited to think freely about white bears. The students who had first suppressed the thought now thought about the bear more often than students who had been allowed to think about it from the start: a rebound effect. Deliberate suppression, in other words, not only fails but can backfire. Later reviews found the rebound real but smaller and less consistent than this first study suggested. The experiment became a cornerstone of the third wave's case against fighting one's own thoughts.

Exam trap

Relational frame theory (Hayes, Barnes-Holmes and Roche, 2001) is the theory of language and cognition on which ACT is built. It has nothing to do with REBT despite the shared letter R, and it is not George Kelly's personal construct theory (Section 5.5). DBT and ACT both use acceptance, but RFT belongs to ACT alone. Keep the problem terms and process terms apart: fusion and experiential avoidance are the problems; defusion and acceptance are the remedies. The word is defusion (taking the fuse out), not "diffusion", and it has nothing to do with Erikson's identity diffusion (Developmental book).

Psychological flexibility and the hexaflex

If fusion and avoidance make people rigid, the aim of therapy is the opposite: psychological flexibility. Hayes and his colleagues define it as the ability to contact the present moment fully, as a conscious human being and without needless defence, and on that basis to persist in or change one's behaviour in the service of chosen values. Read the definition twice and notice what is missing: there is nothing in it about feeling better, thinking positively or getting rid of symptoms. A flexible person may still have panic attacks, or a voice in his head that calls him a failure. What has changed is that these experiences no longer decide what he does.

Psychological flexibility is built from six core processes, which ACT therapists draw as the six corners of a hexagon called the hexaflex (Figure 5.10). Each process has an opposite, a pattern of psychological inflexibility, and every process is linked to every other, so that work on one tends to strengthen the rest. The easiest way to understand them is to follow a client through them.

Imran is a 27-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru who has had panic attacks for two years. He has stopped taking the metro, turned down a promotion because it meant presenting to clients, keeps a water bottle and his phone in his hand "just in case", and has not visited his mother, who is in hospital across the city, because the journey means a crowded bus. He has tried breathing exercises, herbal remedies, two glasses of whisky every night, hours of searching his symptoms online, and a strict rule never to go anywhere he cannot leave quickly.

The ACT therapist begins not with a process but with creative hopelessness. Instead of offering one more technique for controlling panic, she helps Imran list everything he has tried, and then asks of each strategy: How did it work in the short term? How has it worked in the long term? What has it cost you? The answer is painfully clear. Each strategy brought a little relief and a little more shrinking of his life. What is hopeless is not Imran but the agenda of controlling anxiety, and seeing that clearly makes room for something new. The therapist may offer the metaphor of a Chinese finger trap, the woven tube that tightens the harder you pull your fingers out of it and loosens only when you push them in.

acceptance, also called willingness, is the alternative. It means actively and openly making room for unwanted thoughts, feelings and sensations, without unnecessary attempts to change their form or frequency. Acceptance is not resignation, not liking, and not grimly putting up with something while waiting for it to go away. Imran might be asked to notice the sensations of panic in his chest as a curious scientist would, describing their size, shape, temperature and movement, and to breathe around them and allow them to be there. ACT draws a useful distinction between clean pain, the natural discomfort that life brings (anxiety before a presentation, grief after a death), and dirty pain, the extra suffering we create by struggling against the clean pain (panic about the anxiety, shame about the panic, a life organised around avoiding both). Acceptance cannot remove the first; it can dissolve much of the second. Its opposite pole on the hexaflex is experiential avoidance.

cognitive defusion means stepping back from thoughts so that they are seen for what they are: words and pictures passing through the mind, not commands or truths. Its opposite is fusion. Defusion does not test whether a thought is true. Imran's thought "I'm going to collapse" is not weighed against the evidence, as it would be in Beck's therapy; instead he learns to notice it. He may add a prefix ("I'm having the thought that I'm going to collapse"), thank his mind for the thought ("Thanks, mind, very helpful"), name the story ("Ah, the 'I'll collapse on the bus' story again"), or imagine placing each thought on a leaf and watching it float away down a stream. One of the oldest defusion exercises goes back to the structuralist psychologist Edward Bradford Titchener (1867 to 1927), who noted in 1916 that a word repeated aloud quickly loses its meaning. Say "milk" aloud fifty times and it becomes a strange sound; say "I'm weak, I'm weak, I'm weak" fifty times and the same thing happens. The thought is still there, but its grip on behaviour, its function in ACT language, has loosened.

contact with the present moment is flexible, non-judgemental attention to what is happening here and now, outside and inside the skin. Its opposite is attention captured by the remembered past and the imagined future: rumination about what went wrong, worry about what might. ACT uses short mindfulness exercises to build it (noticing five things you can see and five you can hear, following the breath, eating a meal with full attention), but in ACT mindfulness is a set of processes, not a meditation programme.

self-as-context is the hardest process to explain and perhaps the most important. ACT distinguishes three senses of self. The conceptualised self, or self-as-content, is the story we tell about who we are: "I'm an anxious person", "I'm the weak one in my family", even "I'm the topper". Fusion with this story is a form of inflexibility, because we then defend the story at any cost, and it is the opposite pole of this process. The self-as-process is ongoing awareness of experience as it happens ("right now I'm feeling anxious"). Self-as-context, also called the observing self, is the perspective from which all of this is noticed: the "I" that was there when you were five, is here now and will be there tomorrow, whatever thoughts and feelings pass through. RFT traces it to deictic frames (I, here and now, as distinct from you, there and then). The classic image is the chessboard metaphor. Your thoughts and feelings are the black and white pieces, locked in war, and you have been trying to be a white piece and defeat the black ones. But you are not a piece. You are the board, which holds every piece, is in contact with all of them, and is not harmed by the battle. A gentler version says that you are the sky and thoughts and feelings are the weather.

values, in ACT, are freely chosen directions for living, the qualities a person wants his actions to have: being a caring son, a curious learner, a reliable friend. A value is not a goal. A goal can be completed and ticked off (visiting his mother on Sunday); a value can never be finished, only lived, like travelling west by compass, which never brings you to a place called "West". Values work proceeds through questions such as "What do you want your life to stand for?" or "At your eightieth birthday, what would you hope the people you love would say about you?" The opposite pole is a life without clear values, steered by avoidance, by other people's approval or by the rules of the mind. ACT adds that pain and values are two sides of one coin: we hurt where we care. Imran's anxiety about the hospital visit is intense precisely because he loves his mother.

committed action, finally, is building larger and larger patterns of effective action linked to those values, step by step, and returning to them after every slip. Its opposite is inaction, impulsivity or persistent avoidance. Here ACT shows its behaviour-therapy roots, using goal setting, skills training and exposure; but exposure is framed differently. Its purpose is not to make the fear go down but to practise willingness in the service of something that matters. Imran's first committed action might be two stops on the metro with his hands free of the water bottle, carrying his fear with him; his larger one is the bus ride to his mother's bedside. ACT's passengers on the bus metaphor captures the stance. Imran is the driver; his thoughts, memories and sensations are noisy, frightening passengers who threaten him whenever he turns towards where he wants to go. They can shout, but they cannot take the wheel, and for all their noise they have never actually been able to hurt him.

The six processes are often grouped into pairs. The Australian ACT trainer Russ Harris summarises them as open up (acceptance and defusion), be present (contact with the present moment and self-as-context) and do what matters (values and committed action). Hayes and his colleagues group them a little differently, into two overlapping sets of four. Acceptance, defusion, the present moment and self-as-context are the mindfulness and acceptance processes; the present moment, self-as-context, values and committed action are the commitment and behaviour-change processes. ACT in practice: evidence and critics

An ACT session looks quite unlike a cognitive therapy session. There are few worksheets and little disputing; there are many metaphors, paradoxes and experiential exercises. The reason follows from RFT. If language is what entangles people, then more argument, which is more language, is likely to entangle them further. A metaphor such as the chessboard does not argue; it lets the client see his situation from a new angle. Courses of ACT range from a single workshop to sixteen or more sessions, and it is delivered individually, in groups and through self-help books. Because its target, flexibility, is the same whatever the diagnosis, ACT is a transdiagnostic therapy: one model is applied across depression, anxiety, psychosis, addiction, chronic pain and workplace stress.

Key study: four sessions of ACT for psychosis (Bach and Hayes, 2002)

Could a brief, acceptance-based therapy help people with hallucinations and delusions? Patricia Bach and Steven Hayes randomly assigned 80 psychiatric inpatients with positive psychotic symptoms either to their usual treatment or to usual treatment plus just four individual sessions of ACT. The sessions taught patients to notice their voices and unusual beliefs as experiences, without either arguing with them or obeying them, and to act on their own values. Over the next four months the ACT group was rehospitalised at about half the rate of the comparison group. The most interesting finding was this: the ACT patients were more willing to report their symptoms, but they believed them less. The symptoms had not disappeared; their power over behaviour had shrunk, which is exactly what defusion predicts. The trial was small and the follow-up short, and a later replication by Brandon Gaudiano and James Herbert (2006) found smaller effects, but it showed that acceptance could be offered even to people with psychosis.

ACT now has hundreds of randomised trials. The Society of Clinical Psychology (Division 12 of the American Psychological Association) lists it as having strong research support for chronic pain, and modest support for depression, mixed anxiety problems, obsessive-compulsive disorder and psychosis. Meta-analyses, such as one by A-Tjak and colleagues in 2015, find ACT clearly better than waiting lists, placebo conditions and treatment as usual, and roughly as effective as established CBT, though not clearly better.

Critics have raised three objections. First, in 2008 the Swedish psychologist Lars-Göran Öst reviewed the trials of the third-wave therapies and concluded that their methods were, on average, less rigorous than those of CBT trials from the same period, and that none of the third-wave therapies yet met the formal criteria for an empirically supported treatment. The trials have since improved. Second, Stefan G. Hofmann and Gordon Asmundson (2008) asked whether the third wave was a "new wave or old hat", pointing out that cognitive therapy had always contained acceptance-like elements: Beck himself had described distancing, learning to regard one's thoughts as hypotheses rather than facts, in 1976. On this view ACT is a valuable member of the CBT family, not a revolution against it. Third, relational frame theory is complex, is not accepted by all behaviour analysts, and the link between the laboratory theory and the metaphors used in the clinic is looser than ACT's advocates sometimes suggest. What is mindfulness?

The word "mindfulness" has now appeared in this chapter several times. It is time to define it properly, because the next two therapies are built on it. The most quoted definition comes from Jon Kabat-Zinn, whose work is described next: mindfulness is "paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgementally" (1994). In 2004 a group of researchers led by Scott Bishop proposed a two-part working definition for science. The first part is the self-regulation of attention, so that attention is kept on immediate experience (the breath, a sound, a sensation, a thought as it arises). The second is a particular orientation towards that experience, marked by curiosity, openness and acceptance.

The practice is ancient. The Pali word sati, usually translated as mindfulness, means awareness or remembering (remembering to come back to the present), and training in it has been part of Buddhist practice for some 2,500 years, especially in the vipassana ("insight") meditation of the Theravada tradition and in Zen. The clinical programmes deliberately teach it in a secular form, without religious belief, so that anyone can practise it.

It helps to know what mindfulness is not. It is not relaxation: a mindful person may become calmer, but the instruction is to notice whatever is present, including tension, rather than to produce a relaxed state. It is not emptying the mind: the mind will wander, again and again, and the practice consists precisely in noticing that it has wandered and bringing it back, kindly and without self-criticism. It is not positive thinking, and it is not an escape from difficulty; if anything, it is a way of turning towards difficulty with steadier attention. How often does the mind wander? In a 2010 study, Matthew A. Killingsworth and Daniel T. Gilbert of Harvard used a smartphone application to ask about 2,250 adults, at random moments, what they were doing and what they were thinking about. Their minds were wandering in nearly half of the samples, and people were generally less happy when their minds wandered than when they attended to what they were doing: "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind," as the paper's title put it.

Mindfulness can be practised formally, in set periods of meditation, or informally, by bringing full attention to ordinary activities such as walking, eating or washing dishes. Notice that you have now met it in three different roles. In DBT it is a set of skills, the first of the four skills modules (Section 5.8). In ACT it is a set of four processes within the hexaflex, practised mainly through brief exercises and metaphors. In the mindfulness-based programmes that follow, formal meditation practice is the heart of the treatment itself.

Indian context

Many Indian clients already know a version of these practices. Vipassana is taught across India in ten-day silent residential courses, most widely through the centres founded by S. N. Goenka (1924 to 2013), the best known of them at Igatpuri in Maharashtra, and in the 1990s Kiran Bedi, then in charge of Delhi's Tihar Jail, introduced vipassana courses for prisoners. The mindful movement used in MBSR is drawn from hatha yoga. Familiarity helps, but it can also mislead: a client may expect a religious practice, or a technique for blanking the mind. A clinician should explain that clinical mindfulness is secular, that it is about noticing rather than stopping thoughts, and that it is offered as a skill, not as a belief.

Exam trap

Mindfulness is attention to the present moment, on purpose and non-judgementally (Kabat-Zinn). It is not relaxation, not suppression of thoughts and not positive thinking. If a question asks from which tradition DBT's core mindfulness skills are derived, the answer is Zen Buddhism (Section 5.8), not Stoicism (the root of Ellis's and Beck's cognitive idea), psychoanalysis or existentialism. MBSR draws on both Zen and vipassana, and on hatha yoga.

Jon Kabat-Zinn and mindfulness-based stress reduction

Jon Kabat-Zinn (born 1944) did not set out to be a psychologist. He earned a doctorate in molecular biology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1971, in the laboratory of the Nobel laureate Salvador Luria, and had begun meditating as a student, learning from Zen teachers and later from teachers of vipassana. In 1979, during a meditation retreat, an idea came to him, he later said, in a flash lasting only seconds: he could take the essence of these practices, without the Buddhist framework, to the patients whom hospital medicine had failed to help, people living with chronic pain, heart disease, cancer and the stress of long illness. That same year he founded the Stress Reduction Clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School in Worcester. His programme, first called the Stress Reduction and Relaxation Program, became known as mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR). His 1990 book about it, Full Catastrophe Living, took its title from a line in Zorba the Greek, in which Zorba sums up his life as "wife, children, house, everything: the full catastrophe". The point of the title is that the programme is not an escape from life's troubles but a way of living fully in the middle of them.

MBSR is an eight-week group course, with classes that can include thirty or more people with a wide range of problems. Participants meet weekly for about two and a half hours, attend one all-day silent session late in the course, and are asked to practise at home for about 45 minutes a day, six days a week, using guided recordings. Four formal practices form its core:

  • The body scan: lying down, the participant moves attention slowly through the body from the toes to the top of the head, noticing each sensation, including pain, without trying to change it.
  • Sitting meditation: attention to the breath, widening later to sounds, thoughts and feelings, returning gently to the breath whenever the mind wanders.
  • Mindful movement: gentle hatha yoga postures, done with attention to the body's sensations and limits rather than to achievement.
  • Walking meditation: walking slowly and attending to each step.

The first class famously begins with the raisin exercise. Each participant is given a raisin and asked to examine it as if he had never seen one before: its weight, its wrinkles, the light on its surface, its smell, the faint sound it makes when rolled between the fingers, and finally its taste, chewed slowly. Most people discover how much of ordinary life, eating included, they live on "automatic pilot". Alongside the formal practice, participants practise informally, bringing the same attention to brushing their teeth, drinking their morning chai or waiting at a traffic signal.

What does MBSR achieve? In his first published study (1982), Kabat-Zinn followed 51 patients with chronic pain through the programme; many reported large reductions in pain and in mood disturbance, though the study had no control group. In a later trial (1998), patients with the skin disease psoriasis who listened to guided meditation while receiving ultraviolet light treatment cleared their skin significantly faster than patients who received the light treatment alone, an early hint that a mental practice could influence a bodily process. Large reviews have since given a more sober verdict. A meta-analysis of 47 trials by Madhav Goyal and colleagues (2014) found that mindfulness meditation programmes produce small to moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and pain, of about the size seen with antidepressant medication in primary care, but are not better than other active treatments such as exercise. MBSR is best understood as a broad programme for stress, pain and illness, not as a treatment for a particular psychiatric disorder. For a disorder-focused version, the next step was needed.

Exam trap

MBSR: Jon Kabat-Zinn, 1979, University of Massachusetts Medical School, an eight-week group course for stress, chronic pain and illness. MBCT: Segal, Williams and Teasdale, 2002, MBSR combined with elements of cognitive therapy to prevent depressive relapse. Do not confuse mindfulness with the relaxation response of the Harvard cardiologist Herbert Benson (1935 to 2022), described in 1975, which aims at physiological relaxation and grew out of research on Transcendental Meditation, a mantra-based technique of concentration. Mindfulness trains open, non-judgemental awareness; relaxation is at most a side effect.

Segal, Williams and Teasdale: mindfulness-based cognitive therapy

Section 5.3 ended with a warning: when depression lifts, the negative schema does not vanish; it goes quiet and can be reawakened. That warning has numbers attached. Commonly cited estimates are that about half of the people who recover from a first episode of major depression will have another, that the risk rises to about 70 percent after two episodes, and to about 90 percent after three. Antidepressant medication protects against relapse only for as long as it is taken. Cognitive therapy has a lasting protective effect (Section 5.11), but it needs a trained therapist for every client. Could a cheaper, group-based programme be taught to people while they were well, to stop the next episode before it began?

That was the question put in the early 1990s, through a research network on depression funded by the MacArthur Foundation in the United States, to three cognitive psychologists: Zindel V. Segal in Toronto, J. Mark G. Williams in Wales (later at Oxford) and John D. Teasdale in Cambridge. Teasdale had already proposed the theory they would build on. His differential activation hypothesis (1988) holds that during an episode of depression, sad mood becomes strongly associated with a particular pattern of negative thinking: global, hopeless and self-critical. After recovery, a small dip in mood (a bad day, a sharp word from a colleague) can reactivate the whole pattern, and the reactivated thinking deepens the mood, which feeds more negative thinking. The ease with which this happens is called cognitive reactivity, and each episode strengthens the association, which is one reason the risk climbs with every episode. What turns a passing sadness into relapse is not the sadness itself but rumination: repetitive, analytical brooding on the mood, its causes and its meaning ("Why am I like this? What is wrong with me? Why can't I snap out of it?").

The three researchers first planned a kind of "attentional control training", a slimmed-down maintenance version of cognitive therapy. Then they visited Kabat-Zinn's clinic in Massachusetts, and what they saw changed the plan. The MBSR instructors were not trying to fix their participants' problems; they practised alongside them and taught a different way of being with difficulty. Segal, Williams and Teasdale came to describe two modes of mind. In the doing mode (or "driven-doing" mode), the mind notices a gap between how things are and how it wants them to be, and works to close the gap. Doing mode is excellent for building a bridge or finishing a syllabus. Applied to a sad mood, it becomes rumination: the mind keeps asking why the gap exists and how to close it, and in doing so keeps the mood alive. In the being mode, the mind allows things to be as they are for the moment, attends to experience directly rather than through concepts, and treats thoughts as passing events. The new therapy would train people to recognise when they had slipped into driven-doing, and to shift deliberately into being.

The key skill has a name. decentering is the ability to observe one's thoughts and feelings as temporary events in the mind, rather than as facts or as accurate reflections of oneself. The phrase MBCT participants learn is "thoughts are not facts", which is also the title of one of its sessions. The old thought "I'm a failure" may still appear when the mood dips; a person who can decentre sees it as a familiar visitor, a sign that the old pattern has been touched, and not as the truth about her life. Teasdale called the capacity metacognitive awareness, and his research found that it predicts lower relapse. Figure 5.11 shows the relapse cycle and where MBCT breaks it.

The result was mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), set out in 2002 in Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression: A New Approach to Preventing Relapse. MBCT is an eight-week group programme, usually for up to about twelve people, meeting weekly for about two hours, with daily home practice. It keeps the core of MBSR (the raisin exercise, the body scan, sitting meditation and mindful movement) and adds elements of cognitive therapy: teaching about depression and how relapse happens, exercises on the links between thoughts and feelings, lists of activities that bring mastery and pleasure (Section 5.4), and the drawing up of a relapse signature (the person's own early warning signs) and an action plan. What it does not borrow from cognitive therapy is the challenging of thought content. In the first edition of the manual, the eight sessions were:

  1. Automatic pilot.
  2. Dealing with barriers.
  3. Mindfulness of the breath.
  4. Staying present.
  5. Allowing, or letting be.
  6. Thoughts are not facts.
  7. How can I best take care of myself?
  8. Using what has been learned to deal with future moods.

Two exercises show how MBCT works. The first is the signature practice of the programme, the three-minute breathing space, a mini-meditation in three steps that can be used anywhere, especially when mood starts to dip. First, awareness: stop, and acknowledge what is here right now (thoughts, feelings, bodily sensations) without changing it. Second, gathering: narrow attention to the breath in the belly for a minute. Third, expanding: widen attention to the whole body, its posture and the space around it. Teachers describe its shape as an hourglass: wide, narrow, wide again. It is not a way of escaping the mood; it is a way of stepping out of automatic pilot, so that the next step is chosen rather than driven.

The second is an exercise from an early session, and you have met its situation before. Participants are asked to imagine walking down a street and seeing someone they know on the other side. They smile and wave; the other person does not respond and walks on. What went through your mind, and what did you feel? Some participants think "She's ignoring me; she must be upset with me" and feel hurt; others think "She didn't see me" and feel nothing much; some think "She's probably preoccupied; I hope she's all right." In Section 5.3 this was the textbook case of arbitrary inference, and a cognitive therapist would go on to examine the evidence. MBCT draws a different lesson. The same event produces different thoughts in different people, and in the same person in different moods (participants in a low mood find themselves far more likely to think "she's ignoring me"). Thoughts, in other words, are interpretations shaped by mood, events in the mind rather than readings of reality. Once that is seen, there is no need to argue with them.

Key study: MBCT halves relapse in recurrent depression (Teasdale, Segal, Williams and colleagues, 2000)

The first trial of MBCT was run at three sites, in Cambridge, Bangor and Toronto. The researchers recruited 145 people who had recovered from recurrent major depression and were no longer taking antidepressants, and randomly assigned them to treatment as usual or to treatment as usual plus the eight-week MBCT programme. They then followed them for 60 weeks. For participants who had had three or more previous episodes (about three-quarters of the sample), MBCT almost halved the relapse rate: 37 percent relapsed, against 66 percent of those receiving usual care. Surprisingly, for participants with only two previous episodes MBCT made no difference. A replication by Ma and Teasdale (2004) found the same pattern (36 percent against 78 percent). The explanation offered was that in people with many past episodes, relapse is set off less by major life events and more by the autonomous, self-perpetuating cycle of mood and rumination that MBCT targets. Later trials led by Willem Kuyken found MBCT about as protective as continuing maintenance antidepressant medication, and a 2016 pooled analysis of nine trials found that it reduced the risk of relapse over 60 weeks by roughly a third compared with treatments that did not include it.

On the strength of this evidence, Britain's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends MBCT for people who are currently well but have a history of recurrent depression, originally those with three or more episodes. Notice who MBCT is for. It is a relapse-prevention programme, taught to people when they are in remission, not a treatment for an acute episode. A person in the depths of depression usually cannot concentrate well enough to meditate, and the programme was never designed for that state. Clinical link

Mindfulness is not harmless for everyone. Intensive meditation can occasionally bring up traumatic memories, dissociation, panic or, rarely, psychotic symptoms. MBCT teachers therefore interview every candidate before the course, usually defer people who are acutely depressed or actively suicidal, take special care with people who have a history of trauma or psychosis, and tell participants that they may step out of any practice at any time. Teachers are also expected to have a personal meditation practice of their own, because the programme is taught from the inside rather than read from a manual.

Compassion-focused therapy: when logic is not enough

The British clinical psychologist Paul Gilbert (born 1951), working with depressed patients in the National Health Service in Derby, kept hearing the same sentence from people who were doing well in cognitive therapy on paper. They could fill in a thought record, find the evidence against "I'm a failure" and write a sensible balanced alternative. Then they would say: "I can see the logic, but I don't feel any different." Gilbert noticed something about how they said their alternative thoughts to themselves. They often used a cold, flat or impatient inner voice ("Oh, come on, there's evidence you're not a failure, stop being so pathetic"), the same voice they used to attack themselves. The content of the thought had changed; its emotional tone had not. And the patients who said this most were those high in shame and self-criticism, very often people who had grown up with abuse, neglect, bullying or constant criticism, and who had little experience of being soothed by anyone, themselves included.

Why should a warm inner voice matter more than a correct argument? Gilbert's answer came from evolutionary psychology and the neuroscience of emotion. Human beings, he argued, have a "tricky brain". Our "old brain" contains emotional and motivational systems that evolved long ago to deal with threats and rewards; our "new brain" adds imagination, language, self-monitoring and rumination. The combination means that we can switch on our threat system with our own thoughts, attacking ourselves as if we were our own predator, and a self-critical thought ("you pathetic idiot") is processed by the body much as an attack from outside would be. None of this is anyone's fault: we did not choose our evolved brains, nor the families and circumstances that shaped them. But, in Gilbert's often-repeated phrase, it is not our fault, but it is our responsibility: once we understand how our minds work, we can choose to train them. Hearing this is itself a relief to people drowning in shame.

compassion-focused therapy (CFT) is an integrative therapy, developed by Gilbert from the 1990s onwards and set out in The Compassionate Mind (2009) and Compassion Focused Therapy (2010), that helps people high in shame and self-criticism to develop compassion for themselves and others, so that they can feel safe and soothed and not merely think differently. Gilbert defines compassion as "a sensitivity to suffering in self and others, with a commitment to try to alleviate and prevent it". The definition has two parts: the courage to turn towards suffering rather than away from it, and the wisdom to do something helpful about it. Compassion in this sense is not softness, self-pity or letting oneself off the hook; Gilbert likes to compare it with the courage of a firefighter walking into a burning building. In 2006 he founded the Compassionate Mind Foundation, a charity that trains therapists in the approach.

Three emotion-regulation systems and compassionate mind training

At the centre of CFT is a simple model of emotion, usually drawn as three circles (Figure 5.12). Gilbert proposed that human emotions are organised by three interacting emotion-regulation systems, each with its own evolutionary job:

  • The threat and self-protection system detects danger and drives us to protect ourselves. Its emotions are anxiety, anger and disgust, and its behaviours are fight, flight, freezing and submission. It is fast and works on the principle "better safe than sorry", so it readily fires at false alarms.
  • The drive, excitement and resource-seeking system motivates us to pursue the things we need and want: food, status, achievement, success. Its feelings are wanting, excitement and the pleasure of getting.
  • The soothing, contentment and safeness system operates when we are neither threatened nor striving. Its feelings are calm, contentment, safeness and connection, and it is switched on above all by kindness and care, in the way an infant is calmed by a parent's voice and touch.

Gilbert linked the systems loosely with different brain chemistry (stress hormones for threat, dopamine for drive, oxytocin and endorphins for soothing), a simplification he himself acknowledges. The clinical point is about balance. In people high in shame and self-criticism the threat system is overactive, the drive system is often recruited into its service ("I must achieve or I'll be rejected"), and the soothing system is underdeveloped, because it was rarely switched on in childhood. Such a person can work hard and succeed and still never feel safe.

The aim of CFT is therefore to build up the soothing system, so that it can calm the threat system and balance the drive system. Its methods are called compassionate mind training (CMT). They begin with the body: soothing rhythm breathing, slowing the breath to a gentle, steady rhythm, together with a friendly facial expression and a warm tone of voice. They use imagery: a safe place; an imagined ideal compassionate other who is wise, strong and warm and wholly on one's side; and, most important, the compassionate self, which the client practises being much as an actor practises a role, imagining himself with those same qualities. They include compassionate letter writing, in which the client writes to himself about a difficulty in the voice of the compassionate self. And they include chair work, in which the client gives voice to the inner critic and then answers it from the compassionate self. Gilbert describes three flows of compassion: from oneself to others, from others to oneself, and from oneself to oneself. People high in self-criticism often find the second and third the hardest, and Gilbert and his colleagues (2011) developed scales to measure these fears of compassion ("If I'm kind to myself I'll become lazy"; "I don't deserve kindness"; "If I let my guard down, I'll fall apart").

Consider Priya, a 30-year-old chartered accountant in Pune, whose inner critic speaks in a voice she recognises as her father's: "You're pathetic. Anyone else would have finished by now." Cognitive therapy helped her see that the criticism was distorted, but she still felt it. Her CFT therapist begins by asking what the critic is for: what does it fear would happen if it stopped? Priya's answer ("I'd become lazy and everyone would see I'm not good enough") reveals the critic as a threat-system safety strategy, an anxious attempt to protect her from rejection. The therapist then teaches her to tell shame-based self-attack, which looks backwards, punishes and frightens (like a bullying teacher), from compassionate self-correction, which looks forwards, encourages and focuses on growth (like a good coach). Over the following weeks Priya practises soothing rhythm breathing, builds an image of her compassionate self, and writes letters to herself about the mistakes at work that used to keep her awake. The critic does not vanish, but it no longer has the last word.

CFT is often confused with a related idea. The American psychologist Kristin Neff introduced the concept of self-compassion in 2003, together with a Self-Compassion Scale to measure it. She described it as three components, each with an opposite: self-kindness rather than self-judgement, common humanity (seeing one's suffering as part of the shared human condition) rather than isolation, and mindfulness rather than over-identification with painful thoughts. Neff's work began as a research construct and a basis for self-help programmes; Gilbert's is a therapy built on an evolutionary model. The evidence for CFT itself is promising but still thin: early reviews found benefits, particularly for people high in self-criticism, but few large, rigorous trials.

Exam trap

Compassion-focused therapy and compassionate mind training: Paul Gilbert. Its three systems are threat, drive and soothing. Do not confuse them with three other famous trios: the three response systems of fear (verbal or cognitive, physiological and behavioural) described by Peter J. Lang in 1968; DBT's three states of mind (reasonable mind, emotion mind and wise mind; Section 5.8); or Neff's three components of self-compassion (self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness). And do not confuse the two Gilberts: Paul Gilbert of CFT is a British clinical psychologist; Daniel Gilbert, of the mind-wandering study in Section 5.9, is a Harvard social psychologist who studies happiness and how people predict their feelings.

Adrian Wells and metacognitive therapy

Nearly everyone has had the thought "What if I have cancer?" or "Maybe I'm a failure." Most people have it, shrug and move on; a few go on to develop an anxiety disorder or depression. Beck's explanation of the difference lay in the content of the thought and the beliefs behind it. The British clinical psychologist Adrian Wells, of the University of Manchester, proposed a different answer. What matters, he argued, is not what a person thinks but how he responds to his thinking: whether a passing thought is left alone, or seized on and turned into hours of worry and brooding. With Gerald Matthews he set out the theory in 1994 as the self-regulatory executive function (S-REF) model, and in 2009 he described the treatment in Metacognitive Therapy for Anxiety and Depression.

Metacognition means thinking about thinking: the knowledge, beliefs and processes by which we monitor, interpret and control our own thoughts (the term was introduced into developmental psychology by John H. Flavell in the 1970s; Developmental book). metacognitive therapy (MCT) is a treatment that aims to change these metacognitive beliefs and processes rather than the content of the client's thoughts. Its central concept is the cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS), a style of thinking that Wells believes is common to most psychological disorders. It has three parts:

  • Perseverative thinking: worry (repetitive "what if" thinking about the future) and rumination (repetitive "why" thinking about the past and one's moods).
  • Threat monitoring: attention locked on to possible danger, such as a health-anxious person scanning his body for symptoms or a socially anxious person monitoring how she comes across.
  • Unhelpful coping behaviours: thought suppression, avoidance, reassurance seeking, checking and the use of alcohol or drugs, all of which keep the problem going.

The CAS, in turn, is driven by two kinds of metacognitive belief. Positive metacognitive beliefs hold that the CAS is useful: "Worrying helps me prepare for the worst", "If I analyse why I feel low, I'll find the answer", "Staying alert to danger keeps me safe". These set the process going. Negative metacognitive beliefs hold that thinking is uncontrollable or dangerous: "My worrying is out of control", "Worrying this much will give me a heart attack or make me go mad". These make the person feel trapped, and anxious about the thinking itself.

Wells's model of generalised anxiety disorder (1995) shows the two working together (the disorder itself is described in the Disorders book). Lakshmi, a 45-year-old teacher in Chennai, has a son who rides a motorbike to college. Her Type 1 worry is about the outside world: "What if he has an accident? What if he is lying on the road and nobody helps him?" She believes that worrying keeps him safe (a positive belief), so she worries. Then comes Type 2 worry, worry about worry, or meta-worry: "I can't stop this; it's going to damage my heart; I'm going mad." Type 2 worry is what turns a mother's ordinary concern into a disorder. A cognitive therapist might examine the evidence about the risk of road accidents. A metacognitive therapist does not discuss the motorbike at all; she targets Lakshmi's beliefs about worrying. MCT's techniques follow from the model. In worry postponement, Lakshmi is asked, whenever a worry is triggered, to notice it and put off engaging with it until a fixed fifteen-minute "worry time" in the evening; many clients find, when the time comes, that they no longer need it, which is direct evidence against the belief that worry is uncontrollable. The therapist also runs experiments in the session ("Try as hard as you can to worry yourself into a heart attack, right now") to disconfirm the belief that worry is dangerous, and examines the positive beliefs (has worrying ever actually kept her son safe?). Two attentional techniques are distinctive. detached mindfulness, a term coined by Wells and Matthews, means being aware of a thought as an event in the mind while neither engaging with it (no analysing, arguing or worrying) nor trying to push it away, like watching a train pass through a station without getting on board. It is practised with brief exercises such as the tiger task: imagine a tiger and simply watch what it does, without trying to make it do anything. It is not meditation, involves no focus on the breath, and takes minutes rather than weeks. The attention training technique (ATT; Wells, 1990) is a twelve-minute listening exercise. The client hears several sounds at once (a ticking clock, traffic, a voice, a tapping) coming from different places, and practises first focusing on one sound at a time, then switching rapidly between them, and finally spreading attention across all of them together. Its purpose is to strengthen flexible control of attention and to loosen the grip of self-focused attention. It is not distraction: thoughts and feelings are allowed to stay in the background, like more noise.

Trials, many of them from Wells's own group, report large effects in generalised anxiety disorder, depression, post-traumatic stress and obsessive-compulsive disorder, and a 2018 meta-analysis found MCT more effective than waiting lists and, in head-to-head comparisons, somewhat more effective than CBT. The trials are few and small, however, and independent replication is still needed before strong conclusions can be drawn.

Exam trap

Metacognitive therapy: Adrian Wells. Its core concept is the cognitive attentional syndrome (worry and rumination, threat monitoring and unhelpful coping), driven by positive and negative metacognitive beliefs; Type 2 worry is worry about worry. MCT changes how a person relates to thinking, not what he thinks, so it is not Beck's content-focused therapy, and its detached mindfulness is not the meditation of MBCT. Beware the double acronym: metacognitive training (also abbreviated MCT), developed by Steffen Moritz and Todd S. Woodward (2007), is a group programme for people with psychosis that teaches them to recognise cognitive biases such as jumping to conclusions. Same letters, different treatment.

Functional analytic psychotherapy: the relationship as the laboratory

Most therapy is talk about behaviour that happened somewhere else: the argument at home last week, the panic in the metro on Monday. Behaviourists know that change is fastest when a behaviour is reinforced immediately, as it happens. So what if the client's problem could be caught happening right there in the room? In the 1980s two psychologists in Seattle, Robert J. Kohlenberg and Mavis Tsai, noticed that a few of their behaviour therapy clients improved far more than the techniques used could explain, and that these were the clients with whom an intense and genuine relationship had formed. They explained the observation in strictly behavioural terms in Functional Analytic Psychotherapy: Creating Intense and Curative Therapeutic Relationships (1991).

functional analytic psychotherapy (FAP) is a behaviour therapy, grounded in Skinner's radical behaviourism, that uses the therapeutic relationship as a live social environment in which the client's interpersonal problems actually occur, so that the therapist can notice, evoke and naturally reinforce improvements at the moment they happen. Its central concept is that of clinically relevant behaviours (CRBs), of which there are three kinds:

  • CRB1: occurrences of the client's problem in the session itself. A client who complains that nobody gets close to him keeps the therapist at a distance with jokes, changes the subject whenever feelings arise, or arrives late.
  • CRB2: improvements occurring in the session. The same client says, for the first time, "That hurt when you said it."
  • CRB3: the client's own interpretations of his behaviour and its causes. "I notice I go quiet whenever I feel criticised, just as I did with my father."

The therapist's work is summarised in five rules. Rule 1: watch for CRBs. Rule 2: evoke CRBs, by creating situations in therapy that call for the client's problem behaviours and their improvements. Rule 3: reinforce CRB2s naturally. Rule 4: notice the effect of your own behaviour on the client, to check that what you intended as reinforcement actually works as reinforcement. Rule 5: offer functional interpretations and help the client to generalise what he has learned to his life outside. "Naturally" in Rule 3 is the key word. Behaviour analysts distinguish arbitrary reinforcement (a token, a sweet, "good job") from natural reinforcement, the consequence the behaviour would earn in ordinary life. When a client who never asks for anything finally asks to change his appointment time, the natural reinforcer is that the therapist genuinely changes it and is visibly glad to; praise for "being assertive" would be arbitrary, and it would not carry over to his marriage. Tsai and her colleagues later summarised the therapist's stance in three words: awareness, courage and love.

Sameer, a 32-year-old bank officer in Lucknow, comes to therapy because he cannot tell his wife or his manager what he wants. In the session he agrees with everything and ends every other sentence with "whatever you think is best, ma'am." A FAP therapist sees this as CRB1 (Rule 1) and gently evokes the alternative (Rule 2): "I've noticed you've agreed with everything I've suggested. Is there anything about how we work that you'd like to be different?" After a long pause Sameer says that the Saturday appointment is hard for him. The therapist thanks him warmly and moves the appointment (Rule 3), watches whether he speaks up more in the following weeks (Rule 4), and helps him see the link between what happened in the room and what happens at home (Rule 5).

FAP's evidence is modest: mostly case studies and small trials, and its use as an enhancement of other therapies, such as FAP-enhanced cognitive therapy for depression (Kohlenberg and colleagues, 2002). Because it encourages an intense relationship, it places great weight on the therapist's self-awareness, on clear ethical boundaries and on supervision.

Exam trap

Functional analytic psychotherapy: Robert Kohlenberg and Mavis Tsai (1991). Its terms are CRB1 (the problem in the session), CRB2 (improvement in the session) and CRB3 (the client's own functional interpretations), and its method is natural reinforcement of improvements within the relationship. Its interest in what happens between client and therapist resembles the psychodynamic idea of transference (Chapter 3), but FAP explains it by reinforcement, not by unconscious repetition. Nor does FAP claim that the relationship alone produces change, which is closer to Carl Rogers's position (Chapter 6); for FAP the relationship is the setting in which reinforcement shapes new behaviour. And do not confuse FAP with functional analysis, the antecedent-behaviour-consequence assessment of behaviour therapy (Chapter 4), which FAP uses but which is not itself a therapy.

Behavioural activation: back to behaviour

Behavioural activation is the oldest idea in this section and, in a sense, the newest therapy. Its roots lie in two behavioural accounts of depression from the early 1970s. In 1973 Charles B. Ferster (1922 to 1981), who had worked with Skinner on schedules of reinforcement, described depression as a reduced frequency of positively reinforced behaviour combined with an increase in avoidance and escape. In 1974 Peter M. Lewinsohn, at the University of Oregon, gave the idea its best-known form. Depression, he proposed, results from a low rate of response-contingent positive reinforcement (RCPR): rewards that follow from one's own actions, such as a friend's laugh at your joke or the satisfaction of finishing a chapter. RCPR can be low for three reasons: because few events are potentially rewarding for the person, because the environment offers few rewards (after a bereavement, a job loss or a move to a strange city), or because the person lacks the skills, social skills especially, to obtain the rewards that are available. Low mood then lowers activity, which lowers reinforcement further, in a downward spiral. Lewinsohn's group developed the Pleasant Events Schedule, a long list of activities that people rate for how often they do them and how much they enjoy them, and in 1984 a structured, classroom-style Coping with Depression Course.

Beck took the same methods into cognitive therapy (Section 5.4), but with a different purpose: there, activity scheduling and graded tasks served chiefly to produce evidence against beliefs such as "I can't do anything." Was the cognitive work actually necessary? In the 1990s Neil S. Jacobson (1949 to 1999) and his colleagues at the University of Washington in Seattle set out to find out. Their component analysis (1996), told in full in Section 5.11 (Table 5.26), gave 150 depressed outpatients behavioural activation alone, behavioural activation plus work on automatic thoughts, or the full cognitive therapy package, and all three did equally well, with no difference in relapse two years later. If activation alone did as well as the full package, perhaps it deserved to be a therapy in its own right. Jacobson died in 1999, but his colleagues carried the idea forward.

The result is behavioural activation (BA): a structured, brief treatment for depression that aims to increase contact with sources of reward and to reduce avoidance and withdrawal, by scheduling and gradually increasing valued and rewarding activities, guided by a functional analysis of what the person's behaviour is doing for him. The modern version was set out by Christopher R. Martell, Michael E. Addis and Jacobson in Depression in Context: Strategies for Guided Action (2001). It works from the outside in: the client acts according to a plan rather than according to his mood, instead of waiting until he feels like it. Avoidance is analysed with two acronyms. In a TRAP, a trigger (a quarrel at work) produces a response (feeling low and tired), which leads to an avoidance pattern (going to bed at six and skipping dinner with friends) that brings relief now and deeper depression later. The client learns to get back on TRAC: the same trigger and response, followed by alternative coping (a short walk and the dinner, even though he does not feel like it). Rumination itself is treated as a behaviour, an avoidant one, to be noticed and replaced with action. A related short form, the brief behavioural activation treatment for depression of Carl Lejuez, Derek Hopko and colleagues (2001), builds the activity plan directly from the client's values, much as ACT does.

Does it work? In a large Seattle trial led by Sona Dimidjian (2006), 241 adults with major depression were randomly assigned to BA, cognitive therapy, antidepressant medication or placebo pills. Among the more severely depressed patients, BA did as well as medication and better than cognitive therapy. And a large English trial, COBRA (Section 5.11), found that junior mental health workers could deliver BA as effectively as qualified therapists delivered CBT, and more cheaply. Because it is simple to teach, BA has become one of the most important tools for taking psychological treatment beyond specialist clinics.

Indian context

India has very few clinical psychologists for its population, and one answer is task sharing: training lay counsellors to deliver simple, structured psychological treatments under supervision. The best-known Indian example is the Healthy Activity Program (HAP), a brief treatment for depression built on behavioural activation, developed by the Goa-based organisation Sangath with Vikram Patel and colleagues (Section 5.11). In the PREMIUM trial in primary health care in Goa, published in 2017, lay counsellors delivering HAP to adults with moderately severe to severe depression achieved better outcomes than enhanced usual care, at low cost. It is a powerful demonstration that behavioural activation can travel.

Why is behavioural activation counted as third wave, when its techniques are older than Beck's therapy? Hayes included it because of its contextual stance: it asks what a behaviour does for the person in his context (its function) rather than what his thoughts contain, and it deliberately makes no attempt to change thoughts at all.

Exam trap

Depression maintained by "a deficiency of reinforcement in the environment" is Peter Lewinsohn's behavioural account (1974), stated as a low rate of response-contingent positive reinforcement. It is not Beck's cognitive triad (Section 5.3), not Freud's repressed conflicts and not Bowlby's insecure attachment; Martin Seligman's learned helplessness is yet another account (Disorders book). Activity scheduling inside Beck's therapy is used to test beliefs; behavioural activation as a stand-alone therapy (Jacobson, Martell and Addis) aims to restore reward and reduce avoidance, and does not target thoughts. Do not confuse it with the behavioural activation system (BAS) of Jeffrey Gray's reinforcement sensitivity theory, a brain system for sensitivity to reward (Personality and Physiological books).

The third wave in perspective

Step back and look at the whole family, from DBT in Section 5.8 to behavioural activation (Table 5.24). Its members differ in their founders, their theories and their favourite techniques, but several threads run through them. They pay more attention to a person's relationship with thoughts and feelings than to their content. They make wide use of acceptance and mindfulness. They ask what a behaviour or a thought does (its function in context) rather than whether it is true. They give a central place to values and to a meaningful life, not only to reducing symptoms. Several of them (DBT, FAP and CFT) put the therapeutic relationship itself near the centre of change. And most are transdiagnostic, applying one model across many disorders.

Back in Section 5.1 you read that CBT is the most researched form of psychotherapy in the world. You have now met most of the family, from Ellis's disputing to Linehan's skills groups. So ask the question that a sceptical patient, a hospital administrator or an interview panel will ask you: does it actually work, for whom, compared with what, and why? This section surveys the big reviews and the trials that pitted CBT against medication, describes the advantage that shows itself only after treatment ends, asks which ingredients do the work, and follows CBT into whole health systems, in England and in India, including by computer and smartphone. The criticisms come too, because a clinician who can only praise a treatment does not yet understand it.

The evidence base: what the reviews say

Why is there so much research on CBT in particular? Partly because of the features you met in Section 5.1. CBT is brief, so a trial can finish in months rather than years. It is manualised: since Beck's 1979 book, therapists in a trial can be trained to deliver the same treatment, and recordings of their sessions can be rated to check that they did. And it measures: a therapy that rates symptoms every week produces outcome data as a by-product. A therapy that is brief, standardised and measured is easy to test (Chapter 8 explains how trials, meta-analyses and effect sizes work). That cuts both ways: it explains why CBT has more evidence than any other therapy, and why critics warn that more evidence is not the same as a better treatment.

By the early 2000s there were so many trials, and so many meta-analyses of those trials, that researchers began to write reviews of the meta-analyses themselves. The most cited is by Andrew C. Butler, Jason Chapman, Evan Forman and Aaron Beck (2006), who gathered 16 methodologically rigorous meta-analyses covering several hundred studies. Their conclusions are worth knowing almost word for word. CBT produced large effects for unipolar depression, generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder with or without agoraphobia, social phobia, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and depressive and anxiety disorders in childhood. It produced moderate effects for marital distress, anger, childhood somatic disorders and chronic pain. It was somewhat superior to antidepressants for adult depression, and about as effective as behaviour therapy for adult depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). For bulimia nervosa and schizophrenia the effects were large but uncontrolled, that is, measured from before to after treatment without a comparison group, which always flatters a treatment.

Six years later Stefan G. Hofmann (Section 5.9) and his colleagues (2012) reviewed 269 meta-analyses and reached a similar verdict: the support was strongest for the anxiety disorders, somatoform disorders, bulimia, anger control problems and general stress. Guideline bodies have drawn the practical conclusion. The World Health Organization (WHO) and England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), which writes the treatment guidelines for the National Health Service (NHS), recommend CBT, or a therapy built on it, as a first-line psychological treatment for most of the common mental disorders.

"Strong evidence" means that CBT clearly beats waiting lists and placebo conditions and usually matches the best alternatives; it does not mean that every patient recovers. The clearest case is schizophrenia, where antipsychotic medication is the front-line treatment and CBT for psychosis (Section 5.6) is added to reduce distress, persisting symptoms and the risk of relapse. CBT against medication: the landmark trials

Section 5.3 told the story of the first head-to-head trial: in 1977 Rush, Beck, Kovacs and Hollon found that cognitive therapy beat the antidepressant imipramine. A single small trial run by the therapy's own inventors could not settle the matter. In the 1980s the United States National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) set out to settle it properly in its Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (TDCRP), the largest and most carefully controlled psychotherapy trial yet attempted.

Key study: the NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (Elkin and colleagues, 1989)

The question. Under the most careful conditions anyone had yet arranged, how do two short psychotherapies compare with an antidepressant? The method. At three university sites (Pittsburgh, George Washington University and Oklahoma), about 250 outpatients with major depression were randomly assigned to 16 weeks of one of four treatments: CBT; interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT, Klerman and Weissman's therapy of grief, role disputes, role transitions and interpersonal deficits, Section 3.7); imipramine plus clinical management; or a placebo pill plus clinical management. "Clinical management" meant brief weekly meetings with a psychiatrist who offered support and encouragement, so the placebo group was in fact receiving a minimal supportive therapy. Therapists followed manuals and were trained and supervised, the treatment sites were independent of the therapies' developers, and the study was coordinated at the NIMH by Irene Elkin. Chapter 8 (Section 8.8) treats it as a landmark of research design.

The results. All four groups improved, and the overall differences were small. The treatments fell into a consistent order (imipramine best, placebo worst, the two psychotherapies in between and closer to imipramine), but for the less severely depressed there were no significant differences at all, placebo included. Among the more severely depressed, imipramine did clearly better than placebo, IPT showed some evidence of effectiveness, and CBT did not do significantly better than placebo. At an 18-month follow-up, relapse was common in every condition and the treatments did not differ significantly. The criticism. CBT's supporters replied that the trial's cognitive therapists were less experienced than those at Beck's centre and that CBT fared better at some sites than at others: the trial had tested CBT as delivered, not CBT at its best.

The severe-depression finding was taken seriously; for some years American practice guidelines advised that severely depressed patients needed medication. Robert J. DeRubeis and his colleagues (1999) challenged that advice with a "mega-analysis", pooling the raw data on severely depressed patients from four randomised trials, the TDCRP among them. Across the four, CBT did as well as medication. A major test came from a trial run jointly at the University of Pennsylvania and Vanderbilt University (DeRubeis, Steven D. Hollon and colleagues, 2005). About 240 moderately to severely depressed outpatients received 16 weeks of cognitive therapy, the antidepressant paroxetine, or (for the first eight weeks) a placebo pill. Both active treatments beat placebo, and by 16 weeks cognitive therapy had matched medication where the therapists were experienced (where they were less so, it lagged). The accepted conclusion: in the acute treatment of depression, well-delivered CBT and antidepressants are about equally effective, and combining them adds a modest further benefit, especially in severe and chronic depression.

Anxiety showed the same pattern, with a twist. In a multisite trial of panic disorder, David Barlow, Jack Gorman, Katherine Shear and Scott Woods (2000) compared CBT, imipramine, their combination and placebo conditions. CBT and imipramine both beat placebo and did about equally well, and the combination added little. But six months after treatment stopped, the patients who had received CBT without the drug had kept their gains better than those who had been on imipramine. Adolescents are a partial exception. In the Treatment for Adolescents with Depression Study (TADS, 2004), 12 weeks of fluoxetine plus CBT helped about 71 percent of depressed teenagers, fluoxetine alone about 61 percent, CBT alone about 43 percent and placebo about 35 percent; CBT alone caught up with the other active treatments only by 36 weeks. A rough summary of four decades: medication often works a little faster, and CBT tends to last.

Exam trap

The NIMH TDCRP compared exactly four conditions: CBT, interpersonal psychotherapy, imipramine plus clinical management and placebo plus clinical management. It did not include psychoanalysis, behaviour therapy or fluoxetine, and it was coordinated by Irene Elkin, not by Beck. Its headline was rough equivalence, with imipramine best for the severely depressed; it did not show CBT beating medication. The trial in which cognitive therapy beat imipramine was the earlier, smaller one by Rush, Beck, Kovacs and Hollon (1977), and the trial that restored CBT's standing for moderate to severe depression was DeRubeis, Hollon and colleagues (2005).

The enduring effect: CBT and relapse

If CBT and medication are roughly equal while treatment lasts, what is the special case for CBT? It lies in what happens after treatment ends. Depression tends to come back. A relapse is the return of symptoms soon after improvement, within the same episode; a recurrence is a new episode after a period of full recovery. Antidepressants protect against relapse, but mainly for as long as they are taken: as many as half of patients who stop their medication within the first few months of recovering relapse. CBT behaves differently. Its protection outlasts the treatment, and this is called the enduring effect of CBT.

An early summary came from a meta-analysis of cognitive therapy for depression by Gloaguen, Cottraux, Cucherat and Blackburn (1998): in the studies that followed patients up after treatment, relapse after cognitive therapy was roughly half as common as after antidepressants. The clearest evidence came from the follow-up phase of the Pennsylvania and Vanderbilt trial (Hollon and colleagues, 2005). Patients who had responded to medication were either kept on it or switched, without knowing it, to a placebo pill; patients who had responded to cognitive therapy simply stopped, with up to three booster sessions allowed during the year. Figure 5.13 shows what happened over the next twelve months. In the second year, when all treatment had stopped, the difference grew: about 17 percent of the former cognitive therapy patients had a recurrence, against about 54 percent of those who had stayed on medication through the first year.

A related strategy treats the two approaches as relay runners rather than rivals. In sequential treatment, developed by the Italian psychiatrist Giovanni A. Fava and his colleagues, patients who have recovered on antidepressants receive a course of CBT aimed at their residual symptoms while the medication is slowly withdrawn. Six years later, about 40 percent of the CBT group had suffered a recurrence, against about 90 percent of a comparison group given standard clinical management (Fava and colleagues, 2004). Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT, Section 5.9) was designed from the start for the same purpose: preventing relapse in people with recurrent depression.

Why should a course of talking last when a course of tablets does not? Three explanations are offered, and they are not exclusive. The first is skills: the patient leaves with a toolkit (catching automatic thoughts, scheduling activity, testing predictions) and keeps using it; Strunk, DeRubeis and colleagues (2007) found that patients who had acquired more cognitive therapy skills were less likely to relapse. The second is schema change: if therapy has softened the core belief (Section 5.4), the next stress finds less to trigger. The third is a changed relationship to thoughts: patients learn to see a negative thought as an event in the mind rather than a fact, which is the idea MBCT builds on.

Clinical link

Meena, a 34-year-old schoolteacher in Mysuru, has recovered from her second depressive episode on sertraline and asks whether she must take it for ever. The evidence lets you give her a real choice rather than a verdict. Staying on medication protects her while she takes it; a course of CBT, or adding CBT before the medication is tapered with her psychiatrist, offers protection that should outlast the sessions. Whichever she chooses, a written relapse prevention plan (her personal early warning signs, the skills that helped, and whom to contact) and a few booster sessions are good practice.

Exam trap

CBT's distinctive advantage over antidepressants in depression is lower relapse after treatment ends (the enduring effect), not a faster response (medication is usually as fast or faster) and not a much larger effect during treatment. "Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy was developed primarily to..." prevent relapse in recurrent major depression; not to treat acute psychosis, replace medication in bipolar disorder or treat phobias by exposure (Section 5.9).

Which ingredients matter? Dismantling CBT

A full course of cognitive therapy contains many things: a warm relationship, a convincing rationale, activity scheduling, thought records, behavioural experiments and work on core beliefs. When the package works, which parts are doing the work? The research tool for answering is the dismantling study, also called a component analysis: the full treatment is compared with versions from which one or more components have been removed. If a stripped-down version does as well as the whole, the missing parts were not needed, at least for that problem and those patients. Its mirror image is the additive (or constructive) design, which starts with a basic treatment and adds a component to see whether outcomes improve. The best-known dismantling study of CBT was run on Beck's cognitive therapy itself.

Key study: Jacobson's component analysis of cognitive therapy for depression (1996)

Neil S. Jacobson (1949 to 1999; Section 5.10) of the University of Washington in Seattle was a behaviourist who suspected that the behavioural part of cognitive therapy did most of the work. With Keith Dobson (Section 5.1) and six other colleagues he randomly assigned 150 outpatients with major depression to the three conditions in Table 5.26, for up to 20 sessions. The same four experienced cognitive therapists delivered all three treatments, and because they believed in the full package, any bias should have favoured it. The result: there was no evidence that full cognitive therapy did better than either component, either at the end of treatment or at six-month follow-up. Behavioural activation alone even changed negative thinking and attributional style as much as the full therapy did. A two-year follow-up (Gortner, Gollan, Dobson and Jacobson, 1998) found no difference in relapse. The criticism: one trial of 150 patients cannot detect small differences, and asking cognitive therapists to hold back their usual methods is not the same as testing behavioural activation delivered by enthusiasts.

The finding had large consequences. Behavioural activation was developed into a therapy in its own right (Section 5.10 describes it, and the later Seattle trial that compared it with cognitive therapy and medication). In England, the COBRA trial (Cost and Outcome of Behavioural Activation; David Richards and colleagues, 2016) found that behavioural activation delivered by junior mental health workers with no professional psychotherapy training was no less effective than CBT delivered by qualified therapists, and was cheaper. If the simplest component works, it can be taught to people who are not psychologists, an idea you will meet again in Goa.

Jacobson's study also opened a wider argument about how CBT works. Richard Longmore and Michael Worrell (2007) asked, in the title of their review, "Do we need to challenge thoughts in cognitive behavior therapy?", and concluded that there was little evidence that cognitive techniques add to behavioural ones, or that changes in thinking cause the changes in symptoms. Defenders such as Hofmann (2008) replied that cognitive change can be produced by behavioural methods too (a behavioural experiment is a way of changing a belief), so a behavioural component working well does not show that cognition is unimportant. Timing is part of the puzzle. Stephen Ilardi and Edward Craighead (1994) pointed out that much of the improvement in CBT for depression happens in the first few weeks, before the main cognitive techniques have been introduced, which suggests that hope, structure and the relationship do a good deal of the early work. Tony Tang and Robert DeRubeis (1999) then described sudden gains: large, stable drops in symptoms between one session and the next, usually following a session in which the client's thinking had changed substantially; patients who had them did better at the end of treatment and at follow-up. The fair summary is that CBT clearly works, but exactly why is still argued. In the anxiety disorders exposure is the strongest single ingredient (Chapter 4), though cognitive methods add to it for some problems, such as generalised anxiety disorder (Butler, Fennell, Robson and Gelder, 1991).

Exam trap

Jacobson and colleagues (1996) ran a dismantling (component) study, and found that behavioural activation alone did as well as full cognitive therapy; it did not show cognitive restructuring or schema work to be the essential ingredient. A dismantling study removes components from a full package; an additive design adds them to a basic one. Do not confuse three Jacobsons: Neil Jacobson (behavioural activation; the reliable change index with Truax, 1991, Chapter 8), Edmund Jacobson (progressive muscle relaxation; book 1929, revised 1938; Chapter 4) and Lenore Jacobson (Pygmalion in the Classroom, with Robert Rosenthal, 1968). And "sudden gains" are large improvements between consecutive sessions, not the spontaneous remission that Eysenck wrote about (Chapter 8).

Stepped care: NICE and IAPT

Suppose CBT works. A health system still faces a problem of arithmetic: there are far more people with depression and anxiety than there are trained therapists, and a full course of individual CBT costs many hours of a scarce professional's time. One answer is stepped care. As Peter Bower and Simon Gilbody (2005) described it, stepped care rests on two principles. The first is that the treatment offered first should be the least restrictive one (least intensive, least costly and least burdensome for the patient) that is still likely to bring real benefit. The second is that the system is self-correcting: everyone's progress is monitored, and a person who is not improving is "stepped up" to a more intensive treatment. The alternative, sometimes called stratified or matched care, assigns each person to a level of treatment at the start according to the severity of the problem.

NICE's guideline for depression in adults (2009) set out the best-known version, in four steps (Figure 5.14). Step 1 is recognition, assessment, education and active monitoring. Step 2 offers low-intensity interventions for persistent mild to moderate symptoms: guided self-help (a self-help programme based on CBT, in a book or online, with a few brief contacts with a practitioner who supports and encourages rather than delivers therapy), computerised CBT and structured group physical activity. Step 3 offers high-intensity interventions for moderate to severe depression or for people who did not improve at step 2: individual CBT, interpersonal psychotherapy, behavioural activation or behavioural couples therapy, often with medication. Step 4 is specialist and multi-professional care for severe, complex or high-risk cases, including inpatient and crisis care.

England then built a national service around this model. In 2006 the economist Richard Layard (born 1934) of the London School of Economics, working with the clinical psychologist David M. Clark, whose cognitive model of panic you met in Section 5.6, published The Depression Report. Its argument was economic as much as clinical: depression and anxiety were among the biggest causes of incapacity and lost work in Britain, the therapies NICE recommended were barely available, and a course of therapy would largely pay for itself if it helped people return to work. After pilot services in Doncaster and Newham, the Improving Access to Psychological Therapies (IAPT) programme was launched in October 2008. A new workforce of psychological wellbeing practitioners was trained to deliver low-intensity CBT-based help, much of it by telephone; trained CBT therapists delivered the high-intensity step, later joined by therapists in IPT, couples therapy and counselling for depression. Every patient completed two brief questionnaires at every session (the Patient Health Questionnaire, PHQ-9, for depression and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale, GAD-7, for anxiety; Assessment book), so that almost every person treated had a recorded outcome, and each service's results were published.

The programme set a national target that 50 percent of those completing treatment should "recover", meaning that their scores moved from the clinical to the non-clinical range. Recovery rates rose from under 40 percent in the first years to about 50 percent, and in January 2023 the service was renamed NHS Talking Therapies, for anxiety and depression. Its critics point out that recovery is judged by two short questionnaires, that many people who are referred never start or drop out early, that recovery is less likely in deprived areas, and that the pressure of targets falls heavily on a stretched workforce. Even so, IAPT showed that CBT could be delivered, and its results measured, for hundreds of thousands of people a year.

Exam trap

Stepped care starts with the least intensive treatment likely to work and steps up only when monitoring shows too little improvement. It does not start everyone at the most intensive level, and it does not mean "medication first". Guided self-help and computerised CBT are low-intensity (step 2); individual CBT, IPT, behavioural activation and couples therapy are high-intensity (step 3). IAPT is an English (NHS) programme, launched in 2008 and renamed NHS Talking Therapies in 2023; NICE is a guideline body, not a therapy.

CBT in India and other low-resource settings

England's problem of arithmetic is India's, multiplied many times over. The National Mental Health Survey of 2015 to 2016, carried out by the National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro-Sciences (NIMHANS) in Bengaluru, found that for most mental disorders well over half of the people affected were receiving no treatment at all. This difference between the number of people who need care and the number who receive it is called the treatment gap, and India has far too few clinical psychologists to close it by training more of them, however fast. Two ideas have changed what CBT looks like in such settings: adapting the therapy to the culture, and handing parts of it to people who are not mental health professionals.

cultural adaptation is the systematic modification of an evidence-based treatment so that it fits the language, beliefs and circumstances of the people receiving it, while keeping its active ingredients. The best-known framework, from Guillermo Bernal, Janet Bonilla and Carmen Bellido (1995), names eight dimensions a therapist can adapt: language, persons (the match between client and therapist), metaphors, content (cultural knowledge and values), concepts (explaining the problem and the treatment in terms that make sense locally), goals, methods and context. Meta-analyses have found culturally adapted therapies modestly more effective than the same therapies delivered unchanged.

Indian context

Indian clinicians who adapt CBT typically involve the family, since decisions about work, marriage and treatment are rarely made alone; use local idioms of distress ("tension", "weakness", "my mind is not working") and take bodily complaints seriously as the way distress is first presented; treat beliefs about karma, fate and God as resources to work with rather than distortions to dispute; replace written thought records with pictures, stories, proverbs and spoken homework for clients with little schooling; and accept a more directive, teacherly role, which many clients expect (J. S. Neki's guru-chela relationship, Chapter 6). A "should" that expresses family duty is treated as a value, not as musturbation (Section 5.2).

The second idea is task sharing (older writings say task shifting): giving defined parts of care to non-specialists, such as community health workers, nurses or trained members of the community, who are trained and supervised by specialists. The Indian psychiatrist Vikram Patel (born 1964), co-founder in 1996 of the Goa-based research organisation Sangath and author of the manual Where There Is No Psychiatrist (2003), became its leading advocate, and the World Health Organization's Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP, launched in 2008) built it into global policy. The early evidence came from South Asia. In rural Rawalpindi in Pakistan, Atif Rahman and colleagues (2008) trained government Lady Health Workers to weave CBT techniques into their routine home visits to depressed pregnant women and new mothers (the Thinking Healthy Programme). Six months after the birth, 23 percent of mothers in the intervention areas met criteria for major depression, against 53 percent in the control areas. In Goa, the MANAS trial (Patel and colleagues, 2010) placed lay health counsellors in primary care clinics to deliver psychoeducation and interpersonal therapy within a stepped-care team; it improved recovery in government clinics, though not in private ones.

Key study: the Healthy Activity Program trial in Goa (PREMIUM; Patel and colleagues, 2017)

The question. Can a person with no mental health training, after a structured course and with regular supervision, treat severe depression? PREMIUM (the Programme for Effective Mental Health Interventions in Under-resourced Health Systems) developed two brief treatments for lay counsellors in Goa: the Healthy Activity Program (HAP) for depression, and Counselling for Alcohol Problems, based on motivational interviewing, for harmful drinking. HAP was built on behavioural activation, with added problem solving and help in reactivating the client's social network, in six to eight sessions over up to three months. The counsellors were local people with at least a high-school education and no professional mental health training. The method. Adults with moderately severe to severe depression (a PHQ-9 score above 14) attending ten primary health centres were randomly assigned to enhanced usual care alone or enhanced usual care plus HAP. The result. At three months, 64 percent of the HAP group were in remission against 39 percent of the usual-care group, HAP was judged cost-effective, and the advantage was still present at twelve months. The limits. A trial with careful supervision is not yet a public programme; keeping quality high when such treatments are scaled up across a state is the next, harder test.

Similar results have come from Africa: in Zimbabwe's Friendship Bench trial (Dixon Chibanda and colleagues, 2016), older women from the community delivered problem-solving therapy on wooden benches in clinic grounds. Notice the thread that runs back to Jacobson's dismantling study: because behavioural activation proved enough on its own, and is far easier to teach than schema work, it was the natural treatment to hand to a lay counsellor in Goa.

Exam trap

Keep the programmes apart. The Healthy Activity Program (PREMIUM, Goa, 2017) treats moderately severe to severe depression, is based on behavioural activation and is delivered by lay counsellors in primary care; its sister treatment, Counselling for Alcohol Problems, targets harmful drinking. MANAS (Goa, 2010) used lay health counsellors for common mental disorders. The Thinking Healthy Programme (Pakistan, 2008) treated perinatal depression through Lady Health Workers. The Friendship Bench (Zimbabwe, 2016) used problem-solving therapy. Task sharing means specialists train and supervise non-specialists, not that specialists disappear; cultural adaptation changes the packaging of a therapy, not its active ingredients.

Criticisms and limitations

None of this makes CBT a cure-all, and its strongest supporters say so. The first limitation is simple: many patients do not get better. In trials for depression roughly half of patients respond well, fewer reach full remission, many keep residual symptoms, and relapse is common even after CBT; IAPT's recovery rate of about 50 percent means that half of those who complete treatment do not recover by its own measure. The second is the gap between efficacy and effectiveness (Section 8.7): trial patients are often selected to exclude severe comorbidity, suicidality and substance misuse, and are treated by well-supervised therapists; the patients in a district hospital clinic are not.

The third limitation concerns the evidence itself. Pim Cuijpers and his colleagues (2010) showed that the effects of psychotherapy for depression shrink considerably when only high-quality trials are counted, and that unpublished negative studies inflate the averages. Trials that compare CBT with a waiting list, rather than with a credible alternative, flatter it further. Tom Johnsen and Oddgeir Friborg (2015) went further: in 70 trials of CBT for depression published between 1977 and 2014, they reported that the effects had declined steadily over time. That claim was itself disputed; a reanalysis by Ioana Cristea and colleagues (2017) concluded that the apparent fall was most likely spurious. The debate is a good reminder that the numbers in this section are estimates, not constants.

The fourth limitation is that CBT is not clearly superior to other well-delivered therapies. When it is compared with interpersonal psychotherapy, behavioural activation or good short-term psychodynamic therapy, differences are usually small, which is the "dodo bird" finding discussed in Section 8.3, and researchers' allegiance to their own therapy predicts part of the result (Section 8.6). Falk Leichsenring and Christiane Steinert (2017) therefore questioned whether CBT deserves to be called the "gold standard" of psychotherapy and argued for plurality in treatment and research; Daniel David, Ioana Cristea and Stefan Hofmann (2018) replied that it does, because it has the largest evidence base and the best-specified theory of change. For the clinician the point is that more evidence for one therapy is not, by itself, evidence against the others.

The fifth group of criticisms is about depth. Psychodynamic and humanistic critics argue that CBT treats symptoms rather than causes, neglects childhood experience, emotion and the therapeutic relationship, and can become a mechanical exercise in filling in forms. The British psychiatrist and psychotherapist Jeremy Holmes put the case in a 2002 paper in the British Medical Journal pointedly titled "All you need is cognitive behaviour therapy?". CBT's own development is partly an answer: schema therapy and cognitive analytic therapy (Section 5.7) reach back into childhood and the relationship, and the third wave (Sections 5.8 to 5.10) turned towards acceptance, emotion and values. The sixth is social and cultural. CBT locates the problem in the person's thinking, and when the activating event is poverty, caste discrimination or violence at home, a therapy that examines only the client's beliefs can seem to blame the victim (Section 5.2's clinical link makes the same point about REBT). CBT also assumes literacy, homework and some capacity to observe one's own thoughts, and on its own it does not suit acute mania or psychosis, severe cognitive impairment, or a person in immediate danger.

Exam trap

Know which criticisms are fair. Fair: CBT does not help everyone; trial results overstate routine results; it is not clearly superior to other bona fide therapies; why it works is unresolved; it may neglect the past, emotion, the relationship and social causes; it demands literacy and homework. Unfair: that CBT "has no evidence", that it "has not been shown to help depression", that it focuses on sexuality (a criticism of Freud), or that it works only for mild problems (well-delivered CBT matched medication in moderate to severe depression). "Most researched" does not mean "proven best".

Digital CBT: computers, the internet and apps

If CBT is structured, educational and built on homework, can much of it be delivered without a therapist in the room? computerised CBT (cCBT) is a structured CBT programme delivered by computer, first on CD-ROM and later online, in which the software provides the psychoeducation, exercises and homework that a therapist would otherwise give. The best-known British example, Beating the Blues, takes the user through eight weekly interactive sessions; in general practice it did better than usual care for depression and anxiety (Judith Proudfoot and colleagues, 2004). FearFighter, an exposure-based programme for panic and phobia, grew out of Isaac Marks's work in London. In 2006, having declined to recommend computerised CBT in 2002, NICE recommended Beating the Blues for mild to moderate depression and FearFighter for panic and phobia, but not for severe depression.

internet-delivered CBT (iCBT) is CBT delivered through a website or app, usually as a series of modules completed over several weeks. It comes in two forms. In guided iCBT a therapist or trained supporter reviews the client's homework and sends brief written messages, often for only a few minutes per client per week; in unguided iCBT the client works alone. The Swedish psychologist Gerhard Andersson and his colleagues built much of the evidence for the guided form. In Australia, MoodGYM, launched free online in 2001 by Helen Christensen and Kathleen Griffiths at the Australian National University, reduced depressive symptoms in a community trial (2004). The most striking result came from a meta-analysis by Andersson, Cuijpers, Carlbring, Riper and Hedman (2014): across 13 trials that compared the two directly, guided iCBT and face-to-face CBT produced equivalent effects. Guidance matters, though. Meta-analyses (for example Richards and Richardson, 2012) find that supported programmes produce larger effects and fewer dropouts than unsupported ones.

Dropout is the central weakness of digital help. The Internet researcher Gunther Eysenbach (2005) named it the law of attrition: in trials of online health programmes, a large proportion of users stop using the programme before the end, so attrition must be expected and measured rather than treated as an accident. The REEACT trial (Simon Gilbody and colleagues, 2015) showed what this means in practice. Nearly 700 depressed patients from general practices across England were offered usual care, usual care plus Beating the Blues, or usual care plus MoodGYM, with weekly encouraging telephone calls from non-clinicians. Neither programme did better than usual care, mainly because few patients used them much. Computerised CBT works when people use it, and people use it more when a human being is involved.

Smartphone apps are the newest form. Thousands of mental health apps exist, and only a small fraction has ever been tested. A 2017 meta-analysis of randomised trials of smartphone interventions for depressive symptoms (Joseph Firth and colleagues) found a small to moderate benefit, smaller when apps were compared with active control conditions. Chatbots that hold CBT-style text conversations, some developed in India, are being evaluated, and internet-delivered CBT for insomnia has produced clear improvements in sleep (Ritterband and colleagues, 2009). The risks are real: privacy and the sale of users' data, apps that cannot recognise a suicidal crisis, and marketing that runs well ahead of evidence. Table 5.27 summarises the forms.

The case of Beck's guilty patient, revisited

Go back to the young psychoanalyst in Philadelphia and the patient on his couch who had just vented his anger at his analyst and felt, of all things, guilty. You can now name everything that was happening in that hour. The quiet commentary running beside the anger ("I said the wrong thing... I shouldn't have said that... He won't like me... I'm bad") was a string of automatic thoughts, and the guilt followed the thoughts rather than the outburst, exactly as the cognitive mediation hypothesis of Section 5.1 says. Each thought carries a distortion from Table 5.3: "I shouldn't have said that" is a should statement, "He won't like me" is an arbitrary inference about what another person will think, and "I'm bad" is labelling. Ellis would have heard a demand in the first and, in the last, the global rating of the self that REBT disputes. And "I'm bad" already sits near the bottom of the downward arrow: a core belief of the worthless kind, the deepest of the three levels of Figure 5.3. Beck did not interpret the guilt as anger turned inward. He asked what had been going through the patient's mind, and that simple question became the centre of cognitive therapy, of collaborative empiricism and of the thought record.

The rest of the chapter shows how many ways there now are of answering that second stream. A cognitive therapist would treat "He won't like me" as a prediction and test it with a behavioural experiment. A schema therapist might hear in "I'm bad" the voice of a Punitive Parent mode. An ACT therapist would help the patient notice "I'm having the thought that I'm bad" and act on his values anyway; an MBCT teacher would help him see it as a passing event in the mind, not a fact; a compassion-focused therapist would ask whether the voice that says it sounds like a bullying teacher or a good coach. The second wave changes what a thought says and the third changes what a thought does, but both rest on Beck's discovery that the stream is there to be caught. Section 5.11 showed where that discovery led: to the most researched psychotherapy in the world, about as effective as medication for depression while treatment lasts and more protective after it ends, though still not a cure for everyone.

Chapter summary

5.1 From behaviour to belief: the cognitive turn in therapy. Cognitive-behavioural therapy is a family of treatments that share Keith Dobson's three propositions: cognitive activity affects behaviour and emotion, it can be monitored and altered, and changing it changes behaviour and emotion. The first is the cognitive mediation hypothesis. The idea goes back to Epictetus ("men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them") and to Buddhist teaching, and was prepared in modern psychology by Adler's "basic mistakes", Horney's "tyranny of the shoulds" and Kelly's personal constructs (1955). Behaviour therapy turned cognitive in the 1970s, and Bandura's self-efficacy (1977) became a bridge between the two. Steven Hayes (2004) described three waves: behaviour therapy, cognitive therapy, and a third wave that changes a person's relationship with thoughts rather than their content. All CBTs are brief, structured, collaborative, present-focused, homework-based and measured; psychoanalysis belongs to none of the waves.

5.2 Albert Ellis and rational emotive behaviour therapy. Albert Ellis (1913 to 2007) left psychoanalysis to found rational therapy (about 1955), renamed rational-emotive therapy in 1961 and rational emotive behaviour therapy (REBT) in 1993; it is often called the first CBT. In the ABC model an activating event (A) does not cause the emotional and behavioural consequence (C) directly; the person's beliefs (B) about it do. Irrational beliefs are rigid demands (musturbation), from which awfulising, low frustration tolerance and global rating follow, and Ellis grouped them into three major musts about the self, others and the world. Unhealthy negative emotions (anxiety, depression) differ from healthy ones (concern, sadness). Disputing (D), logical, empirical or pragmatic, leads to an effective new philosophy (E) and new feelings (F). REBT aims at unconditional self-acceptance, uses cognitive, emotive and behavioural techniques (rational emotive imagery, shame attacking, homework), and is criticised as confrontational, with thinner evidence than Beck's.

5.3 Aaron Beck's cognitive model. Aaron Beck (1921 to 2021), a psychoanalytically trained psychiatrist at the University of Pennsylvania, found that his depressed patients' dreams and laboratory behaviour did not fit the theory of anger turned inward, and built a cognitive model instead (1963 to 1967; Cognitive Therapy of Depression, 1979). Automatic thoughts are quick, plausible, evaluative thoughts that drive emotion. In depression they share the cognitive triad, negative views of the self, the world and the future, and Beck saw hopelessness as the key to suicide. Judith Beck organised cognition into three levels: core beliefs (held in schemas), intermediate beliefs (attitudes, rules and assumptions) and automatic thoughts, in a diathesis-stress model. Cognitive distortions turn events into evidence for the schema: Beck's six (arbitrary inference, selective abstraction, overgeneralisation, magnification and minimisation, personalisation, dichotomous thinking) and Burns's additions, including mind reading, fortune telling, catastrophising, emotional reasoning, should statements and labelling.

5.4 Cognitive therapy in practice. Cognitive therapy rests on collaborative empiricism: therapist and client work as co-investigators who treat beliefs as hypotheses and make objective assessments of them. Socratic questioning and guided discovery help the client notice previously overlooked information and reach his own conclusions (Padesky, 1993); free association, dream analysis and medication play no part. A typical course for depression runs 12 to 20 sessions with a set structure (mood check, bridge, agenda, homework review, discussion, new homework, summary and feedback), and progress is tracked with Beck's self-report scales (BDI, BAI, BHS), unlike the clinician-rated Hamilton scales. The main techniques are the dysfunctional thought record, decatastrophising, reattribution, activity scheduling with mastery and pleasure ratings, graded task assignment, behavioural experiments and the downward arrow to core beliefs. Beck and Ellis share the view that disturbance comes from thinking rather than events; Ellis disputes irrational beliefs, whereas Beck tests distorted ones.

5.5 Coping skills, problem solving and personal constructs. Some clients lack skills rather than hold false beliefs. Donald Meichenbaum's self-instructional training (with Goodman, 1971), built on Vygotsky and Luria's account of inner speech, teaches impulsive children to guide themselves in five steps, from cognitive modelling to covert self-instruction. His stress inoculation training has three phases (conceptualisation, skills acquisition and rehearsal, application and follow-through), uses coping self-statements and rests on Richard Lazarus's transactional view of stress. D'Zurilla and Goldfried's problem-solving therapy (1971), developed with the Nezus, combines a positive problem orientation with rational problem-solving skills. Arnold Lazarus's multimodal therapy assesses seven modalities, the BASIC ID, and borrows techniques by technical eclecticism. George Kelly's fixed-role therapy (1955) asks a client to live as an invented character for about two weeks, following from his view of the person as a scientist and his constructive alternativism.

5.6 Disorder-specific cognitive models: panic, social anxiety, trauma and psychosis. Beck's cognitive specificity hypothesis holds that each disorder has its own theme of thought. The Oxford group (Clark, Salkovskis, Ehlers, Wells) built models naming each disorder's key appraisal and the processes that maintain it, above all safety behaviours, which prevent disconfirmation and are dropped in therapy. Clark (1986) explained panic as the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations. Clark and Wells (1995) explained social anxiety through self-focused attention, an observer-perspective self-image, safety behaviours and anticipatory and post-event processing; complete treatment combines exposure, cognitive restructuring and social skills work. Ehlers and Clark (2000) explained PTSD as a sense of current threat; Resick's cognitive processing therapy targets stuck points, and the TF-CBT of Cohen, Mannarino and Deblinger centres on a trauma narrative. CBT for psychosis normalises experiences and targets beliefs about them, as an adjunct to antipsychotic medication.

5.7 Going deeper: schema therapy and cognitive analytic therapy. Clients with personality disorders break the assumptions of short-term cognitive therapy. Jeffrey Young's schema therapy (1990; set out fully with Klosko and Weishaar in 2003), designed for those who did not respond to standard CBT, describes 18 early maladaptive schemas in five domains, each rooted in an unmet core emotional need. Schemas are kept alive by three coping styles (surrender, avoidance, overcompensation) and by schema chemistry; schema modes are moment-to-moment states such as the Vulnerable Child, the Detached Protector, the Punitive Parent and the Healthy Adult. Change comes through limited reparenting, empathic confrontation, imagery rescripting, chair work and behavioural pattern-breaking, and the Giesen-Bloo trial (2006) favoured it over transference-focused psychotherapy. Anthony Ryle's cognitive analytic therapy (1990) recasts object relations as reciprocal roles and problem procedures (traps, dilemmas, snags), worked on in about 16 sessions through a reformulation letter, a sequential diagrammatic reformulation and goodbye letters.

5.8 The third wave I: dialectical behaviour therapy. Marsha Linehan (1943 to 2026), once a severely disturbed inpatient herself, built dialectical behaviour therapy (manuals 1993) for chronically suicidal women with borderline personality disorder after standard behaviour therapy failed them. Her biosocial theory explains the disorder as emotion dysregulation arising from the transaction of emotional vulnerability (high sensitivity, high reactivity, slow return to baseline) with an invalidating environment. Dialectics balances acceptance, through validation and skills drawn from Zen, with change, through behavioural chain analysis, skills training and contingency management. Standard DBT has four modes: individual therapy guided by a diary card, group skills training, telephone coaching and a therapist consultation team; Stage 1 follows a target hierarchy headed by life-threatening behaviour. The four skills modules are core mindfulness and distress tolerance (acceptance) and emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness (change). Trials from 1991 onwards show fewer suicide attempts and better retention, though well-organised alternatives can do as well.

5.9 The third wave II: acceptance and commitment therapy and the mindfulness-based therapies. Steven Hayes, with Kirk Strosahl and Kelly Wilson (1999), developed acceptance and commitment therapy, a behaviour therapy grounded in functional contextualism and in relational frame theory (2001), which traces human suffering to derived relational responding and the transformation of stimulus functions. ACT's problems are cognitive fusion and experiential avoidance; its aim is psychological flexibility, built through the six processes of the hexaflex: acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self-as-context, values and committed action. Mindfulness is paying attention on purpose, in the present moment and non-judgementally. Jon Kabat-Zinn founded mindfulness-based stress reduction in 1979, an eight-week course for stress, pain and illness. Segal, Williams and Teasdale (2002) combined it with cognitive therapy in MBCT, based on the differential activation hypothesis; its key skill is decentering, and in people with three or more past episodes it almost halved relapse into depression.

5.10 The third wave III: compassion, metacognition, the therapeutic relationship and activation. Four smaller third-wave therapies each have one founder and one big idea. Paul Gilbert's compassion-focused therapy, for people high in shame and self-criticism, rests on three emotion-regulation systems (threat, drive and soothing) and uses compassionate mind training to strengthen soothing; Kristin Neff's self-compassion is a related research construct. Adrian Wells's metacognitive therapy targets the cognitive attentional syndrome (worry and rumination, threat monitoring, unhelpful coping) and the positive and negative metacognitive beliefs that drive it, using detached mindfulness and the attention training technique. Kohlenberg and Tsai's functional analytic psychotherapy (1991) naturally reinforces clinically relevant behaviours as they occur in the therapy relationship. Behavioural activation grew from Lewinsohn's account of low response-contingent positive reinforcement and Jacobson's component analysis, and was set out by Martell, Addis and Jacobson (2001). The third wave shares attention to function, acceptance and values, and is moving towards process-based therapy.

5.11 How well does CBT work? CBT is the most researched therapy because it is brief, manualised and measured. Butler and colleagues (2006) found large effects for depression, generalised anxiety, panic, social phobia, PTSD and childhood disorders. In the NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (Elkin, 1989) CBT, interpersonal psychotherapy and imipramine differed little, but DeRubeis, Hollon and colleagues (2005) showed well-delivered cognitive therapy matching medication in moderate to severe depression. CBT's distinctive advantage is its enduring effect, less relapse after treatment ends, and Jacobson's dismantling study (1996) found behavioural activation alone as effective as the full package. Stepped care offers the least intensive effective help first, as in England's IAPT (2008), now NHS Talking Therapies; task sharing and cultural adaptation carry CBT to low-resource settings, as in Goa's Healthy Activity Program. Limitations include non-response, inflated trial effects and no clear superiority over other therapies. Guided internet CBT rivals face-to-face therapy, but attrition is high.

From the past papers

  1. "Third-wave CBT" includes all EXCEPT: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (b) Dialectical Behavior Therapy (c) Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (d) Psychoanalysis

    Answer: (d) Psychoanalysis. ACT, DBT and MBCT are the best-known third-wave therapies, which change a person's relationship with thoughts rather than their content. Psychoanalysis is an older psychodynamic tradition and belongs to none of the three waves of behavioural and cognitive therapy.

  2. Which of the following is not a form of psychotherapy used to treat psychological disorders? (RML 2020)

    (a) Cognitive therapy (b) Pharmacotherapy (c) Behavioural therapy (d) Psychodynamic therapy

    Answer: (b) Pharmacotherapy. Treatment with medicines is not a psychotherapy. Cognitive, behavioural and psychodynamic therapies all are, because they work through a psychological relationship and psychological techniques.

  3. Rational Emotive Therapy is given by: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Albert Ellis (b) Sigmund Freud (c) William James (d) Aaron Beck

    Answer: (a) Albert Ellis. Ellis founded rational therapy in the mid-1950s and renamed it rational-emotive therapy in 1961. Beck is the tempting wrong answer, but he founded cognitive therapy; Freud founded psychoanalysis, and William James was a founder of functionalism.

  4. REBT was developed by: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Aaron Beck (b) Albert Ellis (c) Carl Rogers (d) Adler

    Answer: (b) Albert Ellis. Ellis renamed his therapy rational emotive behaviour therapy in 1993. Beck's therapy is cognitive therapy, Rogers's is client-centred therapy, and Adler influenced Ellis without founding REBT.

  5. Within the cognitive approach, the ABC model describes: (RML 2021)

    (a) A = activating event; B = beliefs about oneself; C = consequences of the beliefs about oneself (b) A = activating events; B = beliefs about the events; C = consequences of the beliefs about the event (c) A = activating event; B = beliefs about the events; C = critical analysis of the beliefs about the event (d) A = anticipatory event; B = beliefs about the event; D = consequences of the beliefs about the event

    Answer: (b). In Ellis's model the beliefs (B) are about the activating event (A), and the consequences (C) follow from those beliefs. Option (a) narrows the belief to the self, (c) turns C into "critical analysis" and (d) invents an "anticipatory" event and a misplaced D.

  6. A therapist focuses on helping a client identify and challenge irrational beliefs, such as "I absolutely must be approved by everyone" (musterbation). This therapist is most likely practicing: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Beck's Cognitive Therapy (b) Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) (c) Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) (d) Reality Therapy

    Answer: (b) REBT. "Irrational beliefs", absolute "musts" and Ellis's own word musturbation are the signature of REBT. Beck speaks of automatic thoughts and distortions, DBT of acceptance and skills, and William Glasser's reality therapy (Chapter 6) is a different approach altogether.

  7. In the A-B-C-D-E model of cognitive behaviour therapy (RET), "D" refers to: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Drama (b) Desensitization (c) Discuss (d) Disputing

    Answer: (d) Disputing. D is the logical, empirical and pragmatic challenging of irrational beliefs, which leads to E, an effective new philosophy. Desensitisation is Wolpe's behavioural technique, the tempting look-alike; "drama" and "discuss" are filler.

  8. A criticism of the REBT cognitive approach to understanding mental-health problems is that: (RML 2021)

    (a) There is too much of a focus on sexuality (b) It can be direct and confrontational and so might be seen as intimidatory by the client (c) It has not been shown to be successful at helping individuals with depression (d) It is not relevant in the 21st century

    Answer: (b). Vigorous disputing can feel intimidating or humiliating, especially to a fragile client early in therapy. The focus on sexuality is a criticism of Freud, REBT does have evidence for depression, and (d) is an opinion, not a criticism made in the literature.

  9. A patient sees a friend in the street who fails to acknowledge him; he thinks his friend does not like him anymore. He has the following type of cognitive error: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Selective abstraction (b) Arbitrary inference (c) Personalization (d) Magnification

    Answer: (b) Arbitrary inference. He draws a conclusion for which there is no evidence: the friend may simply not have seen him. No detail is being picked out of a larger picture (selective abstraction), he is not blaming himself for an event he did not cause (personalisation), and nothing is being blown up in size (magnification).

  10. A teacher in his class sees two students bored and feels his class is not liked by all the students. This is which type of cognitive error? (RML 2024)

    (a) Arbitrary inference (b) Selective attention (c) Over generalization (d) Magnification

    Answer: (b) Selective attention, that is, selective abstraction or the mental filter: one detail (two bored faces) is taken out of context and all the attentive faces are ignored. Overgeneralisation tempts because he moves to "all the students", but it means carrying a conclusion from one situation to other, unrelated situations.

  11. Catastrophic thinking is common to several anxiety disorders. The term describes: (RML 2021)

    (a) Categorising all experiences as either extremely positive or extremely negative (b) Focusing on negative events and ignoring positive ones (c) Worrying that the worst will always happen (d) Taking things very personally

    Answer: (c). Catastrophising is the expectation of the worst possible outcome. Option (a) is dichotomous thinking, (b) is the mental filter (selective abstraction) and (d) is personalisation.

  12. Which of the following are not included in the ICD criteria for schizophrenia? (RML 2021)

    (a) Delusions (b) Catastrophic thinking (c) Disorganised speech (d) Hallucinations

    Answer: (b) Catastrophic thinking. It is a cognitive distortion found in anxiety, described in Beck's model, not a symptom of psychosis. Delusions, hallucinations and disorganised speech are core features of schizophrenia (Psychiatric Disorders book).

  13. Which of the following is NOT included as one of the fundamental concepts in cognitive therapy? (RML 2022)

    (a) Collaborative empiricism (b) Socratic dialogue (c) Free association (d) Guided discovery

    Answer: (c) Free association. Saying whatever comes to mind so that the analyst can detect unconscious material is a psychoanalytic method. Collaborative empiricism, Socratic dialogue and guided discovery are the core of Beck's method.

  14. Which of the following methods is used in Beck's cognitive therapy for depression? (RML 2025)

    (a) Encouraging dream analysis (b) Making objective assessments of beliefs (c) Administering psychoactive medication (d) Practicing free association

    Answer: (b). In collaborative empiricism beliefs are treated as hypotheses and weighed against evidence from the client's own life. Dream analysis and free association are Freud's tools, and medication is pharmacotherapy, not cognitive therapy.

  15. What is Socratic questioning? (RML 2022)

    (a) A form of dialogue that helps a person to discover and attend to previously overlooked information and to guide the discovery of the therapist rather than interpretation from the individual (b) A form of dialogue that helps a person to discover and attend to previously attended information and to guide the discovery of the individual rather than interpretation from the therapist (c) A form of dialogue that helps a person to discover and attend to previously overlooked information and to guide the discovery of the individual rather than interpretation from the therapist (d) A form of dialogue that helps a person to discover and attend to previously attended information and to guide the discovery of the therapist rather than interpretation from the individual

    Answer: (c). Two details decide it: the information is previously overlooked, and the discovery belongs to the individual rather than to interpretation from the therapist (Padesky's definition). Each of the other options reverses one or both of these details.

  16. Socratic questioning, a core technique in Cognitive Therapy, involves: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) The therapist interpreting the client's unconscious conflicts (b) The therapist guiding the client to discover their own maladaptive thoughts and generate alternative perspectives through guided discovery (c) The therapist providing direct advice and solutions to the client's problems (d) The client engaging in free association while the therapist listens passively

    Answer: (b). The therapist asks open-ended questions and the client does the discovering. Interpreting unconscious conflict and free association are psychoanalytic, and giving direct advice is exactly what Socratic questioning avoids.

  17. Which of the following is a clinician-rated questionnaire? (RML 2022)

    (a) Beck Depression Inventory (b) Beck Anxiety Inventory (c) Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (d) Both b and c

    Answer: (c) Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale. Max Hamilton's scales are completed by a trained clinician after an interview. The BDI and BAI are self-report inventories filled in by the patient, which is why "both b and c" is wrong.

  18. Fixed role therapy, which encouraged clients to practice new roles and relationships, was developed by: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) George Kelly (b) Aaron Beck (c) Albert Ellis (d) Fritz Perls

    Answer: (a) George Kelly, as part of his personal construct theory (1955): the client lives for about two weeks as an invented character from a fixed-role sketch. Perls tempts because Gestalt therapy uses the empty chair and other experiments, but fixed-role therapy is Kelly's.

  19. A client engages in safety behaviors, such as gripping a shopping cart tightly when feeling dizzy in a store. In CBT for panic disorder, the therapist would likely: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Prescribe these behaviors to prevent fainting (b) Use these behaviors as a measure of the client's insight (c) Work with the client to gradually drop these behaviors to test catastrophic beliefs (d) Explore the childhood origins of the need for safety

    Answer: (c). Safety behaviours stop the catastrophe from being disconfirmed, so dropping them is the test (Salkovskis; Clark). Prescribing them would keep the belief alive, and panicking people almost never faint; exploring childhood origins is a psychodynamic aim, not CBT's.

  20. It is considered that successful CBT treatments of social phobia include elements of the following: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Exposure therapy (b) Social skills retraining (c) Cognitive restructuring (d) All of above

    Answer: (d) All of the above. A complete CBT package for social anxiety combines exposure, cognitive restructuring and social skills work where real deficits exist. Choosing any single element misses the point that each targets a different maintaining process.

  21. The front-line treatment for schizophrenia is: (RML 2021)

    (a) Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) (b) Stress management training (c) Antipsychotic medication (d) Social skills training

    Answer: (c) Antipsychotic medication. CBT for psychosis, stress management and social skills training are valuable adjuncts that reduce distress and disability, but none replaces medication in schizophrenia.

  22. Dialectical Behaviour Therapy is indicated for the treatment of: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Anxiety Disorders (b) Borderline Personality Disorder (c) Acute and Transient Psychotic Disorder (d) Post-traumatic Stress Disorder

    Answer: (b) Borderline personality disorder. Linehan built DBT for chronically suicidal women with the disorder. Adapted versions now exist for PTSD and other problems, which makes (d) tempting, but the primary indication remains borderline personality disorder.

  23. In Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), the "core mindfulness" skills are primarily derived from which tradition? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Stoicism (b) Zen Buddhism (c) Psychoanalysis (d) Existentialism

    Answer: (b) Zen Buddhism. Linehan, herself a Zen practitioner, translated Zen practice into secular behavioural skills. Stoicism is the root of Ellis's REBT, the usual trap, and psychoanalysis and existentialism are not its sources.

  24. Distress tolerance is a major treatment modality in which of the following therapy? (RML 2024)

    (a) Behaviour therapy (b) Dialectical Behaviour therapy (c) Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (d) Psychodynamic therapy

    Answer: (b) DBT. Distress tolerance is one of DBT's four skills modules, an acceptance module alongside core mindfulness. It is not a component of standard behaviour therapy, Beck-style CBT or psychodynamic therapy.

  25. Which therapy focuses on changing maladaptive thought patterns? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Humanistic therapy (b) Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) (c) Psychoanalysis (d) Play therapy

    Answer: (b) CBT. Changing distorted or maladaptive thinking is CBT's defining aim. Humanistic therapy works through the relationship and the client's growth, psychoanalysis through unconscious conflict and insight, and play therapy through children's play.
