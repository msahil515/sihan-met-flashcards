In 1964 a young woman known to the world only as Gloria walked into a small film studio in California to do something no client had done before. She had agreed to talk about the most private worry in her life, on camera, to three of the most famous psychotherapists in America, one after the other. The project was the idea of the psychologist Everett Shostrom, who wanted students to see, rather than merely read about, how the great schools of therapy actually differed. The three therapists were Carl Rogers, the founder of client-centred therapy; Fritz Perls, the founder of Gestalt therapy; and Albert Ellis, the founder of rational emotive therapy. The result, released in 1965 as Three Approaches to Psychotherapy, became one of the most widely shown teaching films in the history of psychotherapy. Generations of students know it simply as "the Gloria films".

Gloria was recently divorced and had a nine-year-old daughter, Pammy. Since the divorce she had begun to see men, and when Pammy asked her whether she had been with anyone since Daddy, Gloria had lied. Now she felt guilty and confused. Would the truth harm her child? Would the lie? She wanted, very simply, to be told what to do.

Rogers did not tell her. He leaned towards her, listened, and kept putting into words what she seemed to be feeling, including her half-spoken sense that she already knew what she wanted to do. As the half hour went on, Gloria spoke more slowly and more honestly, and near the end she said she wished her own father could have talked to her the way Rogers was talking to her. "You look to me like a pretty nice daughter," Rogers replied. Perls behaved almost like her opponent. He pointed out that she was smiling and fidgeting while she claimed to be frightened, and told her, in effect, that she was a phony. Gloria grew angry and fought back, and Perls seemed pleased: for a moment, he thought, she had stopped performing and was really present. Ellis, the third, argued briskly with the beliefs he thought were making her miserable (his approach belongs to Chapter 5).

Three experts, one client, one problem, three utterly different conversations. Which of them helped? Gloria kept in touch with Rogers by letter for years afterwards, and the films have been argued over ever since. Keep Gloria in mind as you read this chapter, because the questions she raises run through all of it. Must a helper give advice, or can people find their own answers? Is a warm, accepting relationship enough to change someone, or must the helper sometimes frustrate, confront or direct? What would a therapist who believes that life is a search for meaning have said to her, and what would the newer brief therapies, which might have asked Gloria to imagine that a miracle had happened overnight, have added? We will come back to her at the end.

6.1 The Third Force: What These Therapies ShareMedium yield · 3 Qs

By the middle of the twentieth century two great systems dominated psychotherapy. The first was psychoanalysis (Chapter 3), which pictured the person as driven by unconscious sexual and aggressive wishes laid down in early childhood, and which cast the therapist as an expert who interprets what the patient cannot see. The second was behaviourism (Chapter 4), which pictured the person as the sum of his or her conditioning, and which cast the therapist as a technician who rearranges stimuli and reinforcers. Both systems were powerful. Both, to a growing number of psychologists in the 1950s, left something out: the person as he or she actually lives, choosing, valuing, hoping, and asking what life is for.

The American psychologist Abraham Harold Maslow (1908 to 1970) gave the new movement its name. Psychoanalysis was the first force in psychology and behaviourism the second; the movement that put human growth, freedom and experience at the centre was the third force. It became organised in the early 1960s, when the Journal of Humanistic Psychology (1961) and the Association for Humanistic Psychology were founded, and Maslow and Carl Rogers were its two best-known figures. Humanistic psychology can be defined as the approach that studies the whole person from the person's own point of view and assumes that people have an inborn potential for growth that flourishes under the right conditions. Maslow's own contributions (the hierarchy of needs, self-actualisation and peak experiences) belong to the Personality book; Maslow was a theorist rather than a therapist. It was Rogers who turned humanistic ideas into a therapy, and we meet him in Section 6.2.

Six shared assumptions

The therapies in this chapter differ a great deal, as Gloria discovered, but most of them rest on a common set of assumptions. It is worth learning them as a list, because examiners like to ask which statement is "not associated with the humanistic perspective".

  • Phenomenology. The phenomenological approach holds that what matters is the world as the person perceives and experiences it, the person's internal frame of reference, rather than an outside expert's classification of it. Two students failing the same examination live in two different worlds.
  • Growth. Human beings have an inborn tendency to grow, to develop their capacities and to move toward health. Problems arise when this tendency is blocked, not because people are basically bad or merely conditioned.
  • Freedom and responsibility. People are not simply pushed by the past or pulled by reinforcers; within limits they choose, and they are responsible for their choices.
  • Holism. The person is an organised whole (body, feeling, thought and relationships together) and cannot be understood by dissecting symptoms.
  • The present. The focus is on the here and now of experience rather than on reconstructing childhood.
  • The relationship. Change happens through an authentic, respectful human relationship, not through the expert application of technique. Diagnosis and testing are usually played down or rejected.

Within this family three branches can be told apart. The humanistic branch (Rogers and Maslow) is optimistic and growth-centred. The existential branch (Binswanger, Boss, May, Frankl and Yalom) shares the respect for experience and freedom but is more sober, because it insists that the person must face the hard limits of life: death, isolation, the burden of freedom and the absence of any ready-made meaning. The difference was real. In 1982 Rollo May published an open letter to Rogers arguing that Rogers's trusting view of human nature left no room for the human capacity for evil. The experiential branch (Gestalt therapy, Gendlin's focusing and Greenberg's emotion-focused therapy) emphasises the moment-by-moment experiencing of bodily sensation and emotion in the session itself.

Researchers today group the person-centred, Gestalt, focusing, emotion-focused and existential therapies together as the humanistic-experiential psychotherapies. Robert Elliott, Leslie Greenberg and their colleagues, who have reviewed the research on them, describe their general aim as helping clients to develop a more fluid and integrated way of organising the self, by attending to and making sense of present experience, especially emotion. Compare that aim with the aims of the other families: psychodynamic therapy seeks to understand and resolve deep-seated unconscious conflicts, behaviour therapy seeks to change maladaptive behaviours, and interpersonal and social skills approaches seek to develop interpersonal skills. Each aim is worthy, but only the first is the signature of the humanistic-experiential tradition.

These assumptions also produce a distinctive account of how psychological problems arise, sometimes called the humanistic-existential model of abnormal behaviour. Distress comes from blocked growth (Rogers's incongruence), from living inauthentically and avoiding responsibility (the existentialists), or from a loss of meaning (Frankl). This is quite different from the diathesis-stress model, in which a biological or psychological vulnerability is triggered by stress, and from the sociocultural and cognitive models, which locate the causes of disorder in social and cultural conditions and in faulty thinking respectively (all three are taught in Chapter 1).

The chapter ends with a group of newer, briefer approaches: narrative therapy, solution-focused brief therapy and motivational interviewing. They were born in the 1970s and 1980s, they keep the humanistic respect for the client as the expert on his or her own life, but they are briefer, more future-focused and more deliberately strategic. Finally, Section 6.10 turns to the helping skills (listening, reflecting, challenging) that every counsellor and clinician uses, whatever the school. Table 6.1 sets the three forces side by side.

Maslow and Rogers are the proponents of the humanistic approach, not the existential approach, even though the two are often taught together. The "third force" is humanistic psychology. The label "fourth force" has been claimed twice: Maslow and Anthony Sutich used it for transpersonal psychology in the late 1960s, and Paul Pedersen later used it for multiculturalism in counselling. If a question lists "availability of reinforcement" among features of the humanistic view, that is the odd one out: reinforcement is a behavioural idea. And if a question asks which model says that a biological predisposition is set off by stress, the answer is the diathesis-stress model, not the humanistic-existential model.

6.2 Carl Rogers and Person-Centred TherapyHigh yield · 18 Qs

No psychotherapist of the twentieth century did more to change how ordinary helpers listen than Carl Ransom Rogers (1902 to 1987). His approach is the most frequently examined topic in this chapter, so we take it slowly: first the man, then the theory of personality, then the conditions he believed were enough to produce change, then what his therapy looks like in practice, then the research it inspired.

From Rochester to La Jolla

Rogers was born in 1902 in Oak Park, a suburb of Chicago, the fourth of six children in a prosperous, loving and strictly religious Protestant family in which dancing, cards and the theatre were forbidden. He went to the University of Wisconsin to study agriculture, switched toward the ministry, and in 1922 travelled to China for an international Christian student conference, an experience that loosened his family's fundamentalism. He studied at Union Theological Seminary in New York, crossed the road to Teachers College, Columbia University, and took his PhD in clinical and educational psychology in 1931.

For twelve years (1928 to 1940) Rogers worked at the child study department of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children in Rochester, New York, seeing delinquent and deprived children. There he met the ideas of Otto Rank, a former member of Freud's inner circle, mainly through Rank's student, the social worker Jessie Taft. Rank saw the client, not the therapist, as the creative force in therapy. Rogers later told a story about how this lesson came home to him. He had been working, with no success, with the mother of a badly behaved boy, patiently trying to help her see that her early rejection of the child lay behind his behaviour. Eventually they both gave up. As she reached the door she turned and asked whether he ever took adults for counselling. When he said yes, she sat down again and poured out her despair about her marriage, and real help began. Rogers concluded that "it is the client who knows what hurts, what directions to go, what problems are crucial."

In 1940 Rogers moved to Ohio State University, and in December of that year a lecture at the University of Minnesota made him realise that his ideas were genuinely new. His book Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942) described what he called non-directive therapy, in which the counsellor does not advise, interpret or steer, but follows the client. The book included the case of "Herbert Bryan", the first complete course of psychotherapy ever published from sound recordings. In 1945 Rogers moved to the University of Chicago, where he directed the counselling centre, and in 1951 he published Client-Centered Therapy, which renamed the approach client-centred therapy and added a full theory of personality. He was president of the American Psychological Association in 1947. In 1957 he moved to the University of Wisconsin, where he published his most famous paper (on the necessary and sufficient conditions for change), led a large and troubled study of therapy with hospitalised schizophrenia patients, and wrote the best-selling On Becoming a Person (1961).

In 1963 Rogers left university life for La Jolla, California, where he joined the Western Behavioral Sciences Institute and then helped to found the Center for Studies of the Person (1968). His interests widened from individual therapy to encounter groups, education (Freedom to Learn, 1969), marriage, and the reduction of political conflict; he ran workshops for Catholics and Protestants from Northern Ireland and for black and white South Africans. As the approach spread beyond the consulting room it became known as the person-centred approach. Rogers died in February 1987.

So Rogers's therapy carried three names over time, and examiners use all three: non-directive (from 1942), client-centred (from 1951) and person-centred (from the 1970s). Rogers also chose the word "client" deliberately. A patient is sick and passive and receives treatment; a client actively and voluntarily seeks a service and keeps responsibility for his or her own life.

The theory: the actualising tendency, the self and conditions of worth

Rogers's theory begins with a single motive. He described the actualising tendency as the inherent tendency of every organism to develop all its capacities in ways that maintain or enhance it. He liked to recall the potatoes stored in the basement of his boyhood home: in the dark, far from any window, they still sent pale sprouts reaching toward the faint light. Life, Rogers thought, presses toward growth even in the worst conditions. A related idea is the organismic valuing process: the infant's built-in way of judging experiences by whether they enhance the organism. The hungry baby values food and rejects it when full; the baby enjoys being held and protests at pain. The infant, in other words, trusts its own experience.

As the child grows, part of its experience becomes differentiated as the self-concept: the organised, consistent picture a person holds of "I" and "me", together with the values attached to it. Rogers also spoke of the ideal self, the self a person would most like to be. Along with the self comes a powerful need for positive regard, that is, for warmth, love and acceptance from the important people in one's life, and later a need for positive self-regard.

Here the trouble starts. Parents, teachers and others rarely give their regard unconditionally. "We love you when you come first in class." "Good boys do not cry." "Girls from our family become doctors." Because the child needs regard so badly, it begins to value its own experiences not by whether they enhance it but by whether they earn approval. Rogers called these internalised rules conditions of worth: the conditions under which a person feels worthy of love and of self-respect. The child takes in, or introjects, other people's values and treats them as its own.

Once a self-concept has been built around conditions of worth, some of the person's real experiences will not fit it. Rogers called this mismatch between the self-concept and actual experience incongruence. Consider Ananya, a student in Mysuru, who grew up hearing that she would be "the doctor of the family". Her self-concept is "I am a future doctor and a good daughter". Her actual experience is that she is bored in anatomy and comes alive only when she draws. Experiences that threaten the self-concept are sensed as threats even before they are fully conscious (Rogers borrowed the term "subception" for this), and they produce anxiety. The person defends against them in two ways: by distortion ("I am just tired this term") and by denial (simply not admitting the pleasure of drawing to awareness). The greater the incongruence, the more vulnerable the person; if a large incongruence is suddenly forced into awareness, the self-structure can break down into disorganised behaviour. Notice what this theory implies for therapy. If psychological distress comes from conditions of worth, then the cure is a relationship in which there are no conditions of worth: a relationship in which the client is accepted whatever he or she feels, so that denied experience can safely be admitted to awareness and the self-concept can be revised to include it. That is exactly what Rogers set out to describe.

Exam trap

"A split between the organismic valuing process and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth, producing tension and confusion" is incongruence. Conflict is a general term with no special place in Rogers's theory. Introjection is how conditions of worth get in (and is also a Gestalt and psychoanalytic term), but the resulting split is incongruence. The actualising tendency is the growth force that incongruence obstructs. Do not confuse Rogers's actualising tendency, which belongs to every organism, with Maslow's self-actualisation, the top of his need hierarchy; Rogers also used "self-actualising tendency" for the part of the actualising tendency expressed through the self.

The six necessary and sufficient conditions (1957)

In 1957 Rogers published a short paper in the Journal of Consulting Psychology with a breathtakingly bold title: "The necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic personality change." He claimed that if six conditions exist and continue over a period of time, constructive personality change will follow, and that nothing else is needed. The six necessary and sufficient conditions are these:

  1. Two persons are in psychological contact: each makes some perceived difference to the other.
  2. The first, the client, is in a state of incongruence, being vulnerable or anxious.
  3. The second, the therapist, is congruent, or integrated, in the relationship.
  4. The therapist experiences unconditional positive regard for the client.
  5. The therapist experiences an empathic understanding of the client's internal frame of reference and tries to communicate this to the client.
  6. The client perceives, at least to a minimal degree, the therapist's empathy and unconditional positive regard.

Conditions 3, 4 and 5 are what the therapist must provide, and they have become famous as the three core conditions. Congruence (also called genuineness, realness or authenticity) means that in the relationship the therapist is what he or she actually is: inner experience, awareness and what is communicated match, with no professional façade. Unconditional positive regard (also called acceptance, prizing, or warmth) means a warm, non-judgemental valuing of the client as a person, without conditions. The term itself was coined by Rogers's student Stanley Standal in 1954. Accurate empathic understanding means, in Rogers's own words, sensing "the client's private world as if it were your own, but without ever losing the 'as if' quality", and communicating that understanding. In later writings Rogers called congruence the most basic of the three. Table 6.2 sets out what each condition is, and what it is not.

Two words in the title deserve attention. Necessary means that without these conditions no constructive change occurs. Sufficient means that nothing more is required: not a diagnosis, not a special technique, not insight into childhood, not even professional training. Rogers also said the conditions were the same for every kind of client and every kind of therapy, which was a direct challenge to every other school.

The core conditions are empathy, unconditional positive regard and congruence, so "all of the above" is right when all three are listed. Every other trio in the options belongs elsewhere: interpretation, transference and free association are psychoanalytic (Chapter 3); reinforcement, shaping and modelling are behavioural (Chapter 4); disputation, homework and cognitive restructuring are cognitive-behavioural (Chapter 5); confrontation and paradoxical intention belong to other schools in this chapter. Do not confuse the six conditions with the three core conditions: psychological contact, client incongruence and the client's perception of the therapist's attitudes are conditions, but not therapist-provided core conditions.

Also note what is not a Rogerian concept. The therapeutic or working alliance, as defined by Edward Bordin in 1979 (agreement on goals and tasks, plus an emotional bond), grew out of psychodynamic thinking and is a pan-theoretical idea (Chapter 8). Person-centred therapists prefer to speak of the relationship itself, the "real relationship", and the core conditions. Psychoanalysis (where the working alliance was born, Section 3.3), cognitive-behaviour therapy and interpersonal psychotherapy (Klerman and Weissman, Section 3.7) all use the term freely. So person-centred therapy is the approach least likely to use the term "therapeutic alliance", and Bordin's alliance is the option that is not a key concept of client-centred therapy, whereas conditions of worth, unconditional positive regard and the actualising tendency all are.

What a person-centred therapist actually does

If the conditions are what matter, what does the therapist actually say? Mostly, the therapist listens and responds to the client's feelings and meanings from within the client's frame of reference. The best-known response is reflection of feeling: putting into words the feeling that the client is expressing, often more clearly than the client has said it, and offering it back for the client to check. Related responses include simple acceptance ("Mm-hm", "I see"), restatement of content, clarification and summarising. Questions are used sparingly. Interpretation, advice, reassurance, persuasion, diagnosis, psychological testing, history-taking and homework are all absent, because each of them puts the therapist's frame of reference in charge. So are the analyst's search for unconscious desires (Chapter 3) and the behaviour therapist's methods, such as aversive conditioning, in which an unwanted behaviour is paired with an unpleasant stimulus, and strict programmes of behaviour modification (Chapter 4).

A worked example shows the difference. A student says: "My parents keep asking about my marks. I am not even sure why I am doing this course any more." Here are four possible replies.

  • Advice: "You should sit down with your parents and tell them how you feel." This directs the client and assumes the counsellor knows best.
  • Analysis or interpretation: "Perhaps you are still angry with your father for choosing your stream." This imports the counsellor's theory.
  • Reassurance: "Do not worry, everybody feels like that in second year." This dismisses the feeling.
  • Reflection: "There is pressure from outside about marks, and underneath it a deeper doubt, whether this course is really yours at all." This stays with the client's own meaning and invites the client to go further.

Only the last is person-centred. In the 1940s Rogers's main opponent was the directive, trait-and-factor counselling of E. G. Williamson at the University of Minnesota, in which the counsellor diagnosed and advised; the contrast between "directive" and "non-directive" counselling was the great debate of the decade. Later, Rogers grew unhappy that reflection had become a mechanical technique, parodied as "parroting". In 1986 he suggested calling such responses "testing understandings" or "checking perceptions", because their purpose is to check whether the therapist has really understood.

Indian context

In 1973 the Indian psychiatrist J. S. Neki argued in a well-known paper that many Indian clients expect a guru-chela (teacher and disciple) relationship: an active, benevolent, somewhat directive helper. To such a client, pure non-directiveness can feel like indifference. Indian clinicians therefore often combine person-centred listening and acceptance with more structure and guidance than Rogers would have offered, while keeping the decision with the client and the family.

Where therapy leads: process, the fully functioning person and the nineteen propositions

By listening to hundreds of recorded sessions, Rogers described in 1958 how clients change as therapy proceeds. He pictured a continuum running from fixity to fluidity. At the fixed end the client talks about externals, is remote from feelings, sees problems as outside the self and construes experience rigidly. At the fluid end feelings are experienced immediately and richly, in the moment, the self is trusted, and experience is construed tentatively and openly. With his colleague R. A. Rablen he turned this into a seven-stage Process Scale for rating recordings. Hold on to the word fluid: it is exactly the word modern reviewers use for the goal of humanistic-experiential therapy.

The endpoint of this process is what Rogers called the fully functioning person, an ideal toward which people move rather than a state they reach. Rogers described five characteristics:

  • Openness to experience: no need for defensive distortion or denial; every feeling can be admitted to awareness.
  • Existential living: living fully in each moment, letting the self emerge from experience rather than forcing experience into a fixed self.
  • Organismic trusting: trusting one's own total reaction to a situation as a guide to what to do.
  • Experiential freedom: a felt sense of free choice and responsibility for one's life.
  • Creativity: adapting and producing new solutions rather than conforming.

"The good life," Rogers wrote, "is a process, not a state of being. It is a direction, not a destination."

The formal theory behind all this was set out in 1951 as nineteen propositions. You do not need to memorise all nineteen, but you should recognise their main themes: every person lives in a continually changing world of experience of which he or she is the centre (the phenomenal field); the organism reacts to this field as it is perceived, so perception is, for the individual, reality; the organism has one basic tendency, to actualise, maintain and enhance itself; behaviour is best understood from the person's internal frame of reference; part of the perceptual field gradually becomes differentiated as the self; experiences inconsistent with the self are denied or distorted; maladjustment exists when significant experiences are denied to awareness, and adjustment when all experiences can be assimilated into a consistent self; and, under conditions of complete absence of threat to the self, inconsistent experiences can be examined and the self revised. The last of these is the theoretical heart of the therapy.

Research and evaluation

Rogers was the first therapist to bring therapy itself under scientific scrutiny. In an era when the only record of a session was the therapist's later notes, he recorded sessions (with clients' permission) so that researchers could study what actually happened, calling the recordings a microscope for examining "the molecules of personality change". His group also needed a way to measure the self-concept, and they found it in a technique devised by the British psychologist William Stephenson.

Key study: the Q-sort and the self-ideal gap (Chicago, early 1950s)

Question. If Rogers was right, successful therapy should bring the perceived self closer to the ideal self. Could this be measured? Method. The Q-sort, introduced by Stephenson in his book The Study of Behavior (1953), gives the person a large set of cards (around 100) carrying self-descriptive statements such as "I am a submissive person" or "I feel insecure within myself". The person sorts them into about nine piles, from "most like me" to "least like me", with a fixed number of cards in each pile so that the sort forms a forced, roughly normal distribution. The client sorts once to describe "myself as I am" and again to describe "the person I would most like to be", and the two sorts are correlated. Because Q-methodology correlates one person's sorts rather than many people's scores, it captures within-person (idiographic) structure. Results. In the case of "Mrs Oak", the correlation between self and ideal rose from about .36 before therapy to about .79 at follow-up after therapy. In the larger study by John Butler and Gerard Haigh, reported in Rogers and Rosalind Dymond's Psychotherapy and Personality Change (1954), the average self-ideal correlation of 25 clients was close to zero before therapy and rose to about +.3 after therapy, while a comparison group not seeking therapy started higher and did not change. Clients who waited 60 days before starting therapy showed little change while waiting. Criticism. A smaller self-ideal gap could reflect a lowering of ideals, or simply learning what a "healthy" sort looks like; all the data were self-reports; and the samples were small.

In 1967 Charles Truax and Robert Carkhuff developed rating scales for accurate empathy, non-possessive warmth and genuineness, and reported that clients whose therapists offered high levels improved more. Later reviews found the relationship real but weaker than these early claims. The most ambitious study, the Wisconsin project on 28 hospitalised patients with schizophrenia (published in 1967), was a disappointment: patients who received high levels of the conditions did better than those who received low levels, but they did little better than untreated controls. One lasting by-product was Eugene Gendlin's discovery that the clients who benefit are those who can attend to their inner experiencing (Section 6.7).

Modern meta-analyses show that therapist empathy, positive regard and genuineness each correlate modestly but reliably with outcome, with correlations in the region of .2 to .3, across all kinds of therapy (Chapter 8). A British trial in general practice, published by Ward, King and colleagues in 2000, found that non-directive counselling and cognitive-behaviour therapy for depression were equally effective after four months and both better than usual care by the general practitioner, although the differences had disappeared by twelve months. So the core conditions are clearly facilitative. Whether they are necessary is doubtful (people improve in behaviour therapy with technically skilled but not especially warm therapists), and whether they are sufficient for every problem is more doubtful still: a severe obsessive-compulsive disorder or a specific phobia usually needs exposure as well. Other criticisms are that Rogers's concepts are hard to measure, that the theory is optimistic to the point of naivety (May's objection), that it reflects an individualistic Western culture, and that non-directiveness can leave some clients floundering. On the other side of the ledger, Rogers made the relationship a respectable research topic, pioneered process research, and gave the whole helping world its basic listening skills, including the foundations of motivational interviewing (Section 6.9).

Exam trap

The Q-sort was devised by William Stephenson (1953); Rogers's Chicago group made it famous by using it to measure the gap between self and ideal self, which is why some questions credit "Stephenson and Rogers". Its defining feature is that one person sorts statements into a forced, roughly normal distribution, so it captures variation within a person rather than differences between people. It was not designed to replace the MMPI or any other inventory, and it is not a diagnostic test. Do not mix up the three scales of this section either: Rogers and Rablen's Process Scale rates fixity versus fluidity in recorded sessions, the Q-sort measures the self-concept, and Truax and Carkhuff's scales rate the therapist's empathy, warmth and genuineness.

Encounter groups

In the 1960s Rogers became one of the leaders of the human potential movement, whose favourite instrument was the encounter group. An encounter group is a small, relatively unstructured group, typically meeting intensively over a weekend or longer, whose purpose is personal growth, greater self-awareness and more honest relationships, achieved through open self-disclosure, here-and-now expression of feelings and frank feedback from other members. Its members are usually ordinary people seeking growth rather than patients seeking treatment, and its aim is neither diagnosis nor team building. Encounter groups grew partly out of the T-groups (training groups) begun by Kurt Lewin and his colleagues in the late 1940s at the National Training Laboratories in Bethel, Maine, whose aim was to teach people about group dynamics and leadership (Chapter 7). Centres such as the Esalen Institute at Big Sur, California, founded in 1962, made such groups famous.

Rogers described his own version, the "basic encounter group", in Carl Rogers on Encounter Groups (1970) and called the intensive group experience perhaps the most significant social invention of the century. He described a typical sequence of about fifteen process patterns, which begins with milling around and resistance to personal expression, moves through the expression of negative feelings, the expression of personally meaningful material, the cracking of façades, feedback and confrontation, and reaches "the basic encounter", in which members meet one another more directly than in ordinary life, followed by expressions of closeness and changes in behaviour.

Were encounter groups helpful? The best study, Encounter Groups: First Facts (1973) by Morton Lieberman, Irvin Yalom and Matthew Miles, assigned about 200 Stanford students to 18 groups representing 10 different approaches. About a third of participants benefited, a similar proportion were unchanged, and about 8 percent were "casualties" who suffered lasting harm. The casualties came mostly from groups run by aggressive, charismatic, confrontational leaders; the best results came from leaders who were caring and who helped members make sense of what was happening to them.

Exam trap

The principal role of an encounter group is to encourage personal growth and self-change through self-disclosure and interaction. Simply "interacting socially", "team building" and "aiding the therapist in diagnosis" are the bait. Keep the group formats apart: encounter groups (Rogers, personal growth), T-groups (Lewin, learning group dynamics and interpersonal skills), psychodrama (Jacob Moreno, acting out one's life on a stage) and the therapeutic community (Maxwell Jones, a whole hospital ward run democratically as a treatment). The last three are taught in Chapters 7 and 9.

6.3 Gestalt TherapyHigh yield · 10 Qs

Go back to the second of the Gloria films. Rogers had followed Gloria wherever she went; the bearded man in his early seventies who interviewed her next seemed almost to go out of his way to unsettle her. Within minutes he was commenting on her smile, her fidgeting hands and the way she held her body, and when she said she was frightened of him he told her she was not frightened at all, merely playing at it. Why would a therapist deliberately frustrate a frightened client? Because, in his view, Gloria's trouble was not a lack of acceptance but a lack of awareness. She was, he thought, playing the helpless little girl so that someone else would do her work for her, and the cure was to frustrate that game skilfully until she discovered the support she already had inside herself. The man was Fritz Perls, and his therapy is the subject of this section.

Gestalt therapy shares the humanistic faith in growth and the existential stress on freedom and responsibility, but its method is quite different from Rogers's. Where the person-centred therapist offers conditions and waits, the Gestalt therapist is active, inventive and sometimes provocative, and works through what the client is doing, feeling and sensing in the room, right now.

Fritz Perls and the making of Gestalt therapy

Fritz Perls (Friedrich Salomon Perls, 1893 to 1970) was born in Berlin, the youngest of three children of a middle-class Jewish family. He was a rebellious pupil who failed a school year twice, served as a medic in the First World War, and qualified in medicine in 1920. In 1926 he became an assistant to the neurologist Kurt Goldstein at an institute for brain-injured soldiers in Frankfurt. Goldstein, who studied his patients through the lens of Gestalt psychology, taught that the organism functions as a whole and strives to actualise itself, and that what a person perceives as figure against ground tells you how that person is organising his world. In Frankfurt Perls also met Laura Perls (born Lore Posner, 1905 to 1990), a student of Gestalt psychology who became his wife and his lifelong intellectual partner.

Perls trained as a psychoanalyst in Vienna and Berlin. He was analysed first by Karen Horney (the neo-Freudian whose theory of personality you meet in the Personality book) and later by Wilhelm Reich, whose attention to posture, voice and "character armour" (chronic muscular tension that holds feelings back) shaped Perls's lifelong habit of watching the body rather than listening only to words. When the Nazis came to power the Perlses fled, and in 1934 they settled in Johannesburg, where Perls founded the South African Institute for Psychoanalysis in 1935. There he read Holism and Evolution (1926) by the South African statesman Jan Smuts, the book that coined the word "holism". In 1936 he travelled to the International Psychoanalytic Congress at Marienbad with a paper on "oral resistances" that was coolly received, and his brief visit to Freud in Vienna the same year lasted only a few minutes and left him feeling snubbed. Perls was moving away from psychoanalysis.

The break appeared in his first book, Ego, Hunger and Aggression, written in South Africa and first published in Durban in 1942 (a London edition followed in 1947), with several chapters written by Laura, who was not credited. Its central metaphor is eating. A healthy person bites off experience, chews it and digests it, keeping what nourishes and rejecting what does not; a disturbed person either refuses to bite at all or swallows other people's ideas whole, without chewing. Perls called his method at this stage "concentration therapy". In 1946 the Perlses moved to New York, where they joined forces with Paul Goodman (1911 to 1972), a writer and social critic. The founding text, Gestalt Therapy: Excitement and Growth in the Human Personality (1951), was written by Perls, Ralph Hefferline (a Columbia University psychologist who contributed a set of awareness exercises) and Goodman (who wrote most of the theory). In 1952 Fritz, Laura and Goodman set up the New York Institute for Gestalt Therapy, which Laura went on to lead for decades.

Perls himself was restless. He travelled, founded training groups in several cities, and from 1964 to 1969 lived as resident teacher at the Esalen Institute at Big Sur, California, the centre of the human potential movement you met in Section 6.2. His weekend demonstration workshops there, recorded in Gestalt Therapy Verbatim (1969), made him famous. In 1969 he moved to Cowichan Lake on Vancouver Island, Canada, to start a Gestalt community, and he died in Chicago in March 1970.

Gestalt therapy is therefore a blend. From psychoanalysis it kept the interest in conflict and resistance (but not the unconscious as a hidden storehouse to be interpreted). From Reich it took the body. From Goldstein, Smuts and Gestalt psychology it took holism and the language of figure and ground. From the philosopher Salomo Friedlaender it took the idea that life is organised in polarities balanced around a creative middle point. From existentialism and phenomenology it took the here and now and the stress on responsibility; from Zen Buddhism, the value of simple, non-judging awareness; and from Jacob Moreno's psychodrama (Chapter 7), the practice of acting things out rather than talking about them.

Exam trap

Gestalt therapy is not Gestalt psychology. Gestalt psychology was the German school of perception founded by Max Wertheimer (with Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka) around 1912; it studied how we perceive organised wholes, and it is taught in the General Psychology book. Gestalt therapy is Perls's psychotherapy of the 1940s and 1950s. The Gestalt psychologists themselves rejected the connection: Mary Henle, one of their students, concluded in 1978 that Perls had borrowed the vocabulary without the substance. Gestalt therapy was founded by Fritz Perls with Laura Perls and Paul Goodman; the 1951 book is by Perls, Hefferline and Goodman. Karen Horney was one of Perls's analysts, not a Gestalt therapist and not the founder of logotherapy.

Core ideas: wholes, figure and ground, the here and now

The German word Gestalt has no exact English equivalent; it means a form, shape or configuration, an organised whole that is more than the sum of its parts. Gestalt therapy can be defined as an experiential and existential therapy that aims at growth and integration through moment-to-moment awareness of what one is feeling, sensing and doing, and how, in the here and now. Its view of the healthy person rests on organismic self-regulation: left to itself, the organism attends to its most pressing need, meets it, and moves on.

Perls described this process in the language of perception. At any moment one need stands out as the figure while everything else recedes into the ground. Imagine a student in a Mysuru examination hall. For an hour the question paper is the figure. Then thirst grows until it becomes the figure, the paper fades into the ground, the student asks for water and drinks, and with the need satisfied, thirst recedes and the paper returns. Each such episode is a gestalt that forms and closes. Later Gestalt therapists, notably Joseph Zinker in Creative Process in Gestalt Therapy (1977), drew the episode as a cycle of experience, shown in Figure 6.2: a sensation arises, becomes awareness, mobilises energy, leads to action, makes contact with the environment, and ends in satisfied withdrawal, ready for the next figure.

What happens when a gestalt cannot close? A son who never told his dying father that he loved him, a woman who swallowed her anger at a humiliation years ago, a girl who was never allowed to grieve: in each case a need was mobilised and then blocked. The incomplete gestalt does not fade. It remains as unfinished business, unexpressed feelings (resentment, grief, guilt, longing) that press for completion and intrude on the present as preoccupation, tension, repeated quarrels or bodily symptoms. Perls thought resentment the most common and most troublesome kind. Much of Gestalt therapy consists of bringing unfinished business into the present so that it can at last be finished.

Notice the phrase "into the present". Gestalt therapy is radically a therapy of the here and now. Perls taught that the past exists only as memory and the future only as anticipation, both of them experienced now; therefore the past is worked with only as it lives in the client today. He even defined anxiety as "the gap between the now and the then": the stage fright of a person rehearsing a future he cannot yet control. For the same reason the Gestalt therapist asks what and how rather than why. "Why do you feel angry with your mother?" invites explanations, theories and excuses, which Perls regarded as a way of talking about life instead of living it. "What are you doing with your hands as you speak of her? How do you feel as you do it?" brings the client back to experience. "Lose your mind and come to your senses" was one of Perls's slogans.

The goal is awareness: full contact with what one is sensing, feeling, wanting and doing. "Awareness per se, by and of itself, can be curative," Perls wrote, because an organism that is fully aware will regulate itself. He distinguished three zones of awareness: the inner zone (bodily sensations and feelings), the outer zone (the world as seen, heard and touched) and the middle zone of fantasy (thinking, planning, remembering, imagining), in which neurotic people spend far too much of their time. With awareness comes responsibility, which Perls liked to spell "response-ability": the ability to respond, and the ownership of one's own feelings and actions. Clients are therefore taught to say "I won't" instead of "I can't", and "I choose to" instead of "I have to". Maturity, in Perls's phrase, is "the transcendence from environmental support to self-support". The neurotic person manipulates the environment for support, by playing helpless, confused or charming, and the therapist's job is to frustrate these manoeuvres warmly but firmly until the client finds his own feet. That is what Perls was doing with Gloria.

How does change happen? Arnold Beisser stated what he called the paradoxical theory of change in 1970: "change occurs when one becomes what he is, not when he tries to become what he is not." A client who fights his sadness stays stuck in the fight; one who fully experiences and owns the sadness finds that it moves. The therapist therefore does not push the client toward a goal but helps him become more fully what he already is.

Perls also pictured the neurotic personality as five layers that must be peeled away on the road to authentic living. The phony (or cliché) layer is made of stereotyped, inauthentic games ("How are you?" "Fine!"). The phobic layer is the avoidance of the pain of seeing ourselves as we are. At the impasse the external supports have been removed but internal support has not yet been found, and the person feels stuck, empty and dead; Perls regarded it as the decisive point of therapy. At the implosive layer the person feels the energy that has been held back, turned in upon itself, and in the explosive layer it is released into authentic experience, which Perls said takes four forms: grief, anger, joy and orgasm.

Contact and its disturbances

Growth, for the Gestalt therapist, happens at the contact boundary, the meeting point between the person and the environment, where the person both connects with and stays separate from what is not himself. "Contact is the lifeblood of growth," wrote Erving and Miriam Polster in Gestalt Therapy Integrated (1973). Good contact means meeting other people and things vividly while keeping one's own identity. Problems arise when a person habitually interrupts contact in one of several characteristic ways, the contact boundary disturbances. Perls, Hefferline and Goodman (1951) described five, ending with egotism; the Polsters' list of five, the one most textbooks use, keeps introjection, projection, retroflection and confluence, drops egotism and adds deflection. Children inevitably introject their parents' values, and some introjection is harmless, but an adult whose "shoulds" are all borrowed lives by rules he has never examined; introjects are the raw material of the top dog you will meet shortly. In projection traffic flows the other way: something inside is placed outside. The person who cannot own his hostility experiences everyone else as hostile; the student who did not prepare sees an unfair examiner. In retroflection the energy that should go out turns back on the self, so that anger becomes self-blame, tension or self-harm, and the wish to be cared for becomes grim self-sufficiency. In deflection the person avoids the impact of contact by blunting it, with humour, abstraction, generalities or a change of subject. In confluence there is no boundary at all: the person merges with others, needs them to agree, and cannot tell where his feelings end and theirs begin. In egotism (a special Gestalt meaning of the word) the person stands outside his own experience, monitoring it, so that nothing is risked and nothing is fully lived.

None of these is pathological in itself. Healthy confluence is part of empathy and of intimacy; healthy retroflection is self-control; healthy introjection lets a child learn. The question the Gestalt therapist asks is whether the person is aware of using the style and whether it lets present needs be met. Notice also the overlap with psychoanalytic vocabulary. In psychoanalysis projection and introjection are defence mechanisms of the ego (taught with the other defences in the Personality book); in Gestalt therapy they are styles of contact. The words are the same, but the frame is different.

Exam trap

"A high need for acceptance and liking, resulting in enmeshment" is confluence, not projection, introjection or retroflection. "Swallowing whole" is introjection; "doing to oneself what one wants to do to others" is retroflection; "turning aside with jokes or vagueness" is deflection. The Polsters' standard five are introjection, projection, retroflection, deflection and confluence; egotism belongs to the original 1951 list.

A related stem from the Personality syllabus asks for "any distorting or selective effect of a person's inner world on his perception or conception of the outer world". RML 2024 keyed denial, but that wording is the broad, classic definition of projection (the idea behind projective tests). Denial refuses to admit one particular painful fact; it does not colour perception in general. If the stem reappears, projection is the defensible answer.

The techniques: experiments in awareness

Gestalt therapists do not call their methods techniques so much as experiments: something the client is invited to try, here and now, to discover what happens. Some are simple. The therapist may ask "What are you aware of now?", or invite the client to begin a string of sentences with "Now I am aware that...", following his attention from moment to moment (the awareness continuum). The therapist reports observations rather than interpretations: "I notice that your voice drops when you mention your brother." When a client shows a small gesture, he may be asked to exaggerate it (a clenched fist made tighter and tighter, until the client says "I want to hit something"). Reversal asks a soft-spoken client to try speaking loudly, or a relentlessly cheerful one to voice a complaint, so that the disowned pole of a polarity comes into awareness. Asked to stay with a feeling rather than escape it, the client learns that the feeling can be lived through. The therapist may ask "May I feed you a sentence?" and offer a statement for the client to try on ("I am angry with you"), to see whether it fits.

Language receives close attention, because the way we speak reveals how we disown experience. A client who says "It is frightening" is asked to say "I am frightened"; "You feel lonely in this city" becomes "I feel lonely"; "I can't tell him" becomes "I won't tell him"; "I need to" becomes "I want to"; "I have to" becomes "I choose to". A question that is really a statement ("Don't you think I should leave the course?") is turned into the statement it conceals ("I want to leave the course"). "But" may be replaced by "and", so that two feelings are allowed to stand side by side. In group work a client may be asked to make the rounds, saying a sentence such as "I am afraid you will laugh at me" to each member in turn and adding "and I take responsibility for it" to statements of feeling.

The most famous experiment of all is the empty chair technique. The client imagines a person (often someone dead, absent or impossible to confront) sitting in an empty chair and speaks to that person directly, in the present tense. He may then move to the other chair and answer as the other person. Because the client speaks to rather than about, feelings that have been held for years come into the room. Consider a worked example. Meera, aged twenty-eight, was working in Bengaluru when her father collapsed in Mysuru; he died before she could reach home, and two years later she still cannot enter his room. Talking about her grief has changed nothing. Her Gestalt therapist places an empty chair in front of her and asks her to see her father in it. Hesitantly at first, then in a flood, Meera tells him that she is sorry she was not there, that she was angry he never told anyone he was ill, and that she loves him. Invited to sit in his chair and answer as him, she hears herself say, in his voice, "You did not fail me. I was proud of you." Back in her own chair she says goodbye. The unfinished business has been finished, not by insight but by completing in the present the act that the past interrupted.

The two-chair technique uses the same method for conflicts inside the person. Each side of an inner split is given its own chair, and the client moves between them, speaking for each side in turn. Perls's favourite split was between the top dog and the underdog. The top dog is the righteous, demanding, perfectionist voice ("You should have cleared the examination last year; you are lazy; you are wasting your parents' money"). The underdog is the apologetic, helpless, wheedling voice ("I am trying my best; I will start properly tomorrow"). The top dog commands and the underdog promises, but the underdog usually wins, by stalling and sabotage. Dialogue between them, with the client fully inhabiting each side, tends to soften the critic and lead to an integration that neither side could reach alone. The same format is used for a dialogue between two parts of the body (one hand against the other), between the client and a symptom ("Be your headache: what do you say to Ravi?"), or between the client and a parent who lives on as an introject.

In his workshops Perls used the hot seat. One volunteer at a time took the chair beside Perls and worked with him on an issue, usually with the empty chair facing them, while the rest of the group watched; the others learned by identifying with the person at work. The format suited demonstrations, and it is one reason Gestalt therapy became known as a dramatic, leader-centred method.

Dreams receive special treatment. Freud had called the dream the royal road to the unconscious; Perls called it "the royal road to integration". He did not interpret dreams. Instead, every person and every object in a dream was treated as a projection of a disowned part of the dreamer, and the client was asked to tell the dream in the present tense and then to become each element in turn, speaking as it ("I am the locked door. I keep everyone out. Nobody gets in to hurt me"). When the dreamer finds that he is also the door, he reowns a part of himself. Gestalt therapists also give homework, asking clients to carry the experiments into daily life.

Perls summed up his individualism in the "Gestalt prayer", printed in Gestalt Therapy Verbatim (1969):

I do my thing and you do your thing. I am not in this world to live up to your expectations, and you are not in this world to live up to mine. You are you, and I am I, and if by chance we find each other, it's beautiful. If not, it can't be helped.

The prayer captured the spirit of the 1960s, and it also captured the criticism that would follow. In 1972 Walter Tubbs published a reply, "Beyond Perls", warning that if each of us simply does his own thing, we risk losing each other and ourselves.

Exam trap

The empty chair belongs to Gestalt therapy (Fritz Perls); it is the single most examined fact in this section. It is not person-centred therapy (reflection), psychoanalysis (free association, interpretation), CBT or REBT (disputing beliefs), dialectical behaviour therapy (skills training) or Bowen's family systems therapy (the genogram, Chapter 7). Emotion-focused therapy (Section 6.7), schema therapy and psychodrama later borrowed chair work, but the hallmark is Gestalt. Keep the chairs apart: the empty chair is for unfinished business with another person; the two-chair dialogue is for a split within the self; the hot seat is the workshop chair beside the leader. Top dog and underdog are Gestalt terms, not the Parent and Child ego states of Eric Berne's transactional analysis.

Evaluation

For many years Gestalt therapy had almost no research behind it; Perls thought that experiments in awareness, not statistics, were the proper test of his ideas. The research that does exist came mainly from Leslie Greenberg and his colleagues, who took the chair techniques into the laboratory. In a series of studies from the late 1970s onward, Greenberg showed that when a client voiced a "split" (two sides of the self in conflict), a two-chair dialogue at that moment led to deeper experiencing and more resolution of the conflict than empathic reflection alone. The empty chair received the same treatment.

Key study: resolving unfinished business with the empty chair (Paivio and Greenberg, 1995)

Question. Does empty-chair work actually resolve long-standing unfinished business with a significant other? Method. Sandra Paivio and Leslie Greenberg, in Toronto, randomly assigned adults troubled by unresolved feelings toward someone important in their lives (most often a parent who had neglected or abused them) either to twelve weeks of experiential therapy built around empty-chair dialogue or to a psychoeducational group about the same issues. Results. The experiential clients improved more on general symptoms, interpersonal problems and the resolution of their unfinished business, and the gains held at follow-up. Conclusion. Chair work can be a specific, effective intervention when it is used with the right problem at the right moment. Criticism. The trial was small, the comparison group was a less intensive treatment, and the therapists were committed to the method.

Broader reviews of the humanistic-experiential family by Robert Elliott, Greenberg and their colleagues have found large improvements from pre-treatment to post-treatment, and roughly equal effectiveness when these therapies are compared head to head with others; the small advantage sometimes found for CBT tends to shrink once the researchers' own allegiance is taken into account (Chapter 8).

The criticisms are as well known as the techniques. Classical Gestalt therapy depended heavily on the personality of Perls himself, and in the hands of imitators it could become confrontational and even harmful; you saw in Section 6.2 that Lieberman, Yalom and Miles found the most casualties in encounter groups led by aggressive, charismatic leaders. Its contempt for "thinking" and explanation could be anti-intellectual, and its individualism (the Gestalt prayer) sat uneasily with clients whose lives are built around families and communities, as most lives in India are. Modern Gestalt therapy has moved a long way from the Esalen stage. Relational or dialogic Gestalt therapists such as Gary Yontef and Lynne Jacobs, drawing on Martin Buber's idea of the I-Thou relationship (Section 6.4), stress a warm, respectful, collaborative relationship, and the chair techniques live on in emotion-focused therapy and schema therapy.

Clinical link

Chair work is powerful, which is exactly why it needs care. A grieving or guilt-ridden client can be helped enormously by an empty-chair goodbye, but a client with psychosis, severe dissociation or a fragile sense of self may be overwhelmed or confused by speaking to an imagined person, and a trauma survivor pushed too quickly to confront an abuser in the chair may be retraumatised. Experienced therapists wait for a clear marker (unfinished business, a self-critical split), explain the experiment, ask permission, and make sure the client leaves the session grounded.

6.4 Existential TherapyMedium yield · 3 Qs

Gestalt therapy borrowed heavily from existential philosophy, but it remained a therapy of techniques. The therapies in this section and the next go to the source. Existential therapy is less a set of methods than a way of looking at human life and its troubles. It asks what it means to be a person who knows that he will die, who must choose without any guarantee of being right, who can never fully share his inner life with another, and who lives in a universe that does not hand him a ready-made purpose. Its first practitioners were European psychiatrists, trained in psychoanalysis, who found that Freud's biological drives explained too little about the anguished, confused, choosing people in front of them.

Existential therapy can be defined as a philosophically based approach that helps clients face the inescapable conditions of human existence (the existential givens, such as death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness) and live more authentically, with awareness, choice and responsibility. Like Gestalt therapy it values the present and experience; like Rogers it values the relationship; but its mood is more sober. Anxiety, for the existentialist, is not a symptom to be removed but a normal part of being human, and sometimes a teacher.

Two of these thinkers matter most for the examination. Martin Heidegger (1889 to 1976) described human existence as Dasein, a German word meaning literally "being there". We are "thrown" into a world we did not choose (a family, a body, a time and place); we tend to live inauthentically, doing what "one" does, absorbed in the anonymous crowd that Heidegger called das Man (the "they"); and we become authentic when we face our own finitude, our being-towards-death, and choose our own way. Jean-Paul Sartre (1905 to 1980) put the existentialist creed into a slogan in a famous lecture of 1945: "existence precedes essence". A paper knife is designed for a purpose before it exists; a human being exists first and must then define himself through choices. We are "condemned to be free", and when we pretend otherwise ("I had no choice; that is just how I am") we are in what Sartre called bad faith.

Daseinsanalysis: Binswanger and Boss

The first psychiatrists to apply Heidegger to their patients were two Swiss. Ludwig Binswanger (1881 to 1966) directed the Bellevue sanatorium at Kreuzlingen, on the Swiss shore of Lake Constance, which his family had run for generations. He was a lifelong friend of Freud, and their friendship survived his growing disagreement with Freud's biological theory. From the late 1920s Binswanger turned to Heidegger and developed what he called Daseinsanalysis (Daseinsanalyse): the attempt to understand a patient's whole way of being-in-the-world, and especially his world-design, the overall pattern through which his world is disclosed to him and which limits what he can experience. His most famous case was Ellen West, a young woman tormented by a dread of becoming fat and a craving for food (today we would speak of an eating disorder), who took her own life in 1921; Binswanger's long analysis of her existence, published in 1944, became a classic of existential psychiatry.

Binswanger described three simultaneous modes in which every person is in the world, and these are the most examined terms in this section. The Umwelt ("world around") is the biological and physical world: the body, its drives and needs, and the natural environment into which we are thrown and which we share with animals; its time is clock time. The Mitwelt ("with-world") is the world of relationships with other people, in which, as Rollo May put it, "in the encounter both persons are changed". The Eigenwelt ("own world") is the person's relationship with himself: self-awareness, the sense of "for me" that colours every experience. These are not three places but three aspects of a single existence. When you eat a meal with your family you are in the Umwelt (the body being fed), the Mitwelt (the conversation and the bonds at the table) and the Eigenwelt (your awareness of what this meal means to you) all at once. Binswanger and May argued that psychoanalysis and behaviourism dealt mainly with the Umwelt and neglected the other two, especially the Eigenwelt. Much later the British existential therapist Emmy van Deurzen added a fourth dimension, the Überwelt ("over-world"), a person's relationship with beliefs, values and the spiritual or ideal world.

Medard Boss (1903 to 1990), a Zurich psychiatrist, had a brief analysis with Freud in Vienna as a young man and trained further as an analyst before coming under Heidegger's influence. The two became close friends, and from 1959 to 1969 Heidegger travelled regularly to Boss's home at Zollikon, near Zurich, to teach philosophy to psychiatrists and trainees (the Zollikon seminars). In Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis (English edition 1963) Boss kept the practice of psychoanalysis, the couch and free association, while discarding Freud's theory of drives and energies. He asked what possibilities of existence a patient had closed off: in what ways he was open or closed to others (the spatiality of existence), how he lived his time (temporality), how his mood attuned him to some things and blinded him to others, and how his inevitable guilt (every choice forecloses other possibilities) and his mortality shaped his life. Boss treated dreams phenomenologically: a dream was not a disguised wish to be decoded but a way the dreamer's existence showed itself, to be explored just as it appeared.

Exam trap

Dasein ("being there", being-in-the-world) is Heidegger's term and belongs to existential psychology, not to behavioural, cognitive or psychoanalytic psychology. Its three modes are Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt (biological world, social world, own world), with van Deurzen's Überwelt as a later fourth. The other trios offered in such questions belong elsewhere: id, ego and superego are Freud's structural model; conscious, preconscious and unconscious are Freud's topographical model; sensorimotor, preoperational and concrete operational are Piaget's stages (Developmental book). Do not confuse Binswanger and Boss's Daseinsanalysis with Frankl's Existenzanalyse, the German name for logotherapy (Section 6.5).

Rollo May and existential anxiety

It was an American, Rollo May (1909 to 1994), who brought these European ideas to the English-speaking world. May was born in Ohio, studied with Alfred Adler in Vienna in the early 1930s, and then studied theology at Union Theological Seminary in New York, where the theologian Paul Tillich became his friend and mentor. In his early thirties he contracted tuberculosis and spent many months in a sanatorium, uncertain whether he would live. There he noticed that the patients who passively accepted their condition seemed to decline, while those who fought it did better, and he began to think about anxiety from the inside. His doctoral dissertation, published as The Meaning of Anxiety (1950), was among the first clinical psychology doctorates awarded by Columbia University. With Ernest Angel and Henri Ellenberger he edited Existence (1958), which translated Binswanger, Minkowski and other European existential psychiatrists for American readers and effectively launched existential psychology in the United States. His later books, Love and Will (1969), The Courage to Create (1975) and Freedom and Destiny (1981), reached a wide public.

For May, anxiety is "the apprehension cued off by a threat to some value which the individual holds essential to his existence as a personality". At bottom it is the awareness that we could cease to be, and the awareness that we are free. May, and later May and Yalom together, distinguished two kinds. Normal anxiety is proportionate to the threat, does not require repression, and can be faced and used creatively; the anxiety of a student before a decisive examination, or of anyone choosing a career or a partner, is of this kind. Neurotic anxiety is out of proportion to the situation, involves repression and other defences, and is destructive rather than constructive, paralysing the person instead of mobilising him. Existential anxiety is the normal anxiety that comes with the givens of existence: death, freedom, choice and the possibility of meaninglessness. Its companion is existential guilt, the guilt we incur not by breaking rules but by failing to live up to our own possibilities. The existential therapist does not try to abolish normal anxiety; he helps the client find the courage to live with it.

May also wrote about the daimonic, any natural function (sex, anger, the craving for power) that can either drive creativity or, if denied, take over the whole person destructively. This concept was at the heart of his 1982 open letter to Rogers, mentioned in Section 6.1: a therapy that sees only the actualising tendency, May argued, cannot account for human evil. The American blend of European existentialism with humanistic warmth, associated with May and with James Bugental (1915 to 2008), author of The Search for Authenticity (1965), is called existential-humanistic therapy. Bugental emphasised the therapist's presence, full attention to the client's subjective, moment-to-moment experience, and the client's search for authenticity.

Irvin Yalom's four ultimate concerns

The clearest map of the existential territory was drawn by Irvin Yalom (born 1931), an American psychiatrist and long-time professor at Stanford University. Yalom is famous in two fields. In group therapy his The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy (first published in 1970) set out the therapeutic factors, eleven in the form you will study in Chapter 7. In individual therapy his Existential Psychotherapy (1980) became the standard textbook, and his books of case stories, beginning with Love's Executioner (1989), made existential therapy readable for the general public.

Yalom argued that four ultimate concerns lie beneath much human distress. Each is an existential conflict between something we wish for and a hard fact of existence:

  • Death. We wish to go on being, and we know that we will die. Death anxiety is the most basic anxiety, and much psychopathology can be understood as a clumsy defence against it.
  • Freedom. In the existential sense, freedom means the absence of any external structure: we are the authors of our own lives, choices and meanings, with no ground beneath us. Freedom brings responsibility, and with it the dread of having to choose and the temptation to avoid choosing.
  • Isolation. However close we become to others, an unbridgeable gap remains: we enter and leave existence alone, and no one can live our life for us.
  • Meaninglessness. We are meaning-seeking creatures thrown into a universe that has no built-in meaning. If we must die, construct our own world and remain ultimately alone, what meaning does life have?

A useful mnemonic is "Death Frees Isolated Minds": death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness. Table 6.5 shows how Yalom tied each concern to characteristic defences and to the work of therapy.

Specialness is the deep, irrational belief that the ordinary laws of mortality do not apply to me, which can show itself in driven achievement, reckless heroics or narcissism. Yalom also distinguished three kinds of isolation: interpersonal isolation (distance from other people, the ordinary loneliness of the friendless), intrapersonal isolation (being cut off from parts of oneself, through defences) and existential isolation (the unbridgeable gulf between oneself and any other being, which no relationship can abolish).

Yalom's convictions were shaped by years of work with groups of people dying of cancer. Again and again he saw that confronting death, far from crushing people, often woke them up: they dropped trivial concerns, said what they had never said, and lived their remaining time more fully. He called this an "awakening experience" and summed it up in a much-quoted line: "Although the physicality of death destroys us, the idea of death saves us." On meaninglessness, Yalom agreed with Frankl (Section 6.5) that meaning cannot be pursued head-on; it ensues from engagement, wholehearted involvement in love, work, creation and the welfare of others.

Exam trap

Yalom's four ultimate concerns are death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. The distractors are invented sets ("past, present, future and eternity"; "love, work, play and transcendence"; "birth, life, death and rebirth"). Do not confuse the four ultimate concerns (Existential Psychotherapy, 1980) with Yalom's eleven therapeutic factors of group therapy (from his group therapy textbook, first published in 1970), such as instillation of hope and universality (Chapter 7); "existential factors" is one of those eleven, which is a common source of mix-ups.

Existential therapy in practice, and its evaluation

What does an existential therapist actually do? There is no manual, and existential therapists distrust technique, which they fear turns the client into an object (an "It" in Buber's language) to be fixed. The work is carried by the relationship, an encounter between two people, and by a steady focus on the client's freedom, choices and responsibility. Yalom's own formula was that "it's the relationship that heals". In practice this means several things. The therapist attends to the here and now of the relationship itself, asking, for example, how the client is relating to the therapist right now, because the way he avoids or engages here mirrors the way he lives. The therapist uses the phenomenological method, which the British existential therapist Ernesto Spinelli summarised as three rules: bracket your assumptions (the epoché), describe rather than explain, and treat every element of the client's account as potentially equally important rather than deciding in advance what matters. The therapist notices, and gently challenges, the ways in which the client disowns responsibility ("My wife makes me angry"; "I can't help it; that is my nature"). And the therapist does not shrink from the big themes: death, loss, guilt, aloneness and purpose are welcome topics, not distractions from the "real" work.

Britain developed its own school. Ronald David Laing (1927 to 1989), a Scottish psychiatrist, used existential phenomenology to make sense of schizophrenia in The Divided Self (1960), describing the "ontological insecurity" of a person who cannot take his own existence and identity for granted; his later experiment at Kingsley Hall in London (1965) made him a hero of the anti-psychiatry movement. Emmy van Deurzen founded the Society for Existential Analysis in London in 1988 and built a systematic existential approach around the four dimensions of existence.

How well does it work? Existential therapy has generated far less controlled research than CBT, partly by choice. The best evidence concerns the meaning-centred therapies descended from Frankl: in a 2015 meta-analysis, Joël Vos, Mick Cooper and Meghan Craig found that structured, meaning-centred existential therapies improved clients' sense of meaning in life considerably and reduced psychopathology moderately, with the clearest results among people with serious physical illness such as cancer. The criticisms are familiar. The approach is vague and hard to teach; it suits reflective, verbal, well-educated clients better than others; and its stress on lonely individual choice reflects European philosophy more than collectivist cultures. Its defenders reply that its themes run through every therapy: you will find them in Yalom's group factors, in the values work of acceptance and commitment therapy (Chapter 5) and in palliative care.

Clinical link

Existential themes come to the clinic uninvited: a young man after a heart attack, a woman whose husband has died, a student who has failed the entrance examination for the third time and asks what the point of anything is. A clinician need not be an existential therapist to listen for them. Questions such as "What has this illness made you think about?" or "What matters most to you now?" open the door, and the existential stance (do not rush to reassure; stay with the client in the hard question) is especially valuable in oncology, palliative care, bereavement and suicide prevention (Chapter 9).

Exam trap

Keep the founders straight. Binswanger and Boss: Daseinsanalysis. Rollo May: existential anxiety, The Meaning of Anxiety (1950), Existence (1958), the daimonic. Yalom: the four ultimate concerns. Bugental: existential-humanistic therapy and presence. Laing: ontological insecurity. Frankl: logotherapy (next section). And keep the families apart: Maslow and Rogers are humanistic; the existentialists see anxiety as a normal and inescapable part of existence, whereas for Rogers anxiety signals incongruence.

6.5 Viktor Frankl and LogotherapyHigh yield · 7 Qs

In October 1944 a train carrying some fifteen hundred prisoners from the Theresienstadt ghetto pulled into the station at Auschwitz. The prisoners were made to file past an SS officer who looked each one over and, with a lazy movement of his forefinger, pointed to the right or to the left. Nobody knew what the gesture meant. One of the prisoners, a thirty-nine-year-old Viennese psychiatrist, straightened his back under a heavy rucksack so that he would look fit for work, and the officer, after a moment's hesitation, turned him by the shoulders to the right. That evening he learned the meaning of the finger game. For about nine out of every ten people on his transport, the left had meant the gas chambers within hours. Within a day he had lost his clothes, his hair, his name (he was now prisoner number 119104) and the possession he valued most, the manuscript of his first book, which he had hidden in his coat.

The prisoner was Viktor Frankl, and the question that the following months forced upon him is the question of this section: when everything has been taken from a person, what is left, and what keeps that person alive? His answer became the best known and the most structured of the existential therapies introduced in Section 6.4, with its own vocabulary of the will to meaning, the existential vacuum, noogenic neurosis, paradoxical intention and dereflection.

From Vienna to the camps

Viktor Emil Frankl (1905 to 1997) was born in Vienna on 26 March 1905, the second of three children of a Jewish civil servant. He was a precocious schoolboy with a passion for psychology. While still at school he began a correspondence with Sigmund Freud, and Freud passed one of the young man's short papers to the International Journal of Psychoanalysis, which published it in 1924. Frankl then moved into the circle of Alfred Adler, the founder of individual psychology (Section 3.8), but he did not stay there long either. His insistence that people need meaning, and not only a sense of belonging and mastery, put him at odds with Adlerian orthodoxy, and in 1927 he was expelled from Adler's society. By then he was already using the word logotherapy in lectures for the approach he was developing.

As a medical student Frankl organised free counselling centres for young people at the time of year when school reports were issued and student suicides peaked. He qualified in medicine at the University of Vienna in 1930, and from 1933 to 1937 he was in charge of the pavilion for suicidal women at the psychiatric hospital Am Steinhof, where he saw thousands of them. It is not hard to see where his lifelong preoccupation with meaning came from.

After Germany annexed Austria in 1938, Jewish doctors were allowed to treat only Jewish patients. From 1940 Frankl headed the neurology department of the Rothschild Hospital, the only hospital in Vienna that still admitted Jews, and there he risked his life by writing false diagnoses so that mentally ill patients would not be handed over to the Nazi "euthanasia" programme, which was killing psychiatric patients. When an American visa finally came through, he let it lapse rather than leave his elderly parents to face deportation alone. In December 1941 he married Tilly Grosser, a nurse at the hospital.

In September 1942 Frankl, his wife and his parents were deported to Theresienstadt, where his father died. In October 1944 he and Tilly were sent on to Auschwitz. His mother was murdered in the gas chambers there, his brother also perished in Auschwitz, and Tilly died in Bergen-Belsen. Frankl himself spent only a few days at Auschwitz before being moved to Kaufering and then Türkheim, subcamps of Dachau in Bavaria, where he worked as a labourer and later volunteered as a doctor among prisoners dying of typhus. He was liberated by American troops in April 1945, himself recovering from typhus, after nearly three years in four camps. He learned only after his return to Vienna that his wife, his mother and his brother were dead.

Exam trap

Logotherapy was founded by Viktor Frankl, not by Irvin Yalom (the four ultimate concerns), Rollo May (existential anxiety) or Karen Horney (a neo-Freudian, and one of Perls's analysts). Question papers often spell him "Victor"; it is the same man. Because Frankl was for a time a member of Adler's circle, his name turns up as a distractor in questions about Adler: the inferiority complex and the striving for superiority belong to Alfred Adler, not to Frankl, Jung or Erikson.

What the camps taught

Back in Vienna in 1945, Frankl dictated in nine days a short book about what he had lived through. It appeared in German in 1946 as Ein Psycholog erlebt das Konzentrationslager ("A psychologist experiences the concentration camp"). The English translation came out in 1959 as From Death-Camp to Existentialism, and from 1962, in a revised and enlarged edition whose second part summarises logotherapy (headed "Basic Concepts of Logotherapy" in the 1960s editions and "Logotherapy in a Nutshell" in later ones), it carried the title by which millions know it: Man's Search for Meaning. It has sold more than ten million copies. In 1946 he also published the book whose manuscript had been taken from him at Auschwitz and which he had reconstructed in shorthand on scraps of paper while ill with typhus: Ärztliche Seelsorge (in English, The Doctor and the Soul, 1955), the first systematic account of logotherapy. From 1946 to 1971 he headed the neurology department of the Vienna Polyclinic; he remarried in 1947, lectured all over the world and died in Vienna on 2 September 1997.

Man's Search for Meaning is not a history of the camps but a psychologist's account of how ordinary prisoners lived inside them. Frankl described three phases in a prisoner's psychological reaction: shock on admission, mixed with the "delusion of reprieve" (the condemned man's belief that he will be spared at the last minute); then apathy, an emotional death that blunted feeling and so protected the prisoner from the daily horror; and, after liberation, depersonalisation (the world felt unreal and joy would not come), with bitterness and disillusionment for many.

Within that general picture Frankl noticed something that the theories he had been taught did not predict. Hunger, cold and brutality stripped prisoners down to their most basic needs, just as a drive theory would expect, and yet people did not all become alike. Some behaved like beasts; others walked through the huts comforting others and gave away their last piece of bread. What distinguished those who held on? Frankl's answer was that they had something to live for. When two prisoners told him that they expected nothing more from life, he turned the question round and asked what life might still expect from them. One had a child waiting for him in another country; the other, a scientist, had a series of books still to finish. Each realised that he was irreplaceable to someone or something, and found a reason to go on. Frankl liked to quote Nietzsche (Table 6.4): "He who has a why to live can bear with almost any how."

The reverse was also true. The chief doctor of one camp told Frankl that in the week between Christmas 1944 and New Year 1945 the death rate rose beyond anything seen before, although work, food and weather had not changed. The doctor's explanation, which Frankl shared, was psychological: many prisoners had lived on the naive hope that they would be home by Christmas, and when the hope collapsed, their resistance collapsed with it. Even humour served survival: Frankl and a friend on his work gang promised each other to invent one funny story every day about life after liberation.

From these observations came the most quoted sentence of the book: "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms, to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way." The camp could take away almost every outer freedom, but it could not decide what stand a prisoner would take toward what was happening to him. On a freezing march before dawn, the image of his wife's face (he did not know that she was already dead) made the suffering bearable, and he grasped, he wrote, that "love is the ultimate and the highest goal to which man can aspire". These observations, not abstract philosophy, are what logotherapy was built from.

Exam trap

Dates for Man's Search for Meaning differ between sources because the book has three birthdays: the German original (1946), the first English translation (1959, as From Death-Camp to Existentialism) and the present English title (from the revised edition of 1962; American textbooks often cite the 1963 paperback or the 1984 and 1992 editions). For the book's first publication, 1946 is the answer. The line "He who has a why to live can bear with almost any how" is Frankl quoting Nietzsche, not a sentence Frankl coined. The "last of the human freedoms" is the freedom to choose one's attitude, which is the root of Frankl's attitudinal values.

The will to meaning and the paths to meaning

The word logotherapy comes from the Greek logos, which Frankl translated as "meaning" (it can also mean word or reason), so logotherapy is therapy through meaning: an existential therapy that helps a person find and fulfil the meaning of his or her life. In German Frankl also called it Existenzanalyse, existential analysis. It rests on three linked assumptions. Freedom of will: within the limits of biology and circumstance, a person is free to take a stand toward his conditions. The will to meaning: the search for meaning is the deepest human motive. Meaning in life: life has meaning under all circumstances, even the most miserable.

The middle assumption is the heart of the theory. The will to meaning is Frankl's name for the basic striving of human beings to find and fulfil meaning and purpose in their lives. Frankl liked to place it in the history of his own city. Vienna had already given psychotherapy two great schools. Freud's psychoanalysis, in Frankl's summary, pictured people as driven by a will to pleasure (the pleasure principle), and Adler's individual psychology pictured them as driven by a will to power (the striving for superiority that compensates for feelings of inferiority). Logotherapy, which became known as the Third Viennese School of Psychotherapy, put the will to meaning first. For Frankl the other two strivings were real but secondary. Pleasure is a by-product of fulfilling a meaning, and power is a means to an end; people chase pleasure and power most frantically when their will to meaning has been frustrated. A drive pushes a person from behind, demanding discharge; a meaning pulls a person from ahead, toward something to be done or someone to be loved, and leaves him free to answer or not. Frankl rejected the reductionist habit of saying that ideals are "nothing but" sublimated drives or defences, and he called logotherapy a height psychology, a complement to the depth psychology of Freud and Adler: depth psychology looks down into instincts and conflicts, height psychology looks up toward values and meanings.

Behind this lies Frankl's picture of the person. A human being, he argued, exists in three dimensions at once: the somatic (the body), the psychic (the mind in the ordinary psychological sense: feelings, drives, habits and memories) and the noetic dimension (from the Greek noos, mind or spirit), the specifically human dimension of meaning, values, conscience, freedom and responsibility. Frankl used the German word geistig, "spiritual", for this dimension, but he insisted that it is not a religious term; an atheist has a noetic dimension as surely as a believer. Animals share our bodies and much of our psychology, but only human beings can ask whether what they are doing is worth doing. The noetic dimension shows itself in two capacities: self-detachment, the capacity to step back from oneself, to observe one's own moods and symptoms from outside and even to laugh at them, as Frankl and his friend did with their daily story; and self-transcendence, the capacity to reach beyond oneself toward a meaning to fulfil or a person to love. Each of Frankl's two best-known techniques recruits one of them.

Self-transcendence is also why Frankl disagreed with the humanistic psychologists of Section 6.1 about self-actualisation. Maslow and Rogers treated self-actualisation as a goal. Frankl argued that it cannot be aimed at directly. "Being human," he wrote, "always points, and is directed, to something, or someone, other than oneself." He compared a person to the eye, which sees the world only so long as it does not see itself; when it begins to see something of itself, such as the cloud of a cataract, something is wrong. In the same way, self-actualisation and happiness arrive only as side-effects of self-transcendence. "Happiness must ensue," Frankl wrote; it cannot be pursued, because the more one aims at it the more one misses it. Hold on to that idea, because it is the key to dereflection.

Meaning, for Frankl, is discovered rather than invented. Sartre (Section 6.4) taught that we must create our own values in an empty universe; Frankl held that a meaning is already present in each situation, waiting to be found, unique to the particular person and the particular moment (he spoke of the "meaning of the moment"), and that conscience is the organ that detects it. He reversed the usual question: it is not we who should ask what the meaning of life is, but life that asks us, and we answer by being responsible. Freedom without responsibility, he warned, degenerates into arbitrariness; he once proposed that the Statue of Liberty on America's east coast be balanced by a Statue of Responsibility on the west.

Where can meaning be found? Frankl named three main avenues, often drawn as a triangle (Table 6.7). The first is creative values, realised through what we give to the world: creating a work or doing a deed. The second is experiential values, realised through what we receive from the world: goodness, truth or beauty in nature, art or culture, and above all the encounter with another human being in his or her uniqueness, which is to say, love. The third is attitudinal values, realised through the stand we take toward a fate we cannot change, unavoidable suffering. Frankl regarded the third as the highest, because it remains open when the other two are closed: a paralysed patient who can no longer work can still choose how to bear his condition. Notice the word unavoidable. Frankl was emphatic that to suffer unnecessarily, when the suffering could be removed, is not heroic but masochistic; suffering that can be cured should be cured.

His stance toward it, which he called "tragic optimism", was that a person can say yes to life in spite of all three: by turning suffering into an achievement, guilt into an opportunity to change for the better, and the transitoriness of life into an incentive to act responsibly now.

Exam trap

The will to meaning is the central concept of logotherapy. The tempting wrong options each belong to someone else. Rational emotive behaviour therapy is Albert Ellis's therapy of irrational beliefs (Section 5.2). Existential-humanistic therapy (Rollo May and James Bugental, Section 6.4) shares Frankl's concerns but is built on presence and authenticity, not on the will to meaning. Acceptance and commitment therapy (Steven Hayes, Section 5.9) does work with values, but it grows out of behaviour analysis and relational frame theory. For the three Viennese schools remember the triad pleasure (Freud), power (Adler), meaning (Frankl). And Frankl, unlike Maslow, held that self-actualisation cannot be pursued directly: it is a by-product of self-transcendence.

Existential frustration, the existential vacuum and noogenic neurosis

What happens when the will to meaning is blocked? Frankl called the experience existential frustration, the frustration of the will to meaning. It is not in itself an illness. A woman who wonders whether her life has been worthwhile, or a young man who despairs of finding a purpose after failing to get into the course he wanted, is showing a distinctly human concern, and Frankl warned doctors against burying such a concern under a diagnosis and a tranquilliser. "A man's concern, even his despair, over the worthwhileness of life is an existential distress but by no means a mental disease," he wrote.

When existential frustration hardens into a lasting state, the result is the existential vacuum: a pervasive feeling of inner emptiness and meaninglessness, of having nothing worth living for. Frankl thought it the characteristic complaint of the twentieth century, and he offered a historical explanation. Unlike an animal, a human being is not told by instinct what he must do, and, unlike his ancestors, he is no longer told by tradition what he ought to do. Often he does not even know what he wishes to do. So he does what other people do, which Frankl called conformism, or he does what other people wish him to do, which Frankl called totalitarianism. The vacuum shows itself mainly as boredom. Frankl described a Sunday neurosis, the depression that afflicts people who become aware of the emptiness of their lives when the rush of the busy week is over, and he saw the same pattern in the crisis of retirement and in the despondency of the young unemployed, many of whom, he noticed in the 1930s, suffered less from poverty than from the feeling of being useless.

Consider a worked example. Suresh, a bank manager in Mysuru, retires at sixty after thirty-five years of rising at six. Within six months he is sleeping late, snapping at his wife and spending his evenings in front of the television. "There is nothing wrong with me," he tells the psychologist his daughter has persuaded him to see. "I just do not see the point of getting up." Nothing in his body or his childhood has changed; what has gone is the structure of tasks and people that gave his days their meaning. A logotherapist would not search his past for a conflict. She would ask what in his life still needs him, and might discover that the neighbourhood's schoolchildren have nobody to help them with mathematics, a task that draws on all of Suresh's old skills.

The vacuum does not stay empty for long. Frankl argued that it is often filled with substitutes: the will to power in its crudest form, the will to money; or the will to pleasure, in sexual compensation and the pursuit of thrills. In his later writing he named a mass neurotic triad of depression, aggression and addiction, three social problems that he believed were fed in large part by the existential vacuum. Addiction counsellors will recognise the young man who drinks to fill evenings that have nothing else in them.

Only some people in an existential vacuum become ill. When existential frustration does produce neurotic symptoms (anxiety, depression, obsessions, insomnia), Frankl spoke of a noogenic neurosis: a neurosis that originates in the noetic dimension, out of existential problems such as the frustration of the will to meaning or a conflict between values, rather than out of conflicts between drives and instincts. The word is built from noos (mind or spirit) and -genic (originating in). Frankl contrasted it with two other origins of neurotic symptoms, shown in Figure 6.3. A psychogenic neurosis, the neurosis of classical psychotherapy, arises in the psychic dimension, from psychological conflicts, traumas and faulty learning. A somatogenic disturbance arises in the body; an overactive thyroid gland, for example, can produce anxiety. The symptoms may look alike. What differs is their source, and therefore what will help. For noogenic neurosis Frankl regarded logotherapy as the specific treatment, because the problem itself is a problem of meaning. For psychogenic neuroses he offered logotherapy as a non-specific treatment: a set of techniques, paradoxical intention and dereflection, that can relieve symptoms whatever their origin, alongside other methods. Somatogenic conditions need medical treatment first.

Frankl also took issue with the idea, popular in the psychology of his day, that a healthy organism seeks a state of equilibrium free of tension. "What man actually needs," he wrote, "is not a tensionless state but rather the striving and struggling for a worthwhile goal." He called the healthy tension between what one is and what one ought to become noodynamics, and he compared it to an architect's trick: to strengthen a decrepit arch, you increase the load on it, because the load binds its parts more firmly together.

Key study: putting the existential vacuum to the test (Crumbaugh and Maholick, 1964)

Question. Is Frankl's existential vacuum a real, measurable condition, and is lack of meaning related to psychological disturbance? Method. The American psychologists James Crumbaugh and Leonard Maholick built the Purpose in Life Test (PIL) directly from Frankl's writings, and reported it in a paper whose title announced its aim: "An experimental study in existentialism: the psychometric approach to Frankl's concept of noogenic neurosis". Its scored part has 20 items, each rated on a seven-point scale between two anchors (for example, "In life I have: no goals or aims at all" at one end and "very clear goals and aims" at the other), giving totals from 20 to 140. They gave it to groups of non-patients and psychiatric patients. Results. On average the non-patient groups reported more purpose than the patients. Conclusion. A sense of meaning can be measured, and its absence goes with distress, as Frankl claimed. Criticism. PIL scores correlate strongly with measures of depression and general well-being, so the test may partly measure low mood rather than a separate noogenic condition; it is a self-report; and correlational data cannot show whether meaninglessness causes disturbance or disturbance drains life of meaning. (The PIL itself belongs to the Assessment book.)

Exam trap

Noogenic neurosis (or noogenic anxiety) is Frankl's concept, so it is described under the existential school. It is not humanistic in the narrow sense of Rogers and Maslow, not Gestalt, and not psychodynamic (the psychodynamic neurosis is what Frankl called psychogenic). Do not misread it as neurogenic (arising in the nervous system) or iatrogenic (caused by treatment). The existential vacuum is Frankl's term; Yalom's nearest equivalent is meaninglessness, one of his four ultimate concerns. And if asked what logotherapy is "best described as", the answer is an existential therapy emphasising the search for meaning, not a behaviour therapy using token reinforcement (Section 4.5), a psychodynamic therapy of unconscious conflict (Chapter 3) or a cognitive therapy of irrational beliefs (Ellis, Section 5.2).

Paradoxical intention and dereflection

For all its philosophy, logotherapy became famous among clinicians for two short, practical techniques. Frankl developed both to deal with a mechanism he saw again and again in anxious patients. A person has an unpleasant experience: he blushes while giving a talk, sweats when shaking hands, or lies awake the night before an examination. The next time a similar situation approaches, he fears that it will happen again. Frankl called this fear anticipatory anxiety, and he pointed out its cruel property: it tends to produce exactly what it fears. The fear of blushing makes the face burn; the fear of sleeplessness keeps the mind alert. Each recurrence confirms the fear and strengthens it, and a vicious circle is set up (Figure 6.4). Fear, as Frankl put it, "brings to pass what one is afraid of".

Two kinds of misdirected effort keep the circle turning. The first, hyper-intention, is trying too hard to bring about something that can come only as a by-product: the insomniac who tries to fall asleep, the man who tries to prove his sexual potency, the candidate who tries to be relaxed at an interview. The harder they try, the more surely they fail, because sleep, sexual pleasure and ease are like happiness; they ensue, and cannot be commanded. The second, hyper-reflection, is excessive attention to oneself: anxious self-observation, watching one's own body, feelings or performance for the first sign of trouble. Patients' own responses make matters worse, Frankl added: the phobic patient runs from the fear and avoids whatever triggers it (flight from fear), the obsessive-compulsive patient fights against his obsessions, which only gives them more force, and the patient with a sexual neurosis fights for pleasure, which drives it away.

Frankl's first technique, paradoxical intention, invites the patient to intend, or to wish for, the very thing he fears, deliberately, in an exaggerated form and with humour, so that the anticipatory anxiety is replaced by a paradoxical wish and the vicious circle is broken. Frankl had been using the method in Vienna long before the war, and his best-known English account of it appeared in the American Journal of Psychotherapy in 1960. His most quoted case is a young doctor for whom the mere expectation of sweating was enough to make him sweat. Frankl advised him, the next time he feared an outbreak, to resolve deliberately to show people how much he could sweat. A week later the doctor reported that whenever he met someone who set off his fear, he had said to himself, "I only sweated out a quart before, but now I'm going to pour at least ten quarts!" After suffering from the phobia for four years, he was free of it within a week of a single session. A colleague in the speech and throat department of the Vienna Polyclinic told Frankl of a lifelong stammerer whose one fluent moment had come when he tried to stammer: at twelve, caught stealing a ride on a tram, he had tried to stammer to win the conductor's pity, and could not.

Frankl's student Elisabeth Lukas described a woman with a mirror compulsion who ran to a mirror up to twenty times a day to check that her hair was perfectly in place. The patient resisted every paradoxical suggestion until Lukas offered to join her in a game of "hair rumpling". The two women messed up their hair as wildly as they could and ran hand in hand around the block, trying to show every passer-by how dishevelled they were. Nobody paid them the slightest attention. The patient learned to tell herself, "Let my hair stand on end; let it be a mess!", and after eight weeks the compulsion had gone. The therapist took the symptom seriously enough to share the joke, and the humour let the patient stand back from the symptom instead of being swallowed by it.

Why should such an odd instruction work? Frankl's explanation was that paradoxical intention mobilises self-detachment, and especially humour. A person cannot be terrified of blushing and, at the same moment, cheerfully trying to blush as red as a tomato. The paradoxical wish also turns the mechanism of hyper-intention against itself: if trying hard for something makes it fail, then trying hard for the symptom makes the symptom fail. A behaviour therapist would add that the patient stops avoiding the feared situation and so receives exposure to it (Section 4.3). Frankl used paradoxical intention mainly for anticipatory anxiety, phobias, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, blushing, sweating, stammering and sleep-onset insomnia (the insomniac is told to try to stay awake for as long as possible). It needs a patient who trusts the therapist and can find some humour in his plight. Logotherapists warn that it must never be used with severely depressed or suicidal patients, for whom an instruction to wish for the worst could be dangerous. Section 4.10 shows how behaviour therapists later put paradoxical intention to the test in controlled trials.

The companion technique, dereflection, is aimed at hyper-reflection and hyper-intention. The patient is helped to stop watching himself and to stop straining for the effect, and to turn his attention outward, to another person or to a meaningful task, so that the disturbed function can recover by itself. Paradoxical intention says "try to make it happen"; dereflection says "stop attending to it, and attend to something worth attending to". Frankl's best-known case was a young woman who came to him because she could not reach orgasm. She had been sexually abused by her father in childhood, but Frankl traced her difficulty less to the abuse itself than to what she had made of it: having read popular psychoanalytic books, she lived in fearful expectation that the experience would one day take its toll, and in bed with her partner she watched herself anxiously for the damage to show. She was trying hard for orgasm (hyper-intention) and observing herself throughout (hyper-reflection). Frankl told her that he could not begin treatment for a couple of months, and asked her, in the meantime, not to concern herself with whether she was capable of orgasm but to give her attention to her partner. She came back within days rather than months, having experienced orgasm for the first time.

The same principle helps a man with erectile difficulties who monitors every moment of lovemaking, and Frankl sometimes removed the demand altogether by having the couple agree that intercourse was, for the time being, off the agenda. William Masters and Virginia Johnson arrived at a similar idea in their sex therapy of 1970. They called anxious self-monitoring during sex "spectatoring" and treated it with sensate focus, a graded programme of non-demand touching exercises during which intercourse is at first banned (Section 7.9). Dereflection is not only a sex technique, however. The man preoccupied with his palpitations, the student who lies awake listening to her own breathing, the chronically ill patient whose whole day revolves around his symptoms: each is helped by being turned away from the self and toward something worth attending to, which is self-transcendence put to clinical use. Socratic dialogue, attitude modulation and evaluation

Paradoxical intention and dereflection deal with symptoms. The heart of logotherapy, for noogenic problems, is a conversation about meaning, and its main tool is the Socratic dialogue: a series of questions, in the manner of the Greek philosopher Socrates, that help the client to discover for himself meanings and values that he already half knows but has lost sight of. The logotherapist does not hand out meanings. Frankl was firm that meaning cannot be given, only found, and he said that the therapist's role is that of an eye specialist rather than a painter: a painter shows us the world as he sees it, whereas an eye specialist helps us to see the world as it really is.

Frankl's own most famous example is short enough to give whole. An elderly general practitioner came to him, severely depressed two years after the death of the wife he had loved above everything. Frankl offered no comfort. Instead he asked what would have happened if the doctor had died first and his wife had had to survive him. "Oh," said the doctor, "for her this would have been terrible; how she would have suffered!" Frankl replied that she had been spared this suffering, and that it was he who had spared her, at the price of surviving and mourning her himself. The man said nothing, shook Frankl's hand and quietly left the office. His fate had not changed, but its meaning had: his grief had become the price of a sacrifice made for her.

The case also illustrates attitude modulation, in which the therapist helps a client replace an attitude toward an unchangeable situation that makes it unbearable with one that makes it bearable and even meaningful; it is attitudinal values put into practice. Logotherapists also invite clients to look at their lives from the perspective of the end, and Frankl's maxim for this was a thought experiment: "Live as if you were living already for the second time and as if you had acted the first time as wrongly as you are about to act now." Compare the Socratic questioning of Beck's cognitive therapy (Section 5.4). Beck's questions test whether a belief is true; the logotherapist's questions look for what is still worth living for.

In practice logotherapy is usually brief and active, often a matter of weeks or a few months, and it looks forward rather than back. Frankl called it less retrospective and less introspective than psychoanalysis, and enjoyed telling how an American doctor once asked him to define the difference in a single sentence. Frankl asked the doctor first to define psychoanalysis. "In psychoanalysis," came the reply, "the patient must lie down on a couch and tell you things which sometimes are very disagreeable to tell." To which Frankl answered that in logotherapy the patient may remain sitting erect, but he must hear things which sometimes are very disagreeable to hear.

How should logotherapy be judged? Its reach is enormous, and the meaning-centred therapies descended from Frankl have the best research record of all the existential approaches, as the 2015 meta-analysis by Vos, Cooper and Craig showed (Section 6.4); the meaning-centred psychotherapy that William Breitbart developed for people with advanced cancer is described in Chapter 9. Paradoxical intention has been tested more rigorously than the rest of logotherapy, mostly by behaviour therapists, with encouraging results for sleep-onset insomnia and some anxiety problems (Section 4.10). The criticisms are just as clear. Some existential therapists, Yalom among them, felt that Frankl leaned on technique at the expense of the relationship and of the client's own slow search for meaning, and critics have found his manner authoritarian and moralising: a doctor telling patients what is worth living for, despite his own warnings against doing so. The spiritual flavour of his language (in The Unconscious God he argued for a "spiritual unconscious" in every person) attracted clergy but made secular readers uneasy, even though Frankl insisted that the noetic dimension is not religious. And the central theory is hard to test. The will to meaning and noogenic neurosis are plausible ideas, but it is difficult to show that a noogenic neurosis is a different condition from an ordinary depression, as the criticism of the Purpose in Life Test showed.

Clinical link

In a palliative care unit in Bengaluru, a retired schoolteacher with advanced cancer tells the psychologist that he is "just waiting". A meaning-centred conversation might ask what he would like his grandchildren to remember about how he faced this time; he decides to record the stories of his village childhood for them, a creative value, and to stop hiding his illness from his wife, an attitudinal one. Three cautions apply. A client who is acutely suicidal needs a risk assessment and a safety plan first (Chapter 9), not a discussion of meaning. The clinician must not supply a meaning of her own. And many Indian clients already draw meaning from dharma, karma, seva or devotion; the logotherapeutic stance is to work within the client's own framework, helping him find what it asks of him now, rather than replacing it.

6.6 Reality Therapy and Choice TheoryMedium yield · 2 Qs

Frankl taught that even in a concentration camp a person keeps the freedom to choose his attitude. In the same years an American psychiatrist was pushing the idea of choice in a more down-to-earth direction. In 1956 he became consulting psychiatrist at the Ventura School for Girls, a California state institution for delinquent adolescent girls. Many of the girls had already been through probation officers, counsellors and psychiatrists, and many could explain fluently why they behaved as they did: the broken home, the drunken father, the rough neighbourhood. The psychiatrist was not much interested in the explanations. His approach was to become warmly and personally involved with the girls, and then to ask them, again and again, what they were doing now, whether it was getting them what they wanted, and what they were going to do instead. He accepted no excuses, he did not punish, and he did not give up. Staff who had at first resisted his ideas about discipline came to find them helpful, and he reported that fewer girls returned to the institution after release. The psychiatrist was William Glasser, and in 1965 he described his approach in a book whose title gave it its name: Reality Therapy.

William Glasser and the making of reality therapy

William Glasser (1925 to 2013) was born in Cleveland, Ohio, on 11 May 1925. He took a degree in chemical engineering at the age of nineteen, then a master's degree in clinical psychology, and then a medical degree from Case Western Reserve University, which he completed at twenty-eight. He trained in psychiatry at the Veterans Administration Center in Los Angeles and the University of California at Los Angeles, and became a certified psychiatrist in 1961. His residency was a training in psychoanalysis, and it left him dissatisfied. Psychoanalysis, as he saw it, taught patients to look into the past for someone to blame, and it seemed to make little difference to how they actually behaved. In the third year of his residency he confided these doubts to his clinical supervisor, the psychiatrist G. L. Harrington. Harrington, who had come to question psychoanalytic practice himself, was sympathetic, and he served as Glasser's mentor for the next seven years; Glasser acknowledged him as a major influence on reality therapy.

Glasser's first book, Mental Health or Mental Illness? (1961), already posed in its title a question that would run through his whole career. He coined the term reality therapy in 1962 and set out the approach in Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry (1965). In 1967 he founded the Institute for Reality Therapy in Los Angeles (later renamed the William Glasser Institute), which trains and certifies practitioners. He carried his ideas into education with Schools Without Failure (1969), into social criticism with The Identity Society (1972) and into self-help with Positive Addiction (1976). His first wife, Naomi Glasser, edited the first casebook of the approach and gave it a title that captures it in three words: What Are You Doing? (1980). Glasser died in Los Angeles in 2013.

Glasser's reality therapy can be defined as a brief, active, present-focused counselling approach in which a warm, involved therapist helps clients to evaluate their current behaviour and to choose more responsible and effective ways of meeting their basic needs. Responsibility was the key word from the start. Glasser defined it in 1965 as "the ability to fulfil one's needs, and to do so in a way that does not deprive others of the ability to fulfil their needs", and his early approach is often summarised as the "three Rs": reality, responsibility, and right (the client judges for himself whether what he is doing is right). The word "reality" in the name does not mean that the therapist decides what is real and lectures the client about it. It means that the client is helped to face the real consequences of what he is doing, and to stop hiding from them behind excuses, symptoms or the past. In 1965 Glasser described only two basic needs: the need to love and be loved, and the need to feel worthwhile to oneself and to others. The fuller theory came later.

For fifteen years reality therapy was a practice with little formal theory behind it. Then in 1977 Glasser read Behavior: The Control of Perception (1973) by the American control-systems engineer William T. Powers. Powers argued that living organisms do not simply respond to stimuli; they act to control their perceptions, keeping what they perceive close to what they want, much as a thermostat switches the heating on and off to hold the perceived temperature at its setting. Glasser adapted the idea as control theory in Stations of the Mind (1981) and the more popular Control Theory (1985). People kept misreading "control" as controlling others, and his version had in any case moved a long way from Powers's, so Glasser renamed it choice theory and set it out in Choice Theory: A New Psychology of Personal Freedom (1998). Choice theory is Glasser's explanation of why people behave as they do; reality therapy is the method of counselling built on it. The method itself was systematised by Robert E. Wubbolding, for many years director of training of the William Glasser Institute, whose WDEP system you will meet below.

Choice theory: five basic needs and the quality world

Choice theory begins, as Rogers's and Frankl's theories do, with motivation. Glasser held that all human behaviour is driven by five basic needs, which are built into our genes: survival, love and belonging, power, freedom and fun (Table 6.9). Survival covers the physical needs for food, water, shelter, health, safety and sex. Love and belonging covers friendship, family, intimacy and cooperation; it can be met by people, and to a lesser extent even by pets. Power in Glasser's sense is not domination but achievement, competence, recognition and self-worth, the sense of being listened to and of counting for something, so that it is often written "power or achievement". Freedom covers independence and choice: how we live, whom we spend time with, what we say, read and believe. Fun covers enjoyment, play and laughter, and Glasser called it the genetic reward for learning, the reason children and adults alike enjoy whatever stretches them.

Two features of the needs are worth knowing. First, Glasser regarded love and belonging as the most important need in practice, because it is the one most often frustrated in people who seek help. In his view almost all long-lasting psychological problems are, at bottom, relationship problems, and an unhappy client nearly always lacks at least one satisfying relationship. Second, the needs are not arranged in a fixed hierarchy as Maslow's are. Their strength differs from person to person (one person has a strong need for freedom, another a strong need for power), and they often conflict. The need for power in a marriage can wreck the need for love; Glasser believed that power struggles, more than any shortage of love, destroy marriages. A need for freedom so strong that it keeps everyone at a distance leaves a person lonely.

Needs are general; what we actually want is specific. From birth onward, Glasser said, each of us builds a quality world, a personal album of "pictures" of the particular people, things, activities, ideas and beliefs that we have found satisfy our needs best: this friend, that career, a new cricket bat, the belief that hard work pays. The most important pictures are of people. The quality world is unique to each person, it need not be sensible (a man with a drinking problem keeps a picture of the evening drink as the best way to relax, and a girl with anorexia a picture of herself as fat), and it can change, slowly, as one picture is replaced by another that satisfies the same need. Alongside it each of us carries a perceived world, the world as we experience it through our senses and our habits of interpretation. At what Glasser called the comparing place, the brain constantly weighs what we perceive we are getting against the pictures in the quality world. When the two match, we feel pleasure; when they do not, a frustration signal arises, and we choose a behaviour to close the gap. This is Powers's thermostat in human form. It also explains why, in choice theory, "Face reality!" is a suspect demand: none of us has access to reality itself, only to our perceptions of it, so "Face reality" usually means "See it my way".

From this model Glasser drew a social message. If behaviour is generated from inside, by the gap between what we want and what we perceive, then nobody can make another person do anything. The only behaviour we can control is our own, and all we can give another person is information. Glasser called the opposite belief, that we can and should control others with rewards and punishments, external control psychology, and he blamed it for much of the unhappiness in families, schools and workplaces. In his later writing he contrasted seven "deadly habits" that destroy relationships (criticising, blaming, complaining, nagging, threatening, punishing, and bribing or rewarding to control) with seven "caring habits" that build them (supporting, encouraging, listening, accepting, trusting, respecting and negotiating differences). Anyone who has watched an anxious parent try to make a teenager study will recognise the first list.

Exam trap

Glasser's five basic needs are survival, love and belonging, power (achievement), freedom and fun. When a question asks which option is one of them, the distractors are borrowed from other theorists: self-actualisation is the top of Maslow's hierarchy (and close to Rogers's actualising tendency), the collective unconscious is Carl Jung's (Section 3.8 and the Personality book), and unconditional positive regard is one of Rogers's core conditions (Section 6.2). Watch also for Maslow's own levels, safety and esteem, slipped in as distractors: Glasser's survival need is the nearest he comes to Maslow's physiological and safety needs, and Glasser's needs are not arranged in a hierarchy. The 1965 version of reality therapy named only two needs (to love and be loved, and to feel worthwhile); the five came with control theory in the 1980s.

Total behaviour: why Glasser said "depressing"

Choice theory's most provocative claim concerns what we usually call symptoms. Glasser argued that all behaviour is total behaviour, made up of four components that always occur together: acting (what we do), thinking (voluntary and involuntary thoughts, including daydreams), feeling (pleasant or painful emotions) and physiology (the bodily processes that go with them, such as sweating, a racing heart or a headache). We have direct control only over acting and thinking; feeling and physiology follow them. Glasser's image for this was a car (Figure 6.5). The five basic needs are the engine; our wants, the pictures in the quality world, do the steering; acting and thinking are the front wheels, which we can turn; feeling and physiology are the back wheels, which go wherever the front wheels take them. We cannot make ourselves feel happy by willing it, any more than we can steer a car by its back wheels, but we can change what we are doing and thinking, and our feelings and physiology will follow.

If total behaviour is chosen, so, in part, is misery. Glasser therefore turned the adjectives of distress into verbs. A person is not "depressed" but "depressing"; not "angry" but "angering"; he does not have a headache, he is "headaching". The point was not to insult sufferers but to remind them that some part of their misery lies in what they are doing now, and can therefore change. Sadness may come unbidden after a loss, Glasser granted; but when it continues month after month, the person is choosing, without realising it, to go on depressing. Why would anyone choose pain? Glasser gave four reasons. Depressing keeps anger under control, and is far safer than angering, which can lead to violence and prison. Depressing is a way of asking for help ("Depressing is a way we ask for help without begging," he wrote). It excuses us from doing something more effective but more frightening, such as looking for a new job after being dismissed. And it gives us power over others, who must comfort us, look after us and do things for us. Glasser went further and regarded even hallucinations and delusions as creative, if desperate, behaviours. He rejected the idea of mental illness for everything except clear brain disease, and he was a fierce critic of psychiatric medication; one of his last books was titled Warning: Psychiatry Can Be Hazardous to Your Mental Health (2003).

Clinical link

The language of choice is powerful and double-edged. For a student who "cannot" start studying, or a man who "cannot help" shouting at his wife, the question "What are you choosing to do, and is it working?" can be liberating. For a woman in a severe depressive episode, a man hearing voices or a patient with bipolar disorder, being told that he or she is "choosing" the symptoms can deepen guilt and shame and delay effective treatment, including medication (the disorders and their treatment belong to the Disorders book). Most clinicians who draw on reality therapy keep its useful core, the focus on what the client can do today, and leave aside Glasser's rejection of diagnosis and drugs. In Indian families, too, a young client's quality world often holds his parents' pictures as well as his own, so plans usually have to be negotiated with the family rather than made by the individual alone.

Doing reality therapy: involvement and the WDEP system

Reality therapy is less a collection of techniques than a cycle of counselling: a friendly, involved relationship, inside which a few procedures are repeated until the client's behaviour changes. It begins with involvement. Glasser thought the distant, neutral analyst the worst possible model for his clients, who were usually short of satisfying relationships to begin with. The reality therapist is warm, personal, optimistic and honest. She uses "I" and "me", discloses something of herself when it helps, and is willing to talk about almost anything the client cares about, from cricket to cooking, because the relationship itself begins to meet the client's need for belonging. She is herself, not a screen for the client's transference, and she does not let the past or feelings become the main topic. Feelings are listened to, but the focus moves steadily toward what the client is doing.

Glasser's early writings set out the procedure as a series of steps, usually taught as eight:

  1. Make friends: become personally involved with the client.
  2. Focus on present behaviour: ask "What are you doing now?" rather than "Why?" or "How do you feel?"
  3. Ask the client to evaluate that behaviour: is it helping?
  4. Help the client make a plan to do better.
  5. Get a commitment to the plan, spoken or written.
  6. Accept no excuses when a plan is not carried out; make a new plan instead.
  7. Do not punish or criticise, but do not protect the client from the reasonable consequences of what he does.
  8. Never give up.

Wubbolding later reorganised these procedures into the WDEP system, the form in which reality therapy is now taught. Each letter stands for a cluster of questions and interventions. W is for wants, together with needs and perceptions: the counsellor explores what the client wants, what he "really" wants, what he wants from the people around him and how he sees his situation; behind the wants lie the needs. D is for direction and doing: where is the client's life heading, and what exactly is he doing now? The counsellor asks for specifics: what happened, who was there, what you did next, what you did yesterday from the moment you woke up. E is for evaluation, which Wubbolding regarded as the heart of the method. The client is asked to judge his own behaviour, and the questions are characteristic: "Is what you are doing helping you or hurting you?" "Is it getting you what you want?" "Are you breaking the rules?" "Are your wants realistic?" "Does it help you to look at it that way?" It is the client, not the counsellor, who makes the value judgement. P is for planning: once the client has decided that something must change, he and the counsellor make a plan. Wubbolding summed up a good plan in an acronym, usually given as SAMIC3 ("SAMIC cubed", because three of its letters are C): simple, attainable, measurable, immediate, controlled by the planner (it does not depend on someone else), committed to, and consistently (in some versions, continuously) carried out. A good plan is also positive, a matter of doing something rather than merely stopping something: "I will phone Priya on Saturday" beats "I will stop sitting alone".

A worked example shows the system in motion. Rohan, a nineteen-year-old commerce student in Mysuru, is brought by his parents because he is failing his examinations. He plays online games until three in the morning, misses his morning classes and has stopped speaking to his father except to shout. His father, for his part, criticises, threatens to stop his pocket money and compares him with a cousin who is studying engineering. A reality therapist would spend much of the first session simply getting to know Rohan, his games, his friends and what he is good at, and only then begin the WDEP cycle (Table 6.10).

At the next session the therapist would not ask why the plan had or had not been carried out, because, as Wubbolding put it, asking "Why?" invites excuses. If Rohan had kept the plan, she would praise him and help him build on it. If he had not, she would accept no excuses, impose no punishment and help him make a smaller plan that he could keep. She might also invite his father to a session to talk about the deadly habits and what might replace them.

Notice how different this is from Rogers. Had Glasser interviewed Gloria, in the films described at the start of this chapter, he would not have reflected her feelings about the lie she told her daughter, and he would certainly not have asked why she told it. He would have asked what she wanted for herself and for Pammy, what she was actually doing about it, and whether that was working. Reality therapists use a handful of further strategies: skilful questioning; being positive (meeting a complaint by asking what the client will do about it); using the client's own metaphors; humour, since fun is a basic need and a shared joke equalises power; confrontation, which in reality therapy simply means declining to accept excuses, without arguing or criticising; and, sparingly, paradoxical instructions of the kind you met in Section 6.5. Glasser's Positive Addiction (1976) added one more idea. A positive addiction is a strengthening habit, such as running or meditation, which the person comes to need and which, Glasser believed, gives easy access to strength and creativity. To qualify, he said, the activity should be non-competitive, done alone without much mental effort, of some physical, mental or spiritual value to the person, and done without self-criticism.

Exam trap

WDEP stands for wants, direction and doing, evaluation, planning; it is Robert Wubbolding's formulation of Glasser's method. The E is the client's self-evaluation, not the therapist's verdict, and the P is a plan that is simple, attainable, measurable, immediate and within the client's control. Reality therapy asks "what" rather than "why", because "why" invites excuses; it uses consequences and praise but never punishment or criticism. "No excuses" does not mean "no feelings": feelings are heard, but change is sought through doing and thinking, the front wheels of the car.

Schools, prisons and the evidence

Reality therapy spread fast because it was easy to explain, brief and hopeful, and because it was built for the people whom other therapies found hardest to reach. Glasser carried it into schools. In Schools Without Failure (1969) and The Identity Society (1972) he argued that children who experience repeated failure, and who lack warm involvement with adults, develop a failure identity, a lonely, self-defeating picture of themselves that leads to delinquency or withdrawal, whereas involvement and experiences of success build a success identity. In The Quality School (1990), drawing on the American quality-management expert W. Edwards Deming, he contrasted boss management, which coerces with rewards and punishments, with lead management, which persuades, involves and lets students evaluate their own work. The approach is also widely used in prisons and juvenile justice, in addiction services and in marriage counselling, and South Korea in particular has a large reality therapy movement. Glasser's own favourite evidence came from the early years.

Key study: Harrington's ward at the Los Angeles veterans hospital (1960s)

Question. Could an active, involved, responsibility-centred approach help even chronic, long-hospitalised psychotic patients? Method. In 1962 Glasser's mentor G. L. Harrington took charge of a ward at the Veterans Administration neuropsychiatric hospital in West Los Angeles that housed more than 210 chronic, regressed patients. They had been confined for an average of seventeen years and had received custodial care but almost no therapy, and about two a year were discharged. Harrington reorganised the ward around warm involvement, rising expectations and increasing responsibility for one's own behaviour. Results. As Glasser and Leonard Zunin later reported, 45 patients were discharged in the first year, 85 in the second and 90 in the third. Conclusion. Glasser took the results as evidence that people labelled hopelessly ill can learn to behave responsibly when someone becomes involved with them and expects it of them. Criticism. There was no control ward; the figures were reported by the approach's own advocates; discharge is not the same as recovery, and there was no systematic follow-up; and the change coincided with the spread of antipsychotic drugs and the movement to discharge long-stay patients into the community (Chapter 9), which emptied wards across the United States in the same years.

Beyond such reports, the research base is thin. Glasser was more interested in practice than in experiments, certified reality therapists are not trained in research, and most studies are small, often doctoral dissertations or articles in the approach's own International Journal of Reality Therapy. The best-known synthesis is a meta-analysis by Kim and Hwang (2006) of 43 Korean studies of group reality therapy in schools and colleges, which found that participants gained more in self-esteem and in an internal locus of control than members of control groups. Small trials have also reported benefits with male perpetrators of domestic violence, bullied children and students addicted to the internet. There is nothing like the body of randomised trials behind CBT, and reality therapy is not among the treatments that clinical guidelines recommend for particular disorders.

The criticisms are serious. Glasser's rejection of mental illness and his hostility to medication run against strong evidence that conditions such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and severe depression have biological components and respond to medication. By deliberately setting aside the unconscious, the past, dreams and transference, reality therapy may miss what keeps some problems going, and its insistence on individual choice can slide into blaming people for the effects of poverty, abuse, caste or discrimination, which they did not choose. Critics have also worried that "responsible" and "realistic" may come to mean whatever the therapist, the school or the prison finds acceptable, a real danger in institutions where the counsellor also holds power over the client. And the approach can look deceptively simple; Glasser himself insisted that it takes years of training to do well. Its strengths are just as real. It is clear, brief and teachable. It gives hope and a sense of agency to clients who have been told that they are helpless. It works in settings (classrooms, juvenile homes, prisons, de-addiction centres) where long insight-oriented therapy is impossible. And its four questions ("What do you want? What are you doing? Is it working? What will you do instead?") are so useful that counsellors of every school borrow them. Its spirit is close to Frankl's, with whom Glasser shared the conviction that people are responsible for their choices, and its warm involvement owes something to Rogers; Table 6.11 compares the three. Motivational interviewing, in Section 6.9, takes a different route to change, drawing out the client's own reasons rather than asking for a plan and a commitment straight away.

6.7 Focusing, Emotion-Focused Therapy and Positive PsychotherapyMedium yield · 2 Qs

Rogers taught therapists how to listen, and Perls taught them how to turn talk into experiment. Both left questions behind. If the core conditions are what heal, why do some clients of the same warm, accurate therapist change while others, just as well treated, do not? If emotion is so central to change, how exactly does an emotion change, and can a therapist guide that process without becoming the expert who directs it? And must therapy always start from what is wrong with a person, or could it start from what is right? This section follows three answers. The first came from Eugene Gendlin, a philosopher in Rogers's own team, who found the answer inside the client's body. The second came from Leslie Greenberg, who joined Rogers's relationship to Perls's chair work and turned the result into a researched therapy of emotion. The third, which turns from suffering to strengths, came from the positive psychology of Martin Seligman and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi and, quite separately and earlier, from the Iranian-German psychiatrist Nossrat Peseschkian.

Eugene Gendlin and the felt sense

Eugene Gendlin (1926 to 2017) was born in Vienna and was still a boy when his family fled the Nazis in 1938 and made their way to the United States. He became a philosopher, not a psychologist, and his lifelong question was a philosopher's one: how do words come out of what we feel? When we finally say something that is exactly right, what is it right to? While he was a graduate student in philosophy at the University of Chicago in the early 1950s, he joined Carl Rogers's counselling centre, trained as a counsellor and became one of Rogers's closest research colleagues. His doctorate in philosophy (1958) and his book Experiencing and the Creation of Meaning (1962) dealt with how felt experience comes to be symbolised in words. He was one of the leaders of the Wisconsin study of therapy with hospitalised schizophrenia patients that you met in Section 6.2, whose report he edited with Rogers, Donald Kiesler and Charles Truax in 1967, and he taught for many years at the University of Chicago.

The research that made Gendlin's name began with a puzzle that every therapist recognises. The Chicago counselling centre was full of tape recordings, and its therapists were carefully trained in the core conditions. Yet some clients changed and others, seen by the same therapists, did not. What made the difference?

Key study: who succeeds in therapy? (Gendlin and colleagues, Chicago and Wisconsin, 1950s and 1960s)

Question. If the therapist's conditions are much the same, what distinguishes the clients who change from those who do not? Method. Gendlin and his colleagues, among them Marjorie Klein, Philippa Mathieu and Donald Kiesler, listened to recorded segments of sessions and rated them, without knowing the outcome, on what became the Experiencing Scale: a seven-stage scale of how far a client's talk refers to, and works from, his or her own inner felt experience. At the low stages the client narrates events or describes other people impersonally; in the middle stages he describes his feelings; at the high stages he speaks from a directly felt inner sense, tentatively and searchingly, so that new meanings emerge from it and lead on to further steps. Results. The clients' level of experiencing, measurable even in early sessions, predicted who would succeed. Successful clients, at some point in a session, slowed down, groped for words and checked what they said against something they could feel but could not yet say ("It's not exactly anger... it's more like... yes, like being left out"). Unsuccessful clients talked fluently about events, or analysed themselves from the outside, and stayed the same however long therapy went on. What clients talked about, and even the therapist's technique, mattered less than how they talked. Conclusion. The client's way of processing his own experience is a major ingredient of change. Criticism. The findings are correlational: high experiencing might simply mark clients who were healthier or more psychologically minded to begin with. Later studies have, however, found that deeper experiencing in sessions predicts better outcome in several therapies, including cognitive therapy for depression and emotion-focused therapy.

The finding raised a hopeful question. If the successful clients were doing something, could the others be taught to do it? To answer, Gendlin first had to describe exactly what the successful clients were attending to, and his name for it is the most examined term in this subsection. A felt sense is a bodily felt, at first unclear and wordless sense of a whole situation or problem: not a thought, and not yet an emotion, but the body's sense of "all of that" about something. Here is an everyday version. You leave your hostel for the examination hall and, halfway down the road, you have the uneasy sense that you have forgotten something. You cannot say what it is, yet the sense is perfectly definite: it is not a vague worry, it is that something. You check. Pen? No, the unease stays. Watch? Still there. Hall ticket! And the body lets go with a small "yes, that's it". The unease was a felt sense. The release when the right word arrived is what Gendlin called a felt shift: a bodily easing which tells you that something has moved and that a real step of change has happened.

A felt sense is broader than an emotion. Anger at your brother is an emotion; the felt sense is "the whole thing about my brother", which holds the anger and also some guilt, an old rivalry, affection and something you do not yet understand. It is usually felt in the middle of the body (the throat, chest or stomach), and it always contains more than you can say. Gendlin called the continuous, bodily felt flow of experience experiencing, and he argued, in a paper called "A theory of personality change" (1964), that therapy works when experiencing that has been frozen into rigid, repeating patterns is "carried forward": when words, images or actions are found that fit it, so that it moves on. Notice the overlap with Rogers's process continuum from fixity to fluidity (Section 6.2). Gendlin was giving that continuum a mechanism.

The six steps of focusing

Gendlin found that the ability to attend to a felt sense could indeed be taught, and in 1978 he published a short self-help book, Focusing, which went on to sell widely around the world. Focusing is the method of turning attention inward, in a friendly and unhurried way, to the bodily felt sense of a problem, and letting words and steps of change come from it. Gendlin taught it in six "movements", shown in Figure 6.6, while insisting that they are scaffolding for beginners rather than a rigid procedure.

  1. Clearing a space. The person sits quietly, attends to the middle of the body and asks, "What stands between me and feeling fine right now?" As each concern comes up, he does not go into it but acknowledges it and imagines setting it down a little distance away, like a parcel placed on a bench.
  2. Felt sense. He chooses one concern and, instead of thinking about it, lets himself feel the whole of it in the body: the unclear, murky sense of "all of that".
  3. Handle. He lets a word, phrase or image come from the felt sense itself ("heavy", "jumpy", "like a closed fist"), a handle by which to hold it.
  4. Resonating. He goes back and forth between the handle and the felt sense, checking whether the word really fits and letting either change until it does. A good fit brings a small felt shift.
  5. Asking. He asks the felt sense an open question ("What is it about this whole thing that makes it so heavy?" "What does it need?") and waits, without supplying an answer from the head, until something comes with a felt shift.
  6. Receiving. He welcomes whatever came, without arguing with it or rushing to act on it, and lets it settle, knowing that he can return to it.

Consider a worked example. Priya, a twenty-four-year-old nursing student in Mysuru, tells her counsellor that she feels "something heavy" whenever she thinks about going home for Diwali, though her family is loving and nothing is wrong. Thinking about it has got her nowhere. Her counsellor invites her to pause, close her eyes if she likes, and first set aside the other things that are pressing on her: the ward examination, a quarrel with a roommate, money (clearing a space). Then she lets herself sense the whole of "going home". After a silence she says it is a tight band across her chest (felt sense). Asked whether there is a word for it, she tries "heavy", shakes her head, and then says "like holding my breath" (handle). She checks it: "Yes... holding my breath until they say it's all right" (resonating, with a small easing). Her counsellor asks gently what it is about going home that is so "holding my breath". She waits, and what comes is not an idea but a recognition, with tears and a long breath out: "I have not told them that I want to work in Bengaluru, not near home" (asking, and a clear felt shift). She sits with it for a while (receiving). Focusing has not told Priya what to do. It has done something earlier and harder: it has turned a vague, heavy dread into a clear, speakable problem that she can now work on.

Gendlin later described how any therapist can use the method in Focusing-Oriented Psychotherapy (1996). The therapist listens in a way that leaves room, slows the client down at promising moments ("Can you sense, in your body, the whole of that?" "Is there a word that fits it?"), and treats the felt sense, rather than any theory, as the final judge of whether a step is right. Focusing was also designed to be done without a therapist at all. Gendlin and his colleagues taught it to ordinary people, who practise in pairs, taking turns to focus and to listen, and The Focusing Institute, which he founded with colleagues, spread it internationally. Later teachers, notably Ann Weiser Cornell and Barbara McGavin with their "inner relationship focusing", developed the method further. Within psychotherapy, Gendlin's influence runs straight into emotion-focused therapy, which uses focusing as one of its standard tasks.

Exam trap

Focusing and the felt sense belong to Eugene Gendlin, a colleague of Rogers, not to Perls, Greenberg or Frankl. The felt sense is a bodily, pre-verbal sense of a whole situation; it is not an emotion and not a thought. The felt shift is the bodily release when the right symbol is found. Keep two process measures apart: Gendlin's Experiencing Scale rates how deeply the client works from inner experience, whereas Rogers and Rablen's Process Scale (Section 6.2) rates movement from fixity to fluidity. And do not confuse Gendlin's focusing with focusing, the second of the four processes of motivational interviewing (Section 6.9), or with mindfulness (Chapter 5), which cultivates non-judging awareness of whatever arises rather than inviting the felt sense of one particular problem to speak.

Leslie Greenberg and emotion-focused therapy

The second answer came from Toronto. Leslie Greenberg (born 1945), a South African-born psychologist who trained first as an engineer, took his doctorate at York University in Toronto, where his teacher was Laura Rice, a client-centred therapist who had worked with Rogers in Chicago. He also trained in Gestalt therapy. Greenberg was, by temperament, an engineer of process. He wanted to know not just whether therapy worked but how, step by step, a client moves from being stuck to being unstuck in a particular kind of moment. With Rice he developed task analysis, the intensive study of recorded "change events" in order to build a model of how clients resolve one specific kind of problem, which they set out in Patterns of Change (1984). You have already met some of the results in Section 6.3: the studies showing that two-chair dialogue at a moment of inner conflict leads to deeper experiencing and better resolution than reflection alone.

Out of this work grew a therapy. Greenberg, Rice and Robert Elliott described it in Facilitating Emotional Change (1993) as process-experiential therapy, and in the early 2000s it took its present name, set out in Greenberg's Emotion-Focused Therapy: Coaching Clients to Work Through Their Feelings (2002) and in Learning Emotion-Focused Therapy (2004) by Elliott, Jeanne Watson, Rhonda Goldman and Greenberg. Emotion-focused therapy (EFT) can be defined as a humanistic-experiential therapy that combines the person-centred relationship with active experiential tasks, derived from Gestalt therapy and from focusing, in order to help clients become aware of, accept, make sense of, regulate and transform their emotions. In a phrase, it is Rogers's relationship plus Perls's experiments plus Gendlin's attention to the body, all guided by a research-based theory of emotion. (In the 1980s Greenberg also worked with Susan Johnson on an emotionally focused therapy for couples; Johnson's attachment-based development of it is taught in Section 7.8.)

The theory starts from a claim that may surprise you: emotions are not the enemy. Greenberg, drawing on modern emotion research, sees emotion as an evolved, rapid appraisal system that tells us that something important to our wellbeing is happening and prepares us to act. Fear says "danger: escape". Sadness says "loss: reach out for comfort, or withdraw to recover". Anger says "violation: assert yourself, set a boundary". Psychological problems arise, in this view, not because people have too much emotion but because they avoid their emotions, misread them, or are governed by old emotional responses that no longer fit their lives. Emotional responses are produced by emotion schemes: internal organisations of past emotional experience, combining bodily sensations, feelings, memories, meanings and action tendencies, which are activated automatically and largely outside words. Greenberg spells the word "schemes" on purpose, to set them apart from the more verbal, belief-like schemas of cognitive therapy (Chapter 5).

The most useful practical tool in the theory is a classification of emotional responses into four kinds, set out in Table 6.12. A therapist who can tell them apart knows what to do with each.

A worked example makes the distinctions concrete. Ravi, a forty-year-old bank manager in Pune, comes to therapy because he keeps exploding at his fifteen-year-old son over marks, and then hates himself for it. His rage is real, but it is a secondary emotion: a reaction that covers something else. As the therapist follows him slowly through the last outburst ("What happened inside you just before you shouted?"), Ravi reaches a sinking, shrinking feeling in his stomach and the words "I am a failure, and everyone can see it". This is a primary maladaptive emotion, a core shame laid down when his own father mocked him in front of relatives for his school results; it is old, familiar, and it does him no good. A primary adaptive emotion, by contrast, is one that fits the present situation and points somewhere useful. The therapist does not argue Ravi out of his shame, as a cognitive therapist might dispute a belief. Instead, having helped him stay with it long enough to arrive at it, she helps him reach the healthier feelings that the shame has been blocking: anger at having been humiliated as a child ("It was not fair; I was a boy who was trying") and grief for the encouragement he never had. As these adaptive emotions are felt and expressed, often in an empty-chair dialogue with the remembered father, the shame begins to lose its grip, and Ravi finds he can sit beside his son's report card without exploding. The fourth kind, instrumental emotion, is expressed in order to influence others (the tears that win sympathy, the anger that wins arguments), and with it the therapist explores what the emotion is for rather than deepening it.

Greenberg summarised how emotions change in six principles. Awareness comes first: the client must notice and name what he feels, and Greenberg liked to say that you cannot leave a place until you have arrived at it. Expression means overcoming avoidance and letting the feeling be felt and voiced in the session. Regulation means helping clients who are overwhelmed (by panic, rage or despair) gain enough distance to soothe themselves, through breathing, self-soothing and the calm presence of the therapist. Reflection means making sense of the experience in words and building a new account of it. Transformation is the heart of the method: a maladaptive emotion is changed not by reasoning but by activating a different, adaptive emotion alongside it. Greenberg quoted the seventeenth-century philosopher Baruch Spinoza, who wrote that an emotion can be restrained or removed only by an opposed and stronger emotion; his own slogan is "changing emotion with emotion". Finally, corrective emotional experience means that new lived experiences, above all being met with acceptance where contempt was expected, change old feelings in a way that insight alone cannot.

Where does that leave the relationship? EFT keeps Rogers's core conditions as its foundation, and its therapists spend much of their time in empathic exploration of the client's experience. But the stance differs from classical client-centred therapy in one important way. The EFT therapist is process-guiding: she follows the client's lead about what matters, but actively guides how the client works with it, suggesting at the right moment that he focus inward, or speak to the critic in the other chair. Greenberg describes this as a dialectic of following and leading. It is gentler and less confrontational than Gestalt therapy in Perls's hands, and more directive than Rogers.

Exam trap

Four look-alikes. Emotion-focused therapy (EFT) for individuals is Leslie Greenberg's (earlier called process-experiential therapy). Emotionally focused couple therapy is chiefly Susan Johnson's, based on attachment theory (Section 7.8), although Greenberg and Johnson first wrote it up together in 1988. Emotion-focused coping, managing one's feelings about a stressor rather than changing the stressor itself (problem-focused coping), comes from Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman's theory of stress (General Psychology book) and is not a therapy. Emotional intelligence was named by Peter Salovey and John Mayer (1990) and popularised by Daniel Goleman (1995), whose original five domains are knowing one's own emotions, managing emotions, motivating oneself, recognising emotions in others and handling relationships (General Psychology book); the same five were later relabelled self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills, and that later list is the usual distractor when an item asks for Goleman's original wording. Within EFT itself, anger that covers hurt is a secondary emotion, and old core shame is primary maladaptive.

Markers, tasks and the evidence

What makes EFT distinctive in practice is its system of markers and tasks, the direct product of Rice and Greenberg's task analysis. A marker is something the client says or does in the session that signals that he is in a particular kind of problem state and is ready to work on it now. Each marker calls for a particular task, a structured piece of experiential work with known steps toward resolution. The therapist does not plan the session in advance. She listens for markers and offers the matching task when one appears. Table 6.13 lists the main pairs.

Three of these tasks you know from Gestalt therapy, now tamed and studied. In two-chair dialogue for a self-critical split, the critic (Perls's top dog) speaks from one chair, and the "experiencing self" that it attacks responds from the other. Greenberg's research showed what resolution looks like: the experiencing self moves from collapse to an assertion of its needs ("I need you to stop; I need some support"), and the critic softens from contempt into something closer to concern or even compassion. In two-chair enactment for self-interruption, the client plays the part of himself that shuts feeling down ("Stop crying, don't be weak"), so that he discovers how he does it and can choose otherwise. Empty-chair work for unfinished business follows the pattern of Paivio and Greenberg's 1995 study (Section 6.3). The other tasks come from Rogers's tradition. In systematic evocative unfolding, devised by Laura Rice, the therapist helps the client re-enter, slowly and vividly, the scene of a puzzling over-reaction until the hidden meaning of the trigger emerges: the conductor's shout, it turns out, was the voice of every adult who had ever shamed her in public. Focusing is Gendlin's method applied to an unclear felt sense. And for moments of acute vulnerability there is no technique at all. The therapist offers empathic affirmation, a quiet, accepting presence that lets the client touch bottom and begin, by himself, to rise.

Look back at the aim that Section 6.1 gave for the whole humanistic-experiential family: to help clients develop a more fluid and integrated way of organising the self. EFT puts that aim into practice almost literally. Two-chair work integrates the warring parts of a split self; transformation integrates old emotions with new ones; reflection integrates feeling with meaning. Rogers's "fluidity" has become a set of tasks that can be taught, observed and tested.

Key study: the York depression studies (Toronto, 1990s and 2000s)

Question. Do EFT's active tasks add anything to Rogers's relationship conditions, or are the conditions enough, as Rogers claimed? Method. In the first York study, published in 1998, Greenberg and Jeanne Watson randomly assigned adults with major depression to about 16 to 20 weekly sessions of either client-centred therapy (empathy, acceptance and genuineness only) or process-experiential therapy (the same relationship plus markers and tasks such as chair work and focusing). Results. Both groups improved substantially and were similar on depression by the end, but the process-experiential group changed faster, was ahead by mid-treatment, and had improved more on self-esteem and interpersonal problems by the end. A second York study, by Rhonda Goldman, Greenberg and Lynne Angus (first reported in 2000 and published in full in 2006), found a clearer advantage for EFT on depressive symptoms, and a follow-up 18 months later (Ellison and colleagues, 2009) found that the EFT clients had fared better over time. A separate Toronto trial led by Watson (2003) compared process-experiential therapy with cognitive-behaviour therapy over 16 sessions in 66 depressed clients: both helped equally with depression and self-esteem, and the process-experiential clients reported fewer interpersonal problems. Conclusion. The core conditions are a strong base, but specific experiential tasks seem to add speed and depth, which is a partial answer to Rogers's claim that the conditions are sufficient (Section 6.2). Criticism. The trials were modest in size, and most were run by EFT's own developers, so researcher allegiance (Chapter 8) cannot be ruled out.

Exam trap

Match the marker to the task. A self-critical split calls for the two-chair dialogue; unfinished business with another person calls for the empty chair; an unclear felt sense calls for focusing; a puzzling over-reaction calls for systematic evocative unfolding; vulnerability calls for empathic affirmation, not a technique. EFT is process-guiding (it guides how the client works, not what he should conclude), so it is wrong to call it either "non-directive" or "confrontational". If a question asks for the general aim of the humanistic-experiential therapies, the answer is to promote a fluid and integrative self-organisation (Section 6.1), not to resolve deep-seated conflicts (psychodynamic), change maladaptive behaviours (behavioural) or develop interpersonal skills.

Martin Seligman, positive psychology and positive psychotherapy

All the therapies you have met so far, however hopeful, begin with a problem: incongruence, unfinished business, a maladaptive emotion. In the late 1990s a movement began which asked whether psychology had become too good at studying what goes wrong with people and too poor at studying what goes right. Its founder tells the story of how the idea came to him. Martin E. P. Seligman (born 1942), of the University of Pennsylvania, had recently been elected president of the American Psychological Association when he was weeding his garden with his five-year-old daughter, Nikki. He was trying to get the job done; she was throwing weeds into the air and dancing, and he shouted at her. She walked away, came back and said: "Daddy, do you remember before my fifth birthday? From when I was three until when I was five, I was a whiner. I whined every day. On my fifth birthday, I decided I wasn't going to whine anymore. That was the hardest thing I've ever done. And if I can stop whining, you can stop being such a grouch." Seligman realised that raising Nikki was not about correcting her whining; she had done that herself. It was about recognising and nurturing the strength she had just shown. And he wondered whether psychology, too, had lost its way.

Seligman was an unlikely prophet of happiness. He was famous for discovering learned helplessness: in experiments with Steven Maier in the late 1960s, dogs that had received shocks they could not escape later failed to escape shocks that they easily could, and the finding became a model of depression (taught in the Psychiatric Disorders book). He went on to study the explanatory style that protects people against helplessness and wrote Learned Optimism (1990). In his presidential address of 1998 he argued that since the Second World War psychology had become a science of healing damage, shaped by the funding of bodies such as the Veterans Administration and the National Institute of Mental Health, which paid psychologists to treat mental illness, and that it had neglected its two other missions: making the lives of ordinary people more fulfilling, and identifying and nurturing high talent. With Csikszentmihalyi he edited a special issue of the American Psychologist in January 2000 that launched the field. Positive psychology is the scientific study of positive experiences, of positive individual traits such as strengths and virtues, and of the institutions that enable them: in its slogan, building what is strong as well as fixing what is wrong.

What, then, is a good life, and can it be studied? Seligman's first answer, in Authentic Happiness (2002), was that there are three routes to it: the pleasant life (positive emotion about the past, present and future), the engaged life (using one's strengths to become absorbed in work, love and play, in the state of flow described below) and the meaningful life (using one's strengths in the service of something larger than oneself). In Flourish (2011) he widened this into a theory of wellbeing with five elements, remembered by the acronym PERMA: positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning and accomplishment. With Christopher Peterson (1950 to 2012) he also produced Character Strengths and Virtues (2004), the Values in Action (VIA) classification, which its authors offered as a "manual of the sanities" to stand beside the DSM. It describes 24 character strengths grouped under six virtues found across cultures and religious traditions: wisdom and knowledge, courage, humanity, justice, temperance and transcendence. A person's top few strengths, the ones that feel most authentically his own and most energising to use, are his signature strengths.

These ideas soon became a therapy. In 2006 Seligman, Tayyab Rashid and Acacia Parks published a paper titled "Positive psychotherapy" in the American Psychologist. Positive psychotherapy, in their sense, is a treatment for depression and other problems that works directly to build positive emotion, engagement and meaning alongside attention to symptoms, on the premise that depression is not only the presence of sadness but also the absence of these positive states. A course runs over about fourteen sessions (fewer in groups), and each session introduces an exercise that is practised as homework. The best known are these:

  • Signature strengths. The client writes a story of "you at your best", identifies his signature strengths, and uses one of them in a new way every day.
  • Three good things. Each night the client writes down three things that went well that day and why they happened, a "blessings journal".
  • The gratitude visit. The client writes a letter of thanks to someone who was important to him but was never properly thanked, and then delivers it and reads it aloud in person.
  • Forgiveness. The client writes, but need not send, a letter that lets go of anger toward someone who hurt him.
  • Active-constructive responding. When someone shares good news, the client responds with enthusiastic interest and questions, rather than a passive "nice" or a deflating comment.
  • Savouring and legacy. The client deliberately prolongs his enjoyment of ordinary pleasures, and writes how he would like to be remembered.
Key study: testing happiness exercises on the internet (Seligman, Steen, Park and Peterson, 2005)

Question. Do simple positive psychology exercises make people happier and less depressed, and for how long? Method. Several hundred adults who visited a website were randomly assigned to do one exercise for one week: the gratitude visit, three good things, "you at your best", identifying signature strengths, using signature strengths in a new way, or a placebo exercise (writing each night about early memories). Happiness and depressive symptoms were measured before, straight after, and one week, one month, three months and six months later. Results. The gratitude visit produced the largest early gains, but they lasted only about a month and had gone by the three-month follow-up. Three good things and using signature strengths in a new way produced gains that appeared more slowly but lasted for six months. The placebo and the remaining exercises produced only brief effects. Participants who went on doing their exercise of their own accord did best. Conclusion. Brief, specific exercises can produce lasting changes in wellbeing, especially if they are kept up. Criticism. The participants were self-selected visitors to a positive psychology website, many dropped out, and the researchers had devised the exercises themselves.

Seligman, Rashid and Parks reported in 2006 that group positive psychotherapy reduced depressive symptoms in mildly to moderately depressed university students for a year afterwards, and that individual positive psychotherapy produced more remission in clients with major depression at a university counselling centre than treatment as usual, with or without antidepressant medication. These were small pilot trials. Larger reviews are more sober. A meta-analysis by Nancy Sin and Sonja Lyubomirsky (2009) found that positive psychology interventions moderately increase wellbeing and reduce depressive symptoms, and a stricter meta-analysis by Linda Bolier and colleagues (2013) confirmed that the effects are real but small. Critics have also pointed to a "tyranny of positive thinking" that can make suffering people feel they are failing at happiness, to the Western, individualist picture of the good life built into some exercises, and to the field's occasional overreach. A much-publicised claim by Barbara Fredrickson and Marcial Losada (2005), that flourishing requires a ratio of positive to negative emotions of 2.9013, was shown in 2013 by Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman to rest on misapplied mathematics, and the mathematical part of the paper was formally withdrawn.

Clinical link

Positive interventions are adjuncts, not replacements. A severely depressed client asked to list "three good things" may find none and conclude that he is failing even at therapy; a recently bereaved client handed a gratitude exercise may feel that his grief is being brushed aside. Clinicians therefore acknowledge the suffering first (the groundwork of Section 6.2), time the exercises carefully and adapt them to the client's world. A gratitude letter to a teacher, a grandparent or an elder sibling, or a strengths conversation that includes what the family is proud of, often fits an Indian client better than a solitary journal.

Exam trap

Positive psychology was launched by Martin Seligman during his APA presidency (1998), with Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi as co-founder (the special issue of 2000). The phrase itself is older: Abraham Maslow used it as the title of the last chapter of Motivation and Personality (1954), "Toward a positive psychology", so "Maslow used the term first; Seligman founded the field" is the safe formula. Seligman is also the name behind learned helplessness (with Maier), learned optimism, PERMA and, with Peterson, the VIA classification of 24 strengths under six virtues. He did not introduce the concept of flow.

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi and flow

Seligman's "engaged life" rests on the work of his co-founder. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934 to 2021; the name is pronounced roughly "me-high cheek-sent-me-high") was born to a Hungarian family in Fiume, then part of Italy and now Rijeka in Croatia. As a boy during the Second World War he watched capable adults lose their homes, their work and their composure, and he began to wonder what makes a life worth living. He moved to the United States in the 1950s, took his doctorate at the University of Chicago and spent most of his career there, later moving to Claremont Graduate University in California.

His central discovery began with painters. Studying artists for his doctoral research in the 1960s, he noticed that they would work at a canvas for hours, oblivious to hunger, fatigue and discomfort, utterly absorbed, and yet would lose interest in the painting almost as soon as it was finished. The reward was obviously not the product; it was the activity itself. He went on to interview rock climbers, chess players, dancers, surgeons and composers, and was struck that they described their best moments with the same image, of being carried along effortlessly by a current. He called the state flow: a state of complete absorption in a challenging activity, in which action and awareness merge, attention is wholly focused, self-consciousness disappears, the sense of time is altered and the activity becomes rewarding in itself. He described it first in Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975), and for a general audience in Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (1990).

Flow has three conditions and several characteristic features. The conditions are clear goals, immediate feedback about how one is doing, and a balance between the perceived challenge of the task and one's skills, with both stretched. The features are the merging of action and awareness, complete concentration on the task, a sense of control, the loss of self-consciousness, the transformation of time (hours pass like minutes, or a split second stretches out) and an autotelic quality, from the Greek auto (self) and telos (goal): the activity is done for its own sake. A batsman "in the zone", a sitar player lost in a raga, a surgeon midway through a difficult operation and a student wholly absorbed in a hard problem are all in flow.

The balance of challenge and skill is easiest to see in a picture. Csikszentmihalyi's own example was a boy learning tennis, and Figure 6.7 follows its logic. At first (A1) the learner can do little, and simply getting the ball over the net is a just-right challenge: she is in flow. With practice her skill grows, and getting the ball over the net becomes dull (A2, boredom). Or she is matched too soon against a far stronger player and is overwhelmed (A3, anxiety). Neither state is pleasant, and both push her back toward the channel: from boredom she seeks a harder challenge, and from anxiety she builds her skill. Either way she reaches A4, flow again at a more complex level. Flow, in other words, is not only enjoyable; it is a ladder of growth, because staying in it demands ever-increasing skill. Later versions of the model, developed with the Italian researchers Fausto Massimini and Massimo Carli, added that flow occurs when both challenge and skill are above the person's own average level, and that when both are low the result is not flow but apathy.

To study flow in everyday life, Csikszentmihalyi and Reed Larson developed the experience sampling method. Participants carry a pager (today, a phone) that signals at random moments, about eight times a day for a week, and each time they note what they are doing, with whom, and how they feel. The method produced a famous paradox. In a study with Judith LeFevre (1989), working adults reported flow far more often at work than in their leisure time, which was dominated by passive activities such as watching television, and yet at work they said that they wished they were somewhere else. People, it seems, do not always know what makes them feel most alive.

Flow matters clinically in several ways. It is the "engagement" of Seligman's PERMA and of positive psychotherapy. Depressed clients rarely experience it, and behavioural activation (Chapter 4) works partly by scheduling activities that offer a just-manageable challenge. Occupational and rehabilitation programmes (Chapter 9) aim to restore it. And flow has a darker side: the absorbing, finely graded challenges of gambling machines and video games are designed to produce something very like it, which helps to explain why they are so hard to stop.

Exam trap

Flow was introduced by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (RML 2023 asked exactly this). Each distractor is famous for something nearby. Maslow described peak experiences: brief, intense, often unexpected moments of awe, ecstasy and unity, more common in self-actualising people. Flow, by contrast, is a sustained state of effortful absorption in a challenging task, often recognised as enjoyable only afterwards. Rogers described the fully functioning person (Section 6.2), and Frankl the will to meaning (Section 6.5). The conditions of flow are clear goals, immediate feedback and a balance of challenge and skill: challenge above skill gives anxiety, skill above challenge gives boredom, and both low gives apathy.

Nossrat Peseschkian's positive psychotherapy

Examiners sometimes ask about "positive psychotherapy" in a way that has nothing to do with Seligman, because a quite different therapy with the same name is some three decades older. Nossrat Peseschkian (1933 to 2010) was born in Kashan, Iran, went to Germany in the 1950s to study medicine, and trained there in neurology, psychiatry and psychotherapy. Working in Wiesbaden from the late 1960s, and struck by how differently his Iranian and German patients thought about illness, family, time and politeness, he developed a transcultural, resource-oriented therapy, which he set out in Positive Psychotherapie (1977; English translation, Positive Psychotherapy, 1987).

The word "positive" is the first trap. Peseschkian did not mean positive thinking. He took it from the Latin positum, "the given" or "the factual": a positive approach looks at the whole of what is actually there, which includes the person's capacities as well as his disorder. Every person, in his view, has two basic capacities, the capacity to know and the capacity to love, which unfold into actual capacities. The primary capacities belong to the capacity to love and concern emotional relationships: love, patience, time, trust, contact, hope and faith. The secondary capacities belong to the capacity to know and concern the norms of social life: punctuality, orderliness, cleanliness, politeness, honesty, thrift, diligence, reliability and justice. Conflicts, he argued, come less often from single dramatic traumas than from repeated small frictions over these everyday capacities (the husband who is always late, the daughter-in-law who keeps the kitchen differently, the son who is "lazy") which pile up until they produce symptoms. Anyone who has lived in a large joint family will recognise the idea.

Three tools follow from this. The first is the positive interpretation of symptoms. Instead of treating a symptom only as a defect, the therapist offers a reinterpretation that names the capacity hidden in it. Depression, in one of Peseschkian's best-known examples, can be understood as the ability to react to conflicts with deep emotionality. The aim is not to deny the suffering but to widen the frame, so that the client and the family can see the person and not just the disorder. The second tool is the use of stories, fables, proverbs and humour as mediators, drawn especially from Persian and other Eastern traditions. His book Oriental Stories as Tools in Psychotherapy is subtitled The Merchant and the Parrot, after a tale by the thirteenth-century Persian poet Rumi. In it, a merchant about to travel to India asks his caged parrot what he should bring back. The parrot asks only that he tell the free parrots of India about its captivity. When the merchant does so, one wild parrot drops from the tree as if dead; when he reports this at home, his own parrot drops too, the grieving merchant throws it out of the cage, and it flies away. It had received a message about how to free itself. Stories like this let a client hear a new idea without feeling attacked.

The third tool is the balance model, Peseschkian's map of the four areas of life through which people experience and handle conflict: the body (health, the senses, sleep, food, appearance), achievement (work, study, career), contact (relationships with partner, family, friends and community) and the future (fantasy, meaning, religion and spirituality). In health, energy is shared among all four. Under stress people tend to flee into one or two of them: into work, into illness and the body, into socialising, or into fantasy and daydreams. Therapy looks at which areas the client has neglected and helps to restore the balance. Peseschkian added that cultures differ in which areas they emphasise, so that a therapist from one culture can easily misread a client from another.

Treatment follows five stages, and the logic of each is worth knowing. In observation and distancing, the client (often with the family) describes the conflict concretely: who annoys whom, about what, and when. In taking inventory, therapist and client map the actual capacities involved, with the help of a structured questionnaire. In situational encouragement, the client learns to notice and reinforce the good qualities of the person he is in conflict with, instead of only the faults. In verbalisation, the parties learn to talk about the conflict openly and step by step. In broadening of goals, the client looks beyond the symptom to new aims in all four areas of life. The method rests on three principles: hope (the positive interpretation), balance (the four areas) and consultation (the client and family as active self-helpers, guided through the five stages). Its evidence comes mainly from naturalistic studies in German-speaking countries; controlled trials are few. Table 6.14 sets the two positive psychotherapies side by side.

6.8 Narrative and Solution-Focused Brief TherapyMedium yield · 2 Qs

Every therapy in this chapter so far assumes that there is something true inside the client waiting to be found: an actualising tendency, a felt sense, a primary emotion, a meaning. In the 1980s two small groups of family therapists, on opposite sides of the world, began from a different assumption. There is no single true story of a life, they said. There are many possible stories, made in language and in relationships, and some of them help people to live better than others. In Adelaide in South Australia and Auckland in New Zealand, Michael White and David Epston built narrative therapy, which helps people re-author the stories by which they understand themselves. In Milwaukee, Wisconsin, Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg built solution-focused brief therapy, which hardly bothers with the problem's story at all and goes straight to building the solution. Both grew out of family therapy, and their family applications are taught in Section 7.7, together with the brief strategic therapy of the Mental Research Institute from which they partly descend. Here we study them as approaches to individual clients, because they share, in a new form, the humanistic conviction that the client is the expert on his or her own life.

The postmodern turn: problems as stories

Both therapies belong to what is called the postmodern, or social constructionist, movement in therapy. Social constructionism is the view that what we take to be reality, including our identities and our problems, is not simply discovered but is constructed through language and social interaction, within a particular culture and history. "Depressed", "lazy", "a good son", "a failure": none of these is a fact found inside a person, like a blood group. Each is a description, made by people talking to one another, which then shapes how the person is treated and how he sees himself. The idea is a cousin of constructivism, the view of George Kelly (Section 5.5) that each individual builds personal constructs to make sense of the world. The difference is one of emphasis. Constructivism stresses the individual mind as a builder of meaning; social constructionism stresses the shared, cultural and linguistic processes through which meanings are built and imposed.

Three consequences follow for therapy. First, the therapist is not an expert on the truth about the client. Harlene Anderson and Harold Goolishian, family therapists in Galveston, Texas, called this the "not-knowing" stance in a 1992 paper titled "The client is the expert". Second, a diagnosis is itself a story, often a powerful one that can shrink a person's identity to a label. Third, change happens through conversation: new ways of talking make new ways of living possible. Both therapies also drew on the earlier brief therapy movement: on the anthropologist Gregory Bateson's idea that information is "news of difference", on the Mental Research Institute's observation that people's attempted solutions often keep their problems going (Chapter 7), and on the American psychiatrist and hypnotherapist Milton Erickson (1901 to 1980), who was famous for utilising whatever the client brought, including his symptoms and his own language, and for orienting clients toward a future in which the problem had been solved.

Michael White, David Epston and the stories people live by

Michael White (1948 to 2008) began his working life in Adelaide as an electrical and mechanical draughtsman, then retrained as a social worker, and worked at the Dulwich Centre in Adelaide, which became the hub of narrative therapy worldwide. David Epston, a family therapist with a background in anthropology who worked in Auckland, met White in the early 1980s, and the two found that they had been arriving at similar ideas from different directions. Their joint book, first published in Adelaide in 1989 as Literate Means to Therapeutic Ends and in the United States in 1990 as Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends, launched the approach. White's later Maps of Narrative Practice (2007) set out its methods in detail.

Narrative therapy can be defined as an approach that helps people separate their lives and identities from the problems that trouble them, and re-author the stories by which they understand themselves, so that these stories include their own skills, values and preferred ways of living. Its starting point is that people make sense of their lives by linking events together in sequence across time according to a plot: as a story. Such a story is always selective. Out of the countless things that happen to us, we select those that fit the dominant plot and leave out the rest. A person who comes to therapy usually brings a problem-saturated story: a dominant account of his life in which the problem colours everything, so that every event seems to confirm it and everything that does not fit is overlooked. Problem-saturated stories produce what White called thin conclusions about identity ("I am weak", "she is a manipulator", "he is an alcoholic"). White borrowed from the anthropologist Clifford Geertz the contrast between thin descriptions, which reduce a person to a label, and thick descriptions, which are rich, many-stranded and full of context.

Where do problem-saturated stories come from? White was strongly influenced by the French philosopher Michel Foucault (1926 to 1984), who studied how the knowledge of experts and institutions (medicine, psychiatry, schools, prisons) defines what counts as normal and, in doing so, exercises power over people, who learn to watch and judge themselves by those standards. Dominant cultural stories about success, gender, beauty, family duty and mental illness can recruit people into seeing themselves as failures. A young woman who starves herself is, in this view, not simply "an anorexic"; she is a person who has been recruited by powerful cultural ideas about thinness and female self-control. Narrative therapy is therefore unusually political. It invites clients to notice and question (to deconstruct) the cultural stories that support their problems. From the American psychologist Jerome Bruner (1915 to 2016) White took the idea that stories have two landscapes, which you will meet in the re-authoring conversations below.

The therapist's stance follows from all this. The narrative therapist is curious, asks far more questions than she makes statements, and treats the client as the expert on his own life. White described the ideal position as "decentred but influential": the client's knowledge and preferences are at the centre, while the therapist influences the direction of the conversation through the questions she chooses to ask. The approach is summed up in its best-known slogan: the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem.

Exam trap

"Self story", "re-authoring" or "re-storying" a life narrative points to narrative therapy (Michael White and David Epston, 1980s; Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends, 1990). The distractors in the 2025 paper each work on something else: CBT changes thoughts and behaviours; psychodynamic therapy works with unconscious conflict and transference (although some analysts, such as Donald Spence, have written about "narrative truth"); Gestalt therapy works on here-and-now awareness. Three other near neighbours: George Kelly's fixed-role therapy (1955) is constructivist (Section 5.5); narrative exposure therapy is a trauma treatment in which survivors build a chronological account of their lives so that traumatic memories can be processed, which makes it an exposure method rather than narrative therapy; and Dan McAdams's life-story model of identity belongs to the Personality book.

Externalising the problem

The best-known practice of narrative therapy is externalising: speaking about the problem as an entity separate from the person, with its own intentions and tactics, so that the person can stand back from it, examine its influence and decide how to respond to it. The shift is made in language. "Arjun is lazy" becomes "the Worry has been keeping Arjun in bed in the mornings". "She is an anorexic" becomes "Anorexia has been telling her that she must not eat". "We are a fighting family" becomes "the Arguments have been taking over our evenings". Clients are invited to give the problem their own, experience-near name, and the name can be playful, especially with children. Once the problem is outside the person, the person is no longer under attack, blame and shame fall away, and family members who were blaming one another can join forces against a common opponent.

Key study: Nick and Sneaky Poo (Michael White, 1984)

Problem. Nick, aged six, was brought to White by his parents, Sue and Ron, with a long history of soiling. Hardly a day passed without an "accident", many treatments had failed, and the soiling had become a source of shame and conflict for the whole family. Sue doubted that she was a good mother, Ron was too embarrassed to talk about it with anyone and felt isolated, and Nick had come to be seen, and to see himself, as a dirty, naughty boy. Method. White did not ask why Nick soiled. Instead, he and the family gave the problem a name, "Sneaky Poo", and he asked two sets of questions, which he called relative influence questioning. The first set mapped the influence of the problem on the family: how had Sneaky Poo made Nick's life miserable, kept him from friends, driven Sue to despair and cut Ron off from other people? The second set mapped the influence of the family on the problem: had there been times when Nick had not let Sneaky Poo "outwit" him? Times when Sue had refused to let it make her miserable? It turned out that there had. Result. These overlooked moments became the start of a new story, of a boy and a family who could beat Sneaky Poo. Encouraged not to let Sneaky Poo get the better of him, Nick began to win; within weeks the accidents had become rare, and at follow-up the family reported that Nick had triumphed and that each of them felt more capable. Conclusion. When the problem rather than the child becomes the target, a family can unite against it, and its own competence becomes visible. Criticism. This is a single case reported by its own therapist; it illustrates the method rather than proving that it works.

In Maps of Narrative Practice White set out the steps of an externalising conversation as a "statement of position map". The therapist helps the person (1) negotiate a particular, experience-near name for the problem; (2) map its effects across the areas of his life, such as home, school or work, friendships, health and hopes for the future; (3) evaluate those effects ("Is this okay with you? Are you for it, against it, or somewhere in between?"); and (4) justify that evaluation ("Why is it not okay? What does that tell me about what matters to you?"). The last two steps matter more than they look. The therapist does not assume that the person is against the problem; she asks, and the person's own reasons for opposing it become the seeds of a new story.

Consider how this might go with Arjun, a seventeen-year-old Class 12 student in Mysuru whose parents bring him because, they say, he has become lazy: he has stopped going to his coaching classes, sleeps late and did badly in the last test. Arjun agrees that he is "useless". Asked what he would call the thing that has been happening to him, he eventually says "the Worry". The therapist asks how the Worry operates. It turns out that it whispers, the moment he opens a book, that he will fail anyway and shame the family, so he might as well not try; it keeps him in bed; it has pushed his friends away; it has started quarrels at the dinner table. Is this okay with him? "No." Why not? "Because I actually used to like maths. And because Amma looks so tired." Notice what has happened. Arjun has moved from being the problem ("I am useless") to being someone who is being pushed around by a problem that he has his own reasons to resist. His parents, listening, have moved from criticising him to wondering with him how to deal with the Worry.

Externalising is never used to excuse harmful behaviour. Narrative therapists working with men who are violent do not externalise the violence itself, for which the man remains responsible; they externalise the beliefs and habits that support it ("the idea that a husband has the right to control his wife"), so that he can examine and reject them.

Exam trap

Externalising the problem is a narrative therapy technique (White and Epston): the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem. Do not confuse it with externalising disorders (conduct problems, oppositional behaviour, ADHD), the grouping in child psychopathology that is contrasted with internalising disorders such as anxiety and depression (Psychiatric Disorders book), or with projection, the defence of attributing one's own unacceptable feelings to others (Section 6.3 and the Personality book). Externalising is a deliberate, shared way of talking; projection is an unconscious defence. Relative influence questioning asks about the influence of the problem on the person and of the person on the problem.

Unique outcomes, re-authoring and the audience

Once the problem has been externalised, the therapist listens for the moments that the problem-saturated story leaves out. White called them unique outcomes, a term he borrowed from the sociologist Erving Goffman: events, thoughts, feelings or actions that contradict the dominant story and could not have been predicted from it. Narrative therapists also call them "sparkling moments". In Nick's family they were the times when Nick had not let Sneaky Poo outwit him. For Arjun they come out slowly. Last week he spent an evening teaching his younger cousin algebra, and enjoyed it. On Sunday he finished a whole chapter before the Worry noticed. Once he told the Worry "not now" and went to class anyway. And he has started asking his favourite teacher for help. On their own, such moments are easily dismissed ("that was nothing"). The work of therapy is to link them together into a new story.

That work is done in re-authoring conversations, in which the therapist helps the client connect unique outcomes into an alternative, preferred story of his life and identity. White, following Bruner, described every story as having two landscapes. The landscape of action is the sequence of events: what happened, when and where, who was there and what led up to it ("How did you manage to finish that chapter? What did you do first? Who noticed?"). The landscape of identity (which White first called the landscape of consciousness) is what those events say about the person's values, hopes, intentions and commitments ("What does it say about you that you spent your evening helping your cousin? What were you standing for when you told the Worry 'not now'?"). The therapist zigzags between the two landscapes, so that each new event acquires meaning and each new meaning calls up further events from the past. Gradually a thin description ("lazy, useless") gives way to a thick one ("someone who loves learning when he is not being judged, who cares about his family, and who has started to stand up to the Worry"). Figure 6.8 pictures the process.

Narrative therapists also make sure that the new story has an audience, because identities are sustained socially, not privately. In re-membering conversations, a term taken from the anthropologist Barbara Myerhoff, the client reviews the "membership" of his life, giving more weight to the voices that support his preferred identity and less to those that do not ("Who in your life would be least surprised to hear that you taught your cousin algebra?" "My grandfather. He always said I explained things well"). In definitional ceremonies, also named after Myerhoff, the client tells his story to outsider witnesses: people invited to listen (family members, friends, or others who have faced similar problems) who then retell what caught their attention, what image of the person it evoked, what it touched in their own lives and where it has moved them, before the client in turn responds to their retelling. And the new story is written down. Epston became known for therapeutic letters, sent after sessions, which summarise the client's own words and the unique outcomes; clients reread them for years, and in one small survey clients rated a single letter as worth several sessions of face-to-face therapy. Certificates (for a child who has "beaten" Temper), declarations and "leagues" of people fighting the same problem, such as the anti-anorexia leagues that Epston and his colleagues helped to start, serve the same purpose.

Exam trap

Unique outcomes (narrative therapy) and exceptions (solution-focused therapy, below) both mean times when the problem did not have its way, and examiners swap them. The difference is in their use: a unique outcome is the entry point to a new story about identity, whereas an exception is studied as a practical blueprint ("do more of what works"). Re-authoring belongs to narrative therapy; reframing belongs to strategic and family therapy (Chapter 7); imagery rescripting belongs to cognitive and schema therapy (Chapter 5). The landscapes of action and identity come from Jerome Bruner, "unique outcome" from Erving Goffman, "thick description" from Clifford Geertz, and re-membering, definitional ceremonies and outsider witnesses from Barbara Myerhoff.

Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg and the solution-focused stance

Steve de Shazer (1940 to 2005) was a Milwaukee-born social worker who had trained first as a classical musician and played jazz saxophone. Insoo Kim Berg (1934 to 2007; some sources give her year of birth as 1937) was born in Korea, studied pharmacy in Seoul, came to the United States in 1957 to continue her studies, and turned to social work and family therapy. Both were influenced by the brief therapy of the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto, especially by John Weakland, and by Milton Erickson. They married, and in 1978, with a group of colleagues, they founded the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, where solution-focused brief therapy was developed over the following decade, largely by watching sessions through a one-way mirror and asking what had happened just before clients began to improve.

What they found reversed a long tradition. Therapists had always assumed that to solve a problem you must first understand it: its causes, its history, its function. The Milwaukee team noticed that the solutions their clients found often bore no obvious relation to the problems, and that clients improved when the conversation turned to times when things were better and to what they wanted instead. De Shazer put it in a metaphor in Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy (1985). A client's complaint is like a lock on a door. The therapist does not need to study the lock in detail, only to find a key that fits, and some "skeleton keys" fit a great many locks. Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) can be defined as a brief, future-oriented, goal-directed approach that helps clients construct solutions by describing the future they prefer and by finding and building on exceptions and existing resources, rather than by analysing the problem. Courses of therapy are short, often only a handful of sessions, and a single session is sometimes enough.

Remember

The central philosophy of SFBT is three rules. If it isn't broken, don't fix it. Once you know what works, do more of it. If it doesn't work, don't do it again; do something different. Add to these the assumptions that change is constant and inevitable, that small changes lead to larger ones, that no problem happens all the time, that the client is the expert on his own life and goals, and that the future is created and negotiable rather than fixed by the past.

In practice this means a deliberate shift from problem talk to solution talk. The therapist listens respectfully to the complaint, but she spends most of the session asking what the client wants, what is already working and what the next small step would be. Goals matter a great deal, and solution-focused therapists help clients shape well-formed goals: goals that are important to the client; small, concrete and described in behaviour that others could see; stated as the presence of something rather than the absence of something ("We will be talking at dinner" rather than "We won't fight"); seen as the start of something rather than the end of it; and realistic. Change may even be under way before the first session. Michele Weiner-Davis, de Shazer and Wallace Gingerich reported in 1987 that about two-thirds of clients, when asked what changes they had noticed since they called to make the appointment, described some pre-session change, and solution-focused therapists often begin by asking about it and building on it.

The Milwaukee team noticed, too, that clients arrive in different relationships to change, and de Shazer (1988) described three. In a visitor relationship the client has no complaint of his own: he has been sent by a court, a school or a worried family, and has simply come to visit. The therapist compliments him for coming, explores what he might want (perhaps only "to get them off my back") and gives no task. In a complainant relationship the client describes a problem but sees the solution as lying with someone else ("My son must change"), or does not yet see himself as able to act; the therapist compliments him and may suggest an observation task, such as noticing the times when things go a little better. In a customer relationship the client sees himself as part of the solution and is ready to act, and now behavioural tasks fit. These labels describe the relationship at a given moment, not a fixed trait of the client, and the relationship can shift within a single session. (The parallel with the stages of change of the transtheoretical model, Section 6.9, is close but not exact.)

Exam trap

SFBT was founded by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg at the Brief Family Therapy Center, Milwaukee (1978). It is not the brief therapy of the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto (Paul Watzlawick, John Weakland, Richard Fisch), which is problem-focused: it asks which attempted solution is keeping the problem going and prescribes something different (Chapter 7). The contrast is neat: the MRI looks at what is not working and stops it; SFBT looks at what is working and does more of it. Nor is SFBT one of the brief psychodynamic therapies of Malan, Sifneos, Davanloo and Mann (Chapter 3). Bill O'Hanlon's solution-oriented therapy is a close cousin. Visitor, complainant and customer describe relationships, not types of client.

The solution-focused questions

Solution-focused therapy is conducted almost entirely through questions, and examiners love them. The most famous is the miracle question, which de Shazer published in Clues: Investigating Solutions in Brief Therapy (1988). According to the story told at the Milwaukee centre, it was born when an overwhelmed client told Berg that perhaps only a miracle would help, and Berg took her at her word. In its standard form it runs like this:

Suppose that one night, while you are asleep, a miracle happens and the problems we have been talking about disappear. Because you were asleep, you do not know that the miracle has happened. When you wake up, what will be the first signs for you that a miracle has happened?

The question is asked slowly, and the answer is followed up in detail. "What else would be different? What would you be doing instead? Who would be the first to notice? What would your mother see you doing that would tell her?" The miracle question does three things at once. It moves the conversation out of the problem and into a preferred future. It produces goals, described in concrete, observable behaviour, often small and surprisingly ordinary ("I would get up when the alarm rings and make tea"). And it builds hope, because the client finds himself describing a life that he can almost see. If a client answers with a fantasy ("I would win the lottery"), the therapist accepts it lightly and asks what difference that would make, or asks the question again in a more modest form.

Exception questions ask about times when the problem was absent, less severe or better handled, and about what was different then: "Tell me about a recent time when the arguments did not happen. What was different? What did you do?" They often follow the miracle question directly: "When has a small piece of this miracle already happened?" Exceptions show the client that the problem does not happen all the time and that he already knows part of the solution. The therapist distinguishes deliberate exceptions, which the client brought about and can repeat ("I went for a walk first"), from spontaneous ones, which simply happened. For spontaneous exceptions the client may be asked to predict each night whether the next day will be a better one, and then to notice what made the difference.

Scaling questions ask the client to rate something on a scale, usually from 0 to 10, and then use the number as a lever: "On a scale where 10 is the day after the miracle and 0 is the worst things have ever been, where are you today?" Whatever the answer, the next questions are about progress and small steps, not about the gap. "Three. What puts you at three and not at zero?" (which draws out resources and exceptions). "What will be happening when you are at four?" (which defines the next small step). "What would your best friend notice you doing differently at four?" The same device can scale confidence ("How confident are you, from 0 to 10, that you can do this?") and willingness to work.

Sometimes a client is so hopeless that he can find no exceptions and cannot imagine a miracle. Then the therapist uses coping questions, which turn attention to how the client has survived: "With everything that has been happening, how have you managed to keep going? How do you get yourself out of bed in the morning? How come things are not even worse?" The question takes the client's distress seriously while quietly uncovering strengths, and Berg showed how much a single coping question can do. A young mother from a harsh family background was asked how she had worked out that she wanted to be a different kind of mother to her baby than her own mother had been to her. Nobody had taught her, she said; she had watched other people, read magazines and thought about it all the time. The worker's reply, "You are a very thoughtful person", was a compliment built entirely from the client's own evidence.

Compliments, in fact, are a technique in their own right. Solution-focused therapists give direct compliments, based on what the client has actually done ("You came today even though you had a night shift"); indirect compliments, through questions that imply a strength ("How did you manage to do that?" "What would your sister say you are good at?"); and invitations to self-compliment, in which the client is asked to describe his own successes. Toward the end of a first session the therapist may set the formula first session task, which de Shazer described in 1985: "Between now and next time we meet, I would like you to observe, so that you can describe to me next time, what happens in your life (or family, or marriage) that you want to continue to have happen." Notice the wording. It asks not whether something good happens but what happens, which presupposes that it will. Many solution-focused therapists also take a short break near the end of each session, often to consult colleagues behind the one-way mirror, and then deliver a brief message in three parts: compliments, a bridging statement that links them to the client's goals, and a task. Later sessions open not with "How have you been?" but with "What is better?", and the therapist follows each improvement through four steps that Peter De Jong and Berg summarised as EARS: elicit the improvement, amplify it with detailed questions, reinforce it with compliments, and start again with "What else is better?" Exam trap

The miracle question is the signature of solution-focused brief therapy (de Shazer and Berg), not of psychoanalysis, Gestalt therapy or classical CBT. It has two ancestors that examiners occasionally use as bait: Alfred Adler's "The Question" ("What would you do if you were well?"), which Adler used mainly to judge what purpose a symptom served (Chapter 3), and Milton Erickson's "crystal ball" technique, in which a hypnotised client imagined a future in which the problem had been solved. Keep the SFBT questions apart: exception questions look back at times when the problem was absent; the miracle question looks forward; scaling measures progress, with 10 as the goal (unlike Wolpe's subjective units of distress in Chapter 4, where 100 is maximum distress); coping questions are for the hopeless client. Compliments are a deliberate technique, not mere politeness.

Stories and solutions compared, and the evidence

Narrative therapy and SFBT are often taught together, and they share a great deal: a social constructionist philosophy, respect for the client's expertise, a refusal to pathologise, close attention to language, and a search for the times when the problem does not have its way. But they are not the same therapy, as Table 6.16 shows. Narrative therapy is interested in the problem (its history, its tactics, its effects and the cultural ideas that support it) and it is deeply interested in identity and meaning. SFBT is minimalist and pragmatic. It deliberately spends as little time as possible on the problem, does not ask what the problem means, and judges success by whether the client is doing more of what works.

How well do they work? SFBT has the larger body of research. In 2000 Wallace Gingerich and Sheri Eisengart reviewed fifteen controlled outcome studies and found preliminary support for the approach, strongest in the five best-controlled trials. A meta-analysis by Johnny Kim (2008) found effects that were small but positive, clearest for internalising problems such as depression and anxiety. A later review by Gingerich and Lance Peterson (2013) of 43 controlled studies found that about three-quarters reported significant benefits, and several suggested that SFBT reached outcomes comparable to other treatments in fewer sessions. Narrative therapy has a thinner evidence base: mainly case studies, qualitative research and small trials, for example with depressed adults and with children and families, which are encouraging but not conclusive. Some narrative practitioners have been reluctant to measure outcomes with standardised scales, which, they argue, embody the very expert knowledge that the approach questions.

Critics of SFBT call it "problem-phobic": a client who is pushed into solution talk before he feels heard may feel dismissed, and Eve Lipchik, one of the founding members of the Milwaukee team, herself argued for giving emotion and the therapeutic relationship more room. Its brevity and simplicity can look superficial, it says little about why problems arise, and it is not on its own a treatment for severe mental illness. Critics of narrative therapy point to its vagueness, to political commitments that not every client shares, and to its weak evidence base. Defenders of both reply that they are respectful, strengths-based, collaborative and brief; that they travel well across cultures because they work with the client's own language and meanings; and that they are especially useful in schools, in community and social work, and wherever a clinician has only one or two contacts with a client. Both have been widely taken up in family work (Section 7.7), and methods descended from narrative practice, such as the Tree of Life exercise, which invites people to describe their roots, skills, hopes and relationships through the parts of a tree, have been used with communities affected by poverty, conflict and illness in many countries.

Clinical link

A solution-focused stance never replaces a risk assessment. Lakshmi's low mood, or a client's remark that "only a miracle can help", must still prompt careful questions about hopelessness and suicidal thinking (Chapter 2); coping questions can then be used within that assessment ("What has stopped you from acting on these thoughts?"). In the same way, narrative externalising should never separate a person from responsibility for violence or abuse. Used with these cautions, both approaches suit the brief, often single, contacts of busy outpatient clinics, school counselling and crisis work, and both suit clients who resist being labelled. The next section turns to another brief approach built on Rogers's listening, but aimed squarely at a client's ambivalence about change: motivational interviewing.

6.9 Motivational Interviewing and the Stages of ChangeHigh yield · 7 Qs

Every clinician meets him sooner or later: a man of forty-six who drinks most evenings, brought to the clinic by a worried wife and a physician's letter about his liver. Or she is a young woman with diabetes who knows that she should walk every evening and does not. The therapies in this chapter so far assume a client who has come for help. What do you do with a person who has come, or been brought, but is not at all sure that he wants to change? For most of the twentieth century the answer in the addictions field was blunt: break down his denial. Motivational interviewing began as a deliberate rejection of that answer, and the transtheoretical model, which grew up beside it, gave clinicians a map of where such a person stands on the road to change.

From confrontation to conversation

In the 1960s and 1970s American alcohol treatment was dominated by a confrontational style. Problem drinkers were assumed to share a characteristic defence called denial, and the counsellor's job (the counsellor was often a recovering alcoholic) was to attack it until the client admitted that he was an alcoholic. In some therapeutic communities for drug users, such as Synanon in California, sessions of harsh group attack were a central ritual. If the client argued back, that only proved how deep his denial went.

William R. Miller, a young psychologist at the University of New Mexico, began to doubt all this. In a trial of behaviour therapy for problem drinkers that he published with two colleagues in 1980, the different behavioural methods made little difference to outcome, but something the researchers had not set out to study made a great deal. Each counsellor's accurate empathy had been rated during treatment, and those ratings predicted how much the counsellor's clients were drinking six months later, accounting for about two thirds of the variation in outcome. The way the counsellor listened seemed to matter more than the technique the counsellor delivered.

In 1982 Miller spent a sabbatical at the Hjellestad Clinic near Bergen, in Norway, where a group of young psychologists asked him to show them how he worked. They role-played their own clients, Miller responded, and they kept stopping him to ask why he had said what he said. For the first time he had to put into words rules he had been following without ever stating them, and he wrote them up as "Motivational interviewing with problem drinkers", published in Behavioural Psychotherapy in 1983. Stephen Rollnick, a clinical psychologist working in Wales, had meanwhile begun to use and teach the approach in Britain. The two met in Australia in 1989, and their book Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior appeared in 1991. The second edition (2002) widened the scope beyond addiction; the third, subtitled Helping People Change (2013), rebuilt the method around four processes and introduced most of the terms examiners now use; a fourth followed in 2023. So the approach has two founding dates: 1983 for Miller's first paper and 1991 for the first book with Rollnick.

In the 2013 edition Miller and Rollnick defined motivational interviewing (MI), in everyday language, as "a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation and commitment to change". An earlier definition (Rollnick and Miller, 1995) put the paradox at its heart more sharply: a directive, client-centred counselling style for eliciting behaviour change by helping clients to explore and resolve ambivalence.

At the heart of both definitions is ambivalence, feeling two ways about the same thing at the same time: "I want to stop drinking, and I don't want to stop drinking." Miller and Rollnick insisted that it is neither pathology nor denial but the normal state of a person considering a change, and the place where people get stuck. Listen to an ambivalent man thinking aloud and you hear him argue with himself: "I should cut down, my liver test was bad ... but it's the only way I relax ... but my daughter's wedding is next year ... but everyone in the office drinks." Now notice what happens when a helper takes one side of that argument. If the helper argues for change ("You must stop; your liver is in danger"), the client voices the other side ("My uncle drank all his life"). Because people tend to become more convinced of what they hear themselves say (an idea Miller and Rollnick drew from Daryl Bem's self-perception theory, taught in the Social Psychology book), the helper's well-meant arguments end up strengthening the case for not changing. Miller and Rollnick called the helper's urge to fix, warn and persuade the righting reflex. Learning MI begins with learning to resist it.

The word directive is what separates MI from Rogers. A person-centred therapist follows the client wherever he goes, with no preference for one outcome over another (Section 6.2). An MI practitioner has a direction, toward a particular change such as drinking less or taking medicines regularly. He uses Rogers's warmth and listening skills, but reflects selectively, giving more attention to the client's arguments for change than to his arguments against it, and asks questions whose likely answers are reasons for change. Miller and Rollnick called this a guiding style, halfway between directing (telling people what to do) and following (simply going where they go): the style of a mountain guide, who neither drags the climber up the slope nor sits waiting at the bottom. Selective reflection used with respect helps a person hear his own reasons; used without respect, it would be manipulation. That is why MI's attitude, its spirit, matters as much as its skills.

Key study: the more the counsellor confronted, the more the client drank (Miller, Benefield and Tonigan, 1993)

Question. Is a problem drinker's "denial" a trait he brings with him, or is it partly produced by the way the helper talks? Method. William Miller, Gary Benefield and Scott Tonigan, in New Mexico, offered 42 problem drinkers a "Drinker's Check-up": an assessment followed by a session of personal feedback about the results. Drinkers were randomly assigned to receive the feedback from counsellors using a directive, confrontational style or from counsellors using a client-centred, motivational style, or to a waiting-list control. The sessions were recorded and coded. Results. Both check-ups reduced drinking compared with waiting (overall by more than half within six weeks, a gain maintained at one year), and the two styles did not differ significantly in their overall effect on drinking. But the confrontational style provoked much more resistance in the session (arguing, interrupting, denying that there was a problem), and resistance predicted poorer outcomes. Most strikingly, a single counsellor behaviour predicted drinking a year later: the more the counsellor confronted, the more the client drank. Conclusion. Resistance is not simply a property of the client; it is a signal about the interaction, and the counsellor's style can raise or lower it. Criticism. The sample was small, the outcome measures relied largely on what the drinkers reported, and a research version of confrontation may not match what happens in real treatment programmes.

The distractors are real names from neighbouring topics. Prochaska and DiClemente created the transtheoretical (stages of change) model, taught later in this section, which is often paired with MI but is a different thing. Beck (cognitive therapy), Wolpe (systematic desensitisation) and Jung (analytical psychology) have nothing to do with it. And remember where MI was born: in the treatment of problem drinking.

The spirit of motivational interviewing

Practitioners who learned MI's techniques without its underlying attitude, Miller and Rollnick found, produced something that looked like MI and did not work. So they described the attitude itself, which they called the spirit of motivational interviewing. In the 2002 edition it had three elements, each defined against its opposite: collaboration rather than confrontation, evocation rather than education, and autonomy rather than authority. In 2013 it became four elements, which trainers remember by the acronym PACE (Table 6.17).

  • Partnership. MI is done "for and with" a person, not "on" or "to" him: a collaboration between two experts, the clinician who knows about alcohol and livers and the client who knows about his own life, values and reasons. Miller and Rollnick compared it to dancing rather than wrestling.
  • Acceptance. This element comes almost straight from Rogers and has four parts: absolute worth (Rogers's unconditional positive regard), accurate empathy, autonomy support (honouring the person's irrevocable right to choose his own course, including the choice not to change) and affirmation (seeking out and acknowledging strengths and efforts). Acceptance is not approval: you can accept a man fully without approving of his drinking.
  • Compassion. Added in 2013, compassion means actively promoting the other person's welfare. It is the safeguard that stops MI skills being used to sell a product or to steer someone into a change that serves the helper rather than the client.
  • Evocation. People already have within them much of the motivation and many of the resources they need; the clinician's task is to draw these out, not to install them. This principle of evocation is what most clearly distinguishes MI from advice-giving.

A different PACE (playfulness, acceptance, curiosity, empathy) belongs to Daniel Hughes's attachment-focused work with children, not to MI.

The four processes and the core skills

In 2013 Miller and Rollnick described four processes. Engaging is establishing a helpful connection and a working relationship; a client who feels judged in the first ten minutes may never come back. Focusing is agreeing on a specific direction, the change to be discussed; in a health setting there may be several candidates (drinking, smoking, diet, medicines), and the two choose together. (This focusing has nothing to do with Gendlin's focusing, Section 6.7.) Evoking is drawing out the client's own motivations for change; it is the heart of MI and what makes it different from other approaches. Planning is developing commitment and a concrete plan of action, when, and only when, the client is ready. The processes are sequential but recursive, like a staircase on which each step rests on the one below and on which you sometimes step back down: if a client turns defensive during planning, the wise move is to return to engaging. Evoking without engaging feels like pressure, and planning without evoking produces a plan the client will not follow.

Within every process the practitioner relies on four communication skills, remembered as OARS. You met most of them as interviewing skills in Chapter 2 (Section 2.2); in MI they are used with a strategic purpose.

  • Open questions invite the client to think and talk ("What concerns you about your drinking?") rather than answer yes or no. The most useful are evocative questions whose natural answer is a reason for change.
  • An affirmation is a genuine statement recognising the client's strengths, efforts or values: "You care a great deal about being a good father." It is about the client, not about the counsellor's approval; "I'm proud of you" quietly casts the counsellor as judge.
  • Reflections are the core skill. A reflection is a statement, not a question; the voice goes down at the end ("You're worried about your liver"). A simple reflection repeats or rephrases what the client said. A complex reflection adds meaning or emphasis: it guesses at what the client means but has not said, names a feeling, or "continues the paragraph" by voicing the client's likely next sentence. Skilled practitioners offer at least two reflections for every question.
  • Summaries pull together several things the client has said. A collecting summary gathers his statements about change and hands them back, like flowers gathered into a bouquet; linking and transitional summaries connect themes and move the conversation on.

The third edition added a fifth skill, informing and advising, on one condition: ask permission first. The recommended format is elicit-provide-elicit: elicit what the person already knows or wants to know ("What do you know about how alcohol affects the liver?"), provide information briefly and neutrally, then elicit his reaction ("What do you make of that?"). Information handed over this way is heard as information; the same facts delivered as a lecture are heard as an attack.

A worked example shows the skills in action. Ramesh, a 46-year-old bank clerk in Mysuru, has been sent by his physician after a blood test showed raised liver enzymes. His wife sits in the waiting room.

  • Counsellor: "Dr Rao asked you to come and see me. What would you like us to talk about today?" (Open question.)
  • Ramesh: "Honestly, I came because my wife made me. Everyone is making a big issue out of a few drinks."
  • Counsellor: "It feels as if everyone has ganged up on you." (Complex reflection of feeling; the counsellor does not argue.)
  • Ramesh: "Exactly. I work hard all day. Two or three pegs in the evening is how I relax."
  • Counsellor: "The evening drink is the one part of the day that is just for you. And at the same time, you agreed to come here today." (Double-sided reflection, explained below.)
  • Ramesh: "Well ... the liver report did worry me. My father had liver trouble. He died at fifty-eight. I don't want my children going through what we went through."
  • Counsellor: "Your children matter enormously to you." (Affirmation and complex reflection.)
  • Ramesh: "Yes. Maybe I could stop on weekdays. I don't know if I can, though."
  • Counsellor: "Let me see if I have this right. You came because your wife insisted, and it feels as if people are making too much of it. A drink helps you unwind. At the same time the liver report worried you, you remember your father, and you don't want that for your children. You're even wondering about stopping on weekdays, though you're not sure you could. What else?" (Collecting summary.)

The counsellor never argued, warned, labelled Ramesh an alcoholic or told him what to do. Every reason for change in that conversation came out of Ramesh's own mouth.

Before 2013 the method was taught through general principles rather than processes, and these still appear in exam questions. The 1991 book listed five: express empathy, develop discrepancy, avoid argumentation, roll with resistance and support self-efficacy; the 2002 edition folded avoiding argumentation into rolling with resistance, leaving four. In developing discrepancy the practitioner helps the client see the gap between his present behaviour and his own important goals and values ("You want to be well for your daughter's wedding, and you're drinking more than you'd like"); motivation grows as the gap becomes vivid, provided the client also believes change is possible. The principle of rolling with resistance means not opposing the client's arguments against change but responding in ways that let him reconsider (the next subsection shows how). Supporting self-efficacy, a concept taken from Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021), means building the client's belief in his own ability to carry out the change; a client who sees the danger but believes he cannot change falls back on excuses. For busy health settings, Rollnick, Miller and Christopher Butler (2008) condensed the principles into RULE: resist the righting reflex, understand the patient's own motivations, listen with empathy and empower the patient. Miller and Rollnick (2002) also named the commonest traps a helper falls into: the question-answer trap (a string of closed questions that makes the client passive), the confrontation-denial trap (taking sides), the expert trap, the labelling trap, the premature focus trap and the blaming trap.

Exam trap

OARS stands for open questions, affirmations, reflections (reflective listening) and summaries. The R is never reframing, rewards or responsibility, and the S is never support, suggestion or sanctions; "outcomes, assessment, rewards, sanctions" and "openness, acceptance, responsibility, support" are invented sets. The skills (OARS, plus informing with permission) are used across all four processes; the processes are engaging, focusing, evoking and planning, in that order. If an older question asks for the principles, they are express empathy, develop discrepancy, roll with resistance and support self-efficacy (with "avoid argumentation" as a fifth in 1991). In MI a reflection is a statement, not a question.

Change talk, sustain talk and discord

The single most important thing an MI practitioner listens for is a particular kind of speech. The client's change talk is any speech that favours movement toward change ("I'd like to cut down", "My children need a healthy father"); his sustain talk is the opposite, his own arguments for keeping things as they are ("I need a drink to sleep"). Both come out of ambivalence, often in the same breath. In the evoking process the practitioner draws out change talk, responds to it so that the client says more, and responds to sustain talk without strengthening it, because the balance between the two in a session predicts what the client will do afterwards.

Miller and Rollnick, working with the psycholinguist Paul Amrhein, divided change talk into two kinds, remembered as DARN-CAT. Preparatory change talk expresses the motivation that comes before a decision: Desire ("I want to ..."), Ability ("I could ..."), Reasons ("I would feel better if ...") and Need ("I have to ..."). Mobilising change talk signals movement toward action: Commitment ("I will ..."), Activation ("I'm ready to ...") and Taking steps ("This week I didn't buy a bottle"). Each kind has a mirror image in sustain talk, as Table 6.18 shows.

Key study: the language of commitment (Amrhein, Miller and colleagues, 2003)

Question. Does what clients say during an MI session predict what they do afterwards? Method. Paul Amrhein, William Miller and their colleagues analysed video recordings of MI sessions with 84 drug users, coding every client statement about change for its type (desire, ability, reasons, need, readiness, commitment) and its strength, minute by minute through the session, and related the patterns to drug use over the following year. Results. Desire, ability, reasons and need did not predict outcome directly. What predicted outcome was the strength of commitment language, especially toward the end of the session; desire, ability, reasons and need, in turn, predicted how strong commitment became. The researchers also noticed that some clients' commitment language fell away at the very end of the session, at the point where the protocol required the counsellor to complete a change plan, and these clients did worse. Conclusion. Preparatory change talk builds toward commitment, and commitment talk predicts change; pushing a plan on a client who is not yet ready can undo the good work of the session. Criticism. The findings are correlational: commitment talk may simply reveal motivation that already existed, rather than create it.

The simplest way to evoke change talk is to ask for it with evocative questions shaped around DARN: "What would you like to be different?" "If you did decide to cut down, how might you go about it?" "What are the three best reasons for making this change?" A widely used tool is the importance ruler, a 0 to 10 scale on which the client rates how important the change is to him, often paired with a confidence ruler for how confident he is that he could make it. Suppose Ramesh rates importance at 4. The counsellor does not ask "Why only 4?", which invites sustain talk, but "Why 4 and not 1?", whose answer is change talk ("Because of the liver report and my children"), and then "What would it take to move you from 4 to 6?" (The rulers resemble the scaling questions of solution-focused therapy, Section 6.8, but here they evoke reasons for change.) Other strategies are querying extremes ("What is the worst that could happen if you carried on as you are?"), looking back to life before the problem, looking forward to life after the change, and exploring the client's values to find what the behaviour conflicts with. When change talk appears, the practitioner responds with EARS: asking the client to elaborate, affirming, reflecting and summarising, so that he hears his own reasons again.

One caution concerns the decisional balance, the familiar exercise of listing the pros and cons of changing and of not changing, which comes from Irving Janis and Leon Mann's work on decision making and is part of the transtheoretical model (later in this section). Because it asks the client to argue both sides equally, it draws out sustain talk as well as change talk. Miller and Rollnick therefore advised against using it routinely when the aim is to help someone move toward change, and kept it mainly for situations in which the counsellor deliberately wants to stay neutral, such as helping a woman decide whether to continue a pregnancy.

What became of the old idea of resistance? In 2013 Miller and Rollnick dropped the word, because it seemed to blame the client, and replaced it with two ideas. Sustain talk, as you have seen, concerns the target behaviour and is the normal voice of ambivalence. The second idea, discord, concerns the relationship: arguing, interrupting, discounting or ignoring the counsellor. It is a signal that the counsellor has done something (pushed, lectured, labelled) that the client experiences as a threat to his freedom. Both are met by "rolling with" them rather than opposing them, with responses worth knowing by name:

  • A simple reflection of the sustain talk, which often lets the client hear it and soften it himself.
  • An amplified reflection, which reflects the statement in a slightly exaggerated form, without a trace of sarcasm. Ramesh: "My drinking doesn't affect anyone." Counsellor: "Nobody at all is affected by it." Ramesh, usually: "Well, my wife does get upset."
  • A double-sided reflection, which reflects both sides of the ambivalence: "On the one hand a drink helps you relax, and on the other hand you're worried about your liver." The change side goes second, because people respond to what they hear last.
  • Emphasising autonomy ("It really is your decision; nobody can make it for you"), since people defend their freedom less when it is acknowledged.
  • Reframing, which offers a new meaning ("My wife nags me" becomes "She seems to care a great deal about what happens to you"), and agreeing with a twist, agreement followed by a reframe.
  • Shifting focus away from a stuck point, and coming alongside, siding with the sustain talk ("Maybe drinking is so important to you that it's worth the risk to your liver"), which often prompts the client to take up the other side himself.
Exam trap

DARN (desire, ability, reasons, need) is preparatory change talk; CAT (commitment, activation, taking steps) is mobilising change talk. Commitment language is the best predictor of change (Amrhein and colleagues, 2003). Sustain talk is the client's own speech in favour of the status quo; discord is friction in the relationship. These two replaced the older term "resistance" in 2013, so "rolling with resistance" in an older question corresponds to responding to sustain talk and discord without opposing them. An amplified reflection must never be sarcastic, and in a double-sided reflection the change side comes last. A full pros-and-cons decisional balance is a transtheoretical model tool that MI now uses with caution.

Where motivational interviewing is used, and how well it works

MI's most established uses remain in the addictions where it was born: alcohol, illicit drugs and, more recently, tobacco. From there it spread to almost every situation in which a person's own behaviour affects his health: diet and physical activity, diabetes self-care, medication adherence (in HIV, hypertension and severe mental illness), safer sex, gambling, people with both a mental illness and a substance problem, and offenders. It is also used as a short prelude to other treatments, to build the motivation a client needs to tolerate them; a few sessions of MI before cognitive-behaviour therapy for generalised anxiety, for example, have been found to improve outcomes for some clients.

One adaptation has its own name: motivational enhancement therapy (MET), which combines the MI style with structured, personalised feedback from an assessment (how much the person drinks compared with others of the same age and sex, what his test results show), in a small number of sessions. It was developed for Project MATCH, the largest psychotherapy trial ever conducted in the alcohol field (1997), in which four sessions of MET produced outcomes broadly similar to twelve sessions of cognitive-behavioural coping skills therapy and twelve of twelve-step facilitation (Section 8.8). MI also shaped the brief interventions of a few minutes to an hour, given in primary care or emergency departments to people drinking at risky levels, whose effective ingredients were summarised in the early 1990s as FRAMES: Feedback of personal risk, emphasis on the client's Responsibility, clear Advice to change, a Menu of options, an Empathic style and support for Self-efficacy.

Does it work? The first major meta-analysis, by Brian Burke, Hal Arkowitz and Marisa Menchola in 2003, pooled 30 controlled trials of adaptations of MI. MI was as effective as other active treatments, and better than no treatment or placebo, for problems involving alcohol, drugs, and diet and exercise (including trials with obese women with Type 2 diabetes), but the trials then available did not support its efficacy for smoking or HIV-risk behaviour (Section 9.4). A review of 72 trials by Jennifer Hettema, Julie Steele and Miller (2005) found that effects varied enormously from one study, site and counsellor to another, so the quality of delivery matters, and that effects were larger with clients from ethnic minority groups. A meta-analysis of 119 studies by Brad Lundahl and colleagues (2010) found a small but reliable advantage over no treatment or weak comparisons, and results comparable to other active treatments. MI is not a treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder, which needs exposure and response prevention (Chapter 4), nor a primary treatment for personality disorders or bipolar disorder, though MI-style conversations are used to support medication adherence in all of these. In 2009, worried by misunderstandings, Miller and Rollnick published "Ten things that motivational interviewing is not": it is not based on the transtheoretical model, not a way of tricking people, not a technique, not the decisional balance, not dependent on assessment feedback, not a form of cognitive-behaviour therapy, not just client-centred counselling, not easy, not what most practitioners were already doing, and not a panacea.

Clinical link

In India the person with a drinking problem is very often brought by the family, and at home the family has usually tried every version of the righting reflex already: pleading, scolding, hiding the money, taking vows at the temple. The MI stance (respect, curiosity, the client's own reasons) can feel surprising to such a client, and that surprise is often where engagement begins. Families can be helped too. Miller, Robert Meyers and Scott Tonigan (1999) compared three ways of helping family members of drinkers who refused treatment. With Community Reinforcement and Family Training (CRAFT), which teaches relatives to reward sober behaviour, let natural consequences happen and invite the drinker into treatment at a good moment, 64 percent of the drinkers entered treatment; with a confrontational family "intervention" the figure was 30 percent, and with referral to Al-Anon, a self-help group for families, 13 percent. Gentle, well-timed invitations worked better than ambushes.

Exam trap

"Motivational interviewing is used in the management of" has the keyed answer substance abuse (IHBAS 2020), because that is where MI was developed and where its evidence is strongest. "Non-compliance to treatment" is tempting, and MI is indeed used to improve adherence, but it is a later extension; anxious-avoidant personality disorder and bipolar disorder are not its targets. For Burke, Arkowitz and Menchola (2003), the problems studied were alcohol, drugs, diet and exercise (diabetes-related trials included), smoking and HIV risk (studied but not supported); obsessive-compulsive disorder was not among them (RML 2022). Note the third author's name, Menchola, often misspelt in papers.

The transtheoretical model: stages and processes of change

At about the same time, another American psychologist was asking a different question: not how a helper should talk, but how people change at all, with or without a helper. James O. Prochaska, at the University of Rhode Island, had published Systems of Psychotherapy: A Transtheoretical Analysis in 1979, a comparison of the leading schools of therapy, and concluded that beneath their different theories they relied on a small number of common processes of change. Because the framework cut across all the theories, he called it transtheoretical. With Carlo C. DiClemente he then turned to the people who change most often without any therapy at all: smokers who quit on their own.

Key study: how smokers change on their own (Prochaska and DiClemente, 1983)

Question. Do people who stop smoking by themselves use the same processes as people in therapy, and do they use them all at once or at different times? Method. Prochaska and DiClemente gave questionnaires to several hundred smokers and former smokers who were at different points in changing, from those not yet thinking about quitting, through those thinking about it and those who had recently quit, to those who had stayed quit and those who had relapsed, and asked how often they used each of ten processes of change. Results. The use of the processes varied systematically with the person's position: people thinking about quitting relied most on becoming aware of the problem and re-evaluating themselves, while people who had recently quit relied on the behavioural processes, such as rewarding themselves, substituting other behaviours and seeking support. Conclusion. Change is not a single event but a movement through stages, and different processes help at different stages. Criticism. The study was cross-sectional (it compared different people rather than following the same people through time), relied on self-report and was limited to smoking.

From that beginning grew the transtheoretical model (TTM), also called the stages of change model, which Prochaska, DiClemente and later John C. Norcross elaborated over the following decades and popularised in Changing for Good (1994). It has four parts: the stages of change (when people change), the processes of change (how they change), the decisional balance and self-efficacy (what moves them), and the levels of change (what is to be changed). The stages are the part everyone remembers. The commonly taught version has five, with a sixth added later, each defined by the person's intentions and behaviour over a fixed period (Table 6.19 and Figure 6.9).

In precontemplation the person does not intend to change in the next six months. He may be unaware of the problem, demoralised by failed attempts or simply unpersuaded, and precontemplation is not the same as denial. DiClemente later described four kinds of precontemplator, the "four Rs": the reluctant, held back by lack of information or inertia; the rebellious, heavily invested in the behaviour and in making their own decisions; the resigned, who have given up hope; and the rationalising, who have every answer about why it is not a problem. None of them is helped by a lecture. In contemplation the person is seriously thinking about changing within six months but has not committed; this is ambivalence in its purest form, and some smokers in Prochaska's studies stayed there for years (chronic contemplation, or behavioural procrastination). In preparation the person intends to act within the next month and has taken small steps, such as telling his family, buying nicotine gum or (in the definition used for smokers) making at least one quit attempt of a day or more in the past year. In older versions of the model, including the "wheel of change" that Miller and Rollnick printed in 1991, this stage was called determination.

In the action stage the person has visibly changed the behaviour within the past six months, to a criterion that experts agree reduces risk: for smoking, total abstinence, not merely cutting down. Action is the busiest stage, and the one that traditional treatment programmes assumed everyone was in. In maintenance the change has lasted more than six months (up to about five years) and the person works to prevent relapse; temptation is lower and confidence higher than in action, but the risk remains. Later versions added termination, in which the person feels no temptation in any situation and has complete confidence that he will not return to the old behaviour. Only a minority of people with addictions reach it, and for many problems maintenance is lifelong.

Where is relapse? Some early versions drew it as a stage of its own, and some textbooks still list it. In the modern model relapse is an event, a return from action or maintenance to an earlier stage, and Prochaska, DiClemente and Norcross (1992) found it to be the rule rather than the exception: most people who quit smoking relapse several times before they succeed. But relapse rarely sends a person back to the beginning. In their data on smokers, only about 15 percent of those who relapsed went all the way back to precontemplation; about 85 percent recycled to contemplation or preparation and tried again, usually having learned something. Hence the spiral model of change, in which a person can circle back without losing all the ground he has gained.

The second part of the model is the ten processes of change, the covert and overt activities people use to move through the stages (Table 6.20). Five are experiential (they work through thinking and feeling) and five are behavioural (they work through doing), and they work best when matched to the stage. Consciousness raising, dramatic relief and environmental re-evaluation help people move from precontemplation to contemplation; self-re-evaluation helps them move from contemplation to preparation; self-liberation (choosing and committing) carries them into action; and reinforcement management, helping relationships, counterconditioning and stimulus control sustain action and maintenance. Teaching a precontemplator how to avoid cues to smoke is like teaching someone to swim who has no intention of entering the water.

A worked example makes the matching concrete. Five men attend the tobacco cessation clinic of a district hospital on the same morning. Mahesh, a farmer who chews gutka, came only because his son insisted: a precontemplator, he needs engagement, a question about what he knows of gutka and mouth cancer, and an offer, with permission, to look together at the white patch inside his cheek (consciousness raising). Imran, a tailor, knows bidis are ruining his chest but cannot imagine his workday without them: a contemplator, he needs his ambivalence explored and his reasons evoked, perhaps through how his breathlessness affects his work and his children (self-re-evaluation, environmental re-evaluation). Suresh has decided to quit on his daughter's birthday next week and has told his family: in preparation, he needs a concrete plan, a choice among options such as nicotine replacement, and a firmer commitment (self-liberation). Joseph stopped a month ago and is struggling with cravings after meals: in action, he needs behavioural tools, such as removing lighters, a walk after dinner, rewards and a friend who has also quit (stimulus control, counterconditioning, reinforcement management, helping relationships). Ravi has not smoked for eighteen months but dreads a wedding season full of old smoking companions: in maintenance, he needs relapse prevention planning (Section 9.9). Five men, one behaviour, five different conversations.

What drives movement between stages? Prochaska borrowed the decisional balance from Irving Janis (1918 to 1990) and Leon Mann, whose 1977 book on decision making described how people weigh the gains and losses of a choice. In the TTM the pros of changing rise and the cons fall as people move through the stages, usually crossing over between contemplation and action. Across twelve problem behaviours Prochaska (1994) found that moving from precontemplation to action involved a rise in the pros of about one standard deviation but a fall in the cons of only about half a standard deviation (his "strong" and "weak" principles of progress): raising the benefits of change does more than attacking its costs. Alongside the decisional balance runs self-efficacy in Bandura's sense: confidence that one can keep the change in tempting situations rises across the stages, while temptation falls.

The fourth part, the levels of change, describes what is being changed. Prochaska and Norcross distinguished five increasingly deep and interrelated levels: symptoms or situational problems, maladaptive cognitions, current interpersonal conflicts, family or systems conflicts, and intrapersonal conflicts. Different schools suit different levels (behaviour therapy for symptoms, cognitive therapy for maladaptive thoughts, family therapy for family conflicts, and psychodynamic, Gestalt or existential therapy for intrapersonal conflicts), and transtheoretical therapists usually start at the most concrete level, the symptom, and go deeper only when needed. With "Mrs C", a mother with severe compulsive hand-washing, the therapist worked at all five: her washing, her thoughts about dirt, her relationships with her children, her own mother's rules about cleanliness, and her overcontrol of feelings.

How do MI and the TTM fit together? They grew up together in the 1980s among people who knew one another, and they complement each other neatly. The TTM tells the clinician where the client is and what kind of help is likely to be useful; MI tells the clinician how to talk with an ambivalent client, which describes most clients in precontemplation and contemplation. MI is therefore often called the method of choice for the early stages, and the action-oriented therapies (behaviour therapy, relapse prevention) for the later ones. But, as you saw, Miller and Rollnick insisted that MI is not based on the TTM; the two are separate ideas that happen to fit.

The TTM has been enormously popular in smoking cessation, alcohol treatment, exercise promotion and HIV prevention, because it is intuitive and frees clinicians from expecting every client to be ready for action. Its critics are equally vocal. In a widely read editorial in 2005 the British psychologist Robert West argued that it was time to put the model to rest. The boundaries between stages are arbitrary (why six months and not five?), the stages may not be truly distinct, and many smokers report moving from not thinking about quitting to quitting within a single day. Most important, systematic reviews of smoking cessation, such as that by Robert Riemsma and colleagues in 2003, found little evidence that interventions tailored to a person's stage work better than interventions that are not. The model survives as a useful clinical map, but not as a law of change.

Exam trap

The stages, in order, are precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance, with termination as a later sixth stage; Indian exam notes often list all six. "Determination" is the older name for preparation, and relapse is now treated as an event and a return to an earlier stage rather than a stage of its own. Know the time frames: no intention within six months (precontemplation), intention within six months (contemplation), within the next month (preparation), changed for less than six months (action), more than six months (maintenance). The model is Prochaska and DiClemente's (1983), not Miller and Rollnick's. Recognise models by their constructs: "precontemplation" and "processes of change" mean the transtheoretical model; "perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers" mean the health belief model; "attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioural control" mean the theory of planned behaviour; and "self-efficacy, outcome expectancies, observational learning" mean social cognitive theory (all compared in Section 9.2).

Motivational interviewing taught a hard lesson: the same assessment and the same advice, delivered with more or less confrontation, can leave one client drinking less and another drinking more. If that is true, then the skills of listening and responding are not a pleasant extra laid on top of "real" treatment. They are the working tools of every helper, whether the helper is a psychoanalyst, a behaviour therapist, a school counsellor or a nurse in a primary health centre. This last section of the chapter turns from schools of therapy to those tools. It asks first what counselling is and how it differs from psychotherapy, and then looks at the three best-known attempts to turn helping into a set of teachable skills: Allen Ivey's microskills hierarchy, Robert Carkhuff's helping model and Gerard Egan's skilled helper. The individual skills themselves (attending and SOLER, open and closed questions, minimal encouragers, paraphrasing, reflection of feeling, summarising, and primary and advanced empathy) were taught in Chapter 2 (Section 2.2), and this section builds on them rather than repeating them.

What counselling is

Few words in the helping professions are used as loosely as "counselling". A friend gives "counselling" over tea; a bank offers "debt counselling"; and in India the word is stretched further still, because the "counselling" for medical and engineering admissions is a process of allotting seats, not a helping conversation at all. In its professional sense the word means something much more specific. In 2010 a group of American counselling organisations agreed on a consensus definition: "Counseling is a professional relationship that empowers diverse individuals, families, and groups to accomplish mental health, wellness, education, and career goals." Put more fully, counselling is a professional helping relationship in which a trained person uses psychological knowledge and communication skills to help people understand themselves and their situation, resolve problems of living, make decisions and grow, usually with people whose everyday functioning is basically intact.

Counselling has a different family tree from clinical psychology. Clinical psychology grew out of mental testing and the psychiatric clinic (Chapter 1); counselling grew out of the guidance movement, the giving of information and advice to help people make educational and vocational choices. Its founder is usually taken to be Frank Parsons (1854 to 1908), a Boston social reformer who opened the Vocation Bureau in 1908 to help young people choose work. In his book Choosing a Vocation, published in 1909, the year after his death, Parsons set out three steps: a clear understanding of oneself (one's aptitudes, abilities, interests and limitations), a knowledge of the requirements and prospects of different kinds of work, and "true reasoning" on the relations between the two. This matching of person to job became known as the trait-and-factor approach, and Parsons is remembered as the father of vocational guidance. In the 1930s Edmund Griffith Williamson (1900 to 1979) at the University of Minnesota turned trait-and-factor guidance into a full directive counselling method, in which the counsellor analysed test data, diagnosed the problem and advised the student; his How to Counsel Students appeared in 1939. You met the other side of the story in Section 6.2: Rogers's Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942) challenged Williamson's directive method and turned counselling from advice-giving into a therapeutic relationship. After the Second World War the need to help returning soldiers find work and adjust to civilian life created a large demand for counsellors, and at a conference at Northwestern University in 1951 American psychologists adopted the title counselling psychologist for the new specialty. (How counselling psychology and clinical psychology differ as professions is discussed in Chapter 1; the areas of counselling, such as school, career, marital and grief counselling, and the theories of career counselling, are taught in Chapter 10.)

Counselling versus psychotherapy

Is counselling simply a lighter form of psychotherapy, or the same thing under another name? Examiners like the question because there is a traditional answer and a modern one, and you need both. The traditional answer draws a set of contrasts between guidance, counselling and psychotherapy, summarised in Table 6.21. Counselling, on this view, serves people who are basically well but face problems of living: a decision, a transition, a relationship difficulty, stress, grief. It works mainly with conscious material and present concerns, aims at problem solving, adjustment, prevention and growth, tends to be shorter, and takes place in schools, colleges, workplaces and community settings. Psychotherapy serves people with psychological disorders or long-standing personality problems, works with the past as well as the present and with what lies outside awareness, aims at symptom relief and deeper change in personality, tends to be longer, and takes place in hospitals, clinics and private practice. Guidance sits at the other end of the line from psychotherapy: it is the most information-centred and the least concerned with feelings.

The modern answer is that these are differences of degree, not of kind. One influential American textbook, Therapeutic Psychology (first published in 1960) by Lawrence Brammer and Everett Shostrom (the same Shostrom who later made the Gloria films), treated counselling and psychotherapy as overlapping regions of a single continuum rather than as separate activities. Rogers had already refused to separate them: the title of his 1942 book put the two words side by side, and he used them interchangeably for the rest of his life. Later writers such as C. H. Patterson argued that there is no essential difference between the two in theory, relationship or method, only in the severity of the client's difficulties and the setting. The practical reality bears this out. A counsellor in a de-addiction centre works with people who have a diagnosable disorder; a psychotherapist may spend a session helping a client decide which job to take; and both rely on exactly the same listening skills. The fairest summary is that counselling tends to be shorter, more present-focused, more concerned with development and prevention, and offered to a wider range of people, while psychotherapy tends to be longer, deeper and more concerned with disorder; but the two overlap so much that a sharp line cannot be drawn. Egan, as you will see, avoided the problem by speaking simply of "helping".

One further distinction matters in practice: counselling is not advice. Advice tells a person what to do; guidance supplies information so that a person can choose; counselling helps the person work out for himself what he wants and how to get it. A counsellor may give information, and sometimes advice, but the decision stays with the client.

Exam trap

If asked how counselling differs from psychotherapy, the expected contrasts are: counselling deals with relatively normal people, conscious material, present and developmental problems, and is shorter and more educational or preventive; psychotherapy deals with more disturbed people, deeper and unconscious material, and longer-term personality change. But textbooks agree that the difference is one of degree, not kind, and many (following Rogers) use the terms interchangeably. Guidance is the most directive and information-centred of the three; its founder is Frank Parsons (Vocation Bureau, Boston, 1908; Choosing a Vocation, 1909), the father of vocational guidance. Williamson's directive trait-and-factor counselling is the "Minnesota" approach that Rogers opposed. In Indian usage, admission "counselling" means seat allotment, which has nothing to do with the profession.

Allen Ivey and the microskills hierarchy

In the early 1960s counsellors were trained largely by apprenticeship. A trainee read about theory, watched an experienced counsellor, tried a few interviews and received feedback such as "You need to be more empathic." But what does a trainee actually do to be more empathic? Allen E. Ivey, then at Colorado State University, set out with his colleagues to answer that question. They borrowed an idea from teacher training, the "microteaching" developed by Dwight Allen and his colleagues at Stanford University, in which a complex performance was broken into small skills that could be practised one at a time. Ivey's group first described the result, microcounselling, in 1968, and Ivey's book Microcounseling followed in 1971.

The method rests on a simple idea: an interview is made of small, observable, teachable units of behaviour, which Ivey called microskills, such as attending, asking an open question or reflecting a feeling. Each skill is defined, shown and practised separately, in a format called microtraining. The trainee records a short baseline interview, reads a brief description of one skill, watches video recordings of the skill used well and badly, practises it in a short role-played interview, watches his own recording to see whether he used the skill and what effect it had on the client, and practises again. Because each skill is concrete, its effect can be seen: when a trainee uses more open questions, the client talks more; when he paraphrases accurately, the client goes further into the topic. Research in the following decades showed that the skills could be learned quickly and that they transferred across professions (doctors, nurses, managers, police officers and teachers) and across cultures. The approach was later developed in Ivey's textbook Intentional Interviewing and Counseling, written with Mary Bradford Ivey and later Carlos Zalaquett.

That title points to Ivey's central idea, intentionality: the ability to choose, from a range of possible responses, the one most likely to help this client at this moment, and to change course when a response does not work. As Ivey put it, an intentional counsellor always has more than one possible response available. A trainee who knows only one move, however good, will use it whether it fits or not. Ivey later widened the idea to cultural intentionality: the ability to respond in ways that fit the client's culture, so that eye contact, physical distance, directness and the role of the family are adjusted to the person in front of you. (Recall the note in Section 2.2 that SOLER's direct eye contact can be uncomfortable for many Indian clients.)

Ivey arranged the microskills in a microskills hierarchy, usually drawn as a pyramid (Figure 6.10). The details have changed a little from edition to edition, but the logic has not: simpler skills at the bottom support more complex ones above. At the foundation are ethics, multicultural competence and an emphasis on wellness and the client's strengths. Above them come the skills of listening: attending behaviour (culturally appropriate eye contact, vocal qualities, verbal tracking and body language), open and closed questions, client observation (noticing nonverbal behaviour, discrepancies and the client's characteristic words), encouraging, paraphrasing and summarising, and reflection of feeling. Together these form the basic listening sequence, which Ivey found to be the core of effective interviews in almost every helping profession; a doctor taking a history, a manager hearing a complaint and a counsellor in a first session all draw out the story with it.

Above the basic listening sequence sits the five-stage interview structure, a map of a well-formed session that Ivey's recent editions call empathic relationship (building rapport and structuring), story and strengths (drawing out the client's concerns and resources), goals (agreeing what the client wants), restory (exploring alternatives and new ways of seeing the problem) and action (generalising the learning to daily life). Older editions used other labels for the same five steps.

Higher in the pyramid are the skills that do more than reflect. Confrontation, which Ivey also called supportive challenge, means listening for and pointing out discrepancies and conflicts (between words and actions, between two statements, between the client's goals and his behaviour) within a supportive relationship. Ivey proposed a Client Change Scale for judging whether a confrontation has worked, according to how the client responds: with denial (level 1), partial examination (level 2), acceptance and recognition but no change (level 3), the creation of a new solution (level 4) or transcendence, a new and larger way of understanding the problem (level 5).

Focusing, in Ivey's sense, means deliberately choosing where the conversation directs its attention. Ivey listed seven possible foci: the client, the main theme or problem, other people, the family, mutuality (the "we" of the helping relationship), the interviewer, and the cultural or environmental context. Consider Arjun, 22, whose father expects him to take over the family sweet shop in Mysuru while Arjun wants to study for an MA in psychology. The counsellor can focus on Arjun ("What do you want?"), on the problem ("Let's look at the options in front of you"), on others ("What does your father hope for?"), on the family ("How have such decisions been made in your family before?"), on mutuality ("We can work on this together over the next few weeks"), on the interviewer ("I faced a similar choice once"), or on the context ("In many Indian families the eldest son is expected to carry on the business; how does that expectation affect you?"). Each focus opens a different conversation, and the intentional counsellor shifts among them deliberately. (Ivey's focusing is unrelated to Gendlin's focusing, the attention to a bodily felt sense described in Section 6.7.)

The skill of reflection of meaning goes beneath feelings to the values, purposes and meanings that give an experience its significance: "Behind the argument about the shop, it sounds as if the real question is whether you can be a good son and still live your own life." It is the microskill closest to the existential therapies, and to Frankl's search for meaning (Section 6.5). Interpretation and reframing, which offer the client a new frame of reference, sit close beside it. Above these come the influencing skills, in which the counsellor actively contributes: logical consequences (helping the client foresee what will follow from a choice), self-disclosure, feedback, information and psychoeducation, and directives (instructions to do something, such as a relaxation exercise, a Gestalt empty-chair experiment or a homework task). At the top of the pyramid stand skill integration, the ability to put all the skills together in a full interview, and the counsellor's own personal style and theory: Ivey's point is that a mature helper, whatever school he belongs to, should be able to name the skills he is using and say why.

Exam trap

Microcounselling and the microskills hierarchy are Allen Ivey's (first described in 1968; Microcounseling, 1971), not Egan's or Carkhuff's. Microteaching (Dwight Allen, Stanford) was its inspiration, not its name. The base of the hierarchy is ethics and multicultural competence, with attending behaviour as the first skill; the basic listening sequence is attending, questions, observation, encouraging, paraphrasing, summarising and reflection of feeling. Intentionality means having more than one response available and choosing deliberately. SOLER, by contrast, is Egan's acronym for physical attending (Section 2.2). And do not confuse Ivey's focusing (choosing the focus of the conversation) with Gendlin's focusing (Section 6.7) or with the focusing process of motivational interviewing (Section 6.9).

Robert Carkhuff and the human resource development model

You met Robert R. Carkhuff in Section 6.2 as Charles Truax's partner in measuring Rogers's core conditions. In Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy (1967) Truax and Carkhuff developed rating scales for accurate empathy, non-possessive warmth and genuineness, and in Beyond Counseling and Therapy (1967), written with Bernard Berenson, and the two volumes of Helping and Human Relations (1969), Carkhuff took the work further in two directions. He refined the scales, and he turned them into a training programme and a model of the whole helping process.

Carkhuff's scales rate a helper's response on five levels. The best known is the scale of empathic understanding. At levels 1 and 2 the response is subtractive: it ignores, distorts or noticeably weakens the feeling the client has expressed. At level 3 the response is an interchangeable response: it expresses essentially the same feeling and meaning as the client, adding nothing and taking nothing away, so that the client's statement and the helper's could be exchanged. Carkhuff called level 3 the minimally facilitative level. At levels 4 and 5 the response is additive: it expresses feelings and meanings a little deeper than the client has been able to express, which is roughly what Egan later called advanced empathy. Carkhuff built similar five-level scales for respect, genuineness, concreteness (specificity), self-disclosure, confrontation and immediacy, and grouped them into the facilitative conditions (empathy, respect and concreteness), which help the client explore, and the action-oriented conditions (genuineness, confrontation, immediacy and self-disclosure), which help the client act.

Two findings made Carkhuff's work influential far beyond the counselling room. The first was that helping can be, in his phrase, "for better or for worse". Clients of helpers who functioned at high levels of the facilitative conditions improved; clients of helpers who functioned at low levels did no better than untreated people, and sometimes deteriorated (the "deterioration effect" discussed in Chapter 8). Professional credentials, Carkhuff found, did not guarantee high functioning: many trained professionals responded at levels below 3. The second finding followed from the first. In a review published in 1968 Carkhuff reported that lay helpers (hospital attendants, volunteers, parents) given brief, systematic training in these skills could function at levels comparable to professionals, and could achieve comparable results with clients. That finding laid part of the foundation for training paraprofessional and lay counsellors, a strategy that matters greatly in countries with too few mental health professionals (Section 9.6).

Carkhuff's model of the helping process, which he called the human resource development model (HRD), and which he set out for a wide readership in The Art of Helping (first published in the early 1970s and revised many times), describes four sets of helper skills, each of which produces a client process. In the pre-helping phase the helper attends (physically and psychologically), and the client becomes involved in the helping process. In phase 1 the helper responds to the client's feelings and meanings with interchangeable responses, and the client explores where he is now ("Where am I?"). In phase 2 the helper personalises, helping the client to own his part in the problem and to see the goal implied by it, and the client comes to understand where he is in relation to where he wants to be ("Where do I want to be?"). In phase 3 the helper initiates, helping the client to define goals and develop step-by-step programmes, and the client acts ("How do I get there?"). The results of action feed back into renewed exploration, so the cycle can begin again. Carkhuff gave trainees simple formats for each phase. A responding statement takes the form "You feel [feeling] because [reason]." A personalised statement takes the client's part in the problem and the goal into account: "You feel [feeling] because you cannot [the client's own deficit], and you want to [goal]." "You feel angry because your manager passed you over" is a response; "You feel disappointed in yourself because you haven't been able to speak up for your work, and you want to learn how" is personalising.

Exam trap

On Carkhuff's five-level scales, level 3 is the interchangeable, minimally facilitative response; levels 1 and 2 are subtractive, levels 4 and 5 additive. Do not reverse the scale: higher is better. Carkhuff's helping model runs attending, responding, personalising, initiating (helper) and involvement, exploration, understanding, action (client). The empathy, warmth and genuineness rating scales of 1967 are Truax and Carkhuff's; Rogers proposed the conditions, but he did not build these scales. Carkhuff's "helping is for better or for worse" and the effectiveness of trained lay helpers are his two most cited findings.

Gerard Egan and the skilled helper

The most widely used helping model in counselling courses around the world is the work of Gerard Egan, a psychologist at Loyola University of Chicago, whose The Skilled Helper was first published in 1975 and has been revised in many editions since (the recent ones with Robert Reese). Egan built on Carkhuff's model and on Rogers's core conditions, and added the structure of practical problem solving. He called the result the skilled helper model, or in its later subtitle a problem-management and opportunity-development approach to helping. Its assumptions are pragmatic. Helping, for Egan, is not about curing illness but about helping clients manage the problems of living more effectively and develop opportunities they have left unused, and in the process become better at helping themselves. Success is judged by outcomes in the client's life, not by insight in the session. The model is deliberately open: Egan presented it as a framework into which techniques from many schools can be fitted.

The model has three stages, each with three tasks, and an action arrow that runs beneath all of them (Figure 6.11). Stage I asks "What is going on?" and builds a picture of the client's current situation, the current picture. Its tasks are to help clients tell their stories (I-A); to help them become aware of blind spots and develop new perspectives, getting to what Egan calls the real story (I-B); and to help them choose the issues that will make the most difference, the issues with the greatest leverage (I-C). Stage II asks "What do I want instead?" and builds the preferred picture. Its tasks are to help clients explore possibilities for a better future (II-A), to choose realistic and challenging goals from among them (II-B), and to find the incentives that will sustain commitment, which Egan put as the question "What am I willing to pay for what I want?" (II-C). Stage III asks "How do I get what I need or want?" and plans the way forward. Its tasks are to help clients brainstorm possible strategies (III-A), choose the strategies that best fit their resources and circumstances (III-B), and turn them into a plan with steps in sequence (III-C). The action arrow stands for implementation: clients can begin acting from the first session, and the stages are not a rigid sequence. A helper may move back and forth among them as the story changes.

Two families of skills carry the model. The communication skills are used throughout: attending (Egan's SOLER, Section 2.2), active listening, basic empathy, probing (prompts and questions that help the client fill in the story) and summarising. The challenging skills help clients see what they have not seen. They include advanced empathy (voicing what the client has only hinted at, the themes running through several stories, or the implications he has not drawn), information-sharing, helper self-disclosure, suggestions, confrontation, and immediacy, which Egan described as "you-me talk": discussing what is happening in the helping relationship itself, either in the relationship as a whole ("I notice we keep returning to your manager and never to you") or in the present moment ("You seem annoyed with me right now; can we talk about that?"). Challenge in Egan's model has a precise purpose, to help clients notice their blind spots, the things about themselves, their situation or their behaviour that they fail to see, avoid or misread. And it has a precise condition: the right to challenge must be earned by first understanding. In the early editions of The Skilled Helper the three stages were called exploration, new understanding and action, and the challenging skills, advanced empathy among them, belonged to the second stage, after the client's story had been heard with basic empathy. That is why advanced empathy is regarded as inappropriate in an initial session, while basic (primary) empathy is appropriate from the first minute.

Egan also wrote frankly about what he called the shadow side of helping, the things that go wrong and that textbooks rarely discuss: helpers who are tired, judgemental or more interested in their own theory than in the client, and clients who hold back. He distinguished reluctance, a client's hesitancy to engage in change, often born of fear, shame or ambivalence, from resistance, the client's push-back against feeling coerced, and argued (much as Miller and Rollnick later did) that both are normal and should be worked with, not blamed.

A worked example takes one client through the model. Kavya, 24, a software engineer in Bengaluru, comes to a counsellor saying that she cannot sleep and wants to resign. In Stage I the counsellor listens with basic empathy and probes gently, and the story emerges: long hours, a critical manager, loneliness in a city far from her family in Kerala. Kavya calls herself "bad at the job", yet she mentions that her ratings are good and that she never refuses extra work. Once trust is established, the counsellor offers a challenge (advanced empathy and a gentle confrontation): "You describe yourself as bad at the job, and at the same time you're the one the team turns to when something urgent comes up. I wonder whether the real difficulty is less the job than how hard it is for you to say no." Kavya is quiet, then agrees. The issue with the most leverage is not the job itself but her difficulty setting limits. In Stage II they explore what a better working life would look like, and Kavya chooses a goal: to leave the office by seven on at least four evenings a week and to refuse weekend work except in emergencies. Asked what she is willing to pay for it, she admits that she fears her manager's disapproval, and decides the risk is worth her health. In Stage III they brainstorm strategies (talking to her manager, asking to move teams, finding a mentor, practising assertive replies, rebuilding a social life through a dance class) and choose the two that fit best: a conversation with her manager, prepared through role-play, and practice in saying no to small requests first. The action arrow begins that week: she declines one weekend request and reports back in the next session. She did not, in the end, resign.

Exam trap

"Which kind of empathy is inappropriate in an initial counselling session?" The answer is advanced empathy (RML 2022). It goes beyond what the client has said, so it depends on trust and on knowing the client well; offered too early it sounds presumptuous and intrusive. Primary (basic) empathy, which reflects what the client has openly expressed, is appropriate from the first minute, so "both" is wrong. Culturally sensitive empathy, understanding the client within his cultural world (Ivey's cultural intentionality), is always appropriate. Egan's stages are current picture, preferred picture and the way forward (in early editions exploration, new understanding and action), plus the action arrow; SOLER is Egan's; and advanced empathy corresponds roughly to Carkhuff's additive levels 4 and 5.

Bringing the helping models together

Set the models side by side and a striking convergence appears. Ivey, Carkhuff and Egan all begin with attending and listening, move through a phase in which the client comes to understand the problem in a new way, and end with action. A later model, by the American counselling psychologist Clara E. Hill, makes the pattern explicit. In Helping Skills: Facilitating Exploration, Insight, and Action (1999, with Karen O'Brien) Hill described three stages: exploration, which draws on the client-centred tradition (attending, listening, reflection of feelings); insight, which draws on the psychodynamic tradition (challenge, interpretation, immediacy); and action, which draws on the behavioural tradition (information, goal setting, homework). The helping models, in other words, are a practical integration of the three great families of therapy you have studied in Chapters 3 to 6. Why does all this matter to a clinical psychologist? Clinical link

Interviewers for clinical psychology programmes often ask candidates to "show" a skill, for example by responding to a statement as a counsellor would. The models in this section give you a ready answer. Begin with an interchangeable response (Carkhuff's level 3: "You feel ... because ..."), add an open question to invite more of the story, and hold back advanced empathy and challenge until you have earned them. If you are asked what you would do with a client who is not ready to change, describe the stage of change you think the client is in and how you would use motivational interviewing to engage him rather than argue. And if asked how counselling differs from psychotherapy, give the traditional contrasts and then add that the difference is one of degree, not kind.

The case of Gloria, revisited

You can now see why the Gloria films have been argued over for so long: each of the three conversations was a faithful demonstration of a theory. Rogers did not tell Gloria what to do because, on his theory, she already knew. Her guilt about the lie she had told Pammy pointed to a gap between the self she felt she ought to be and what she was actually experiencing, the incongruence of Section 6.2, and the remedy was a relationship without conditions of worth: congruence, unconditional positive regard and accurate empathy, carried by reflection of feeling. Her wish that her own father could have talked to her that way is exactly the kind of moment the core conditions are meant to produce. Perls worked on something else, awareness in the here and now. When he pointed to her smile and her fidgeting hands, he was refusing to support what he saw as a game of the helpless little girl, frustrating her bid for outside support until she found support in herself; when she grew angry and fought back, she was, for him, at last really present (Section 6.3). Ellis's brisk argument with her beliefs belongs to Chapter 5.

The rest of the chapter adds voices that were not in the studio. A logotherapist would have asked what her situation was asking of her, and what she wanted to stand for as a mother (Section 6.5). Glasser would have asked what she wanted for herself and for Pammy, what she was actually doing about it, and whether it was working (Section 6.6). A solution-focused therapist might have asked the miracle question, and then when a small piece of that miracle had already happened (Section 6.8). A motivational interviewer would have heard her ambivalence ("Would the truth harm her child? Would the lie?"), reflected both sides and left the decision with her (Section 6.9), and the helping-skills models of Section 6.10 would have described much of what Rogers did as basic empathy offered from the first minute, with challenge held back until trust had been earned. Which of the three helped most is a question the films cannot settle. The research in this chapter suggests a quieter answer: the warmth, genuineness and empathy that Rogers showed are reliably, if modestly, linked to good outcomes in every kind of therapy, while specific methods (chair work, focusing, the miracle question, evoking change talk) can add speed and depth for particular problems. Gloria, who kept writing to Rogers for years afterwards, had found what every school in this chapter values in its own way: a helper who treated her as the expert on her own life.

Chapter summary

6.1 The Third Force: What These Therapies Share. Abraham Maslow called humanistic psychology the third force, after psychoanalysis and behaviourism; it became organised in the early 1960s, with Maslow and Carl Rogers as its best-known figures. The therapies of this chapter share six assumptions: phenomenology (the client's internal frame of reference), an inborn tendency to grow, freedom and responsibility, holism, a focus on the here and now, and change through an authentic relationship rather than technique. The humanistic branch (Rogers, Maslow) is optimistic; the existential branch (Binswanger, Boss, May, Frankl, Yalom) is more sober about death, isolation, freedom and meaninglessness; the experiential branch (Gestalt, focusing, emotion-focused therapy) works with moment-by-moment experiencing. The general aim of the humanistic-experiential psychotherapies is a more fluid and integrated organisation of the self, and their account of distress (blocked growth, inauthenticity, lost meaning) differs from the diathesis-stress model.

6.2 Carl Rogers and Person-Centred Therapy. Carl Rogers (1902 to 1987) called his approach non-directive (1942), then client-centred (1951) and later person-centred. His theory starts from the actualising tendency and the organismic valuing process; the need for positive regard leads the child to introject conditions of worth, and the resulting mismatch between self-concept and experience is incongruence, defended by distortion and denial. In 1957 he named six necessary and sufficient conditions, three of which (congruence, unconditional positive regard and accurate empathic understanding) are the core conditions the therapist provides. The signature response is reflection of feeling; advice, interpretation and diagnosis are absent. Clients move from fixity to fluidity toward the fully functioning person. Stephenson's Q-sort measured the self-ideal gap, Truax and Carkhuff rated the conditions, and research finds them facilitative but probably not sufficient. Rogers's encounter groups aimed at personal growth through self-disclosure and interaction.

6.3 Gestalt Therapy. Gestalt therapy was founded by Fritz Perls (1893 to 1970) with Laura Perls and Paul Goodman; the founding book (1951) is by Perls, Hefferline and Goodman, and the therapy is not Gestalt psychology. Drawing on Goldstein, Reich, Smuts, existentialism and Zen, it aims at awareness in the here and now, asks what and how rather than why, and treats responsibility as response-ability. Needs form as figure against ground and complete a cycle of experience; blocked needs remain as unfinished business. Beisser's paradoxical theory of change holds that change occurs when one becomes what one is. The contact boundary disturbances are introjection, projection, retroflection, deflection, confluence and egotism. The experiments include the empty chair for unfinished business, the two-chair dialogue between top dog and underdog, the hot seat, and dream work in which every element is a projection of the dreamer. Paivio and Greenberg (1995) supported empty-chair work.

6.4 Existential Therapy. Existential therapy helps clients face the existential givens (death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness) and live with awareness, choice and responsibility. Its vocabulary comes from philosophers such as Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Buber and Sartre: Heidegger's Dasein, or being-in-the-world, and Sartre's "existence precedes essence" and bad faith. Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss founded Daseinsanalysis; Binswanger described three modes of being-in-the-world, the Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt, to which Emmy van Deurzen later added the Überwelt. Rollo May brought existential psychology to America (The Meaning of Anxiety, 1950; Existence, 1958) and distinguished normal from neurotic anxiety; with James Bugental he is linked to existential-humanistic therapy. Irvin Yalom's Existential Psychotherapy (1980) named four ultimate concerns: death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. Practice rests on the relationship and the phenomenological method rather than technique, and the meaning-centred therapies have the best evidence.

6.5 Viktor Frankl and Logotherapy. Viktor Frankl (1905 to 1997) survived four camps, among them Auschwitz, and in Man's Search for Meaning (German original 1946) described the last of the human freedoms, the freedom to choose one's attitude. Logotherapy, the Third Viennese School after Freud's will to pleasure and Adler's will to power, holds that the will to meaning is the primary human motive and that meaning is found through creative, experiential and attitudinal values. The person has somatic, psychic and noetic dimensions, with the capacities of self-detachment and self-transcendence. Frustrated meaning brings existential frustration, the existential vacuum (with its Sunday neurosis) and noogenic neurosis, which Crumbaugh and Maholick's Purpose in Life Test (1964) set out to measure. Paradoxical intention, wishing for the feared symptom with humour, breaks the circle of anticipatory anxiety; dereflection, turning attention outward, cures hyper-reflection; Socratic dialogue and attitude modulation help clients find meaning.

6.6 Reality Therapy and Choice Theory. William Glasser (1925 to 2013), mentored by G. L. Harrington and tested at the Ventura School for Girls, coined the term reality therapy in 1962 and published Reality Therapy in 1965: a brief, present-focused approach in which a warm, involved therapist helps clients evaluate their behaviour and choose responsible ways of meeting their needs, accepting no excuses. Building on William Powers's control-systems theory, Glasser's control theory became choice theory (1998): five basic needs (survival, love and belonging, power or achievement, freedom and fun), a quality world of wanted pictures, the comparing place, and total behaviour (acting, thinking, feeling, physiology), pictured as a car steered by its front wheels, hence "depressing". Robert Wubbolding systematised the method as WDEP: wants, direction and doing, evaluation by the client, and planning. The evidence is thin, and the rejection of mental illness is its main weakness.

6.7 Focusing, Emotion-Focused Therapy and Positive Psychotherapy. Eugene Gendlin found that successful clients speak from inner experiencing (the Experiencing Scale) and named the bodily, wordless felt sense and the felt shift; Focusing (1978) teaches six steps: clearing a space, felt sense, handle, resonating, asking and receiving. Leslie Greenberg, with Laura Rice and Robert Elliott, developed process-experiential, later emotion-focused, therapy: emotion schemes; primary adaptive, primary maladaptive, secondary and instrumental emotions; changing emotion with emotion; and markers matched to tasks such as two-chair dialogue, empty-chair work and focusing, supported by the York depression studies. Martin Seligman launched positive psychology in 1998 (PERMA, the VIA strengths) and, with Rashid and Parks, positive psychotherapy (2006). Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi named flow, absorption when challenge matches skill. Nossrat Peseschkian's older positive psychotherapy (1977) works with positum, the balance model and stories.

6.8 Narrative and Solution-Focused Brief Therapy. Both approaches are social constructionist: problems and identities are built in language, and the client is the expert. Michael White and David Epston (Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends, 1990), drawing on Foucault and Bruner, help clients move from a problem-saturated story to a preferred one. Externalising separates the person from the problem ("the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem"), as in the case of Sneaky Poo, and relative influence questions, unique outcomes, re-authoring across the landscapes of action and identity, outsider witnesses and therapeutic letters build the new story. Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg developed solution-focused brief therapy at Milwaukee's Brief Family Therapy Center (1978) on the rule "do more of what works", using the miracle, exception, scaling and coping questions, compliments and the formula first session task. SFBT has a larger evidence base than narrative therapy.

6.9 Motivational Interviewing and the Stages of Change. Motivational interviewing was developed by William Miller (first paper, 1983) and Stephen Rollnick (joint book, 1991) in the treatment of problem drinking, as a guiding style that resolves ambivalence and resists the righting reflex. Its spirit is partnership, acceptance, compassion and evocation (PACE, 2013); its processes are engaging, focusing, evoking and planning; its core skills are OARS. The older principles were express empathy, develop discrepancy, roll with resistance and support self-efficacy. Change talk (preparatory DARN, mobilising CAT) predicts change, commitment language most of all, and resistance was renamed sustain talk and discord in 2013. Burke, Arkowitz and Menchola (2003) supported MI for alcohol, drugs, and diet and exercise. Prochaska and DiClemente's transtheoretical model (1983) adds the stages of change (precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance, termination), ten processes of change, decisional balance and levels of change, with relapse as recycling in a spiral.

6.10 Counselling and the Helping Skills. Counselling is a professional helping relationship, usually with people whose functioning is basically intact; it grew from Frank Parsons's vocational guidance (Vocation Bureau, 1908) and Williamson's directive trait-and-factor method, which Rogers opposed. Traditionally counselling is shorter, present-focused and developmental, and psychotherapy longer, deeper and concerned with disorder, but the difference is one of degree, not kind. Allen Ivey's microcounselling (1968) teaches one skill at a time through a microskills hierarchy, from attending and the basic listening sequence to influencing skills, guided by intentionality. Robert Carkhuff's five-level scales make level 3 the interchangeable response, and his human resource development model runs attending, responding, personalising and initiating; trained lay helpers can match professionals. Gerard Egan's skilled helper (1975) has three stages (current picture, preferred picture, way forward) plus action, with advanced empathy kept for after trust is earned. Clara Hill's model runs exploration, insight and action.

From the past papers

  1. A general aim of humanistic-experiential psychotherapy is: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Promote fluid and integrative self-organization (b) To understand and resolve deep seated psychological conflicts (c) To change maladaptive behaviours (d) To develop interpersonal skills

    Answer: (a) Promote fluid and integrative self-organization. Robert Elliott, Leslie Greenberg and their colleagues describe the shared aim of the person-centred, Gestalt, focusing, emotion-focused and existential therapies as a more fluid and integrated way of organising the self, reached by attending to present experience, especially emotion. Resolving deep-seated conflicts is the psychodynamic aim, changing maladaptive behaviours the behavioural aim, and developing interpersonal skills the aim of interpersonal and social skills approaches.

  2. Which of the following techniques is a client centred therapist most likely to use? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Analysis (b) Giving advice (c) Reflection (d) Aversive conditioning

    Answer: (c) Reflection. Reflection of feeling puts into words the feeling the client is expressing and offers it back to be checked, so the client's frame of reference stays in charge. Analysis (interpretation) imports the therapist's theory, advice directs the client, and aversive conditioning, pairing an unwanted behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus, is a behaviour therapy method.

  3. Which of the following might be considered as the central tenets of Client-Centered Therapy: (RML 2021)

    (a) Empathy (b) Unconditional positive regard (c) Congruence (d) All of the above

    Answer: (d) All of the above. These are Rogers's three core conditions, the therapist-provided conditions 3, 4 and 5 of his six necessary and sufficient conditions (1957). Choosing any one of them alone misses the point that the three work together.

  4. What is the critical feature of Carl Rogers' client-centred therapy? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Analysing unconscious desires (b) Therapist providing unconditional positive regard (c) Implementing strict behaviour modifications (d) Using aversive stimuli

    Answer: (b) Therapist providing unconditional positive regard. Warm valuing of the client without conditions is the direct remedy for the conditions of worth that, in Rogers's theory, produce incongruence. Analysing unconscious desires is psychoanalytic, and strict behaviour modification and aversive stimuli are behavioural methods with no place in person-centred work.

  5. A split between the organismic valuing process and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth which results in feelings of tension and confusion: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Conflict (b) Incongruence (c) Introjection (d) Actualizing tendency

    Answer: (b) Incongruence. Incongruence is Rogers's term for the mismatch between the self-concept and actual experience. Introjection is how conditions of worth are taken in, not the split that results; the actualising tendency is the growth force that incongruence obstructs; and "conflict" is a general word with no special place in Rogers's theory.

  6. Which of the following is NOT a key concept in Carl Rogers' Client-Centered Therapy? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Conditions of Worth (b) Unconditional Positive Regard (c) Actualizing Tendency (d) The Therapeutic Alliance (as defined by Bordin)

    Answer: (d) The Therapeutic Alliance (as defined by Bordin). Edward Bordin's working alliance (1979: agreement on goals and tasks plus an emotional bond) grew out of psychodynamic thinking and is a pan-theoretical idea. Conditions of worth, unconditional positive regard and the actualising tendency are all central to Rogers's theory.

  7. Which therapeutic approach would be LEAST likely to use the term "therapeutic alliance," preferring instead to focus on the "real relationship" and the therapist's unconditional positive regard? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Psychoanalysis (b) Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (c) Person-Centered Therapy (d) Interpersonal Therapy

    Answer: (c) Person-Centered Therapy. Person-centred therapists speak of the relationship itself and the core conditions. Psychoanalysis, where the working alliance was born, cognitive-behaviour therapy and interpersonal psychotherapy (Klerman and Weissman) all use the term freely.

  8. What is the principal role of an "encounter group"? (RML 2022)

    (a) Enables individuals to interact with others in a social setting (b) Encourages team building (c) Aids the therapist in diagnosis (d) Encourages therapy and self-growth through disclosure and interaction

    Answer: (d) Encourages therapy and self-growth through disclosure and interaction. An encounter group, such as Rogers's basic encounter group, is a small, intensive, relatively unstructured group of people seeking personal growth through open self-disclosure, here-and-now feeling and frank feedback. Mere social interaction and team building miss that aim, and encounter groups are not a diagnostic aid.

  9. Which therapy focuses on "empty chair technique"? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) CBT (b) Gestalt therapy (c) Psychoanalysis (d) DBT

    Answer: (b) Gestalt therapy. Speaking directly to an imagined person in an empty chair, usually to finish unfinished business, is the hallmark of Fritz Perls's Gestalt therapy. CBT disputes beliefs, psychoanalysis uses free association and interpretation, and DBT teaches skills; emotion-focused therapy later borrowed chair work, but the technique is Gestalt.

  10. Any distorting or selective effect of a person's inner world on his perception or conception of the outer world is termed: (RML 2024)

    (a) Projection (b) Introjection (c) Empathy (d) Denial

    Answer: (a) Projection; the paper's key, (d) Denial, is wrong. The stem is the broad, classic definition of projection, the idea behind projective tests. Denial refuses to admit one particular painful fact and does not colour perception in general. Introjection is swallowing other people's values whole, and empathy is sensing another person's world as if it were one's own.

  11. The concept of "Dasein," which refers to human existence as "being-in-the-world," is primarily associated with which school of psychology? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Behavioral psychology (b) Existential psychology (c) Cognitive psychology (d) Psychoanalytic psychology

    Answer: (b) Existential psychology. Dasein ("being there") is Martin Heidegger's term in Being and Time (1927), carried into therapy by Binswanger and Boss as Daseinsanalysis. It has no place in behavioural or cognitive theory, and although Boss kept the analytic couch, he discarded Freud's drive theory.

  12. In existential psychology, Dasein encompasses three fundamental psychological dimensions of human existence. Which of the following correctly identifies these three modes of being-in-the-world? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Id, Ego, and Superego (b) Umwelt, Mitwelt, and Eigenwelt (c) Conscious, Preconscious, and Unconscious (d) Sensorimotor, Preoperational, and Concrete Operational

    Answer: (b) Umwelt, Mitwelt, and Eigenwelt. Binswanger's three modes are the biological and physical world, the world of relationships with others and the person's relationship with himself. Id, ego and superego are Freud's structural model, conscious, preconscious and unconscious his topographical model, and the last option lists Piaget's stages.

  13. Viktor Frankl's concept of the "will to meaning," the fundamental motivational force in humans to find purpose in life, is central to which therapeutic school? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) (b) Logotherapy (c) Existential-Humanistic Therapy (d) Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

    Answer: (b) Logotherapy. Frankl's Third Viennese School puts the will to meaning first, after Freud's will to pleasure and Adler's will to power. REBT is Ellis's therapy of irrational beliefs, existential-humanistic therapy (May, Bugental) is built on presence and authenticity, and ACT works with values but grows out of behaviour analysis.

  14. Noogenic anxiety is described under: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Humanistic School (b) Gestalt school (c) Existential school (d) Psychodynamic school

    Answer: (c) Existential school. Noogenic neurosis (or anxiety) is Frankl's term for symptoms that arise in the noetic dimension, from frustrated meaning or a conflict of values, and logotherapy is an existential therapy. It is not humanistic in the narrow sense of Rogers and Maslow, not Gestalt, and not psychodynamic; the psychodynamic kind is what Frankl called psychogenic.

  15. Frankl's technique for 'hyper-reflection', in which a person stops watching himself and turns his attention outward, for example from his own sexual performance to his partner, is: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Paradoxical intention (b) Sensate focus (c) Dereflection (d) Socratic dialogue

    Answer: (c) Dereflection. Dereflection counters hyper-reflection and hyper-intention by turning attention away from the self, as in Frankl's case of the woman who attended to her partner instead of watching herself. Paradoxical intention targets anticipatory anxiety by wishing for the symptom, sensate focus is Masters and Johnson's programme of touching exercises, and Socratic dialogue is logotherapy's questioning method for finding meaning.

  16. In Reality Therapy, developed by William Glasser, which of the following is considered one of the five basic genetic needs? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Self-actualization (b) Power or Achievement (c) Collective Unconscious (d) Unconditional positive regard

    Answer: (b) Power or Achievement. Glasser's five needs are survival, love and belonging, power (achievement, competence, recognition), freedom and fun. Self-actualisation is Maslow's, the collective unconscious is Jung's, and unconditional positive regard is one of Rogers's core conditions.

  17. Who introduced the concept of "flow" in psychology? (RML 2023)

    (a) Carl Rogers (b) Abraham Maslow (c) Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (d) Viktor Frankl

    Answer: (c) Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. He described flow, complete absorption in a task whose challenge matches one's skill, in Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975). Maslow described the briefer peak experience, Rogers the fully functioning person and Frankl the will to meaning.

  18. The concept of "self story" or helping clients re-author their life narratives is most closely associated with which therapeutic approach? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (b) Narrative Therapy (c) Psychodynamic Therapy (d) Gestalt Therapy

    Answer: (b) Narrative Therapy. Michael White and David Epston help clients move from a problem-saturated story to a preferred one through externalising, unique outcomes and re-authoring conversations. CBT changes thoughts and behaviours, psychodynamic therapy works with unconscious conflict and transference, and Gestalt therapy with here-and-now awareness.

  19. Motivational interviewing is used in the management of: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Non-compliance to treatment (b) Substance abuse (c) Anxious-avoidant personality disorder (d) Bipolar disorder

    Answer: (b) Substance abuse. Motivational interviewing was born in the treatment of problem drinking, and its evidence is strongest for alcohol and drug problems. It is also used to improve adherence, which makes (a) tempting, but that is a later extension; personality disorders and bipolar disorder are not its targets.

  20. Which of the following is NOT listed by Burke, Arkowitz and Mechola (2003) as being treatable with motivational interviewing: (RML 2022)

    (a) Substance-related disorders (b) Smoking cessation (c) Diabetes mellitus (d) Obsessive-compulsive disorder

    Answer: (d) Obsessive-compulsive disorder. The 2003 meta-analysis of 30 trials covered alcohol, drugs, diet and exercise (including trials with obese women with Type 2 diabetes), smoking and HIV-risk behaviour; smoking was studied, although the trials then available did not support MI for it. Obsessive-compulsive disorder was not among the problems at all; it needs exposure and response prevention. The third author is Marisa Menchola.

  21. Which kind of empathy is considered inappropriate for an initial counselling interview or session? (RML 2022)

    (a) Advanced empathy (b) Primary empathy (c) Both a and b (d) Culturally sensitive empathy

    Answer: (a) Advanced empathy. Advanced empathy voices what the client has only hinted at, so it depends on trust and on knowing the client; offered too early it sounds presumptuous, which is why Egan placed it among the challenging skills that come after the story has been heard. Primary (basic) empathy is appropriate from the first minute, so (c) is wrong, and culturally sensitive empathy is always appropriate.
