Paris in the 1790s. On the southern edge of the city stood La Bicêtre, a huge hospice for poor, old, sick and criminal men. In its wards for the insane, men were kept in chains, some of them for years, and treated more like dangerous animals than patients. The story most books tell is that in 1793, at the height of the French Revolution, the hospital's physician, Philippe Pinel, ordered the irons struck off, and that men who had not seen the sky for years walked calmly into the courtyard. Nineteenth-century painters made the scene famous.

The real story, pieced together by historians, is less heroic and more useful. The chains at Bicêtre were first removed not by the doctor but by Jean-Baptiste Pussin, the lay superintendent of the ward, who had seen what irons did to the men in his care and had begun to treat them as people: he spoke with them, kept them occupied and replaced irons with gentler restraint. Pinel's contribution was to recognise that Pussin's methods worked, to record his patients' histories carefully and count who recovered, and to take the approach, with Pussin himself, to the women's hospital, the Salpêtrière. Their moral treatment rested on three things: a relationship, a daily routine and careful observation of results.

Keep both men in mind: the attendant who knew how to be with a frightened, disturbed person, and the physician who wrote down what happened and checked whether it helped. Between them they hold the whole of clinical psychology: understanding and assessing a person, building a relationship that heals, choosing treatments by their evidence, caring for people in their communities and protecting their dignity in law. This chapter follows each of these threads, and at its end we return to Bicêtre.

7.1 The Profession: History, Models, Ethics and Indian LawMedium yield · 39 Qs

Before learning how a clinical psychologist interviews, tests or treats, you need to know who this professional is, where the profession came from, which theories of abnormality it uses, and which ethical rules and Indian laws bind it.

What clinical psychology is

The American Psychological Association's Division 12, the Society of Clinical Psychology, describes a field that "integrates science, theory, and practice to understand, predict, and alleviate maladjustment, disability, and discomfort as well as to promote human adaptation, adjustment, and personal development". A clinical psychologist therefore researches, assesses, treats and promotes well-being. In India the profession is defined by qualification and registration: under the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) Act, a clinical psychologist holds an RCI-recognised qualification and is on the RCI's register, and the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 likewise defines one by an RCI-recognised qualification.

The work spans psychological assessment, diagnosis, psychotherapy, research, teaching and supervision, consultation and administration, in hospitals, rehabilitation centres, schools, universities, courts, prisons, the armed forces, private practice and online. From demons to moral treatment

How people explained strange behaviour decided how they treated it. The oldest explanation, the supernatural model, blamed possession, sin or sorcery. Some Stone Age skulls show trephining, a hole chipped through the skull, perhaps to let an evil spirit out. The Greek physician Hippocrates (460 to 377 BC) made the first break, finding natural causes in the brain and body. His humoral theory, developed by the Roman physician Galen (about AD 129 to 198), held that health is a balance of blood, yellow bile, black bile and phlegm, and that excess black bile causes melancholia. Medieval Europe swung back to demonology, and outbreaks of mass madness such as St Vitus's dance and tarantism were taken as signs of possession. From the sixteenth century the mentally ill were confined in asylums that were more prisons than hospitals; London's Bethlehem Hospital, shortened to Bedlam, was so notorious that its name now means uproar.

Reform came at the end of the eighteenth century. In Paris the physician Philippe Pinel (1745 to 1826) and the superintendent Jean-Baptiste Pussin (1746 to 1811) removed the chains from patients at La Bicêtre (in 1793, by the usual account) and then at the Salpêtrière. In England the Quaker William Tuke (1732 to 1822) opened the York Retreat in 1796, where patients lived and worked in a calm, family-like house. Both reforms practised moral treatment ("moral" meaning psychological): kindness, routine, work and social contact. From the 1840s the American schoolteacher Dorothea Dix (1802 to 1887) persuaded legislatures to build state hospitals, but they filled faster than they could be staffed and moral treatment decayed into custodial care. Then a former patient, Clifford Beers (1876 to 1943), described his abuse in hospital in A Mind That Found Itself (1908) and, through the mental hygiene societies he founded in 1908 and 1909, launched the mental hygiene movement for prevention, early treatment and child guidance.

Medicine meanwhile built the biological (medical) model. Emil Kraepelin (1856 to 1926), in a textbook first published in 1883, treated mental disorders as distinct syndromes with their own symptoms, course and outcome, separating dementia praecox (renamed schizophrenia by Eugen Bleuler in 1908) from manic-depressive insanity; modern classification descends from him.

Exam trap

Pinel gets the credit, but Pussin had begun the humane regime before Pinel arrived at Bicêtre (1793; some textbooks print 1791), persuaded him to follow, and himself banned the chains there, a step historians date to 1797. "Mental hygiene movement" usually means Beers (1908 to 1909), though some textbooks also give the name to Dix's crusade; check the date.

Witmer to Vail: a profession is born

The profession began in 1896, when Lightner Witmer (1867 to 1956), a former student of Wundt, opened the first psychological clinic at the University of Pennsylvania for children with learning problems. In 1907 he founded the journal The Psychological Clinic and in it first used the term clinical psychology in print. For decades the work was mainly testing: Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon published the first practical intelligence scale in 1905, and in 1917 Robert Yerkes (1876 to 1956) led the testing of about 1.75 million American recruits with the Army Alpha, a written test for literate men, and the non-verbal Army Beta for those who could not read English. After the Second World War, psychological casualties far outnumbered psychiatrists, and from 1946 the Veterans Administration (VA) paid to train clinical psychologists as therapists.

How should they be trained? The Boulder conference (Colorado, 1949) chose the scientist-practitioner model: a PhD training clinicians equally in research and practice, so that they consume research, evaluate their own work and create new knowledge. The Vail conference (Colorado, 1973) endorsed the practitioner-scholar model and the PsyD (Doctor of Psychology), a professional degree centred on practice. Richard McFall's 1991 "Manifesto for a Science of Clinical Psychology" set out the stricter clinical science model: training grounded in research, and only scientifically supported methods in practice.

The APA formed a clinical section in 1919, which became Division 12 when the APA reorganised into divisions in 1945. The newest debate is over prescription privileges for psychologists trained in psychopharmacology: supporters cite shortages of psychiatrists, opponents doubt the training and fear a loss of psychological identity. New Mexico (2002) and Louisiana (2004) were the first American states to grant them; in India clinical psychologists do not prescribe.

Exam trap

The first clinic and the first printed "clinical psychology" are Witmer's, not Wundt's (first laboratory, 1879), G. Stanley Hall's (first APA president, 1892), James McKeen Cattell's (coined "mental test", 1890), Freud's, Erikson's, Rogers's or Skinner's. Boulder 1949 = scientist-practitioner, PhD; Vail 1973 = practitioner-scholar, PsyD. Salt Lake City hosted a later conference on graduate education (1987), and Adelphi is a university; neither names a training model.

Clinical psychology in India

Girindrasekhar Bose (1887 to 1953) founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society at Calcutta in 1922 (Chapter 1). Formal training began at the All India Institute of Mental Health, Bangalore (founded 1954), which began training clinical psychologists in 1955 and became NIMHANS (National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences) in 1974; the Hospital for Mental Diseases, Ranchi, now the Central Institute of Psychiatry (CIP), had a clinical psychology department from the late 1940s and started its diploma course in 1962. Their post-MA training in medical and social psychology became the two-year M.Phil in Clinical Psychology, for decades the only qualifying degree. The Indian Association of Clinical Psychologists (IACP) was founded in 1968.

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) began in 1986 as a registered society; the RCI Act 1992 made it a statutory body (from 22 June 1993), and the Act was amended in 2000. The RCI regulates rehabilitation and disability professionals, clinical psychologists among them, so it sits under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment rather than the Health Ministry. It recognises courses, sets syllabi and keeps the Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR); practising without registration is an offence under the Act. After the National Education Policy 2020 ended the M.Phil, the RCI replaced it with a two-year professional MA in Clinical Psychology from 2025-26.

Exam trap

1968 is the IACP. NIMHANS dates from 1974 and its community psychiatry unit from 1975; it was made an Institute of National Importance by an Act of Parliament in 2012; the PsyD is the American degree from Vail. RCI: society 1986, statutory body under the 1992 Act; Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, not Health. Registration is mandated by the RCI Act, not the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995 or the mental health Acts.

What is abnormal, and why? The models

No single criterion marks behaviour as abnormal. The statistical criterion calls rare behaviour abnormal, but genius is rare too. The social norm criterion flags rule-breaking, but norms vary: a devotee in trance at a temple festival is following custom. The maladaptiveness criterion asks whether behaviour stops a person meeting daily demands, the personal distress criterion whether the person suffers (a manic person may feel wonderful). Marie Jahoda (1907 to 2001) in 1958 defined ideal mental health instead: positive self-attitude, growth and self-actualisation, integration, autonomy, accurate perception of reality and environmental mastery. Textbooks now condense the criteria into the four Ds: deviance, distress, dysfunction and danger to self or others.

A model then says why a disorder arose and so what will help. Beyond the supernatural and biological models, the psychodynamic model of Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) blames unconscious childhood conflict; the behavioural model maladaptive learning; the cognitive model faulty beliefs; the humanistic-existential model blocked growth and lost meaning, answered with respect, empathy, unconditional positive regard and faith in human potential; the sociocultural model poverty, culture and social labels; and the family systems model disturbed family interaction, with the patient as symptom bearer. Since each model explains only some cases, two integrative models dominate. In a 1977 paper in Science, the psychiatrist George L. Engel (1913 to 1999) argued that the biomedical model cannot explain why two people with the same lesion fare so differently, and proposed the biopsychosocial model: every illness arises from interacting biological, psychological and social factors, and treatment must address all three.

The diathesis-stress model explains who falls ill and when. A diathesis is a predisposition, usually genetic but sometimes psychological (a pessimistic style, an early loss); stress is the triggering event. Neither alone suffices. Paul Meehl (1920 to 2003) applied the idea to schizophrenia in 1962, and Joseph Zubin and Bonnie Spring formalised it in 1977 as the vulnerability-stress model: everyone has a threshold, and the more vulnerable you are, the less stress it takes to cross it (Figure 7.1). Adding protective factors (social support, coping skills, continued medication), which raise the threshold, gives the vulnerability-stress-protective factor model used in relapse prevention.

Exam trap

"Predisposition plus trigger" is diathesis-stress, not the neurodevelopmental model (faulty early brain development in schizophrenia; Chapter 8); "clinical-reductionist" and "syndrome-sign" are not models. Single-cause options (genes, stress, learning, unconscious conflict or spirits alone) are wrong. The biopsychosocial model is Engel's, not Kraepelin's, Bleuler's, Beck's, Freud's, Walter Cannon's (fight or flight) or Richard Lazarus's (stress appraisal). Reinforcement is behavioural, never humanistic.

Ethical principles and practice

The APA's Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (2002, amended 2010 and 2016) has two layers: five aspirational General Principles (A to E) that guide judgement but cannot be enforced, and ten sections of enforceable Ethical Standards (on competence, confidentiality, research, assessment, therapy and more). Behind any code lie two ways of reasoning. A deontological ethic (Immanuel Kant) judges an act by its intrinsic rightness: a duty such as keeping a confidence binds whatever the consequences. A utilitarian ethic (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill) weighs costs against benefits and chooses the greatest good for the greatest number. Competence means working only within one's training and supervised experience and referring beyond it; ethical competence means knowing the codes, recognising, analysing and resolving dilemmas, and taking responsibility for one's actions.

Informed consent is free agreement by a capable person who has been told, understandably, the nature and course of treatment, fees, the limits of confidentiality and the right to withdraw. A child cannot consent legally, so a guardian gives permission and the clinician seeks the child's assent. Research participants must be told the purpose, procedures, risks and benefits and their right to decline or withdraw (the consent form merely records this). Deception is allowed only when justified and must end in debriefing, a full explanation of the study's true purpose after participation. Research ethics go beyond legal compliance: protection from harm, confidential data, ethics committee approval and honest reporting; fabricating or manipulating data is misconduct.

Confidentiality is the clinician's ethical duty not to reveal a client's disclosures; privileged communication is the client's legal right to bar the clinician's testimony in court, which the client may waive. Neither is absolute. Disclosure is allowed or required with the client's consent; when there is serious risk of suicide or of harm to an identifiable person; when child abuse is suspected (India's POCSO Act 2012 makes reporting sexual offences against children mandatory); under a court order, including evidence of guilt or innocence in a criminal case; when clients sue the clinician or put their mental state at issue; and in emergencies. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 grants patients confidentiality with similar exceptions.

Key study: Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (1974, 1976)

In 1969 Prosenjit Poddar, a graduate student from India at Berkeley, told his university therapist he intended to kill a young woman who had rejected him, readily identifiable as Tatiana Tarasoff. Campus police questioned and released him; no one warned her, and two months later he killed her. The California Supreme Court ruled in 1974 that therapists have a duty to warn an identifiable victim, and in 1976 widened it to a duty to protect (warning, informing police, hospitalisation): "The protective privilege ends where the public peril begins." Critics fear it deters dangerous clients from speaking openly.

Exam trap

Duty to warn comes from Tarasoff. The M'Naghten (1843) and Durham (1954) rules are insanity tests, and the Daubert standard (1993) governs expert testimony (7.10). "There are never exceptions to confidentiality" is always false.

A multiple (dual) relationship exists when a psychologist in a professional role with someone also holds another role with that person or someone close to them, or promises one: treating a friend, employee or relative, or doing business with a client. Under APA Standard 3.05 it is unethical when it risks impaired objectivity, exploitation or harm. Co-therapy by two psychologists, a client seeing two therapists or a psychologist working in two clinics is not a dual relationship. Sexual intimacy with a current client is forbidden, and with a former client for at least two years after therapy and even then only in the most unusual circumstances. Thomas Gutheil and Glen Gabbard (1993) distinguished a boundary crossing, a harmless departure from the usual frame (a brief self-disclosure, seeing a client at the workplace for exposure work), from a boundary violation, which exploits or harms the client to serve the therapist, such as a romantic relationship or business deal.

Finally, records must be accurate, secure and open to clients. In assessment, psychologists use valid tests, explain results, release test data (raw scores and responses) to the client or whoever the client names, and guard test security by never releasing manuals or items to untrained people. Teletherapy requires technical competence, secure platforms, consent covering privacy risks, confirming the client's location and an emergency plan. Trainees work under supervision, clients are told so, and the supervisor carries responsibility. The RCI Act lets the Council prescribe a code of ethics, and misconduct can remove a professional from its register.

Exam trap

A romance with a current client is a boundary violation; rapport and convenient appointments are just good practice. The APA has five General Principles; "equity" is not one. Integrity is APA's, not one of Beauchamp and Childress's four (autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice). Non-maleficence is refraining from harm; doing good is beneficence. Consent comes before a study, debriefing after.

Indian mental health law and policy

The Indian Lunacy Act 1912 was custodial, built around detaining "lunatics" in asylums. The Mental Health Act 1987 (in force 1993) modernised terms and licensing but gave few rights, exempted government hospitals from licensing and left attempted suicide a crime under section 309 of the Indian Penal Code. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (MHCA, in force 2018) replaced it with a rights-based law aligned with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

  • Rights. A right to access mental healthcare (section 18) from government services, free below the poverty line, with rights to community living, dignity, equality (including insurance parity with physical illness, section 21(4)), confidentiality, information, legal aid and medical records.
  • Choices. Every person, including one with mental illness, is presumed capable (section 4), and treatment needs informed consent. An adult may write an advance directive (section 5) on how to be treated, or not treated, in a future illness and appoint a nominated representative (NR, section 14). A minor can do neither; a minor's NR is the legal guardian unless the Board orders otherwise (section 15). Under section 87 a minor is admitted only on the NR's application after two independent examinations, with the minor's wishes taken into account, is kept apart from adults with the NR or an attendant always present, and is treated with the NR's consent; the Board is informed within 72 hours and must review any stay beyond 30 days.
  • Prohibitions (section 95). No ECT without anaesthesia and muscle relaxant (unmodified ECT is never lawful, and section 94 bars any ECT as emergency treatment); no ECT for minors unless a psychiatrist judges it necessary, the guardian consents and the Board gives prior permission; no sterilisation as treatment; no chaining. Section 97 also bans seclusion and solitary confinement and allows physical restraint only as a last resort authorised by the psychiatrist.
  • Bodies. The Central Mental Health Authority and State Mental Health Authorities register all mental health establishments, government or private, and professionals; Mental Health Review Boards, each for a district or group of districts, chaired by a serving or retired District Judge and including a psychiatrist, a medical practitioner and two service-user or carer members (section 74), register advance directives, appoint NRs, review admissions and hear complaints.
  • Section 115. Anyone who attempts suicide is presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress and is not punished; in effect, decriminalisation, with a government duty to provide care.
  • Professionals. Section 2 defines a clinical psychologist by an RCI-recognised qualification in clinical psychology, or a postgraduate degree plus a two-year M.Phil in Clinical or Medical and Social Psychology with supervised clinical training; registered with the State Authority, the clinical psychologist is a mental health professional under the Act.

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 (RPwD, in force 2017) replaced the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995 (7 disabilities) with 21 specified disabilities, including mental illness, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder and specific learning disabilities. At least 40 percent of a specified disability is a benchmark disability, qualifying for reservation (4 percent of government posts, 5 percent of higher education seats). Practising in India

Trained in an RCI-recognised programme with supervised hospital work, registered with the RCI and working as a scientist-practitioner, the Indian clinical psychologist also needs cultural competence, the awareness, knowledge and skills to work across cultures. Families usually share treatment decisions, so the clinician works with them; stigma delays help-seeking, and many people first visit faith healers, temples and dargahs, beliefs to respect while explaining what treatment offers. With a treatment gap above 70 percent, task-shifting, training lay counsellors and health workers to deliver simple psychological treatments under supervision, has become central (7.9).

Ravi, 24 (a composite case), has failed the bank examination for the third time, in the week his cousin was selected, and has told his mother that everyone would be better off without him. The clinician will interview him, take his history, examine his mental state, judge his safety and build a formulation: the steps this section follows.

Purposes and types of clinical interview

The clinical interview is a conversation with a professional purpose: it gathers information, tests hypotheses and begins a working relationship at once. Interviews are named by purpose: the intake interview (first contact: why now, how urgent?), the case-history interview, the diagnostic interview (symptoms checked against criteria), the mental status examination, the crisis interview (brief, active, focused on safety; Section 7.9) and the termination interview (reviewing progress and planning relapse prevention).

They also differ in structure. In an unstructured interview the clinician chooses the questions: flexible and good for rapport, but two clinicians may reach different diagnoses. A semi-structured interview fixes topics and core questions but lets a trained clinician rephrase, probe and judge. A fully structured interview fixes every word and scoring rule, so a lay interviewer or computer can give it. Structure buys reliability (interviewers agree) at the cost of flexibility and rapport, but not validity: interviewers can agree and still be wrong, and a schedule misses what it does not ask. Interviewing skills

Allen Ivey's microcounselling (from 1968) teaches the craft one microskill at a time. Rapport, the trust that makes disclosure possible, grows from explaining the purpose and the limits of confidentiality and starting with neutral topics. Attending behaviour shows that you are listening; Gerard Egan (The Skilled Helper, 1975) summed it up as SOLER: face the client Squarely, Open posture, Lean forward, Eye contact, Relaxed, though steady eye contact feels intrusive to many Indian clients.

An open question ("Tell me about...") invites a long answer in the client's own words; a closed question ("When did the sleep go?") pins down a fact. Move from open to closed. Nodding and "umm" are furthering, not prompting (steering to a topic: "What about work?") or checking. Restating in different words is paraphrasing, not questioning, empathy (the broader capacity) or nonverbal communication. "Tell me about" is an open-ended question, even inside an ethnographic interview (learning a culture in the insider's terms) or theory-guided inquiry (questions chosen from a theory).

Common errors are leading questions ("You've been depressed, haven't you?"), double-barrelled questions (two in one), accusing "why" questions, premature reassurance and premature closure on an early hypothesis. With children, use play, drawing and concrete words and add parents' and teachers' reports; with older adults, check hearing and vision before inferring confusion (Section 7.10); with resistant clients, acknowledge that coming was not their idea; with suicidal clients, ask directly, since asking does not plant the idea. Across cultures, elicit the client's explanatory model (his name for the problem, its cause, the cure he expects), as Arthur Kleinman (1980) urged, and respect idioms of distress such as "tension". Use a trained interpreter, never a relative or child.

The case history

The case history, the story of person and illness, follows a fixed order:

  1. Identifying data (age, education, occupation, marital status, religion, socioeconomic status), then the informant and the account's reliability (relationship, closeness of contact, consistency).
  2. Chief complaints, in the patient's own words, each with its duration ("Low mood, 3 months").
  3. History of present illness: onset (abrupt, acute or insidious), course, precipitating factors and treatment so far.
  4. Past psychiatric and medical history, and family history drawn as a genogram (three generations; squares for males, circles for females).
  5. Personal history: birth and development, education, occupation, marital and sexual life, substance use and forensic record.
  6. Premorbid personality: the person before the illness, the baseline for judging change.

The MSE: behaviour, mood, thought and perception

The mental status examination (MSE) is a snapshot of mental functioning during the interview. It records appearance and behaviour, attitude towards the examiner (cooperative, guarded, hostile), psychomotor activity (retardation, agitation) and speech (rate, volume, tone, reaction time, relevance, coherence). Mood is the sustained emotion the patient reports: subjective, the climate. Affect is the emotion the examiner observes: objective, the weather. Affect is rated for range, reactivity, appropriateness and stability: it may be blunted (markedly reduced), flat (virtually absent), labile (shifting rapidly) or inappropriate (laughing about a death).

Thought has four headings: stream (speed and flow), form of thought (thought process: how ideas connect; Table 7.7), content of thought (what is thought: delusions, obsessions, overvalued ideas, phobias, suicidal or homicidal ideas) and possession (whose thoughts they feel to be). A delusion is a false, unshakeable belief out of keeping with the person's culture: persecutory, of reference (chance events carry a personal message), grandiose, nihilistic (the self, body or world does not exist), of jealousy, erotomanic (a person of higher status is secretly in love with one), of guilt or somatic (the body is diseased or changed). An overvalued idea is an understandable belief held with excessive emotion but without delusional fixity. An obsession is an intrusive thought recognised as one's own and resisted (Indian case formats file obsessions under possession, as lost control of thinking); a phobia is an excessive fear of a specific object, situation or idea. In thought alienation thoughts feel inserted, withdrawn or broadcast; these are among the first-rank symptoms of schizophrenia listed by Kurt Schneider (1887 to 1967; Chapter 8).

The MSE: cognition, insight and judgement

Cognitive functions are tested in order: consciousness (alert to coma); orientation to time, place and person (time is lost first); attention and concentration (digit span, serial sevens from 100, months backwards); memory, immediate (repeat three words), recent (recall them after five minutes) and remote (childhood events); intelligence and general information; abstract thinking (proverbs, similarities: literal answers are concrete); and judgement, personal (plans), social (conduct) and test ("What would you do if you saw smoke in a cinema?").

Insight is the patient's awareness and understanding of his own illness, graded on six levels: (1) complete denial; (2) slight awareness of illness while still denying it; (3) awareness, but blamed on others or physical causes; (4) awareness of something unknown within oneself; (5) intellectual insight, accepting the illness without changing behaviour; (6) true emotional insight, awareness that leads to change.

Exam trap

Insight is awareness of one's own illness, even where a case format files it among the cognitive functions. It is not orientation, consciousness, immediate recall, serial subtraction (attention) or hallucinations (perception).

Risk assessment: suicide and violence

Suicide risk factors include a previous attempt (the strongest predictor), depression, substance use, schizophrenia, hopelessness, male sex (women attempt more often), living alone, chronic illness, recent loss and access to means such as pesticides. Protective factors include family support, responsibility for children, religious objections and reasons for living. The SAD PERSONS mnemonic (Patterson and colleagues, 1983) scores one point each for Sex (male), Age (under 19 or over 45), Depression, Previous attempt, Ethanol abuse, Rational thinking loss, Social support lacking, Organised plan, No spouse and Sickness. It aids memory but predicts poorly: suicide is rare, so scales raise many false alarms yet miss many who act. Ask instead about ideation, intent, plan and means, and build a safety plan.

For violence, past violence is the best predictor, with youth, male sex, substance misuse and threats. Structured professional judgement such as the HCR-20 (Historical, Clinical and Risk management items) beats unaided judgement; dynamic factors, unlike static history, can be treated.

Behavioural assessment

Behavioural assessment asks what a behaviour does for the person here and now. Its core, functional analysis, rests on the operant conditioning of Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990): consequences maintain behaviour. The target behaviour is defined operationally and its ABC recorded: Antecedent, Behaviour, Consequence. A boy who throws his glass when told to clear it, and so escapes the chore, is being reinforced. The SORC model adds Organism variables (biology, thoughts, learning history) between Stimulus and Response.

Data come from behavioural interviews (what, when, how often), self-monitoring (the client's own diary, essential for private acts such as purging), role play (the client enacts a problem situation, such as refusing a request, while the clinician watches), and observation that is naturalistic (home, school), analogue (a simulated clinic situation) or participant (by a parent, teacher or nurse already present). Event recording counts occurrences; interval recording notes whether the behaviour occurred in each short interval; time sampling observes at chosen moments. Reactivity is behaviour changing because it is observed; observer drift is observers' gradual shift in applying definitions, corrected by retraining.

Exam trap

Functional analysis is based on operant conditioning, not classical conditioning (Section 7.4), humanistic or psychodynamic principles. Its ABC is Antecedent, Behaviour, Consequence; Albert Ellis's ABC is Activating event, Belief, Consequence (Section 7.5).

Case formulation

A diagnosis places problems in a category: what the person has. A case formulation is an individualised, testable hypothesis about why this person has these problems now (causes, triggers, maintaining factors), revised as therapy proceeds. The commonest framework is the 5 Ps: presenting problem, predisposing factors (early vulnerabilities), precipitating factors (triggers), perpetuating factors (what maintains it) and protective factors (strengths), as Figure 7.2 shows for Ravi. Filling each P with biological, psychological and social entries gives a biopsychosocial formulation, after George Libman Engel (1913 to 1999), who proposed the model in Science in 1977.

In a cognitive-behavioural formulation, the cognitive conceptualisation diagram of Judith S. Beck (1995) links childhood data to core beliefs ("I am a failure"), rules ("If I fail the exam, I am worthless") and compensatory strategies, and traces each situation through automatic thought, meaning, emotion and behaviour. A psychodynamic formulation describes core unconscious conflicts, defences and repeating relationship patterns rooted in early life.

A differential diagnosis lists the conditions that could explain the picture and rules them in or out, physical and substance causes first (hypothyroidism can mimic depression). The clinical report answers the referral question in plain language (sources, history, MSE, results, formulation, diagnosis, recommendations); feedback begins with strengths and ends with an agreed plan.

Exam trap

A formulation explains: it hypothesises causes, precipitants and maintaining factors. Classifying symptoms by DSM-5 is diagnosis, predicting course is prognosis, and ruling out organic causes is differential diagnosis.

7.3 Psychodynamic Therapies and Their DescendantsHigh yield · 39 Qs

Every therapy in this section assumes that hidden forces pushing against one another (hence dynamic) shape what we feel and do, and that bringing them into awareness relieves suffering. The idea began with one patient in Vienna, became Freud's psychoanalysis, and then branched into a large family.

Freud's psychoanalysis as a treatment

Key study: Breuer's Anna O. (1880 to 1882)

The physician Josef Breuer (1842 to 1925) treated Bertha Pappenheim, a young woman with hysterical paralyses and weeks in which she could not drink water. When, in a self-induced trance, she talked through the moment a symptom began and released the feeling she had swallowed then, the symptom eased: her "talking cure". Breuer and Freud published her as "Anna O." in Studies on Hysteria (1895). Criticism: she was not in fact cured, and spent months in sanatoria afterwards.

Breuer's procedure was the cathartic method: hypnotise the patient, take her back to the moment a symptom began and let her relive it. Abreaction is the discharge of the emotion attached to a particular repressed memory by reliving it; catharsis (Greek, "purging") is the release and relief that follow. Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939), the father of psychoanalysis, then dropped hypnosis: many patients could not be hypnotised, its cures faded, and it went round resistance instead of exposing it. At Nancy in 1889 he had watched Hippolyte Bernheim (1840 to 1919) recover "forgotten" memories from waking patients.

The aim of analysis is to make the unconscious conscious, or, in Freud's formula of 1933, "Where id was, there ego shall be". Change comes through insight, an emotionally felt understanding of how present feelings repeat past conflicts, and the target is character, not just the symptom. The patient lies on a couch with the analyst out of sight behind, four or five times a week for years; once- or twice-weekly, face-to-face work on the same principles is psychodynamic psychotherapy. Every analyst first undergoes a training analysis.

The patient's one duty is the fundamental rule: say whatever comes to mind, however trivial or embarrassing, without censoring it. Following the rule is free association, the defining technique of psychoanalysis. When conscious steering relaxes, Freud argued, thoughts follow paths laid down by unconscious wishes, and the places where association stalls point to what is repressed. The analyst brings evenly suspended attention; neutrality, taking no side in the patient's conflicts and giving no advice (the "blank screen"); and abstinence, declining to gratify the patient's wishes for love, praise or comfort so that they stay alive to be understood. Abstinence here has nothing to do with alcohol.

Dreams, Freud wrote in The Interpretation of Dreams (1900), are "the royal road" to the unconscious. The manifest content, the dream as remembered, disguises the latent content, the hidden wish, through the dream-work: condensation, displacement, symbolisation and secondary revision. The patient free-associates to each element to recover it.

Sooner or later the patient arrives late, forgets a session or changes the subject just as something painful approaches. This is resistance: everything in the patient that opposes the treatment and access to unconscious material. It is repression showing itself in the room, so in analysis of resistance the analyst points out the obstruction and explores with the patient what it protects against. Interpretation links what the patient says or does to its unconscious meaning; it is prepared by confrontation and clarification and aimed at the defence before the content it hides. Because one insight rarely changes a lifelong pattern, working through (Freud, 1914) re-examines the same conflicts and interpretations in context after context until lasting change in behaviour and character follows.

Remember

Origin of disorder by school: psychodynamic, unconscious conflict from childhood emotional experience; behavioural, faulty learning (Section 7.4); cognitive, disturbed thought patterns (Section 7.5); humanistic, failure to fulfil one's potential (Section 7.6).

Exam trap

Saying whatever comes to mind, uncensored, is free association; systematic desensitisation, flooding and modelling are behaviour therapy (Section 7.4), and paradoxical intention is Frankl's and Adler's. Obstructing the analysis while the therapist explores why is analysis of resistance. Working through is never "giving the interpretation once", advice or ending after one insight.

Transference and countertransference

In the Dora case (1905) Freud saw that patients come to feel about the analyst as they once felt about their parents. Transference is the patient's unconscious redirection onto the therapist of feelings and expectations that belong to significant figures from the past. It may be positive (affection, idealisation, erotic love) or negative (hostility, suspicion). When the patient's neurosis is re-created around the analyst, it becomes a transference neurosis, whose resolution is, in classical theory, the cure.

Countertransference, named by Freud in 1910, is the therapist's emotional reaction to the patient arising from the therapist's own unresolved conflicts, a blind spot to be recognised (hence the training analysis). Since Paula Heimann's paper of 1950 the term also covers all the therapist's feelings towards the patient, used as information about the patient. Negative countertransference is the therapist's hostility, irritation or boredom towards a patient; positive countertransference is over-involvement or a wish to rescue. Both differ from the working alliance (Ralph Greenson, 1965), the patient's rational collaboration with the therapist (Section 7.8).

Exam trap

Direction decides the term: patient to therapist is transference; therapist to patient is countertransference. "The therapist's emotional reaction to the client" is never transference, "the client's projection onto the therapist" is never countertransference, and a therapist's negative feelings are negative countertransference, not negative transference. Fear of the unknown or low motivation are sources of resistance, not countertransference. Managing both is the hallmark of psychodynamic models, not of behaviour modification, CBT or solution-focused therapy.

In Freud's language of psychic energy, libido is the energy of the sexual (later, life) drive; cathexis is its investment in the mental representation of a particular object, person or idea; fixation is libido left attached to an earlier psychosexual stage; and sublimation redirects a drive into a socially valued aim. Ego psychology, object relations and self psychology

Freud's successors kept the unconscious but changed what they listened for. Ego psychology turned from the id to the ego. Anna Freud (1895 to 1982), in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936), made the analysis of defences central and pioneered child analysis; Heinz Hartmann (1894 to 1970) described a conflict-free ego sphere that develops for adaptation (1939); and Erik Erikson (1902 to 1994) added psychosocial stages and the adolescent identity crisis (Chapters 4 and 6).

Object relations theory holds that the mind is built from internalised representations of self and others ("objects") and of the relationships between them. Melanie Klein (1882 to 1960) analysed children through play. In her paranoid-schizoid position the infant manages terror by splitting the world into wholly good and wholly bad objects and by projective identification (placing unwanted parts of the self in another and then controlling that person); in the later depressive position good and bad are seen as one person, bringing guilt and reparation. Paranoia revives the first position, so its defence is splitting. Donald Winnicott (1896 to 1971) described the good-enough mother, closely attuned at first and then failing tolerably, who lets a true self grow; the transitional object, a blanket or soft toy that bridges inner and outer reality; and therapy as a holding environment, as reliable as a mother's arms. Ronald Fairbairn (1889 to 1964) held that libido is object-seeking, not pleasure-seeking.

Margaret Mahler (1897 to 1985) traced separation-individuation: a normal autistic phase, a symbiotic phase of fusion with the mother, then differentiation, practising, rapprochement and object constancy by about three years. Her theory of psychosis (1952) traces schizophrenia to these first phases: the person remains in the autistic phase and fails to develop an adequate, stable symbiosis (Silvano Arieti, Ping-Nie Pao and Edith Jacobson wrote other analytic accounts of psychosis). Otto Kernberg (born 1928) described borderline personality organisation (identity diffusion, splitting, intact reality testing) and developed transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), which interprets the split images of self and others as they appear in the transference.

Heinz Kohut (1913 to 1981) founded self psychology with The Analysis of the Self (1971). The child needs selfobjects who provide mirroring (confirming the child's worth), an idealised figure to look up to and merge with and, in his later work, twinship (feeling like others). Chronic failure of mirroring and idealising leaves a fragile self that fragments into emptiness and rage. In therapy these needs return as mirror, idealising and twinship transferences, met by the analyst's empathic attunement.

Relational psychoanalysis (Stephen Mitchell, 1946 to 2000, with Jay Greenberg, 1983) treats therapy as a two-person process in which the analyst cannot be neutral. John Bowlby's (1907 to 1990) attachment theory inspired attachment-based therapies in which the therapist is a secure base for revising internal working models. Mentalisation-based treatment (MBT), by Anthony Bateman and Peter Fonagy for borderline personality disorder (first trial 1999), strengthens mentalisation, understanding one's own and others' behaviour in terms of feelings, wishes and intentions, a capacity that collapses under attachment stress.

Exam trap

"Object relations" means internalised representations of self and others and their relationships, not the id-ego-superego relation, defences or the genital stage. A client who feels empty, "no one", afraid of abandonment and hungry for admiration is explained by failed parental mirroring and idealising leading to a fragmented self: that is Kohut's self psychology even when the stem says "object relations". Oral fixation, an unresolved Oedipus complex and a harsh superego are classical Freudian explanations of neurotic conflict and guilt (Chapter 4). For paranoia "as given by Klein", answer splitting; projection is Freud's classical explanation of paranoia.

Interpersonal psychotherapy

Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) was developed by Gerald Klerman (1928 to 1992) and his wife Myrna Weissman in the manual Interpersonal Psychotherapy of Depression (1984). Rooted in the ideas of Adolf Meyer, Sullivan and Bowlby, it works on current relationships rather than childhood conflict, does not interpret the transference, and treats depression as a medical illness, giving the patient the sick role.

IPT runs 12 to 16 weekly sessions in three phases. The initial phase diagnoses and explains depression, reviews key relationships (the interpersonal inventory) and picks one or two of four problem areas: grief (complicated bereavement), interpersonal role disputes (with a spouse, parent or employer), role transitions (marriage, a new job, retirement, illness) and interpersonal deficits (social isolation). The middle phase works on that area through encouragement of affect, clarification, communication analysis (who said what, exactly), role play and decision analysis. Termination treats ending as a small grief and credits the patient's own competence. IPT matched cognitive therapy in the NIMH depression trial (Section 7.8), meta-analyses rate it about equal to CBT, and adaptations treat depressed adolescents (IPT-A), perinatal depression and bulimia.

Exam trap

Asked what IPT does NOT include beside grief, role conflict and role transition, choose "role playing and role reversal": the other three are its problem areas. The fine print: IPT therapists do use role play after communication analysis; role reversal is psychodrama's (Jacob Moreno, Section 7.7). IPT is Klerman and Weissman's; MBT, Bateman and Fonagy's; TFP, Kernberg's; Lorna Smith Benjamin developed interpersonal reconstructive therapy.

Jung, Adler, Horney and Sullivan as therapists

Freud's early dissenters built their own therapies (their personality theories are in Chapter 4). Carl Gustav Jung (1875 to 1961) founded analytical psychology. Beneath the personal unconscious he placed a collective unconscious of inherited archetypes: the persona (the social mask), the shadow, the anima (the unconscious feminine side of a man), the animus (the unconscious masculine side of a woman) and the self. Jung read dreams as direct, creative messages, not disguises. In amplification the analyst enlarges a dream image with parallels from myth and religion; in active imagination the patient, awake, holds a dialogue with a dream or fantasy figure. The goal is individuation, becoming a whole, integrated self.

Alfred Adler (1870 to 1937) left Freud in 1911 and called his school individual psychology. From organ inferiority (1907: compensation for a weak organ) he moved to universal feelings of inferiority that drive a striving for superiority; when they become overwhelming they form an inferiority complex. Therapy is an equal, educational partnership. Lifestyle assessment explores the family constellation, birth order (the dethroned first-born, the competitive second, the pampered youngest) and early recollections, which reveal the present style of life. Its techniques are encouragement, the core one; acting as if (behaving as though one already had the feared ability); spitting in the client's soup (exposing a behaviour's hidden payoff so that it loses its appeal); the push-button technique (Harold Mosak: picturing a pleasant and then an unpleasant memory to show that we create our own feelings); and paradoxical intention. Adlerians do not analyse transference.

Karen Horney (1885 to 1952) traced neurosis to basic anxiety, met by moving towards, against or away from people; her therapy dismantled the idealised self-image and its "tyranny of the shoulds". Harry Stack Sullivan (1892 to 1949) made the therapist a participant observer of interpersonal patterns, including parataxic distortion (seeing others through the template of earlier relationships), and so influenced IPT.

Exam trap

Analysis of transference is the technique an Adlerian counsellor would not use; acting as if, the push button and encouragement are all Adlerian. Collective unconscious, anima and animus: Jung. Individual psychology, inferiority complex, organ inferiority: Adler, not Jung, Horney, Frankl (logotherapy, Section 7.6) or Erikson.

Transactional analysis

Transactional analysis (TA) was founded by Eric Berne (1910 to 1970), who took the term ego state from his analyst Paul Federn, in Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy (1961) and Games People Play (1964). It proceeds through structural, transactional, game and script analysis.

Personality has three ego states. The Parent holds attitudes and behaviour copied from parents and parental figures, critical or nurturing; the Adult processes present reality objectively; the Child holds feelings and responses from childhood, free or adapted. A transaction is a stimulus from one ego state and a response from another (Figure 7.4). Complementary transactions run parallel and talk continues. Crossed ones stop it: "Where are my keys?" (Adult to Adult) answered by "Why do you always blame me?" (Child to Parent). Ulterior transactions carry a hidden message beneath the social one.

People need strokes, units of recognition, and prefer negative strokes to none. A game is a series of ulterior transactions with a predictable payoff of bad feelings, such as "Why Don't You, Yes But", in which every suggestion is rejected. A life script is an unconscious life plan decided in childhood under parental injunctions ("Don't be close", "Don't grow up", sent nonverbally from the parent's Child) and counterinjunctions ("Be perfect", "Work hard", spoken from the parent's Parent); Bob and Mary Goulding's redecision therapy helps clients revise them. Scripts rest on one of four life positions: I'm OK, you're OK (the healthy one); I'm OK, you're not OK; I'm not OK, you're OK; and I'm not OK, you're not OK, the first made famous by Thomas Harris's best-seller I'm OK, You're OK (1969).

Exam trap

Injunctions, counterinjunctions, games, scripts and strokes all mean transactional analysis, not Gestalt, REBT or person-centred therapy (Sections 7.5 and 7.6). The ego state copied from parents is the Parent; "persona" is Jung's archetype, not an ego state.

Supportive therapy, hypnosis, narcoanalysis and the evidence

Psychodynamic work runs along a continuum from expressive (uncovering conflict by interpretation, for patients with ego strength) to supportive. Supportive psychotherapy strengthens defences instead of dismantling them, through a warm relationship, reassurance, advice, encouragement, ventilation of feelings, education and practical changes in the environment. It is the mainstay when the ego is overwhelmed: in crisis management, bereavement, acute psychosis and severe physical illness.

Hypnotherapy uses hypnosis, focused attention with heightened suggestibility. It descends from Franz Anton Mesmer's (1734 to 1815) "animal magnetism" and was named by James Braid in 1843. Today it assists the treatment of pain, habits and anxiety, but memories "recovered" under it are unreliable. Narcoanalysis gives a slow intravenous barbiturate (sodium amobarbital or thiopentone) so that a mute, stuporous or amnesic patient talks and abreacts, as in Second World War battle neuroses. It is no "truth serum": drugged people can still lie and fantasise.

Indian context

In Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010) the Supreme Court held compulsory narcoanalysis, polygraph and brain-mapping unconstitutional (Articles 20(3) and 21); they need consent, and the statements made are not admissible as evidence.

Critics from Hans Eysenck (1952; Section 7.8) to the philosopher Karl Popper, who called its theory unfalsifiable, attacked psychoanalysis as unproven, long and costly. Yet meta-analyses reviewed by Jonathan Shedler (2010) show psychodynamic therapy matching other evidence-based therapies, with gains that grow after treatment ends; short-term dynamic therapy and IPT work for depression, and MBT and TFP for borderline personality disorder. Evidence for the specific mechanism, insight, remains weaker.

7.4 Behaviour TherapyHigh yield · 88 Qs

A child terrified of rabbits, a woman who washes until her hands bleed, a boy with autism who cannot ask for water: behaviour therapy sees in each of them learned behaviour that can be unlearned or replaced. Building on the learning principles of Section 1.6, we trace where the approach came from, then take its techniques family by family.

Origins, assumptions and functional analysis

Behaviour therapy is the application of experimentally established principles of learning to the assessment and treatment of psychological problems. Its spur was a 1952 paper by Hans Jürgen Eysenck (1916 to 1997) of the Maudsley Hospital, London, which claimed that about two-thirds of neurotic patients improved within two years whether or not they had psychotherapy (the debate belongs to Section 7.8). If talking did no better than time, Eysenck argued, the clinic needed a treatment derived from learning theory, aimed at the symptom and testable. His creed was blunt: get rid of the symptom and you have eliminated the neurosis.

The new therapy grew from three roots in the 1950s. In Johannesburg, Joseph Wolpe, Arnold Allan Lazarus (1932 to 2013) and Stanley Rachman worked from classical conditioning. At the Maudsley, Eysenck supplied the theory and Monte Bernard Shapiro the method of treating each patient as a single-case experiment. In the United States, Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990) and his students carried operant methods onto hospital wards and into schools, a strand later called applied behaviour analysis (ABA): operant principles, carefully measured, used to change socially important behaviour. The name behaviour therapy first appeared in a 1953 hospital report by Ogden Lindsley, Skinner and Harry Solomon, reached a journal in Lazarus's 1958 paper and was popularised by Eysenck's 1959 paper.

The shared assumptions all contradicted psychoanalysis. Problem behaviour is learned by the same principles as normal behaviour and can be unlearned. The behaviour is the problem, not the symptom of a hidden conflict, so therapy aims to eliminate it and build adaptive behaviour in its place. What maintains a problem now matters more than how it began, and everything is defined and measured. Applied to anxiety, this gives the learning model of disorder, best known as the two-factor theory of O. Hobart Mowrer (1947): a fear is acquired by classical conditioning and maintained because avoiding the feared object reduces fear and is therefore negatively reinforced. Avoidance stops the person ever learning that the object is harmless, which is why exposure works.

Every case starts with a functional analysis: identifying the observable events that trigger and maintain a specific problem behaviour. Its simplest form is the ABC model of Antecedents, Behaviour (defined so that two observers would agree) and Consequences; Frederick Kanfer and George Saslow (1965) expanded it into SORKC (stimulus, organism, response, contingency, consequence). After recording a baseline, the frequency, duration or intensity of the behaviour before treatment, the therapist asks what the behaviour achieves. If Arjun's tantrums at homework time get him sent to his room, they are rewarded by escape, and the "punishment" feeds them. Treatment follows the function, not the form.

Exam trap

Behaviour therapies apply learning principles to eliminate the unwanted behaviour; "treat the underlying causes" is the psychodynamic aim, "improve learning and insight" that of insight therapies and "communication and social sensitivity" that of encounter groups (Section 7.7). Behaviour analysis rests on operant conditioning, not classical conditioning, modelling, cognitive learning or dream analysis.

Systematic desensitisation

Can a learned fear be unlearned? In 1924 Mary Cover Jones (1897 to 1987), a student of John Watson, treated Peter, a boy of almost three who was terrified of rabbits. He watched other children play happily with a rabbit (modelling), and while he ate a favourite food a caged rabbit was brought a little closer each day, never so close that he stopped eating, until he let it nibble his fingers. This is counterconditioning: attaching to a stimulus a new response that is incompatible with the old one, so that the person learns the stimulus is no longer threatening.

Key study: Wolpe's cats (late 1940s)

Joseph Wolpe (1915 to 1997), a South African psychiatrist, shocked cats in a small cage until they were too anxious to eat there even when hungry. He then fed them in a room very unlike the laboratory, then in rooms progressively more like it, and finally in the cage, until the anxiety vanished. A response incompatible with anxiety, evoked while the anxiety was weak, had eliminated it. Cats are not neurotic patients, which is why later trials with people mattered.

Wolpe borrowed a term from the physiologist Charles Sherrington. Reciprocal inhibition is the principle that two incompatible responses cannot occur together, so a response antagonistic to anxiety, evoked in the presence of anxiety-provoking stimuli, suppresses the anxiety and weakens its bond to them. His main inhibitor was deep relaxation; others were assertion, sexual arousal and, in children, eating. On it he built systematic desensitisation (Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition, 1958), which has three steps.

  1. Relaxation training. The client learns Edmund Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation over about six sessions, practising 10 to 15 minutes twice a day.
  2. Hierarchy construction. An anxiety hierarchy ranks 10 to 20 feared situations from least to most frightening, each rated on the subjective units of distress scale (SUDS), Wolpe's self-rating from 0 (calm) to 100 (the worst imaginable), also called units of disturbance or discomfort.
  3. Desensitisation proper, the step of gradual exposure. With the client relaxed (SUDS below about 25) the therapist describes the lowest item; at any anxiety the client raises a finger and relaxation is restored. Only when an item evokes no anxiety does the client move up (Figure 7.5).

Wolpe worked in imagination, called in vitro desensitisation (Latin, "in glass", that is, in the consulting room); in vivo desensitisation ("in life") climbs the hierarchy in real situations, which generalises better, while imagination suits fears that cannot be staged, such as flying. Gordon Paul (1966) found desensitisation better than insight therapy for fear of public speaking, but dismantling studies showed that it worked without relaxation or a strict hierarchy, never without exposure. Reciprocal inhibition is Wolpe's theory of why it works, not a proven mechanism.

Exam trap

Systematic desensitisation is Wolpe's (not Skinner's, Pavlov's, Watson's, Rogers's, Perls's, Beck's, Freud's or Bandura's) and rests on reciprocal inhibition, not operant extinction, shaping, the law of effect, habituation alone, generalisation, vicarious reinforcement, punishment, adaptation to stress or catharsis. Its relaxation is Jacobson's; cognitive restructuring is not one of its steps; it treats phobia, not hallucination, delusion or depression. SUDS is keyed to desensitisation (IHBAS 2020), though all exposure therapies now use it; multimodal therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy and mindfulness-based stress reduction belong to Section 7.5. The Fear Survey Schedule (Wolpe and Peter Lang, 1964), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale are questionnaires (Chapter 2), not the hierarchy scale.

Exposure, flooding and response prevention

If exposure is the active ingredient, why not go straight to it? Exposure therapy is repeated or prolonged confrontation with a feared stimulus, without escape, until the fear subsides. It works through habituation, the reduction in anxiety through repeated or prolonged encounter with a stimulus, and through extinction, as the stimulus keeps appearing without the feared outcome. Exposure may be graded or intense, in vivo or imaginal. Interoceptive exposure deliberately provokes feared bodily sensations (spinning in a chair, overbreathing) in panic disorder, and virtual reality exposure uses computer-generated scenes for fears of flying, heights or combat.

Flooding exposes the client at once, in real life or in imagination, to the most feared stimulus at full intensity, with no hierarchy and no relaxation, preventing escape until the anxiety peaks and subsides: the person afraid of heights stands on the balcony for as long as it takes. Implosive therapy (Thomas Stampfl, 1966; set out with Donald Levis in 1967) is imaginal flooding with exaggerated, horrific scenes enriched with presumed psychodynamic themes; it is now rare. Both work by extinction, and both are so unpleasant that clients must choose them knowingly.

Exposure and response prevention (ERP) is the first-line behavioural treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Victor Meyer described it in London in 1966, and Edna Foa and colleagues refined and tested it. The client touches the "contaminated" object and is prevented from washing. The ritual brings quick relief, so it is negatively reinforced, and it stops the person learning that the anxiety would pass anyway (Figure 7.6). Blocking it allows habituation and extinction, and ERP helps more than 70 percent of patients. Foa also built prolonged exposure for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD): imaginal reliving of the trauma memory plus in vivo exposure to safe but avoided reminders.

A debated relative of exposure is eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), which Francine Shapiro (1948 to 2019) developed from a 1987 observation and published in 1989, initially for PTSD. The client holds a traumatic image, its negative belief and its body sensation in mind while following the therapist's fingers back and forth; this bilateral stimulation (eye movements, taps or tones) comes in sets of about 15 to 30. Shapiro's standard protocol has eight phases, always in this order: (1) history taking, (2) preparation, (3) assessment of the target image, negative belief and distress rating, (4) desensitisation with the eye movements, (5) installation of a positive belief, (6) body scan for leftover tension, (7) closure and (8) re-evaluation at the next session. EMDR works about as well as trauma-focused cognitive behaviour therapy, but the eye movements seem to add little, since holding the memory in mind is itself imaginal exposure. As critics put it, what is effective in EMDR is not new, and what is new is not effective.

Exam trap

Flooding means full intensity with no hierarchy and no relaxation, in vivo or imaginal; pairing the feared stimulus with relaxation step by step is desensitisation, pairing it with shock is aversion therapy, and replacing irrational beliefs (Ellis) or cognitive restructuring is cognitive therapy (Section 7.5). ERP rests on habituation and extinction, not counterconditioning or reciprocal inhibition, and is first-line for OCD, not phobia, panic, depression, schizophrenia or personality disorder. EMDR is Shapiro's, for PTSD; prolonged exposure is Foa's, cognitive processing therapy Patricia Resick's and dialectical behaviour therapy Marsha Linehan's (Section 7.5). Bessel van der Kolk researches trauma, Pat Ogden founded sensorimotor psychotherapy and Joseph LeDoux maps the brain's fear circuits.

Aversion therapy, covert sensitisation and the bell and pad

Counterconditioning can also run in reverse. Aversion therapy pairs an unwanted behaviour, or the stimulus that tempts it, with an unpleasant stimulus, so that by classical conditioning it loses its appeal: a nausea-inducing drug is given just before the patient tastes alcohol (chemical aversion), or a brief shock accompanies a cigarette or a deviant sexual image (electrical aversion). It has been used for alcohol use, smoking and paraphilias. It inflicts pain, loses many clients, often fades outside the clinic and was misused against homosexuality, so today it needs informed consent and is tried only after milder methods fail.

Joseph Cautela (1967) moved the procedure into the imagination. In covert sensitisation the client imagines lifting a glass of whisky, then vividly imagines vomiting over himself in front of his family, then the relief of turning away; no real aversive stimulus is used.

Exam trap

An undesirable behaviour imagined together with an imagined unpleasant consequence is covert sensitisation (Cautela), not flooding, implosion or desensitisation. Aversion therapy works by pairing (classical conditioning), not by intermittent reward, ignoring the behaviour (extinction) or restructuring beliefs.

The oldest classical technique is the bell-and-pad method for bed-wetting (nocturnal enuresis), built in 1938 by Orval Hobart Mowrer and Willie Mae Mowrer. Urine reaching a pad under the sheet completes a circuit and rings a bell; after repeated pairings, bladder tension alone wakes the child. Treatment takes 6 to 12 weeks, and the alarm remains first-line.

Operant techniques that build behaviour

Operant techniques work on consequences (the grid of reinforcement and punishment, and the schedules, are in Section 1.6). In therapy, positive reinforcement means giving something the person values immediately after a target behaviour, every time while the behaviour is new and then intermittently so that it lasts.

A behaviour that never occurs must be built. Shaping is reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour: for a silent child with autism, first eye contact, then any sound, then "wa", then "water". Chaining links the steps of a task, such as dressing, into a sequence; in backward chaining the last step is taught first, so every practice ends in success. A prompt is an extra cue (a hint, a gesture, a guiding hand) that makes the right response likely, and fading withdraws it gradually until the natural cue alone controls the behaviour.

The Premack principle of David Premack (1959) states that a more probable activity can reinforce a less probable one: video games after homework, or grandma's rule, vegetables before dessert. A token economy rewards target behaviours with tokens (points, stars, chips) that are later exchanged for backup reinforcers such as snacks or privileges. Tokens are secondary reinforcers, valued only for what they buy, and they bridge the delay between behaviour and reward.

Key study: Ayllon and Azrin's token economy (1968)

Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin ran a ward of chronic psychotic women at Anna State Hospital, Illinois. Tokens earned for self-care and ward jobs bought privileges such as privacy or a walk in the grounds. When tokens were handed out regardless of work, the work almost stopped; when the contingency returned, so did the work. Their book The Token Economy (1968) launched the method, though gains often fade outside the ward, and basic needs must never become backup reinforcers.

Contingency management systematically arranges reinforcing consequences for target behaviours, as when clients with substance use disorders earn vouchers for drug-free urine samples. A contingency contract puts the arrangement in a signed agreement: "Ravi studies two hours each weekday; his parents allow cricket on Sunday."

Exam trap

The token economy is operant (secondary reinforcers), so it is the treatment NOT derived from classical conditioning; flooding, desensitisation and aversion therapy are classical. Its developers were Ayllon and Azrin; Amity 2023 keyed Skinner, who supplied only the principles. Premack runs one way: the high-probability behaviour reinforces the low-probability one, never the reverse; it is a behaviour modification technique for children, not a theory of relationships, language or parenting. "Pleasant consequences make a behaviour likely to recur" is Thorndike's law of effect, and continuous reinforcement is the least resistant to extinction (Section 1.6).

Operant techniques that reduce behaviour, and ABA

To reduce a behaviour, therapists start with the least restrictive method. Extinction withholds the reinforcer that maintains a behaviour: parents who rewarded tantrums with attention now ignore them. Expect an extinction burst, a temporary rise when reinforcement stops; giving in at its peak teaches that louder tantrums pay. Extinction works best with differential reinforcement, which reinforces something else instead (Table 7.11).

Overcorrection targets repetitive aggressive behaviour in autism; bed-wetting needs the bell and pad. Lovaas treated autism, not aphasia, depression or alcoholism, and a picture card to request a break is ABA, not CBT, play therapy or psychoanalysis.

Modelling, assertiveness and social skills

Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) turned observational learning into therapy. In live modelling the client watches a real person perform the feared or missing behaviour; in symbolic modelling the model appears on film or in a story; in participant modelling the therapist models the behaviour and then physically guides the client through it. For snake phobia (Bandura, Edward Blanchard and Brunhilde Ritter, 1969), participant modelling beat both symbolic modelling and systematic desensitisation. In behavioural rehearsal the client practises the new behaviour in role-play, with feedback, before trying it in life.

Assertiveness training, pioneered by Andrew Salter (Conditioned Reflex Therapy, 1949) and developed by Wolpe and Lazarus, teaches people to express wishes and feelings directly while respecting others. Passive behaviour surrenders one's rights (silence when someone jumps the bus queue), aggressive behaviour violates other people's (shouting abuse), and assertive behaviour defends one's own without attack ("Please join the queue; we were here first"). Social skills training teaches eye contact, conversation and refusal through instruction, modelling, rehearsal, feedback and homework, in autism, social anxiety and schizophrenia, where antipsychotic medication remains front-line.

Relaxation, biofeedback and self-management

Several methods teach control of one's own body. Edmund Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation (1929, enlarged in 1938) tenses and releases muscle groups in turn; Johannes Heinrich Schultz's autogenic training (1932) uses self-suggestions of heaviness and warmth; breathing retraining slows the overbreathing of panic; and the applied relaxation of Lars-Göran Öst (1987) trains rapid relaxation on cue, used at the first sign of anxiety in panic, generalised anxiety and phobias.

Biofeedback makes a bodily process the person cannot normally sense visible or audible, so that he can learn to control it. Its basis was Neal Miller's 1960s report that rats could change heart rate and other autonomic responses through operant conditioning: the autonomic nervous system could come under voluntary control. Electromyographic (EMG) feedback of muscle tension treats tension-type headache and back pain, thermal feedback of hand temperature treats migraine and Raynaud's disease, and EEG neurofeedback of brain waves is used for ADHD and epilepsy. An early device was the perineometer of Arnold Kegel (1948), a pressure gauge that let a patient watch her pelvic floor squeeze.

Habit reversal training (HRT), from Azrin and Gregory Nunn (1973), is the treatment of choice for tics and habits such as hair pulling and nail biting. Awareness training teaches the client to detect each occurrence and the urge before it; competing response training teaches a response physically incompatible with the habit, inconspicuous and held for about a minute or until the urge passes (clenching the fists against hair pulling); motivation comes from reviewing the habit's costs and from family praise. A variant, regulated breathing, was applied to stuttering. In negative practice (massed practice), proposed by Knight Dunlap (1932), the habit is performed deliberately and repeatedly until fatigue makes it aversive. Thought stopping, popularised by Wolpe, interrupts an unwanted thought with "Stop!", first shouted, later silent; it has faded because suppressed thoughts rebound.

Self-monitoring, the systematic recording of one's own target behaviour, often changes that behaviour before treatment begins; this reactivity spoils the baseline but helps therapy. In self-reinforcement the client rewards himself on meeting his own goal, completing Kanfer's sequence of self-monitoring, self-evaluation and self-reinforcement.

Behavioural activation (BA) treats depression by re-engaging clients in meaningful, rewarding activities. Peter Lewinsohn proposed in the 1970s that depression follows a loss of response-contingent positive reinforcement. Neil Jacobson and colleagues (1996) found the activation component of cognitive therapy as effective as the whole package, and Jacobson and Christopher Martell developed BA in its own right: activity monitoring, scheduling and graded tasks that replace avoidance.

For insomnia, stimulus control (Richard Bootzin, 1972) re-links the bed with sleep: bed only when sleepy and only for sleep and sex, leave it if not asleep within about 10 to 20 minutes, a fixed rising time and no naps. Sleep restriction (Arthur Spielman, 1987) limits time in bed to the time actually slept, extending it as sleep consolidates. Paradoxical intention, from the logotherapist Viktor Frankl (Section 7.6), asks the person to do or wish for the very thing he fears: the insomniac tries hard to stay awake, which removes the performance anxiety that kept him awake. Frankl's dereflection instead turns attention away from the symptom.

Exam trap

Operant control of autonomic functions is biofeedback, not hypnosis (Section 1.5), sublimation (a defence mechanism) or desensitisation; EMG feedback suits tension-type headache, and Kegel's perineometer was early biofeedback. Repeating a habit to fatigue is negative practice, not the competing response of habit reversal. HRT is for tics; OCD needs ERP. Doing or wishing the opposite of the goal is paradoxical intention, not stimulus control, sleep restriction, dereflection, negative practice or reciprocal inhibition. Behavioural activation means engaging in meaningful activity, not analysing childhood, relaxation or hypnosis.

Parents, classrooms and an evaluation

Gerald Patterson of the Oregon Social Learning Center described a coercive cycle at home: a parent makes a demand, the child whines or hits, the parent gives in (negatively reinforcing the aggression) and the child stops (negatively reinforcing the surrender). Parent management training (PMT) teaches parents to give clear commands, attend to and praise good behaviour, reward it with points or stickers, ignore minor misbehaviour, and use brief time-out or loss of privileges for serious misbehaviour; negative reinforcement is what it removes, not what it teaches. Classroom management applies the same principles at school: clear rules, praise, planned ignoring, token systems, daily report cards and group contingencies such as the Good Behaviour Game.

Behaviour therapy is a first choice for specific phobias, OCD, panic, enuresis, tics and habits, insomnia, conduct problems and skills in autism, and behavioural activation rivals cognitive therapy for depression; in psychosis and personality disorder it is an adjunct. Psychoanalysts predicted symptom substitution: remove a symptom without resolving its underlying conflict and another will take its place. Follow-ups from Paul's 1966 trial onwards found little sign of it. Aversive methods raise the sharpest ethical questions: they demand informed consent, the least restrictive alternative first, supervision and special protection for children and institutionalised patients.

7.5 Cognitive and Cognitive-Behavioural Therapies, and the Third WaveHigh yield · 69 Qs

Two students score 48 out of 90 on the same mock test. One is disappointed and studies her mistakes; the other decides he was never good enough and stops opening his books. Same event, opposite reactions: what differed was what each made of the score. That observation underlies every therapy in this section, from the founders, Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, to the "third wave", which asks clients not to argue with their thoughts but to change their relationship with them.

The cognitive turn and the three waves

The term cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) covers a family of treatments that share three propositions: thinking affects emotion and behaviour; thinking can be monitored and changed; and changing maladaptive thought patterns relieves distress, even when the events that triggered it cannot be changed. The idea is old. The Stoic philosopher Epictetus (about 50 to 135 CE) wrote that people are disturbed not by things but by the views they take of them.

In the 1950s and 1960s two therapists, Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, independently concluded that faulty thinking drives emotional disturbance, and both are counted as founders of the cognitive tradition. Behaviour therapists (Section 7.4) joined them in the 1970s; Donald Meichenbaum's Cognitive-Behavior Modification (1977) gave the hybrid its name. Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) supplied a bridge in 1977 with self-efficacy, a person's belief that he or she can carry out the actions a situation demands. He argued that all effective treatments work largely by raising it, which made self-efficacy the classic cognitive-behavioural explanation of how people change.

In 2004 Steven C. Hayes (born 1948) described three waves. The first, behaviour therapy (1950s onwards), applied learning principles to behaviour. The second, the cognitive therapies of Ellis and Beck, changed the content of thoughts from irrational or distorted to realistic. The third wave (from the 1990s: dialectical behaviour therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and compassion-focused, metacognitive and functional analytic therapies) changes a person's relationship to thoughts and feelings: noticing and accepting them while acting on one's values. All CBTs are time-limited, focused on present problems, structured, collaborative and educational; they rely on homework and measure symptoms throughout.

Exam trap

"Third-wave CBT includes all EXCEPT": psychoanalysis (Section 7.3), which belongs to no wave. REBT and Beck's therapy are second wave; token economies are first wave. Self-efficacy is Bandura's concept, not Beck's, Ellis's or Skinner's (Skinner rejected cognitive explanations).

Ellis's rational emotive behaviour therapy

Albert Ellis (1913 to 2007) treated his own shyness first: as a young man he made himself talk to a hundred women in New York's Bronx Botanical Garden in one month, and learned that rejection was unpleasant, not terrible. After a PhD at Columbia University (1947) he practised psychoanalysis until about 1953, frustrated that insight rarely made patients better. Drawing on Stoic philosophy, he began rational therapy in 1955; it became rational-emotive therapy (RET) and, in 1993, rational emotive behaviour therapy (REBT), a name that acknowledged its behavioural homework. His Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy (1962) is the classic text, and REBT is usually called the first cognitive-behavioural therapy.

Its core is the ABC model: A, the activating event; B, the person's beliefs about A; C, the emotional and behavioural consequences. Common sense says A causes C; Ellis says A contributes, but B is the main cause. The two students shared an A, but "one mock does not decide my future" produced disappointment and study, while "I must score well, or I'm a failure" produced depression and avoidance (Figure 7.7). A rational belief is a flexible preference that fits reality, follows logically and serves the person's goals; an irrational belief is a rigid, absolute demand. A rational belief about a real loss still produces a healthy negative emotion (sadness, concern), whereas an irrational one produces an unhealthy emotion (depression, anxiety).

Irrational thinking takes four forms. The root is demandingness, turning preferences into musts, shoulds and oughts, which Ellis called musturbation (also spelt musterbation). From it follow awfulising (rating a setback as more than 100 percent bad, close to Beck's catastrophising), low frustration tolerance ("I can't stand it") and global rating of self or others ("I failed, so I am a failure"). Ellis's three major musts concern the self ("I must do well and win approval, or I am no good"), others ("Others must treat me fairly, or they are rotten") and the world ("Life must give me what I want, quickly and easily"). They breed, respectively, anxiety and depression, anger and rage, and self-pity and procrastination.

The active ingredient is D, disputing the irrational belief. A logical dispute asks whether it follows ("You want to pass; why must you?"), an empirical dispute asks for evidence ("Where is the proof that you are a total failure?") and a pragmatic dispute asks whether it helps ("Where does this belief get you?"). Success yields E, an effective new philosophy, and F, new feelings and behaviour. The antidote to global rating is unconditional self-acceptance: one's acts can be rated, one's whole self cannot. Ellis extended it to others and to life; unlike Carl Rogers (Section 7.6), he thought therapist warmth unnecessary, and his style was famously blunt.

REBT mixes cognitive techniques (disputing, self-help forms, reading), emotive techniques and behavioural homework set at almost every session (in vivo exposure, rewards and penalties). In rational emotive imagery, adopted from the psychiatrist Maxie C. Maultsby Jr., the client imagines the activating event, feels the unhealthy emotion, changes it to its healthy counterpart and notes the self-statement that did it. In a shame-attacking exercise the client does something harmless but embarrassing in public (Ellis's example was calling out station names in a subway train) to learn that disapproval is bearable.

REBT is clear, brief and teachable, but its evidence is thinner than Beck's: a 1991 meta-analysis of 70 studies found it better than control groups and baseline, and a 2008 trial found it comparable with cognitive therapy and medication for depression. Its chief criticism is that it can be direct and confrontational, so clients may find it intimidating; the therapist's idea of "rational" may also impose his own values, and its philosophical claims are hard to test. A focus on sexuality is a criticism of Freud, not of Ellis.

Exam trap

ABC stands for activating event, beliefs, consequences: B is the belief about the activating event, not behaviour, baseline, bias or "beliefs about oneself", and C is the consequence of that belief, not "critical analysis". D = disputing, not desensitisation, drama or "discuss"; E = effective new philosophy. The behavioural ABC of antecedent, behaviour, consequence is functional analysis (Section 7.4), and affect, behaviour, cognition is the ABC of attitudes (Chapter 3). Musts, musturbation and shame attacking signal Ellis, not Freud, William James, Rogers, Adler, Beck, DBT or Glasser's reality therapy (Section 7.6).

Beck's cognitive model and the distortions

Aaron Temkin Beck (1921 to 2021), a psychoanalytically trained psychiatrist at the University of Pennsylvania, noticed in the late 1950s that his patients had a second, barely noticed stream of quick, believable, self-critical thoughts ("He won't like me") that came before their feelings. He called them automatic thoughts. Depressed patients' dreams, expected to show anger turned inward, showed loss and defeat instead. Beck set out the model in Depression (1967) and the treatment in Cognitive Therapy of Depression (1979, with colleagues). He built cognitive therapy for depression and later extended it to anxiety, personality disorders, substance use and psychosis.

At its centre is the cognitive triad: negative views of the self ("I am worthless"), the world or current experience ("everything is against me") and the future ("it will never get better"). Remember it as self, world, future: not past, present, future; not self, others, environment; not the thoughts, feelings and behaviours that CBT links in its cognitive model; and not id, ego and superego. Cognition runs on three levels. The deepest are core beliefs, or schemas: rigid, global beliefs formed in childhood ("I am unlovable"). From them grow intermediate beliefs, also called dysfunctional assumptions: the rules, attitudes and assumptions that follow ("If I am not perfect, I will be rejected"). When a situation activates these, automatic thoughts appear and drive emotion and behaviour. A dormant schema can be woken by a critical incident such as a loss, so the model is a diathesis-stress one. Beck's daughter Judith S. Beck laid out these levels in Cognitive Therapy: Basics and Beyond (1995).

Cognitive therapy in practice

Cognitive therapy treats thoughts as hypotheses to be tested, not facts to be argued away. In collaborative empiricism therapist and client investigate together, making objective assessments of beliefs against evidence. The main method is Socratic questioning, or guided discovery: open questions that help the client discover and attend to previously overlooked information, so that the discovery is the client's own rather than an interpretation from the therapist ("What is the evidence? How else could you see it? If it were true, what would it mean?"). The therapist neither interprets nor tells the client what to think. Collaborative empiricism, Socratic dialogue and guided discovery are the approach's fundamental concepts; free association and dream analysis belong to psychoanalysis (Section 7.3).

  • The dysfunctional thought record (1979) has columns for the situation, the emotion (rated 0 to 100), the automatic thought, a balanced response and the outcome; with it clients learn to identify, examine and restructure their own automatic thoughts.
  • Behavioural experiments test predictions in real life ("If I speak in class, everyone will laugh").
  • Activity scheduling plans the week and rates each activity for mastery and pleasure; graded task assignment breaks a daunting task into small steps so that success comes early.
  • The downward arrow technique asks repeatedly "If that were true, what would it mean to you?" to descend from an automatic thought to the core belief.
  • Decatastrophising (the "what if" technique) weighs how likely and how bad the feared outcome is and how the client would cope; reattribution spreads responsibility fairly when the client blames himself for everything.

A session runs in a set order: mood check, bridge from the last session, agenda, homework review, work on the agenda, new homework, then summary and feedback. Progress is measured with Beck's scales: the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI, 1961; BDI-II, 1996), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI, 1988) and the Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS, 1974), described in Chapter 2.

Exam trap

The BDI and BAI are self-report; the Hamilton depression and anxiety scales of Max Hamilton are clinician-rated. So "Which is clinician-rated?" is the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale, not "both". The downward arrow is not a 0 to 10 distress rating or an exposure hierarchy (Section 7.4).

Other cognitive-behavioural therapies

Donald Meichenbaum (born 1940) worked on self-talk. His self-instructional training, first used with impulsive children in 1971, moves guidance inward in five steps: the therapist performs the task while talking aloud; the child performs it while the therapist instructs aloud; then the child instructs himself aloud, then in a whisper, then silently. His stress inoculation training, named after vaccination, builds resistance by coping with manageable doses of stress, in three phases: conceptualisation (how appraisals and self-talk create stress), skills acquisition and rehearsal (relaxation, cognitive restructuring, problem solving, coping self-statements) and application and follow-through (practice in imagery, role-play and real life, with relapse prevention).

Problem-solving therapy, introduced by Thomas J. D'Zurilla and Marvin R. Goldfried (1971) and extended to depression by Arthur M. Nezu, teaches a positive problem orientation and a sequence: define the problem, brainstorm alternatives, choose one, act and check the result. Arnold A. Lazarus (1932 to 2013) built multimodal therapy, which assesses the BASIC I.D. (Behaviour, Affect, Sensation, Imagery, Cognition, Interpersonal relationships, Drugs and biology) and borrows techniques from any school (technical eclecticism, Section 7.8). In fixed-role therapy, from the personal construct theory (1955) of George Alexander Kelly (1905 to 1967), the client lives for about two weeks as a new character sketched by the therapist, to try out new constructs and new relationships.

For clients who failed standard CBT, especially those with personality disorders, Jeffrey E. Young developed schema therapy (1990). It targets 18 early maladaptive schemas, enduring themes such as abandonment, mistrust, defectiveness and unrelenting standards, formed when childhood needs went unmet, and schema modes, the states a person switches between (vulnerable child, angry child, punitive parent, detached protector, healthy adult). Its signature is limited reparenting, meeting some unmet childhood needs within professional limits, with imagery rescripting and chair work. Anthony Ryle's cognitive analytic therapy (CAT, Britain, 1980s) is a brief therapy, often 16 sessions, joining cognitive therapy to psychoanalytic object relations through a reformulation letter and a diagram of the client's reciprocal roles.

Exam trap

Fixed-role therapy is George Kelly's, not Beck's, Ellis's or Fritz Perls's; BASIC I.D. is Lazarus's; schema therapy, with its schema modes and limited reparenting, is Jeffrey Young's, not DBT's.

Disorder-specific CBT: panic, social anxiety, trauma, psychosis

Beck's cognitive specificity hypothesis (each disorder has its own theme: loss in depression, danger in anxiety) led to disorder-specific models. Panic. David M. Clark (1986) traced panic attacks to the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations: a racing heart read as a heart attack raises anxiety, which intensifies the sensations, in a vicious circle. Paul M. Salkovskis (1991) added safety behaviours, such as gripping a shopping trolley or sitting down, which seem to avert disaster but stop the client learning that it would not have happened. Treatment provokes the feared sensations and gradually drops safety behaviours, as experiments that test the catastrophic beliefs. In the model of social anxiety by Clark and Adrian Wells (1995), self-focused attention and safety behaviours maintain the fear; effective CBT for social phobia combines exposure, cognitive restructuring and social skills training.

Trauma. Prolonged exposure (PE), developed by Edna B. Foa, combines imaginal exposure (repeatedly retelling the trauma) with in vivo exposure to avoided but safe reminders. Cognitive processing therapy (CPT), developed by Patricia A. Resick for rape survivors, challenges "stuck points" about blame, safety, trust, power, esteem and intimacy. Trauma-focused CBT (TF-CBT) for children, by Judith A. Cohen, Anthony P. Mannarino and Esther Deblinger, teaches coping skills first; its central aim is to build and process the trauma narrative and so desensitise the traumatic memories. With eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR) of Francine Shapiro (Section 7.4), these are the first-line trauma treatments.

Exam trap

PE comes from Edna Foa and colleagues, CPT from Resick, EMDR from Shapiro, DBT from Linehan. For survivors of sexual assault, pick the option naming trauma-focused CBT and EMDR (psychodynamic therapy is also commonly used, with less evidence); DBT, ACT, mindfulness-based therapy and interpersonal psychotherapy (Section 7.3) are not trauma-focused treatments. In panic, safety behaviours are dropped, never prescribed.

Psychosis. CBT for psychosis (CBTp), developed in Britain in the 1990s by David Kingdon, Douglas Turkington and others, normalises unusual experiences, tests delusional beliefs collaboratively and teaches ways of coping with voices. It is an adjunct, not an alternative: antipsychotic medication remains the front-line treatment for schizophrenia (Chapter 8), with CBTp, social skills training and stress management as psychosocial add-ons.

Dialectical behaviour therapy

Marsha M. Linehan (born 1943) developed dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT; manuals 1993) for chronically suicidal women, most with borderline personality disorder (BPD; Chapter 8). Standard CBT's constant push for change felt invalidating to them and they dropped out, yet acceptance alone left the self-harm untouched. DBT's dialectic synthesises the two: full acceptance of the client together with a firm push for change. The acceptance side, including core mindfulness, came from Zen Buddhism, which Linehan practised; the change side came from behaviour therapy.

Her biosocial theory sees BPD as pervasive emotion dysregulation arising when a biological emotional vulnerability (high sensitivity and reactivity, slow return to baseline) meets an invalidating environment that dismisses or punishes the child's emotions. Standard DBT has four modes: individual therapy, a weekly skills group (usually for a year), telephone coaching in crises and a therapist consultation team. Individual work targets life-threatening behaviour first, then therapy-interfering and then quality-of-life-interfering behaviour. The four skills modules are core mindfulness (observing without judgement and finding "wise mind" between reasonable mind and emotion mind), distress tolerance (surviving crises through distraction, self-soothing and radical acceptance), emotion regulation (naming emotions, acting opposite to unhelpful urges) and interpersonal effectiveness (asking and refusing while keeping relationships and self-respect).

Key study: Linehan's DBT trials (1991 and 2006)

Linehan and colleagues randomly assigned chronically parasuicidal women with BPD to a year of DBT or treatment as usual; the DBT group made fewer and less medically serious parasuicidal acts, spent fewer days in hospital and dropped out less (1991). Critics cited the small sample and weak control, so in 2006 Linehan compared DBT with treatment by community experts in psychotherapy, and DBT still halved suicide attempts.

Exam trap

DBT is Marsha Linehan's. Its four skills modules are mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness; cognitive restructuring and relaxation are not among them. Family genogram construction is a Bowen technique (Section 7.7), not a DBT mode. DBT's mindfulness is Zen, not Stoic. DBT was built for borderline personality disorder with chronic suicidality, not for schizophrenia, phobias or OCD.

ACT, mindfulness and the rest of the third wave

Hayes, with Kirk D. Strosahl and Kelly G. Wilson, developed acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT, said as the word "act"; their book appeared in 1999). It rests on relational frame theory, Hayes's account of language: because humans relate events to one another through words, a thought carries the sting of what it stands for, so remembering a failure hurts like failing. The usual response, experiential avoidance (trying to escape unwanted thoughts and feelings), backfires and narrows life. ACT therefore aims not at symptom elimination but at psychological flexibility, acting on one's values with whatever thoughts and feelings are present. It builds flexibility through the six processes of the hexaflex (Figure 7.8): acceptance, cognitive defusion (seeing thoughts as words, as in "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure"), contact with the present moment, self-as-context (the observing self), values and committed action.

Jon Kabat-Zinn (born 1944) brought mindfulness (attention paid on purpose, in the present moment and non-judgementally) into medicine with mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), founded in 1979 at the University of Massachusetts Medical School: an eight-week group course of body scan, meditation and gentle yoga for chronic pain and stress. Zindel V. Segal, J. Mark G. Williams and John D. Teasdale merged it with cognitive therapy as mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT; 2002), an eight-week group for recovered patients designed to prevent relapse in recurrent major depression by teaching decentering: seeing thoughts as passing mental events, not facts.

Key study: MBCT and depressive relapse (Teasdale and colleagues, 2000)

Recovered patients with recurrent depression were randomly assigned to usual treatment or usual treatment plus MBCT and followed for 60 weeks. For those with three or more past episodes, relapse fell from 66 to 37 percent; for those with two, MBCT made no difference. MBCT is a relapse-prevention treatment, not a treatment for acute illness.

Compassion-focused therapy (CFT) of Paul Gilbert, built for shame and self-criticism, describes three emotion-regulation systems: threat (protection, anxiety, anger), drive (seeking resources and achievement) and soothing (contentment, safeness, affiliation); compassionate mind training strengthens the soothing system, which self-critical people lack. Metacognitive therapy (MCT) of Adrian Wells targets not what people think but how they respond to thinking. Its central concept, the cognitive attentional syndrome, is persistent worry and rumination, threat monitoring and unhelpful coping (thought suppression, avoidance), driven by metacognitive beliefs such as "Worrying helps me cope" and "My worry is uncontrollable"; attention training and detached mindfulness loosen it. Functional analytic psychotherapy (FAP) of Robert J. Kohlenberg and Mavis Tsai (1991) uses the therapy relationship itself: the client's problem behaviours and improvements appear in session, and the therapist naturally reinforces the improvements. Behavioural activation grew from Peter M. Lewinsohn's view that depression follows a loss of response-contingent positive reinforcement; when Neil S. Jacobson and colleagues (1996) found that the activation component of Beck's therapy worked as well as the whole package, scheduling valued activity and tackling avoidance became a treatment in its own right (Section 7.4).

Exam trap

ACT comes from Steven Hayes and colleagues, not Linehan, Beck or Ellis. Its hexaflex (acceptance, defusion, present moment, self-as-context, values, committed action) is not DBT's list of skills, and ACT aims at psychological flexibility through six core processes, not symptom elimination, insight into childhood conflicts or disputing beliefs. MBSR is Kabat-Zinn's; MBCT prevents depressive relapse and is not a treatment for acute psychosis or phobias.

How well does CBT work?

CBT is the most researched psychotherapy. A 2006 review of 16 meta-analyses, co-authored by Beck, found large effects for depression, generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social phobia, post-traumatic stress disorder and childhood depression and anxiety. CBT is first-line for most anxiety disorders and for depression, where it matches antidepressants and better protects against relapse after treatment ends; it is also first-line for bulimia nervosa and insomnia, and an adjunct to medication in bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. Its methods travel: in Goa, lay counsellors delivering behavioural activation helped depressed primary-care patients in a 2017 trial led by Vikram Patel.

It has limits. Many clients do not fully recover or later relapse; trial samples are more selected than clinic patients; it needs literacy and homework; critics say it neglects social causes such as poverty and assumes a Western, individualist outlook; and against other well-conducted therapies the differences are often small (Section 7.8). Computerised and internet-delivered CBT (iCBT), such as Beating the Blues in Britain and MoodGYM in Australia, delivers modules, thought records and homework on screen. Guided versions approach the results of face-to-face therapy, whereas unguided ones lose many users. It suits stepped care, in which low-intensity help comes first (telepsychology in India, Section 7.10).

7.6 Humanistic, Existential, Experiential and Brief TherapiesHigh yield · 49 Qs

By the 1950s psychotherapy had two systems: psychoanalysis (Section 7.3) and behaviourism (Section 7.4). Abraham Harold Maslow (1908 to 1970) called the movement against both the third force. Its core, humanistic psychology, studies the whole person from his or her own viewpoint and assumes an inborn drive toward growth; Maslow (Chapter 4) and Carl Rogers, who built the therapy, were its leading proponents. Existential therapists share its respect for freedom but make the person face death, isolation and meaninglessness; experiential therapists work with feeling as it happens in the session. Together these humanistic-experiential psychotherapies aim to promote a more fluid and integrated organisation of the self, not to resolve deep-seated conflicts (the psychodynamic aim), change maladaptive behaviours (the behavioural aim) or train interpersonal skills.

Rogers: the self, conditions of worth and incongruence

Carl Ransom Rogers (1902 to 1987) gave his therapy three names: non-directive therapy in Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942), because the counsellor does not advise, interpret or steer; client-centred therapy in Client-Centered Therapy (1951), which added a theory of personality; and the person-centred approach from the 1970s. A "client", unlike a patient, keeps responsibility for his or her own life.

The theory starts from the actualising tendency, the inborn tendency of every organism to develop its capacities in ways that maintain and enhance it (not Maslow's self-actualisation). Infants judge experience by an organismic valuing process: food is good when hungry, pain is bad. Part of experience becomes the self-concept, the organised picture of "I" and "me", beside an ideal self, the self one would like to be. With the self comes a need for positive regard, and parents rarely give it without strings ("we love you when you top the class"). The child introjects these rules as conditions of worth and starts valuing experience by whether it earns approval.

The result is incongruence, a mismatch between self-concept and experience: a split between the organismic valuing process and a self burdened by conditions of worth, felt as tension, anxiety and confusion. Ananya, raised as "the doctor of the family", comes alive only when she draws, so she distorts the experience ("I am just tired") or denies it awareness (Figure 7.9). Rogers's 19 propositions (1951) state the theory: behaviour follows the world as perceived (the phenomenal field), and experience inconsistent with the self is denied or distorted until, in complete safety, it can be admitted. Growth heads toward the fully functioning person, a direction rather than a state: open to experience, living fully in each moment, trusting his or her own organism, free and creative.

Exam trap

"A split between the organismic valuing process and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth" is incongruence. Introjection is how conditions of worth get in, conflict has no special place in the theory, and the actualising tendency is the growth force that incongruence blocks.

The core conditions and person-centred practice

The cure, then, is a relationship without conditions of worth. In a 1957 paper Rogers listed six necessary and sufficient conditions of personality change: (1) two persons are in psychological contact; (2) the client is incongruent, vulnerable or anxious; (3) the therapist is congruent; (4) the therapist feels unconditional positive regard; (5) the therapist empathically understands the client's internal frame of reference and communicates this; (6) the client perceives this empathy and regard, at least minimally. "Necessary and sufficient" means no change without them and nothing more needed. Conditions 3 to 5 are the three core conditions (Table 7.14): unconditional positive regard, a term coined in 1954 by Rogers's student Stanley Standal; congruence, which Rogers later called the most basic; and empathy.

The core conditions are empathy, unconditional positive regard and congruence ("all of the above" when all three are listed). Edward Bordin's working alliance (1979: goals, tasks and bond; Section 7.8) is not Rogerian; person-centred therapy is the approach least likely to use the term, preferring the "real relationship" and the core conditions.

Key study: the Q-sort and the self-ideal gap (Chicago, 1954)

Question. Does therapy bring the perceived self closer to the ideal self? Method. The Q-sort was devised by the British psychologist William Stephenson (The Study of Behavior, 1953) and adapted by Rogers's group. A person sorts about 100 self-statements ("I am a submissive person") from "most like me" to "least like me" into a forced, roughly normal distribution, once for the real and once for the ideal self; correlating the sorts captures within-person variation. Results. For John Butler and Gerard Haigh's clients (1954) the mean self-ideal correlation rose from about zero (minus .01) before therapy to .34 after it, and stood at .31 at follow-up. Criticism. Falling ideals or learning the "healthy" answer could shrink the gap; samples were small.

Using rating scales of empathy, warmth and genuineness, Charles Truax and Robert Carkhuff (1967) found that clients of high-scoring therapists improved more. The conditions help in every therapy (Section 7.8) but are rarely sufficient: a severe phobia still needs exposure.

Rogers also championed the encounter group (Carl Rogers on Encounter Groups, 1970): a small, loosely structured group whose principal role is personal growth and self-change through self-disclosure and honest feedback, not socialising, team building or diagnosis. Kurt Lewin's T-groups and other group formats are in Section 7.7.

Gestalt therapy

Fritz Perls (1893 to 1970), a German-born psychoanalyst, founded Gestalt therapy with his wife Laura Perls and the writer Paul Goodman; the founding book, Gestalt Therapy (1951), was by Perls, Ralph Hefferline and Goodman, and Perls's workshops at the Esalen Institute (1964 to 1969) made it famous. Therapy stays in the here and now. Its goal is awareness of what one is doing and feeling, and how, not why; with awareness comes responsibility, so clients say "I won't" rather than "I can't". Needs organise experience as figure and ground: the most pressing need stands out, is met and recedes. A feeling never expressed and completed (an unsaid goodbye to a dead parent) remains as unfinished business, an incomplete gestalt that intrudes on the present. Health needs good contact at the boundary between self and environment. Perls, Hefferline and Goodman's five disturbances of contact ended with egotism; the five of Erving and Miriam Polster (1973), the list most textbooks use, drop egotism and add deflection (Table 7.15).

The techniques are experiments in awareness. In the empty chair technique the client speaks to an absent person, or a part of the self, imagined in an empty chair, often switching seats to reply: the classic way to finish unfinished business. Exam trap

Gestalt therapy is not Gestalt psychology, the perception school of Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka (Chapter 1), which rejected Perls's borrowing of its terms. The empty chair always means Gestalt therapy, never person-centred therapy, CBT, REBT, dialectical behaviour therapy, psychoanalysis or Bowen's family systems therapy, though emotion-focused therapy later borrowed it.

Existential therapy and logotherapy

Existential therapy is more a view of life than a set of techniques. Its chief philosophical source is Martin Heidegger, whose Being and Time (1927) described human existence as Dasein ("being there"), a being-in-the-world: a person is never a thing apart from a world. It begins from the existential givens, which Irvin Yalom (born 1931) named in Existential Psychotherapy (1980) as four ultimate concerns: death, freedom (with its responsibility), isolation and meaninglessness. Anxiety about them is normal; trouble starts when people flee it into inauthentic living, and the therapist helps the client face the givens and choose.

The Swiss psychiatrists Ludwig Binswanger (1881 to 1966) and Medard Boss (1903 to 1990; Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis, 1963) applied Heidegger to psychiatry as Daseinsanalysis. Binswanger described three simultaneous modes of being-in-the-world: the Umwelt (world around: body, drives and physical environment), the Mitwelt (with-world: relationships with others) and the Eigenwelt (own-world: one's relation to oneself); Emmy van Deurzen later added the Überwelt of spiritual values. Rollo May (1909 to 1994; The Meaning of Anxiety, 1950; Existence, co-edited 1958) brought these ideas to America. For May, existential anxiety is the anxiety of a being who knows it is free and will die: normal anxiety is proportionate, not repressed and usable; neurotic anxiety is disproportionate, repressed and paralysing. Existential-humanistic therapy (May, James Bugental) is the American blend.

Viktor Emil Frankl (1905 to 1997), a Viennese psychiatrist who survived Auschwitz and other Nazi camps, wrote Man's Search for Meaning (German original, 1946). His logotherapy (Greek logos, meaning), the "third Viennese school", holds that the primary human motive is not Freud's will to pleasure or Adler's will to power but the will to meaning. Its frustration brings an existential vacuum of emptiness and boredom and sometimes a noogenic neurosis (Greek noos, mind or spirit): one rooted in an existential problem rather than in conflict between drives. Meaning comes from creating or doing (creative values), from experiencing or loving (experiential values), and from the stand one takes toward unavoidable suffering (attitudinal values).

In paradoxical intention, used for anticipatory anxiety in phobias, obsessions and insomnia, the client deliberately wishes for the feared symptom: a doctor afraid of sweating is told to show people how much he can really sweat, and the fear collapses. In dereflection, which counters hyper-reflection (excessive watching of oneself), attention is turned outward, as when a man anxious about his sexual performance attends to his partner instead. Socratic dialogue, the main technique, uses questions to help clients discover their own meanings.

Exam trap

The will to meaning, noogenic neurosis or anxiety, paradoxical intention and dereflection all belong to Frankl's logotherapy, an existential therapy: not REBT, acceptance and commitment therapy (Section 7.5) or the broader existential-humanistic therapy. Dereflection is not sensate focus, the graded touching exercise of Masters and Johnson (Section 7.7). Yalom's four ultimate concerns are not his group therapeutic factors (Section 7.7).

Reality therapy, focusing, emotion-focused and positive therapies

William Glasser (1925 to 2013), an American psychiatrist, refined reality therapy with delinquent girls at the Ventura School and published Reality Therapy in 1965. Its base, control theory, later renamed choice theory (Choice Theory, 1998), holds that we choose nearly all behaviour to meet five basic, genetically built-in needs: survival, love and belonging, power (achievement), freedom and fun. Behaviour is chosen "total behaviour" (acting, thinking, feeling, physiology), so Glasser spoke of "depressing" rather than being depressed; acting is the easiest part to change. The therapist is warm but firm: present behaviour, responsibility, no excuses, no punishment. Robert Wubbolding summed up the method as WDEP: Wants, Doing (and direction), Evaluation (is it working?) and Planning.

Eugene Gendlin (1926 to 2017), working with Rogers, found that improving clients attended to their inner bodily experiencing, and taught this skill as focusing (1978): attending to the vague bodily felt sense of a problem until a fitting word or image brings a "felt shift". Leslie Greenberg joined person-centred empathy to Gestalt tasks in process-experiential therapy, now emotion-focused therapy. Adaptive emotions are used, maladaptive ones changed by evoking a healthier emotion, and secondary ones (anger covering hurt) explored; two-chair work treats self-critical splits, the empty chair unfinished business. Greenberg and Susan Johnson also built emotionally focused couples therapy (Section 7.7).

"Positive psychotherapy" has two meanings. Martin Seligman (born 1942) launched positive psychology as president of the American Psychological Association in 1998; his positive psychotherapy builds strengths and well-being (PERMA: positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, accomplishment) through exercises such as listing three good things daily. The idea of flow, total absorption when challenge matches skill, comes from Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934 to 2021), not from Rogers, Maslow or Frankl. The Iranian-born German psychiatrist Nossrat Peseschkian (1933 to 2010) founded a separate, transcultural Positive Psychotherapy in the 1970s (Latin positum, the given): it starts from capacities, uses stories from many cultures, balances four areas of life (body, achievement, contact, future) and runs in five stages (observation, inventory, situational encouragement, verbalisation, broadening of goals).

Narrative and solution-focused brief therapy

These brief therapies grew from family therapy and social constructionism: no account of a life is the single true one, and the client is the expert. Narrative therapy was created by Michael White (1948 to 2008) in Adelaide, Australia, and David Epston in New Zealand (Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends, 1990). Clients arrive with a thin, problem-saturated story; the therapist practises externalising the problem ("the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem"), searches for unique outcomes when the problem did not win, and helps the client re-author the self story around them. The solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) of Steve de Shazer (1940 to 2005) and Insoo Kim Berg (died 2007) was developed at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee (founded 1978). It ignores causes and builds, through questions (Table 7.16), on what the client wants and what already works; the miracle question (de Shazer, 1988) is its signature. Brief strategic therapy (the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto; Jay Haley), with reframing and paradoxical directives such as prescribing the symptom, is in Section 7.7.

Motivational interviewing and the stages of change

Motivational interviewing (MI) was first described by William R. Miller in 1983 for problem drinkers and developed with Stephen Rollnick in Motivational Interviewing (1991; later editions 2002 and 2013). It is a collaborative, person-centred but goal-directed way of strengthening a person's own motivation by resolving ambivalence, wanting and not wanting change at once; arguing for change only makes the client defend the status quo. The spirit (2013 edition) is PACE: Partnership, Acceptance, Compassion and Evocation (drawing out the client's own reasons). The four processes are engaging, focusing, evoking and planning, and the core skills are OARS: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections and Summaries. The 2002 edition gave four principles: express empathy, develop discrepancy between behaviour and the client's own goals, roll with resistance (now split into sustain talk for the status quo and discord in the relationship) and support self-efficacy. The counsellor strengthens change talk: DARN (Desire, Ability, Reasons, Need), then CAT (Commitment, Activation, Taking steps).

MI's home ground is substance use; it is also used for smoking, treatment adherence and other health behaviours. The meta-analysis of Burke, Arkowitz and Menchola (2003) found adapted MI effective for alcohol, drug, and diet and exercise problems (central in diabetes care), but not for smoking or HIV-risk behaviour; obsessive-compulsive disorder was not reviewed.

Exam trap

MI was developed by Miller and Rollnick, not by Prochaska and DiClemente (stages of change), Albert Bandura (self-efficacy) or Alan Marlatt (relapse prevention, Section 7.9). Asked what MI is used to manage, choose substance abuse, its original field, over treatment adherence. Asked which problem Burke and colleagues (2003) did not list, choose obsessive-compulsive disorder: smoking was reviewed (with weak results), OCD not at all.

MI is often paired with the transtheoretical model of James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente, built from studies of how smokers quit on their own (1983). People pass through stages of change (Figure 7.10): precontemplation (no intention to change within six months), contemplation (intending to, but ambivalent), preparation (acting within a month; small steps taken), action (changed under six months ago), maintenance (change kept over six months) and termination (no temptation, full confidence). Relapse is recycling to an earlier stage, not failure, so progress is a spiral. Ten processes of change drive movement: experiential ones (consciousness raising, dramatic relief, environmental and self-re-evaluation, social liberation) help most early, behavioural ones (self-liberation or commitment, counterconditioning, stimulus control, contingency management, helping relationships) in action and maintenance. Decisional balance (pros and cons) and self-efficacy track progress; the health belief model is in Section 7.9.

Counselling and the helping skills

Counselling and psychotherapy overlap so much that Rogers used the words interchangeably. Counselling usually helps fairly well-functioning people with developmental, educational, vocational or situational problems, briefly and in the present; psychotherapy treats more severe or long-standing disorders, seeking deeper, slower personality change. Allen Ivey's microcounselling (late 1960s) teaches interviewing one skill at a time. His microskills hierarchy rises from ethics and multicultural competence, through attending behaviour (culturally appropriate eye contact, vocal quality, verbal tracking, body language) and the basic listening sequence (questions, encouraging, paraphrasing, reflecting feeling, summarising), to focusing, empathic confrontation, reflection of meaning and influencing skills, and finally integration into a personal style. Carkhuff's model (Helping and Human Relations, 1969) has the helper attend, respond, personalise and initiate, moving the client through exploration, understanding and action; on his five-level empathy scale, level 3 is interchangeable with what the client said.

Gerard Egan's The Skilled Helper (1975) has three stages. Stage I asks "What is going on?" (the current picture: telling the story, seeing blind spots, choosing the key issue); Stage II asks "What do I want instead?" (the preferred picture: possibilities, goals, commitment); Stage III asks "How do I get there?" (strategies, a plan and action). The helper attends with SOLER: face the client Squarely, Open posture, Lean forward, Eye contact, Relaxed. Egan separates primary empathy, understanding what the client has said and felt, from advanced empathy, voicing what the client only hints at or half sees, which is a form of challenge.

Exam trap

Advanced empathy is inappropriate in an initial session: without trust and enough information it feels like an intrusive interpretation. Primary empathy is right from the first minute, and culturally sensitive empathy is always appropriate.

7.7 Family, Couples and Group TherapiesMedium yield · 28 Qs

For most of its history psychotherapy meant one patient and one therapist. In the 1950s clinicians noticed that patients who recovered in hospital often relapsed soon after going home, and that when one family member improved another sometimes fell ill. Perhaps the problem lay partly in the relationships. This section follows that idea from the family to the couple and the group.

The family as a system

The idea came from biology. Ludwig von Bertalanffy (1901 to 1972) proposed a general systems theory: a system is a set of interacting parts whose whole is more than their sum, so a change in one part changes the whole. Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) added self-regulation by feedback. A family tends toward homeostasis, a balance it defends as a thermostat defends a room temperature. Negative feedback restores the old balance and positive feedback amplifies change; neither word means good or bad.

Systems thinking replaces linear causality (A causes B, so someone is to blame) with circular causality, in which each person's behaviour is both cause and effect of the others' (Figure 7.11). The member brought for treatment is the identified patient, the visible sign of a troubled system rather than its cause; the symptom may even serve the system, as when a son's misbehaviour unites quarrelling parents, and a child made to carry the family's problems is its scapegoat. Families also pass through a family life cycle: Betty Carter and Monica McGoldrick described six stages (leaving home, the new couple, families with young children, with adolescents, launching children, and later life), and symptoms cluster at the transitions.

Families and schizophrenia: from blame to expressed emotion

The first family research asked whether families cause schizophrenia. In 1948 Frieda Fromm-Reichmann (1889 to 1957) blamed a dominating, rejecting schizophrenogenic mother. In 1956 the anthropologist Gregory Bateson (1904 to 1980), with Don Jackson (1920 to 1968), Jay Haley and John Weakland at Palo Alto, proposed the double bind: a person in a vital relationship repeatedly receives two contradictory messages at different levels (typically words contradicted by manner) and can neither comment on the contradiction nor leave, so every response is wrong.

Key study: Bateson's double bind (1956)

A young man recovering from an acute schizophrenic episode put his arm round his visiting mother, and she stiffened. He withdrew it; she asked, "Don't you love me any more?" When he blushed she said, "Dear, you must not be so easily embarrassed and afraid of your feelings." After she left he assaulted an aide. Whatever he did was wrong, and he could not say so. Criticism: anecdotal and never predictive, it is not accepted as a cause.

Theodore Lidz (1910 to 2001) at Yale described marital schism, open conflict in which each parent undermines the other and courts the child, and marital skew, in which one disturbed parent dominates while the other pretends all is normal. Lyman Wynne (1923 to 2007) described pseudomutuality (1958), a facade of harmony that hides distance and leaves no room for a separate identity. None of these survived as a cause of schizophrenia (Chapter 8), and the schizophrenogenic mother is discredited.

What survived concerned relapse. In London George Brown found that discharged patients who spent long hours with their relatives relapsed more often. The emotional climate of the home, the attitudes relatives express about the patient, is called expressed emotion (EE) and is rated from the Camberwell Family Interview. High EE has three components: critical comments, hostility and emotional over-involvement (intrusive, self-sacrificing concern). Brown, James Birley and John Wing (1972) showed that high EE predicts relapse.

Key study: Vaughn and Leff (1976)

Christine Vaughn and Julian Leff followed patients with schizophrenia for nine months after discharge. About half of those returning to high-EE homes relapsed, against about one in eight in low-EE homes. In high-EE homes, regular medication and less than 35 hours a week of face-to-face contact each lowered the risk.

Hence family psychoeducation: teaching relatives about the illness, medication and warning signs, training calm communication and problem solving, and cutting contact hours where the climate will not change (Leff's programmes; Ian Falloon's behavioural family management). It lowers relapse (Section 7.9).

Exam trap

High EE predicts relapse, not onset, negative symptoms or violence. Warmth is rated in the interview but is not a high-EE dimension. Reducing contact hours manages high EE. One parent sending two conflicting messages is double bind communication; first-rank symptoms are Kurt Schneider's (Chapter 8).

Indian context

In the 1950s Vidya Sagar at Amritsar Mental Hospital had families camp on the grounds and share in care. Indian families show the lowest rates of high EE in cross-cultural comparisons (Jenkins and Karno, 1992), and closeness that looks like over-involvement by Western norms may be ordinary support. A therapist unaware of a family's culture and customs will misread it and make wrong assessments.

Bowen's family systems therapy

Murray Bowen (1913 to 1990), who admitted whole families to hospital wards in the 1950s, founded the transgenerational school. Under stress a two-person relationship draws in a third person, usually a child: triangulation. Table 7.17 lists all eight concepts. Bowen worked as a calm coach who stayed out of the family's triangles, and mapped three or more generations in a genogram (squares for males, circles for females, lines for marriages, conflicts and cutoffs).

Minuchin's structural family therapy

Salvador Minuchin (1921 to 2017) worked with poor families of delinquent boys (Families of the Slums, 1967) and then at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic (Families and Family Therapy, 1974). He studied the family's present structure: its subsystems (spousal, parental, sibling), a hierarchy with parents in charge, and boundaries that should be clear, firm yet permeable. Diffuse boundaries produce enmeshment (over-involvement, intrusion, little autonomy); rigid boundaries produce disengagement (isolation, little support), as Figure 7.12 shows. Psychosomatic Families (1978) described families of children with anorexia, diabetes and asthma as enmeshed, overprotective, rigid and unable to resolve conflict.

The therapist begins by joining, accommodating to the family's style, then maps its structure and asks for an enactment: the conflict acted out in the room rather than described. Unbalancing (siding with one member, often an overruled parent, to shift power), boundary making and reframing then restructure the family.

Exam trap

Enmeshment means diffuse boundaries; rigid boundaries mean disengagement. Enmeshment is not a complete lack of communication, nor scapegoating. Structural is Minuchin, strategic is Haley, multigenerational is Bowen, experiential is Satir. A therapist's boundary violation with a client is professional ethics (Section 7.1).

Strategic, systemic and other family therapies

Jay Haley (1923 to 2007), influenced by the hypnotherapist Milton Erickson, made therapy strategic: the therapist plans how to remove the presenting problem through directives (tasks) and does not seek insight. With resistant families he used paradoxical intervention, such as prescribing the symptom (a quarrelling couple told to quarrel at a set hour); in ordeal therapy (1984) the client performs a harmless but tiresome ordeal every time the symptom occurs. Cloe Madanes (1981) added pretend techniques: the child pretends to have the symptom and the parent pretends to help.

At the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto (founded by Jackson, 1959), Paul Watzlawick, Weakland and Richard Fisch offered brief therapy of about ten sessions. In Change (1974) they argued that attempted solutions keep problems going: a wife nags her husband to exercise, he resists, she nags more. Trying harder is first-order change, within unchanged rules; the goal is second-order change, a change in the rules themselves.

The Milan group (Mara Selvini Palazzoli, Luigi Boscolo, Gianfranco Cecchin and Giuliana Prata; Paradox and Counterparadox, 1978) worked as a team behind a one-way mirror. Their positive connotation recasts everyone's behaviour, symptom included, as serving the family's unity, and prescribed rituals interrupt the family's game. Their three guidelines were hypothesising (a provisional systemic explanation, revised each session), circularity (exploring relationships and differences) and neutrality (siding with no one), and circular questioning asks one member about the relationship between others: "Who is most upset when Anu won't eat?"

Among the experiential therapists, Virginia Satir (1916 to 1988; Peoplemaking, 1972) named four stances that protect low self-esteem under stress: placating (always agreeing), blaming, computing or super-reasonable (cold logic) and distracting or irrelevant, against congruent, levelling communication in which words, feelings and body match. In family sculpting she posed members physically to show their relationships. Carl Whitaker (1912 to 1995) practised spontaneous, often absurd symbolic-experiential therapy with a co-therapist. Couples and sex therapy

When a couple's distress keeps an individual problem going, the pair is treated together. For depression bound up with marital discord, marital and couple therapy is the better choice: behavioural marital therapy relieves the depression about as well as individual cognitive therapy and also mends the marriage.

Behavioural couples therapy (Neil Jacobson and Gayla Margolin, 1979) increases positive behaviour exchange and teaches communication and problem solving. Jacobson and Andrew Christensen's integrative behavioural couple therapy adds acceptance of differences that will not change. John Gottman, filming couples in conflict, found that his four horsemen predict divorce: criticism (attacking character), contempt (mockery; the strongest predictor), defensiveness and stonewalling (withdrawal). Stable couples keep at least five positive interactions for every negative one, the 5:1 ratio, and his Sound Relationship House builds from love maps and fondness up to shared meaning, on walls of trust and commitment. Emotionally focused couple therapy (Sue Johnson, 1980s) uses attachment theory: fights are protests at a threatened bond, locked in cycles such as pursue and withdraw.

William Masters and Virginia Johnson (Human Sexual Inadequacy, 1970) treated the couple as the patient, with a male and female co-therapist team. Their sensate focus is graded, non-demand touching with intercourse at first banned, removing performance anxiety; for premature ejaculation they taught the squeeze technique (firm pressure where the head of the penis joins the shaft, just before ejaculation). The stop-start technique, pausing stimulation before the point of no return, is James Semans's (1956). Helen Singer Kaplan (The New Sex Therapy, 1974) blended these tasks with psychodynamic work in brief sex therapy. Jack Annon's PLISSIT model (1976) grades help: Permission, Limited Information, Specific Suggestions, Intensive Therapy.

Exam trap

Contempt, not criticism, is Gottman's best predictor of divorce. Squeeze is Masters and Johnson's; stop-start is Semans's (Masters and Johnson adapted Semans's method, so some textbooks credit Semans with both). Emotionally focused couple therapy (Johnson) is not Greenberg's individual emotion-focused therapy (Section 7.6).

Psychodrama, encounter and self-help groups, therapeutic communities

Jacob Levy Moreno (1889 to 1974), who coined the term group psychotherapy, opened a Theatre of Spontaneity in Vienna in 1921. In psychodrama the protagonist acts out scenes on a stage, guided by the therapist as director, with members as auxiliary egos playing significant others and an audience that shares afterwards. In role reversal the protagonist plays the other person; in doubling an auxiliary voices the protagonist's unspoken feelings; in mirroring someone replays the protagonist's behaviour for them to watch. Moreno's sociometry measures attraction and rejection in groups, and its sociogram draws members as points and choices as arrows, revealing the group's explicit and implicit communication patterns.

Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947) inspired the T-group (training or sensitivity group, National Training Laboratories, 1947), in which members learn group dynamics and their effect on others. The encounter group, championed by Carl Rogers, encourages therapy and self-growth through open disclosure and interaction. Self-help groups are run by members, not professionals: Alcoholics Anonymous, founded in 1935 by Bill Wilson and Bob Smith, works through twelve steps, sponsors and anonymity, and Narcotics Anonymous adapted the steps to drugs.

In milieu therapy the whole ward environment, staff, routine and fellow patients, is the treatment. Its democratic form, the therapeutic community, was pioneered by Maxwell Jones (1907 to 1990) at Belmont Hospital in Surrey, whose unit later became the Henderson Hospital: staff and patients share decisions in daily community meetings, hierarchy is flattened, and every interaction becomes feedback. Addiction communities are in Section 7.9.

Exam trap

Maxwell Jones means the therapeutic community. Milieu therapy is the broader idea; group analysis is S. H. Foulkes's psychoanalytic group therapy; encounter groups are Rogers's and psychodrama Moreno's; the token economy, aversion therapy and EMDR belong to behaviour therapy (Section 7.4), and logotherapy is Frankl's (Section 7.6). Encounter groups aim at growth, not diagnosis or team building.

7.8 Does Psychotherapy Work? Evidence, Common Factors and the AllianceLower yield · 12 Qs

Sections 7.3 to 7.7 described a crowded marketplace of therapies. Does any of it help, and if so, why? The story runs from Eysenck's attack, through the statistics that answered him, to a debate that continues: does the technique heal, or the relationship, the client and the therapist?

Eysenck's challenge and the meta-analytic answer

In 1952 Hans Jürgen Eysenck (1916 to 1997) reviewed the published outcome reports. Neurotic patients given psychoanalysis improved at about 44 percent and those given eclectic therapy at about 64 percent, but comparable patients without psychotherapy (insurance-disability claimants seen by general practitioners, and state-hospital discharges) improved at about 72 percent. Roughly two-thirds of neurotic patients, he concluded, improve within two years with or without therapy, through spontaneous remission: recovery driven by time, life events and ordinary support.

Critics attacked his method: the groups differed in severity, "improved" meant different things in different studies, and the "untreated" patients had received reassurance and sedatives. Allen E. Bergin (1971) showed that counting dropouts differently lifted psychoanalysis to about 85 percent improvement among those who completed it; Michael J. Lambert (1976) showed that Eysenck had inflated untreated improvement, putting spontaneous remission nearer 40 percent. The decisive answer came from a new statistic.

Gene V. Glass coined the term meta-analysis in 1976 for pooling many studies statistically instead of counting how many "worked". Each comparison of a treated with a control group yields an effect size (d): the difference between the group means divided by a standard deviation. Effect sizes, all in the same unit, can then be averaged across studies and compared across therapies. A d of 0.85 places the average treated client above about 80 percent of untreated people (Figure 7.13).

Key study: Smith and Glass (1977)

Mary Lee Smith and Gene Glass pooled 375 controlled studies. The average effect size was 0.68: the typical client was better off than 75 percent of untreated people, and types of therapy differed little. With Thomas Miller (The Benefits of Psychotherapy, 1980) they reached 475 studies and 0.85 (80 percent). Eysenck called it "mega-silliness": good and bad studies pooled ("garbage in, garbage out"), unlike outcomes averaged ("apples and oranges").

Exam trap

Eysenck said therapy adds nothing to spontaneous remission, not that it harms. 0.68 and 75 percent is Smith and Glass (1977); 0.85 and 80 percent is Smith, Glass and Miller (1980).

The dodo bird verdict and common factors

Smith and Glass also found the rival schools roughly equal. Saul Rosenzweig (1907 to 2004) had predicted this in 1936: diverse therapies succeed through implicit common factors, he argued, quoting the Dodo in Alice in Wonderland: "Everybody has won, and all must have prizes." In 1975 Lester Luborsky, Barton Singer and Lise Luborsky reviewed the comparative trials and named the dodo bird verdict: bona fide therapies, competently delivered, produce roughly equivalent outcomes, because change comes mainly from the common factors they share (the alliance, empathy, hope) rather than from specific techniques.

Specific-effects researchers, holding a medical model in which each therapy has active ingredients like a drug, point to exceptions: exposure beats non-exposure therapies for obsessive-compulsive disorder, phobias and panic. Common-factors researchers reply that most comparisons of bona fide therapies show differences near zero (at most about 0.2 in a 1997 meta-analysis) and that apparent winners often reflect allegiance (7.8.4).

Bruce E. Wampold formalised this view as the contextual model (The Great Psychotherapy Debate, 2001; in its fullest form in the 2015 edition with Zac Imel): therapy works through a real relationship; through expectations created by a convincing explanation and treatment plan; and through specific ingredients that help because they get the client doing healthy things, not because they correct a specific deficit.

Its root is Jerome David Frank (1909 to 2005), whose Persuasion and Healing (1961) compared psychotherapy with religious healing and placebo. Clients arrive demoralised, and every healing method restores morale through four shared features:

  • The client has an emotionally charged, confiding relationship with a helper.
  • Help takes place in a healing setting (a clinic, a temple, a consulting room).
  • A rationale or "myth" plausibly explains the symptoms and how to overcome them.
  • A ritual or procedure, believed in by both, requires the client's active participation.
Exam trap

"Healing and Persuasion" is Frank's Persuasion and Healing with the title reversed. Distractors: Gerald Klerman co-developed interpersonal psychotherapy and Donald Winnicott was an object-relations analyst (both 7.3); Alfred Pritz is the Austrian psychotherapist who was founding president of the World Council for Psychotherapy (1995). The dodo verdict never says therapy is ineffective, that only psychoanalysis lasts or that medication always wins: bona fide therapies tie.

Lambert (1992) estimated, in his famous pie (Figure 7.14), that about 40 percent of improvement comes from client and extratherapeutic factors, 30 percent from relationship factors, 15 percent from expectancy (placebo and hope) and only 15 percent from model-specific techniques: rough judgements from the literature, not statistical results.

The therapeutic alliance and the core conditions

The alliance idea began in psychoanalysis. Elizabeth Zetzel (1956) spoke of the therapeutic alliance and Ralph Greenson (1965) of the working alliance, the patient's rational, cooperative tie to the analyst as distinct from transference; Greenson also described the real relationship, the genuine person-to-person bond. Edward S. Bordin (1979) made the therapeutic alliance pantheoretical: a collaborative relationship with three parts, agreement on goals, agreement on the tasks that reach them, and a bond of trust and respect.

A working relationship is thus a contract to work together realistically and collaboratively, visible in how well the two actually work together and resting on a committed belief in the helping relationship; it needs no particular theory. Adam Horvath and Leslie Greenberg's (1989) Working Alliance Inventory (36 items, with a 12-item short form) measures goals, tasks and bond. Across meta-analyses the alliance correlates about 0.28 with outcome: modest but robust.

Jeremy D. Safran and J. Christopher Muran described alliance ruptures: withdrawal (the client falls silent, goes vague, over-agrees) and confrontation (the client complains or attacks). Repair means noticing the strain, talking openly about what is happening between the two and owning one's part; repaired ruptures predict good outcomes.

Carl Ransom Rogers (1957) held that his core conditions (empathy, unconditional positive regard, congruence; 7.6) were necessary and sufficient. Research supports a weaker claim: empathy correlates about 0.3 with outcome, positive regard and congruence a little less (roughly 0.2 to 0.3): facilitative in every therapy, but not sufficient.

Exam trap

Bordin's alliance is not a Rogerian concept; Rogers' terms are the actualising tendency, conditions of worth and unconditional positive regard (7.6). Person-centred therapy is the school least likely to say "therapeutic alliance": it treats the relationship and unconditional positive regard as the therapy itself, whereas psychoanalysis coined the term and cognitive-behavioural and interpersonal therapists use Bordin's version. The alliance is not an outcome score, not two clinicians agreeing on a diagnosis (inter-rater reliability, Chapter 2) and not the client's ability to do homework alone. What a working relationship does NOT need: "a psychodynamic theory base".

Therapist, client and allegiance effects

Consistent differences between individual therapists' outcomes, whatever their approach, are called therapist effects; they account for about 5 percent of outcome variance (roughly 3 in controlled trials, 7 in routine practice), far more than the differences between treatments. An allegiance effect appears when researchers find their own favoured therapy superior; Luborsky and colleagues (1999) showed that researcher allegiance predicted most of the outcome differences in comparative trials.

Client factors carry the biggest slice of the pie. William Schofield (Psychotherapy: The Purchase of Friendship, 1964) noted that therapists prefer, and do best with, the YAVIS client: young, attractive, verbal, intelligent and successful. YAVIS describes patient characteristics, not a technique, an illness or a therapist quality; its opposite, HOUND (homely, old, unattractive, nonverbal, dumb), names neglected clients. Readiness to change (stages of change, 7.6) and realistic expectations of outcome (hope) and of role (what a client does) also predict benefit.

In the hello-goodbye effect, described by Starke R. Hathaway, clients exaggerate distress at intake (to justify seeking help) and well-being at the end (to thank the therapist), so before-and-after self-reports overstate improvement.

Landmark trials, dose and clinical significance

Key study: NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (1989)

Irene Elkin's trial randomly assigned about 250 depressed outpatients at three sites to 16 weeks of cognitive-behavioural therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy, imipramine or a pill placebo (the last two with clinical management). All groups improved and the two psychotherapies did not differ; only in severe depression did imipramine clearly beat placebo, with interpersonal therapy showing some advantage. Re-analyses found that the alliance predicted outcome in every arm, and that therapists differed more than treatments.

Project MATCH (1997) assigned about 1,700 people with alcohol problems to twelve-step facilitation, cognitive-behavioural coping-skills therapy or four-session motivational enhancement therapy, to test whether matching clients to treatments helps. All three worked about equally; matching effects were scarcely found.

Kenneth I. Howard and colleagues (1986), pooling about 2,400 outpatients from 15 studies, described the dose-effect relationship (Figure 7.15): about 50 percent had improved by 8 sessions, 75 percent by 26 and 85 percent by 52. The curve is negatively accelerated; Howard's later phase model reads it as quick remoralisation, then remediation of symptoms, then slow rehabilitation.

A large sample can make a small average gain statistically significant while many clients stay unwell. Neil S. Jacobson and Paula Truax (1991) therefore judged clinical significance client by client, with two tests. The reliable change index (RCI), the change divided by the standard error of the difference (derived from the test's reliability), must exceed 1.96, so the change is unlikely to be measurement error; and the client must cross a cut-off from the dysfunctional into the functional range. Both tests passed means recovered; reliable change alone, improved; reliable worsening, deteriorated.

Take Meena, who falls from 32 to 12 on a depression scale with a standard deviation of 8 and reliability of 0.84. The standard error of measurement is 8 × √0.16 = 3.2, the standard error of the difference √2 × 3.2 ≈ 4.5, so her RCI is 20 ÷ 4.5 ≈ 4.4: reliable. With a functional cut-off of 14, she has recovered. A fall from 32 to 27 (RCI about 1.1) is not reliable change.

Therapy can also harm. Bergin (1966) named the deterioration effect: roughly 5 to 10 percent of adult clients get worse, which is why Lambert's Outcome Questionnaire-45 feeds each client's progress back to the therapist.

Process research and psychotherapy integration

Where outcome research asks whether therapy works, process research studies what happens within sessions and how it produces change. Therapy has a beginning (assessment, alliance, goals, contract, teaching the client's role), a middle or working phase (the main interventions and working through) and a planned termination (consolidating gains, preparing for setbacks, handling the ending).

Many clients end in premature termination, leaving before goals are met and against the therapist's advice: near half of outpatients in a 1993 meta-analysis, about one in five in a 2012 one, more often with young and poor clients, weak alliances and mismatched expectations. Classical analysts shunned therapist self-disclosure and humanistic therapists value it; it helps when brief, rare, client-focused and about resolved issues. Homework, central to cognitive and behavioural therapy, carries therapy into the client's week; completing it predicts better outcome.

Culture-sensitive therapy and therapy in India

Western therapy prizes autonomy, insight and an equal, non-directive relationship. A therapist unaware of a family's culture and customs risks wrong assessments and misinterpretations, such as calling a joint family's shared decisions enmeshment. A culturally adapted therapy modifies an evidence-based treatment's language, metaphors, content, goals and methods to fit the client's culture (Guillermo Bernal's ecological validity framework), and meta-analyses find adapted versions modestly more effective.

N. C. Surya, writing with S. S. Jayaram (1964), argued that Indian patients are more dependent than Western ones and less inclined to integrate inner processes, so the therapist should use the patient's own language and give direct, situational support rather than intrapsychic interpretation. Jaswant Singh Neki (1973) proposed the guru-chela relationship as an Indian therapeutic paradigm: a wise, warm, guiding therapist and a client who accepts a phase of dependence, normal in Indian relationships, which the guru gradually turns into self-reliance. Families usually accompany patients and join sessions, so therapists work with them as allies (7.1). Yoga-based programmes have been tested at NIMHANS as add-on treatment for depression and schizophrenia, and mindfulness is taught in familiar forms alongside pranayama and meditation.

Training, supervision and the therapist's self-care

Clinical supervision builds a trainee's skill and protects clients. Developmental models (Cal Stoltenberg's) trace trainees from anxious dependence to autonomy; psychotherapy-based models supervise in the language of the therapy taught; Janine M. Bernard's (1979) discrimination model has the supervisor switch between teacher, counsellor and consultant roles across intervention, conceptualisation and personalisation. In parallel process the trainee re-enacts with the supervisor what is happening with the client.

Carl Gustav Jung's image of the wounded healer, after the wounded centaur Chiron, holds that a therapist's understood wounds deepen empathy while unexamined ones become countertransference. The risk of burnout, which Christina Maslach described as emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced personal accomplishment, makes self-care (supervision, personal therapy, boundaries, manageable caseloads, rest) an ethical duty.

7.9 Health, Community, Crisis and Rehabilitation PsychologyLower yield · 15 Qs

Clinical psychology does not end at the consulting-room door. Most deaths in India now come from illnesses driven partly by behaviour, and most people with a mental illness never see a specialist. This section follows the psychologist onto the ward, into the community, into crises and into rehabilitation.

Health psychology and the sick role

In 1980 Joseph D. Matarazzo, first president of the American Psychological Association's Division of Health Psychology (Division 38, founded 1978), defined health psychology as the aggregate of psychology's educational, scientific and professional contributions to promoting and maintaining health, preventing and treating illness, and identifying the causes and correlates of health and illness. It is one discipline's field, leaning towards promotion and prevention. Behavioural medicine (Gary Schwartz and Stephen Weiss, 1978) is an interdisciplinary field that integrates behavioural and biomedical knowledge and techniques for prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. Both apply George Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977; Section 7.1).

What a person does with a symptom, ignoring chest pain for a week or rushing to casualty, is illness behaviour (David Mechanic, 1962). Once accepted as ill, a person enters the sick role of Talcott Parsons (The Social System, 1951), with two rights (exemption from normal duties; no blame for the illness) and two obligations (to want to get well; to seek competent help and cooperate with it). It fits acute illness better than chronic or stigmatised illness. Seeking the sick role itself is the motive in factitious disorder; malingering seeks external gain (Chapter 8).

Exam trap

Behavioural medicine, not health psychology, is the interdisciplinary field integrating behavioural and biomedical interventions (JEMAS 2025). Options such as "only uses medication" are nonsense. Health psychology is Division 38; clinical psychology, Division 12.

Models of health behaviour and adherence

Models of health behaviour explain why knowing the risks is not enough (Table 7.23). The health belief model grew from a 1950s puzzle, the poor uptake of free tuberculosis X-rays, studied by Godfrey Hochbaum, Irwin Rosenstock and Stephen Kegeles in the United States: people acted when they felt susceptible, judged the disease severe, saw benefits outweighing barriers and met a cue to action. Rosenstock gave it its classic statement (1966), and Marshall Becker (1974) extended it to sick-role behaviour and adherence. Martin Fishbein and Icek Ajzen's theory of reasoned action (1975) made intention the immediate cause of behaviour; Ajzen's theory of planned behaviour added perceived behavioural control (Figure 7.16). Ronald Rogers's protection motivation theory (1975) shows that fear appeals work only when coping appraisal is also high, and Ralf Schwarzer's health action process approach (1992) adds planning to turn intention into action.

Medicine most needs adherence, following an agreed treatment; the older word compliance implied obedience (in social psychology it means yielding to a direct request, Chapter 3). Only about half of chronically ill patients in developed countries take treatment as prescribed (World Health Organization, 2003). Clear written instructions, simple regimens, reminders, family involvement and a good relationship all help. Thomas Szasz and Marc Hollender (1956) ranged patient-practitioner relationships from activity-passivity (the unconscious patient) through guidance-cooperation to mutual participation, best for chronic illness.

Stress and physical illness

Stress itself is taught in Section 1.11: Hans Selye's general adaptation syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion), the life-events scale of Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe, and psychoneuroimmunology, begun when Robert Ader and Nicholas Cohen (1975) conditioned immune suppression in rats. Health psychology adds that an acute stressor is brief with a clear end (a pop quiz), a chronic stressor persists (marital discord, HIV disease, colon cancer), and chronic stress is what suppresses immunity.

Franz Alexander (Psychosomatic Medicine, 1950) proposed that a specific unconscious conflict acting on a vulnerable organ caused each of seven psychosomatic disorders, the "holy seven": peptic ulcer, essential hypertension, bronchial asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis, neurodermatitis and thyrotoxicosis. The specificity idea failed (most ulcers proved bacterial, from Helicobacter pylori), but psychological factors are now known to shape the course of every illness (Table 7.24).

Behavioural medicine and lifestyle change

Biofeedback displays a bodily signal (muscle tension, finger temperature, skin conductance, brain waves) so that a person can learn to control it, as in tension headache or migraine, though it often does no better than relaxation: Edmund Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation, Johannes Schultz's autogenic training or Herbert Benson's relaxation response (1975). Stress management adds cognitive restructuring and problem solving (Section 7.5).

Chronic insomnia is treated first with cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia: stimulus control (Richard Bootzin, 1972: the bed only for sleep and sex; leave it if awake for about 20 minutes), sleep restriction (Arthur Spielman, 1987: time in bed cut to time asleep) and cognitive work on sleep worries. Sleep hygiene (no evening caffeine, a dark quiet room, regular hours) is not enough alone.

Smokers are helped by the five As (ask, advise, assess, assist, arrange follow-up) and nicotine replacement. Drinkers are screened with the World Health Organization's Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT, 1989): 10 items scored 0 to 40, with 8 or more suggesting hazardous or harmful use and 20 or more possible dependence. A positive screen earns a brief intervention, a few short sessions in the style of motivational interviewing (Section 7.6). Weight management relies on self-monitoring, stimulus control and a modest 5 to 10 percent loss; exercise lasts when it is enjoyable, shared and planned.

Chronic illness, dying and grief

Chronic illness is self-managed for life; depression is common and worsens self-care, so psychologists teach self-management and involve the family. When cure is impossible, palliative care seeks comfort and quality of life for patient and family. It began with Cicely Saunders's St Christopher's Hospice, London (1967), which treated "total pain": physical, emotional, social and spiritual.

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross (On Death and Dying, 1969) described five stages in dying patients: denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance. They are neither fixed nor universal, came from the dying rather than the bereaved, and have weak research support. J. William Worden (1982) proposed four tasks of mourning, in their current wording: accept the reality of the loss, process the pain of grief, adjust to a world without the deceased, and find an enduring connection with the deceased while embarking on a new life. Most grief is normal and runs its course with ordinary support; counselling adds little. Complicated grief, intense yearning that persists and disables (prolonged grief disorder, Chapter 8), needs intervention, usually one to one.

Exam trap

"Crisis-related bereavement should always be considered traumatic grief" is the false statement (RML 2020): grief is usually normal, and "always" gives it away. Worden's are tasks, not stages.

Community mental health and prevention

President John F. Kennedy's Community Mental Health Centers Act (1963) funded local centres to replace state hospitals. With antipsychotic drugs it sped deinstitutionalisation, but too few centres were built, and many discharged patients became homeless, imprisoned or caught in a "revolving door" of readmissions. Community psychology (Swampscott conference, 1965) turned to prevention and social change.

Gerald Caplan (Principles of Preventive Psychiatry, 1964) brought public health's three levels to mental health. Primary prevention lowers new cases (incidence) among the healthy: vaccination, health education. Secondary prevention lowers prevalence by early detection and prompt treatment. Tertiary prevention limits the disability of an established disorder: rehabilitation. Robert Gordon (1983) classified prevention by its target instead: universal (everyone), selective (high-risk groups, such as children of depressed parents) and indicated (individuals with early signs). Caplan (1970) also named four types of mental health consultation, helping another professional rather than the client: client-centred case, consultee-centred case, programme-centred administrative and consultee-centred administrative. Julian Rappaport (1981) preferred empowerment, people gaining control over their own lives.

Where specialists are scarce, task-shifting trains lay counsellors to deliver structured care under supervision. In Zimbabwe, Dixon Chibanda's Friendship Bench has lay health workers, many of them grandmothers, offer problem-solving therapy on clinic benches. In Goa, Vikram Patel's MANAS trial (2010) showed that lay counsellors in public primary-care clinics improved recovery from common mental disorders, and his group's PRIDE programme took lay-counsellor problem solving to adolescents in Indian schools. Stigma (Erving Goffman, 1963) deters help-seeking; contact with people who have recovered reduces it best. These attack the treatment gap, as do India's National Mental Health Programme (1982) and District Mental Health Programme (1996), modelled on a NIMHANS pilot in Bellary, Karnataka (Section 7.1).

Exam trap

Promoting vaccination is primary prevention (JEMAS 2025). Treating a diagnosed illness, surgery for chronic disease and rehabilitation after injury come later (secondary or tertiary). Caplan's levels say when you intervene; Gordon's types say whom you target.

Crisis intervention and suicide prevention

Key study: Lindemann and the Cocoanut Grove fire (1944)

On 28 November 1942 a fire at Boston's Cocoanut Grove nightclub killed 492 people. Erich Lindemann interviewed 101 bereaved people and in 1944 described acute grief as a syndrome (bodily distress, preoccupation with the dead, guilt, hostility) that most work through within weeks by "grief work", while some develop delayed or distorted grief. Timely brief help, he concluded, could prevent lasting disorder: the seed of crisis intervention. Criticism: impressionistic data and too short a time course for grief.

Caplan defined a crisis as an obstacle to important life goals that is, for a time, insurmountable by customary problem solving, followed by disorganisation; it usually resolves, for better or worse, within four to six weeks. Crises are developmental (retirement) or situational. Situational events are large-scale (floods, earthquakes, tornadoes) or small-scale, striking one person or family: violence (child abuse, domestic abuse, crime), psychopathology that triggers a crisis (a suicide attempt, an overdose) and personal catastrophe (a car accident, a life-threatening illness).

Crisis intervention is brief, active, directive and focused on the here and now, aiming to restore previous functioning. Its stance is supportive therapy (listening, reassurance, problem solving, mobilising support), not exploratory work, and it is often named the treatment of choice for adjustment disorder (Chapter 8), a short reaction to an identifiable stressor. Albert Roberts's seven stages are: assess lethality, establish rapport, identify the major problems, deal with feelings, explore alternatives, make an action plan, and follow up.

Suicide risk is assessed by asking directly about thoughts, plans and means (Chapter 2). The safety plan of Barbara Stanley and Gregory Brown (2012) is a written list of warning signs, coping strategies, distracting people and places, people to ask for help, professionals and crisis lines, and ways to make the environment safe. Means restriction (pesticide bans cut suicides sharply in Sri Lanka), gatekeeper training for teachers and police, and helplines such as Tele-MANAS (14416) work at population level.

After disasters, psychological first aid (World Health Organization, 2011) offers practical support: look (for safety and needs), listen (without forcing talk), link (to services and loved ones). It replaced critical incident stress debriefing (Jeffrey Mitchell, 1983), a single group session retelling the event, which does not prevent post-traumatic stress disorder and may harm (Cochrane review), so it is not recommended. Trauma-informed care (US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2014) asks services to realise, recognise and respond to trauma and to resist re-traumatising people.

Exam trap

Crisis management needs supportive therapy (IHBAS 2020), not acceptance and commitment, expressive or cognitive behaviour therapy. A tornado or flood is a large-scale event; personal events are small-scale.

7.10 Working with Children, Older Adults and Special SettingsLower yield · 16 Qs

Most therapies in this chapter assume an adult who talks and who chose to come. Many clients do not fit: a six-year-old who will not discuss his bed-wetting but will bury a toy tiger in a sand tray; a grandmother who asks each evening when her dead husband is coming home; a youth whose lawyer asks whether he was "of unsound mind". This section adapts therapy to them.

Children as clients, and play therapy

Children differ from adult clients. Their developmental level limits what therapy can ask, so clinicians work through play and parents; adults make the referral; and school is a second home. Assessment is therefore multi-informant assessment (parents, teachers, the child, observation in clinic, home and class), not because parents over-report or teachers lack information but because symptoms vary across contexts: a child with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may be calm in a quiet clinic and unmanageable in a class of fifty. Clinic observation alone falls short for the same reason.

Play therapy uses the child's natural language. Melanie Klein (1882 to 1960) treated play as the equivalent of free association and interpreted it early and deeply; Anna Freud (1895 to 1982) began with a preparatory phase to win trust, involved parents and gave the analyst an educative role. Virginia Axline (1911 to 1988) carried Carl Rogers' ideas into the playroom (Play Therapy, 1947; Dibs: In Search of Self, 1964). Her non-directive play therapy rests on eight principles: build warm rapport; accept the child exactly as he is; create permissiveness so that feelings flow freely; recognise feelings and reflect them back; respect the child's ability to solve his own problems; let the child lead; do not hurry; and set only the limits that anchor therapy to reality. Parent training and behaviour change at home and school

Why does defiance worsen in loving homes? Gerald Patterson (1926 to 2016) answered with coercion theory (Coercive Family Process, 1982). A mother orders the television off, her son screams, and she gives in. His screaming is negatively reinforced (the demand goes away), and so is her giving in (the screaming stops; Figure 7.17). Each trains the other, and the exchanges escalate.

The treatment, parent management training (PMT), reverses the trap. Parents give clear commands, attend to and praise good behaviour and reward it on a token chart, ignore minor misbehaviour (extinction; expect a brief extinction burst), and use time-out and response cost (Section 7.4). Named programmes include Russell Barkley's Defiant Children (1987); Carolyn Webster-Stratton's Incredible Years, which teaches parent groups with videotaped scenes; and Sheila Eyberg's parent-child interaction therapy (PCIT, ages two to seven), which coaches the parent live through an earpiece, first in child-directed play, then in discipline.

At school, teachers add token economies (Section 7.4) and the daily report card: the teacher rates a few target behaviours daily and the child earns rewards at home. For repetitive aggression, often in autism or intellectual disability, Richard Foxx and Nathan Azrin devised overcorrection: restoring the damaged scene to better than before (restitution) and rehearsing the correct act (positive practice). For anxious children of 7 to 13, Philip Kendall's 16-session Coping Cat (1990) teaches the FEAR plan (Feeling frightened? Expecting bad things? Attitudes and actions that help; Results and rewards), then graded exposure. Family work (Section 7.7) runs through all of these.

Deliberately ignoring tantrums is extinction; some keys wrongly mark "operant escape". Escape (ending an aversive stimulus already present) and avoidance (preventing it) are negative reinforcement, which strengthens behaviour. Nor is negative reinforcement a PMT technique; PMT breaks that trap.

Elimination problems, school refusal and ADHD

Enuresis (wetting from age five) is treated best with the bell-and-pad of O. Hobart Mowrer and Willie Mae Mowrer (1938): urine completes a circuit and rings a bell until bladder tension alone wakes the child, and overlearning cuts relapse. Encopresis (soiling from age four) is usually retentive, with constipation and overflow; laxatives are combined with scheduled toilet sitting and rewards.

In school refusal the child refuses out of anxiety, with the parents' knowledge; in truancy the child hides the absence, shows no anxiety and often has conduct problems. Christopher Kearney and Wendy Silverman classified refusal by its function: avoiding distress caused by school, escaping social or evaluative situations, seeking the parents' attention, or gaining tangible rewards outside school. Treatment centres on an early, graded return to school.

Key study: the MTA study (1999)

The US National Institute of Mental Health randomly assigned 579 children with ADHD, aged 7 to 9, to 14 months of managed medication, intensive behavioural treatment, both, or community care. Medication and the combination beat the others on core symptoms; the combination added small social and family gains at lower doses. Criticism: the advantage faded after the trial. Hence the rule: behavioural treatment plus medication.

Autism, intellectual disability and learning disability

Autism was first described in 1943 by Leo Kanner (1894 to 1981) of Johns Hopkins, in eleven children with extreme aloneness and an anxious insistence on sameness (the disorder is in Chapter 8).

Treatment is early and behavioural, based on applied behaviour analysis (ABA), which applies operant principles to socially important behaviour. Ole Ivar Lovaas (1927 to 2010), at the University of California, Los Angeles, pioneered early intensive behavioural intervention using discrete trial training: a skill is broken into small steps, each taught in repeated trials (instruction, response, prompt, reinforcement), with prompts faded. In functional communication training, a child whose tantrums serve to escape demands learns an acceptable substitute, such as handing over a picture card that says "break". The Indian autism tool is the ISAA; some keys mark the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), which is American (Schopler). The CBCL is Thomas Achenbach's Child Behavior Checklist; the CGAS rates global functioning.

Child abuse, POCSO and the adolescent client

Child abuse (physical, sexual, emotional, neglect) entered medicine with C. Henry Kempe's "battered child syndrome" (1962). Safety and reporting come first. The best-supported therapy is trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT) of Judith Cohen, Anthony Mannarino and Esther Deblinger, for ages 3 to 18, with a non-offending parent. Its PRACTICE components (psychoeducation, relaxation, affect modulation, cognitive coping, trauma narrative, in vivo mastery, conjoint sessions, safety) build coping skills first, but the central goal is the trauma narrative: retelling the trauma desensitises the memories and corrects beliefs such as "it was my fault". (Emotion regulation and mindfulness skills define dialectical behaviour therapy, Section 7.5.)

Indian context

The POCSO Act 2012 (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences) covers everyone under 18 and is gender-neutral. Reporting is mandatory (section 19): anyone, psychologists included, who knows of an offence or fears one is likely must inform the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police, and failing to do so is punishable under section 21 (up to six months' imprisonment, a fine or both). It also sets up Special Courts and child-friendly procedures.

Adolescence, Erik Erikson's stage of identity versus role confusion (Chapter 6), is the peak age for risk behaviour such as substance use, unsafe sex and self-harm. Anna Freud and Peter Blos held that healthy teenagers must detach from parents; research now shows that continuing interdependence is healthy, so parents remain allies. Engagement comes first: be collaborative and see the teenager alone for part of each session. The limits of confidentiality with minors are explained at the start: privacy holds except for risk to life, abuse or legal duties such as POCSO, and the teenager is told before anything is shared; guardians give consent. Schools offer counsellors and WHO life skills education. Adolescent depression is treated with CBT or interpersonal psychotherapy, adding fluoxetine with monitoring for suicidal thinking; self-harm calls for a safety plan, restricted means, family involvement and adolescent dialectical behaviour therapy.

Older adults: dementia care, depression and capacity

The field of geropsychology applies clinical psychology to older adults. Robert Butler (1927 to 2010) coined "ageism" (1969) and described reminiscence as a healthy life review (1963). In India families, mostly women, give most dementia care; their caregiver burden, objective (time, money) and subjective (distress, guilt), is measured with the Zarit Burden Interview and eased by psychoeducation, behaviour-management training, support groups and respite. Late-life depression often shows as bodily, sleep and memory complaints rather than sadness. A person's decision-making capacity is presumed, and judged for one decision at one time. Counselling and career guidance

Guidance helps people make educational and vocational choices through information, tests and advice. Counselling is a face-to-face relationship that helps a basically healthy person resolve a problem of living and reach his own decision. Psychotherapy treats more disturbed clients, longer and deeper (Section 7.6). Counselling is called the heart of the guidance programme, and its instrument, the interview, was called the heart of the counselling process by Ruth Strang. Other tools are the cumulative record (a school-long file of each pupil's background, health, tests and achievement), the case study (an in-depth picture of one pupil drawn from all sources) and tests.

Exam trap

One key names the aptitude test "the heart of the guidance programme". The sources are not uniform: Indian guidance texts give that title to counselling, Strang gave "heart of the counselling process" to the interview, and a few lesser sources use similar words for the cumulative record, but no standard text found gives it to the aptitude test, which only predicts trainability. If counselling is not offered, choose the interview, and expect some keys to differ.

Frank Parsons (1854 to 1908), father of vocational guidance, set out the trait-factor approach in Choosing a Vocation (1909): know yourself, know the world of work, and match them by "true reasoning". The counselling process runs from relationship building through assessment, goals and intervention to termination (microskills are in Section 7.2). Areas include school, career, marital, rehabilitation, de-addiction, grief and HIV counselling (before and after testing, under the five Cs: consent, confidentiality, counselling, correct results, connection to care). Affirmative therapy for LGBTQ+ clients affirms their identities, addresses minority stress and rejects conversion therapy, which India's National Medical Commission called professional misconduct in 2022.

John Holland (1919 to 2008) sorted people and work environments into six RIASEC types (Figure 7.18); satisfaction follows from congruence between person and environment. Donald Super (1910 to 1994), in his life-span, life-space theory, saw a career as lifelong self-concept implementation through five stages (growth to 14, exploration to 24, establishment to 44, maintenance to 64, then decline) and many life roles, drawn as the Life-Career Rainbow; readiness for choice is career maturity.

Forensic clinical psychology

Forensic psychology answers legal questions for a client that is often the court, so confidentiality is limited. An accused must have competence to stand trial: the present ability to understand the proceedings and help his lawyer (Dusky v. United States, 1960). Criminal responsibility concerns his mind at the offence: the insanity defence (Table 7.29). It began in 1843, when Daniel M'Naghten, deluded that he was persecuted, shot Prime Minister Robert Peel's secretary; his acquittal produced the M'Naghten rules, which section 84 of the Indian Penal Code (now section 22 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023) follows. This legal insanity is narrower than medical insanity, and the accused must prove it.

The cognitive interview of Ronald Fisher and Edward Geiselman, which aids eyewitness recall, and offender profiling, which infers an unknown offender's traits from the crime, are investigative tools, not treatments.

Neuropsychology, creative therapies and telepsychology

Clinical neuropsychology, which assesses the effects of brain damage on behaviour, is covered in Chapters 2 and 5; rehabilitation psychology is in Section 7.9.

Art therapy has two founders: Margaret Naumburg (1890 to 1983) treated art as symbolic speech from the unconscious (art psychotherapy), and Edith Kramer (1916 to 2014) held that the creative process heals through sublimation (art as therapy). Music therapy uses listening and making music; dance and movement therapy (founded by Marian Chace, 1896 to 1970) uses movement and mirroring; drama therapy uses role play (psychodrama is in Section 7.7); bibliotherapy is guided reading. Modern animal-assisted therapy began in the 1960s with Boris Levinson (1907 to 1984), whose dog Jingles drew a withdrawn child into talk. In India yoga and meditation are common adjuncts, and NIMHANS trials support yoga in schizophrenia and depression.

Telepsychology delivers care by phone, video, messaging or apps. Guided internet CBT rivals face-to-face CBT for depression and anxiety, while apps vary widely in quality. India's Tele-MANAS, launched on 10 October 2022 with NIMHANS as nodal centre, gives free round-the-clock counselling on 14416. Its ethical issues are consent covering the technology's limits, data security, verifying identity and location, emergencies at a distance, jurisdiction and the digital divide.

The Unchaining at Bicêtre, Revisited

Look back at Bicêtre with what this chapter has taught. Pinel, recording his cases and counting recoveries, was a scientist-practitioner a century and a half before Boulder, and his case notes were ancestors of the interview, mental status examination and formulation of section 7.2. Pussin's conversations and routines anticipated what outcome research found in section 7.8: much of what heals in psychodynamic, behavioural, cognitive and humanistic therapy alike is the relationship and the other common factors. Moral treatment collapsed when asylums grew too crowded to give each person attention, the lesson behind community care and rehabilitation in section 7.9. And the chains? India's Mental Healthcare Act 2017 still had to ban chaining in section 95, a reminder that ethics and law protect patients only when clinicians practise them.

Chapter summary

7.1 The Profession: History, Models, Ethics and Indian Law. Clinical psychology joins science and practice to understand, predict and relieve distress; in India it rests on an RCI-recognised qualification and RCI registration. Care moved from supernatural ideas and the humours through the asylums to Pinel and Pussin's moral treatment, Dix and Beers. Witmer opened the first clinic (1896); Boulder (1949) set the scientist-practitioner model and Vail (1973) the PsyD. In India the IACP dates from 1968 and the RCI Act from 1992. Engel's biopsychosocial and the diathesis-stress models integrate the older models. Ethics rests on five APA principles, consent, confidentiality and its limits (Tarasoff) and boundaries; the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 adds rights, advance directives and section 115.

7.2 The Clinical Interview, Mental Status Examination and Formulation. The clinical interview may be intake, diagnostic, crisis or termination, and unstructured, semi-structured (SCID, PSE and SCAN, K-SADS, ADIS) or fully structured (DIS): structure buys reliability, not validity. Microskills include SOLER attending, open questions, furthering, paraphrasing and reflection of feeling. The case history runs from identifying data to premorbid personality. The MSE records appearance, speech, mood (subjective) and affect (objective), the stream, form, content and possession of thought, perception, cognition and insight (six grades). SAD PERSONS aids memory but cannot predict suicide. Functional analysis (ABC, SORC) is operant. A formulation (5 Ps, biopsychosocial) explains, a diagnosis classifies; kappa measures diagnostic reliability, and Rosenhan (1973) exposed labelling.

7.3 Psychodynamic Therapies and Their Descendants. Psychodynamic therapies hold that unconscious conflict drives symptoms. Freud's psychoanalysis grew from Breuer's cathartic method with Anna O.; it relies on the fundamental rule and free association, dream analysis, interpretation, analysis of resistance and of transference and countertransference, and working through, under neutrality and abstinence. Ego psychology, object relations (Klein, Winnicott, Mahler, Kernberg), Kohut's self psychology and mentalisation-based treatment changed what analysts listen for. Brief dynamic therapies (Malan, Sifneos, Davanloo, Mann, Luborsky, Strupp and Binder) and Klerman and Weissman's interpersonal psychotherapy shortened treatment. Jung, Adler, Horney, Sullivan and Berne's transactional analysis offered rival techniques, alongside supportive therapy, hypnosis and narcoanalysis.

7.4 Behaviour Therapy. Behaviour therapy applies learning principles to problem behaviour. Spurred by Eysenck's 1952 critique, it grew from Wolpe's Johannesburg, the Maudsley and Skinner's operant work, and every case starts with a functional analysis. Classical techniques include Jones's counterconditioning, Wolpe's systematic desensitisation (reciprocal inhibition, a SUDS-rated hierarchy), flooding, implosion, exposure and response prevention for OCD, aversion therapy, covert sensitisation, the bell and pad and the debated EMDR. Operant techniques build behaviour (shaping, chaining, the Premack principle, Ayllon and Azrin's token economy) or reduce it (extinction, time-out, response cost, overcorrection), as in ABA for autism. Modelling, assertiveness, relaxation, biofeedback, habit reversal, behavioural activation, insomnia methods and parent training complete the toolkit.

7.5 Cognitive and Cognitive-Behavioural Therapies, and the Third Wave. Cognitive-behavioural therapies hold that emotional disturbance follows mainly from how people interpret events. Albert Ellis's REBT (1955; renamed 1993) uses the ABC model, disputes irrational musts and aims at unconditional self-acceptance. Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy targets the cognitive triad, core and intermediate beliefs, automatic thoughts and distortions through collaborative empiricism, Socratic questioning and the thought record. Meichenbaum's stress inoculation, problem-solving and multimodal therapy, Kelly's fixed-role therapy, Young's schema therapy and disorder-specific models for panic, trauma and psychosis followed. The third wave (DBT, ACT, MBSR, MBCT, CFT, MCT, FAP, behavioural activation) changes the relationship to thoughts. CBT is the most researched psychotherapy, now also delivered online.

7.6 Humanistic, Existential, Experiential and Brief Therapies. Carl Rogers's person-centred therapy holds that conditions of worth create incongruence between self-concept and experience, and that six necessary and sufficient conditions, above all empathy, unconditional positive regard and congruence, are enough for change. Fritz Perls's Gestalt therapy works on here-and-now awareness, unfinished business and the empty chair. Existential therapists (Binswanger, Boss, May, Yalom) confront death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness, and Frankl's logotherapy rests on the will to meaning. Reality, focusing, emotion-focused, positive, narrative and solution-focused therapies follow. Motivational interviewing (Miller and Rollnick) resolves ambivalence alongside Prochaska and DiClemente's stages of change, and Ivey, Carkhuff and Egan model the helping skills.

7.7 Family, Couples and Group Therapies. Family therapy grew from general systems theory: families keep homeostasis through feedback, causality is circular, and the identified patient signals a troubled system. Theories that families cause schizophrenia (the schizophrenogenic mother, Bateson's double bind, Lidz's schism and skew, Wynne's pseudomutuality) failed, but high expressed emotion predicts relapse, which psychoeducation reduces. Bowen stressed differentiation and triangles, Minuchin boundaries, Haley directives and paradox, the MRI second-order change, the Milan team circular questioning. Couples work runs from behavioural therapy to Gottman's four horsemen; Masters and Johnson founded sex therapy. Groups heal through Yalom's eleven factors; Moreno created psychodrama and Maxwell Jones the therapeutic community.

7.8 Does Psychotherapy Work? Evidence, Common Factors and the Alliance. Eysenck (1952) claimed that about two-thirds of neurotic patients improve without therapy (spontaneous remission); Smith and Glass's meta-analysis (1977, effect size 0.68; 0.85 in 1980) answered him. Rival therapies tie (the dodo bird verdict: Rosenzweig 1936; Luborsky and colleagues 1975), which points to common factors: Frank's Persuasion and Healing (1961), Wampold's contextual model, Lambert's pie and Bordin's alliance of goals, tasks and bond. Outcome is tested in efficacy and effectiveness studies, RCTs with waiting-list, placebo-attention and usual-care controls, EST criteria and evidence-based practice (APA 2006), and judged by the dose-effect curve and the reliable change index. Integration, culturally adapted therapy (Neki's guru-chela relationship), supervision and self-care complete the picture.

7.9 Health, Community, Crisis and Rehabilitation Psychology. Health psychology (Matarazzo, 1980) is psychology's contribution to health; behavioural medicine (Schwartz and Weiss, 1978) is interdisciplinary. Parsons's sick role carries two rights and two duties. The health belief model, the theory of planned behaviour and protection motivation theory explain health behaviour and adherence. Alexander's holy seven, Type A hostility, Fordyce's operant pain treatment, CBT for insomnia and the AUDIT link mind and body; Kübler-Ross and Worden describe dying and grief. Caplan supplied prevention levels, consultation and crisis theory: crisis work is brief and supportive, and debriefing is not recommended. Rehabilitation uses assertive community treatment, case management, errorless learning and relapse prevention.

7.10 Working with Children, Older Adults and Special Settings. Children are assessed through several informants because symptoms vary across contexts, and treated with play therapy (Klein, Anna Freud, Axline's non-directive principles, filial therapy, sandplay), parent management training built on Patterson's coercion theory, the bell-and-pad for enuresis, behavioural treatment plus medication for ADHD (the MTA study) and applied behaviour analysis for autism (Lovaas 1987). TF-CBT treats abuse, and the POCSO Act 2012 makes reporting mandatory. Dementia care uses reality orientation, reminiscence, life review, validation and cognitive stimulation therapy. Holland's RIASEC and Super's life-span, life-space theory guide careers. Forensic work covers competence, the M'Naghten-based section 84 (now BNS 22), risk assessment and the Juvenile Justice Act 2015.

From the past papers

  1. To describe a predisposition towards and risk triggers for a mental disorder, modern theorists use a (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Diathesis-Stress model (b) Neurodevelopmental model (c) Clinical-Reductionist model (d) Syndrome-Sign model

    Answer: (a) Diathesis-stress model. The diathesis is the predisposition and stress is the trigger; disorder appears when stress exceeds the person's threshold. The neurodevelopmental model traces schizophrenia to faulty early brain development, and the other two options are not real models.

  2. All of the following statements are TRUE about exceptions to the rule of confidentiality EXCEPT: (RML 2020)

    (a) a court order requires the release of information (b) the information is essential to safeguard the health of others (duty to protect; warn) (c) the information is probative of guilt or innocence in a criminal matter (d) there are never exceptions to the rule of confidentiality

    Answer: (d) There are never exceptions to the rule of confidentiality. Confidentiality is not absolute: court orders, the Tarasoff duty to warn and protect, and evidence a court needs in a criminal case are all recognised exceptions, so (a) to (c) are true.

  3. Which of the following is a key legal provision under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Suicide attempts are decriminalized, recognizing mental illness as a contributing factor (b) Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) can be administered without consent in all cases (c) Only psychiatrists can provide therapy; psychologists are excluded (d) Mental health establishments are exempt from registration if government-run

    Answer: (a). Section 115 presumes severe stress in anyone who attempts suicide, so the person is not punished. Treatment under the Act needs informed consent, and ECT is banned outright without anaesthesia and muscle relaxant; clinical psychologists are mental health professionals under the Act; and every establishment, government or private, must register (it was the 1987 Act that exempted government hospitals from licensing).

  4. A structured interview commonly used in clinical psychology is : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) MMSE (b) SCID (c) WAIS (d) TAT

    Answer: (b) SCID. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM takes the clinician through the diagnostic criteria with standard questions (strictly it is semi-structured). The MMSE is a 30-point cognitive screening test, the WAIS an intelligence test and the TAT a projective test.

  5. Leaning forward, saying umm, nodding, are examples of which one of the following interview techniques? (RML 2020)

    (a) Paraphrasing (b) Prompting (c) Furthering (d) Checking

    Answer: (c) Furthering. Furthering responses (minimal encouragers) keep the client talking without changing the topic. Paraphrasing restates content in new words, prompting steers the client to a particular topic, and checking asks the client to confirm the interviewer's understanding.

  6. In the context of psychopathology, a "formulation" differs from a "diagnosis" in that a formulation primarily aims to: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Classify the client's symptoms according to a standardized nosological system like DSM-5 (b) Provide a hypothesis about the cause, precipitants, and maintaining factors of an individual's difficulties (c) Determine the prognosis and likely course of the disorder over time (d) Rule out organic causes for the presenting psychological symptoms

    Answer: (b) A hypothesis about cause, precipitants and maintaining factors. A formulation (for example the 5 Ps) is an individualised explanation. Classifying symptoms is diagnosis, predicting course is prognosis, and ruling out organic causes belongs to differential diagnosis.

  7. 'Counter transference' in psychotherapy refers to (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) therapists' emotional reactions to the client based on the therapists' own unresolved conflict (b) client's projection onto the therapist (c) client's resistance to therapy (d) behavioral reinforcement techniques

    Answer: (a) The therapist's emotional reactions to the client, based on the therapist's own unresolved conflict. Countertransference runs from therapist to client. The client's projection onto the therapist is transference, obstruction of therapy is resistance, and reinforcement belongs to behaviour therapy.

  8. Which of the following technique or techniques would not typically be included within the repertoire of an Adlerian counsellor? (RML 2022)

    (a) Acting "as if" (b) Push button (c) Encouragement (d) Analysis of transference

    Answer: (d) Analysis of transference. Adlerian therapy is an equal, educational partnership that does not cultivate or analyse a transference. Acting as if, Mosak's push-button technique and encouragement (the core Adlerian technique) are all Adlerian.

  9. Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) does NOT typically include which of the following techniques or focus areas? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Role playing and role reversal techniques (b) Grief and bereavement work (c) Role conflict resolution (d) Role transition support

    Answer: (a) Role playing and role reversal techniques. Grief, role disputes and role transitions are three of IPT's four problem areas (the fourth is interpersonal deficits). Strictly, IPT does use role play after communication analysis, but role reversal is a psychodrama technique, so (a) is the best choice.

  10. A technique in which a response that inhibits anxiety is paired with an anxiety-provoking stimulus, step by step, to break the conditioned link between that stimulus and anxiety is: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Systematic desensitization (b) Flooding (c) Aversion therapy (d) Exposure and response prevention

    Answer: (a) Systematic desensitization. Wolpe paired relaxation, a response incompatible with anxiety, with each step of a graded hierarchy (reciprocal inhibition). Flooding uses full-intensity exposure with no hierarchy or relaxation, aversion therapy pairs a behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus, and ERP blocks rituals so that anxiety habituates.

  11. Exposure & response prevention procedure is based on behaviour therapy principal of : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Counter conditioning (b) Habituation and extinction (c) Reciprocal inhibition (d) Graded exposure

    Answer: (b) Habituation and extinction. Kept in contact with the obsessional trigger and prevented from performing the ritual, the patient's anxiety falls by itself (habituation) and the feared outcome fails to occur (extinction). Counterconditioning and reciprocal inhibition underlie systematic desensitisation; graded exposure describes how steps are arranged, not why fear falls.

  12. The behavioral technique that involves removing a desirable stimulus following an undesirable behavior in order to reduce the frequency of that behavior is called: (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Negative reinforcement (b) Response cost (c) Positive punishment (d) Extinction

    Answer: (b) Response cost. Taking away a reinforcer the person already has (tokens, privileges) after a behaviour is response cost, a form of negative punishment. Negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant and increases behaviour, positive punishment adds something unpleasant, and extinction merely withholds the reinforcer that maintained the behaviour.

  13. A patient sees a friend in the street who fails to acknowledge him; he thinks his friend does not like him anymore. He has the following type of cognitive error: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Selective abstraction (b) Arbitrary inference (c) Personalization (d) Magnification

    Answer: (b) Arbitrary inference. He draws a specific negative conclusion with no evidence for it, which is Beck's arbitrary inference (mind reading is a narrower name for the same jump). Selective abstraction would need him to fix on one detail while ignoring the rest, personalisation would blame himself for an unrelated event, and magnification would blow up a fault.

  14. In the A-B-C-D-E model of cognitive behaviour therapy (RET) 'D' refers to ___________ . (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Drama (b) Desensitization (c) Discuss (d) Disputing

    Answer: (d) Disputing. In Ellis's model D is disputing the irrational belief with logical, empirical and pragmatic arguments, which leads to E, an effective new philosophy. Desensitisation is Wolpe's behavioural technique, and drama and discuss are fillers.

  15. Distress tolerance is a major treatment modality in which of the following therapy? (RML 2024)

    (a) Behaviour therapy (b) Dialectical Behaviour therapy (c) Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (d) Psychodynamic therapy

    Answer: (b) Dialectical behaviour therapy. Distress tolerance is one of Marsha Linehan's four DBT skills modules, with mindfulness, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Standard behaviour therapy, Beck's CBT and psychodynamic therapy have no such module.

  16. A split between the organismic valuing process and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth which results in feelings of tension and confusion: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Conflict (b) Incongruence (c) Introjection (d) Actualizing tendency

    Answer: (b) Incongruence. Rogers's term for the mismatch between self-concept and experience, felt as tension and anxiety. Introjection is only how conditions of worth get in, conflict has no special place in his theory, and the actualising tendency is the growth force that incongruence blocks.

  17. In existential psychology, Dasein encompasses three fundamental psychological dimensions of human existence. Which of the following correctly identifies these three modes of being-in-the-world? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Id, Ego, and Superego (b) Umwelt, Mitwelt, and Eigenwelt (c) Conscious, Preconscious, and Unconscious (d) Sensorimotor, Preoperational, and Concrete Operational

    Answer: (b) Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt. Binswanger's three modes: the biological and physical world around, the with-world of relationships and the own-world of one's relation to oneself. The other options are Freud's structural and topographical models and Piaget's stages.

  18. Frankl's technique for 'hyper-reflection', in which a person stops watching himself and turns his attention outward, for example from his own sexual performance to his partner, is: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Paradoxical intention (b) Sensate focus (c) Dereflection (d) Socratic dialogue

    Answer: (c) Dereflection. It counters hyper-reflection by turning attention away from the self. Paradoxical intention, also Frankl's, has the client wish for the feared symptom to defuse anticipatory anxiety; Socratic dialogue uncovers meaning; sensate focus is Masters and Johnson's sex-therapy exercise.

  19. Which one of the following captures implicit and explicit group communication and interaction patterns? (RML 2020)

    (a) A genogram (b) A cognitive map (c) An ecomap (d) A sociogram

    Answer: (d) A sociogram. Moreno's sociometric diagram draws group members as points and their choices as arrows, exposing stars, isolates, cliques and lines of communication. A genogram maps a family across generations (Bowen), an ecomap maps a family's links with outside systems (Hartman), and a cognitive map is Tolman's mental map of space.

  20. Expressed Emotion (EE) is a significant predictor of relapse in schizophrenia. Which of the following is NOT one of the three core dimensions of high Expressed Emotion? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Hostility (b) Critical Comments (c) Emotional Over-involvement (d) Warmth and Positive Regard

    Answer: (d) Warmth and Positive Regard. High EE is defined by critical comments, hostility and emotional over-involvement. Warmth and positive remarks are also rated in the Camberwell Family Interview, but they do not count toward high EE.

  21. Which of the following therapeutic modalities was pioneered by Maxwell Jones and is characterized by the democratic involvement of all community members (staff and patients) in decision-making and daily activities to promote rehabilitation? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Encounter Groups (b) Therapeutic Community (c) Group Analysis (d) Milieu Therapy

    Answer: (b) Therapeutic Community. Maxwell Jones built the democratic therapeutic community at Belmont Hospital (later the Henderson Hospital), with shared decisions, daily community meetings and a flattened hierarchy. Milieu therapy is the broader idea of the ward as treatment, group analysis is Foulkes's psychoanalytic group therapy, and encounter groups belong to Rogers.

  22. According to the "Dodo Bird Verdict," what is the primary factor responsible for equivalent outcomes across different therapeutic modalities? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) The specific techniques used by the therapist (b) Common factors such as the therapeutic alliance and empathy (c) The theoretical orientation of the clinician (d) The specific diagnosis of the patient

    Answer: (b) Common factors such as the therapeutic alliance and empathy. The verdict (Rosenzweig 1936; named by Luborsky, Singer and Luborsky 1975) holds that bona fide therapies produce roughly equal outcomes because they share common factors. Specific techniques and theoretical orientation are exactly what it says do not make the difference, and diagnosis is not the explanation offered.

  23. 'Healing and Persuasion' is the work of: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Alfred Pritz (b) Jerome Frank (c) Charles Winnicot (d) Jeff Klerman

    Answer: (b) Jerome Frank. The book is Persuasion and Healing (1961), its title reversed in the question: all healing restores morale through a confiding relationship, a healing setting, a rationale and a ritual. Gerald Klerman co-developed interpersonal psychotherapy, Donald Winnicott was an object-relations analyst and Alfred Pritz was founding president of the World Council for Psychotherapy.

  24. All of the following are the characteristics of efficacy studies EXCEPT (RML 2020)

    (a) Therapist actions are controlled by a protocol or treatment manual (b) Therapist flexibility is encouraged (c) The treatment is applied to a homogenous client population (d) The research design relies on random selection and random assignment to a treatment or control condition

    Answer: (b) Therapist flexibility is encouraged. Efficacy studies test a treatment under ideal, controlled conditions: manualised therapists, homogeneous samples and random assignment. Flexible, client-tailored therapy belongs to effectiveness research in routine practice.

  25. Which model explains health behaviour by focussing on perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Health Belief Model (b) Theory of Planned Behaviour (c) Social Cognitive Theory (d) Transtheoretical Model

    Answer: (a) Health Belief Model. Susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers (with cues to action and, later, self-efficacy) are its signature constructs. The theory of planned behaviour uses attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control; social cognitive theory centres on self-efficacy; the transtheoretical model describes stages of change.

  26. Which of the following factor is most directly linked to a higher risk for coronary heart disease? (RML 2024)

    (a) Type A personality (b) Type B personality (c) Hostility (d) Self-efficacy

    Answer: (a) Type A personality (as keyed). Friedman and Rosenman's Type A pattern (time urgency, competitiveness, hostility) predicted coronary heart disease, but later research found hostility to be the toxic component, so choose hostility when a question asks for the component. Type B is relaxed and lower-risk; self-efficacy is not a risk factor.

  27. Which therapy is mostly needed in 'Crisis Management'? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Cognitive behavior therapy (b) Acceptance & commitment therapy (c) Expressive therapy (d) Supportive therapy

    Answer: (d) Supportive therapy. Crisis intervention is brief, active and here-and-now: it listens, reassures, solves immediate problems and mobilises support to restore the previous level of functioning. CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy and expressive therapy are structured or exploratory treatments, not the first response to a crisis.

  28. A parent deliberately ignores a child's temper tantrums in an attempt to discourage them. The parent's strategy is in accordance with: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Negative reinforcement (b) Extinction (c) Operant escape (d) Operant avoidance

    Answer: (b) Extinction. The source key gives (c), operant escape, which is wrong. Ignoring removes the attention that maintained the tantrums, so they weaken: that is extinction. Escape and avoidance are both forms of negative reinforcement, which strengthen behaviour.

  29. Which of the following disabilities is explicitly covered under the scope of The National Trust Act (1999) in India for the welfare of persons with the disability? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Autism Spectrum Disorder (b) Specific Learning Disability (c) Intellectual Disability (Mental Retardation) (d) Both (a) and (c)

    Answer: (d) Both (a) and (c). The National Trust Act 1999 covers exactly four conditions: autism, cerebral palsy, mental retardation (intellectual disability) and multiple disabilities. Specific learning disability is a specified disability under the RPwD Act 2016, not under the National Trust Act.

  30. Infantile autism was first described by : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Hans Asperger (b) Leo Kanner (c) Eugen Bleuler (d) Kurt Schneider

    Answer: (b) Leo Kanner. Kanner described early infantile autism in eleven children in 1943. Asperger described more able children a year later, Bleuler coined the word autism in 1911 for withdrawal in schizophrenia, and Kurt Schneider is known for the first-rank symptoms of schizophrenia.
