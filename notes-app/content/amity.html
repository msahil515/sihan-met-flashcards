1 · Abnormal Psychology & Psychopathology

Psychopathology is the systematic study of mental disorder: its signs and symptoms, its course over time, the underlying mechanisms, and the rules by which we decide a pattern of distress crosses from normal-range suffering into a diagnosable condition. For a clinical entrance examination the most heavily tested material is not vague description but precise architecture: how the two great classification systems are built, what philosophy each rests on, and above all the exact duration thresholds that separate one diagnosis from its near-neighbour. A candidate who can state that schizophrenia requires at least six months of continuous disturbance while brief psychotic disorder resolves inside one month has already secured marks that vaguer candidates lose. This section teaches each disorder from its mechanism upward, always pairing criteria with their differential diagnoses, because in clinical practice the question is rarely "is this person unwell" but "which of several similar conditions is this."

A. Diagnostic Frameworks: DSM-5-TR and ICD-11

The two dominant nosologies are the American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR, 2022) and the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11), whose mental-health chapter came into effect in January 2022. Both are descriptive classifications: they group disorders by observable clinical features rather than by presumed cause, a deliberate atheoretical stance inherited from DSM-III (1980) that replaced the older psychoanalytic, aetiology-driven framework. The DSM is produced for a primarily American clinical and research audience and is famously operationalised, giving explicit symptom counts and time frames. The ICD is a global public-health instrument used for mortality and morbidity statistics across member states; its Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR) are written as flexible prototypes rather than rigid checklists, reflecting its need to function across very different health systems.

Definition

Categorical classification treats a disorder as a discrete kind: a person either meets criteria or does not (the DSM's dominant logic). Dimensional classification rates a problem along a continuum of severity or trait level, accepting that pathology shades into normality. The historical critique of pure categorical systems is that they impose artificial boundaries, generate excessive comorbidity, and leave many patients in residual "not otherwise specified" categories.

The single most important conceptual shift to know is in personality disorder. The traditional model in both systems was categorical: a fixed list of named types. DSM-5 kept the ten categorical personality disorders in its main text but placed an Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) in Section III (emerging measures), which rates level of personality functioning plus five pathological trait domains. ICD-11 went much further and abolished the categorical types entirely. Its model first assigns a global severity (mild, moderate, severe, or "personality difficulty" below threshold) and then optionally qualifies this with up to five trait domains: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, and anankastia, plus a borderline pattern qualifier. This dimensional turn for personality is the headline difference between the systems and a recurrent examination favourite.

B. Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders

Psychosis denotes a loss of contact with reality, expressed through delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking and speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, and negative symptoms. Schizophrenia is the prototypical disorder of this chapter. DSM-5 requires at least two of five characteristic (Criterion A) symptoms for a significant portion of one month (active phase), at least one being delusions, hallucinations, or disorganised speech; continuous signs must persist for at least six months. The five Criterion A symptoms are delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, and negative symptoms.

Definition

Positive symptoms are pathological additions to normal experience: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, and bizarre behaviour. Negative symptoms are deficits, the absence of normal function: blunted affect (diminished emotional expression), avolition (loss of goal-directed drive), alogia (poverty of speech), anhedonia, and asociality. Negative symptoms predict poorer long-term prognosis and respond least well to antipsychotics.

The German psychiatrist Kurt Schneider proposed first-rank symptoms, once thought near-pathognomonic of schizophrenia: audible thoughts (thought echo), voices arguing or commenting in the third person, thought withdrawal, thought insertion, thought broadcasting, made feelings, made impulses, made volitional acts, somatic passivity, and delusional perception. DSM-5 deliberately removed the special weighting once given to bizarre delusions and Schneiderian first-rank auditory hallucinations, because research showed they were not specific to schizophrenia. This loss of special status is a frequently tested point.

The dominant neurochemical account is the dopamine hypothesis: positive symptoms arise from excess dopaminergic transmission in the mesolimbic pathway, while negative and cognitive symptoms reflect dopaminergic hypofunction in the mesocortical pathway (prefrontal cortex). Evidence includes the psychotogenic effect of dopamine agonists such as amphetamine and the therapeutic action of D2-receptor-blocking antipsychotics.

The spectrum comprises a graded family. Schizophreniform disorder is symptomatically identical to schizophrenia but lasts one to six months; impaired social or occupational function is not required. Brief psychotic disorder lasts from one day to under one month with eventual full return to premorbid functioning, often precipitated by marked stress. Schizoaffective disorder requires an uninterrupted period in which a major mood episode (depressive or manic) coincides with Criterion A symptoms, plus at least two weeks of delusions or hallucinations without prominent mood symptoms (this last clause distinguishes it from a mood disorder with psychotic features). Delusional disorder features one or more delusions for at least one month with otherwise relatively preserved functioning and without the other Criterion A symptoms dominating; subtypes include erotomanic, grandiose, jealous, persecutory, and somatic. Schizotypal disorder sits at the spectrum's mild end; DSM lists it both here (as part of the spectrum) and among the personality disorders.

Exam trap

The differential among brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, and schizophrenia is decided purely by duration once the symptom picture is similar: under one month equals brief psychotic; one to six months equals schizophreniform; six months or more equals schizophrenia. A classic distractor presents two weeks of identical symptoms and tempts you toward schizophrenia: at two weeks it cannot yet be schizophreniform either, since that needs a full month of active symptoms.

Mood Disorders: Depression and Bipolar

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) requires at least one major depressive episode: five or more of nine symptoms present nearly every day for at least two weeks, with at least one being depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure (anhedonia). The remaining symptoms span the mnemonic territory of sleep change, interest loss, guilt or worthlessness, energy loss, concentration impairment, appetite or weight change, psychomotor agitation or retardation, and suicidal ideation. The episode must cause distress or impairment and not be better explained by substances or another medical condition. DSM-5 controversially removed the bereavement exclusion, so a depressive episode can be diagnosed even within the first weeks of grief if criteria are met.

Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) is a chronic, lower-grade depression: depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for at least two years (one year in children and adolescents), never symptom-free for more than two months at a stretch. It can coexist with superimposed major episodes ("double depression").

Definition

A manic episode is a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood plus increased energy, lasting at least one week (or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary), with three or more of: inflated self-esteem/grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, and excessive risky pleasurable activity. A hypomanic episode has the same symptom menu but lasts at least four consecutive days and is not severe enough to cause marked impairment, require hospitalisation, or include psychotic features.

Bipolar I disorder requires at least one lifetime manic episode (depressive episodes are common but not strictly required). Bipolar II disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, and crucially no full manic episode ever: a single past manic episode reclassifies the patient as bipolar I permanently. The presence of psychosis automatically signals mania, hence bipolar I. Cyclothymic disorder is the chronic, sub-threshold analogue: at least two years (one in children) of numerous hypomanic and depressive periods that never meet full episode criteria.

The bipolar I versus II hinge is the manic episode, not the depression. A single lifetime manic episode equals bipolar I forever, even with prominent depressions. Bipolar II is defined by hypomania plus depression with mania never present. Do not let a severe depression mislead you: severity of depression does not distinguish the two.

Anxiety Disorders

The DSM-5 anxiety chapter now contains generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, agoraphobia, specific phobia, social anxiety disorder, separation anxiety disorder, and selective mutism. Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is excessive, hard-to-control worry about multiple domains for more days than not over at least six months, with at least three of six somatic/cognitive symptoms (restlessness, fatigue, concentration difficulty, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance). Panic disorder is recurrent unexpected panic attacks (abrupt surges of intense fear peaking within minutes with somatic symptoms such as palpitations, dyspnoea, chest pain, derealisation, and fear of dying) followed by at least one month of persistent worry about further attacks or maladaptive avoidance. Agoraphobia is marked fear of two or more situations (public transport, open spaces, enclosed spaces, queues/crowds, being outside the home alone) where escape might be difficult; it is now a free-standing diagnosis, not merely an add-on to panic.

Specific phobia is marked fear of a circumscribed object or situation; social anxiety disorder is fear of scrutiny and negative evaluation in social or performance settings; separation anxiety disorder, moved into this chapter and no longer restricted to childhood, is excessive fear of separation from attachment figures. For these phobic conditions DSM-5 requires the fear to be persistent, typically six months or more, which prevents over-diagnosis of transient fears.

High-yield

GAD is the six-month disorder; panic disorder needs only one month of anticipatory worry after the attacks; the phobias generally require six months. The panic attack itself peaks within minutes, but the disorder is defined by the month of worry/avoidance that follows.

Trauma- and Stressor-Related and Obsessive-Compulsive Disorders

One of DSM-5's structural reforms, examined relentlessly, was the dismantling of the old single anxiety chapter. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was moved OUT of the anxiety disorders into a new chapter, "Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders," alongside acute stress disorder, adjustment disorders, reactive attachment disorder, disinhibited social engagement disorder, and now prolonged grief disorder. Likewise obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) was moved OUT of the anxiety disorders into its own chapter, "Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders." Both moves recognise that, although anxiety is present, the core mechanisms (trauma exposure; obsessions and compulsions) differ from the worry-based anxiety disorders.

Exam trap

In DSM-IV both OCD and PTSD lived inside the anxiety disorders chapter. DSM-5 relocated each to a distinct chapter of its own. This single fact is one of the most frequently tested points in the whole section. If a question asks which disorders were removed from the anxiety chapter in DSM-5, the answer is OCD and PTSD.

PTSD follows exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, with four symptom clusters: intrusion (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and marked alterations in arousal and reactivity (hypervigilance, startle). Symptoms must persist more than one month. Acute stress disorder covers the same kind of reaction but in the window of three days to one month post-trauma; if symptoms persist past a month the diagnosis converts to PTSD. Adjustment disorder is a maladaptive emotional or behavioural response that begins within three months of an identifiable (not necessarily traumatic) stressor and resolves within six months once the stressor and its consequences have ended.

Exam trap

The trauma timeline trips many candidates. Less than three days: no diagnosis (normal acute reaction). Three days to one month: acute stress disorder. More than one month: PTSD. The same symptoms simply change name as the clock advances.

The Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders chapter includes OCD itself plus body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder), and excoriation (skin-picking) disorder. OCD features obsessions (recurrent intrusive unwanted thoughts, urges, or images causing anxiety) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to neutralise the obsession), which are time-consuming (more than one hour per day) or markedly distressing. Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is preoccupation with perceived defects in physical appearance invisible or slight to others, with repetitive checking or mirror behaviours. Hoarding disorder is persistent difficulty discarding possessions due to a perceived need to save them, producing clutter that compromises living space; it is now distinct from OCD. Trichotillomania is recurrent hair-pulling causing hair loss, and excoriation is recurrent skin-picking causing lesions, both with repeated attempts to stop.

C. Specialized Profiles: Neurodevelopmental Disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders have onset in the developmental period and produce deficits in personal, social, academic, or occupational functioning. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) merged the older separate labels (autistic disorder, Asperger's, PDD-NOS) into one spectrum defined by two core domains: persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction across contexts, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities (including stereotypies, insistence on sameness, fixated interests, and sensory abnormalities). Severity is rated by level of support needed. Symptoms must be present from early development.

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) requires a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (at least six symptoms in either domain, or five for those seventeen and older) present before age twelve, in two or more settings, for at least six months. Three presentations exist: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined. Specific Learning Disorder (SLD) is difficulty in reading (dyslexia), written expression (dysgraphia), or mathematics (dyscalculia) substantially below age expectation despite adequate instruction. Intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder) requires deficits in both intellectual functions (reasoning, problem-solving, confirmed by testing) and adaptive functioning, with onset in the developmental period; severity (mild, moderate, severe, profound) is now graded chiefly by adaptive functioning rather than IQ score alone.

High-yield

ADHD onset is now set "before age twelve" (raised from age seven in DSM-IV). Intellectual disability severity is judged primarily by adaptive functioning, not by the IQ number. Both shifts are commonly tested.

Personality Disorders

A personality disorder is an enduring, pervasive, inflexible pattern of inner experience and behaviour deviating markedly from cultural expectation, stable across time, with onset traceable to adolescence or early adulthood, manifest in cognition, affectivity, interpersonal functioning, or impulse control. This dimensional reform is the modern direction of travel for personality nosology and a likely examination focus.

Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders

This chapter distinguishes several constructs that examiners love to contrast. Substance intoxication is a reversible, substance-specific syndrome from recent use. Substance withdrawal is a characteristic syndrome on cessation or reduction in a person who used heavily and persistently. Tolerance is the need for markedly increased amounts to achieve the same effect (or diminished effect at the same dose), a physiological adaptation. Dependence, in the physiological sense, is the body's reliance signalled by tolerance and withdrawal; it is not synonymous with addiction, since dependence can occur with prescribed medication taken correctly.

High-yield

DSM-5 abolished the DSM-IV split between "substance abuse" and "substance dependence" and merged them into a single substance use disorder rated on a severity continuum: mild (2 to 3 criteria), moderate (4 to 5), severe (6 or more) out of eleven criteria spanning impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological criteria (tolerance and withdrawal). This single-spectrum move is a frequent examination item, as is the point that tolerance and withdrawal expected from appropriately prescribed medication do not by themselves count toward the diagnosis.

Eating Disorders

Anorexia nervosa is restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain or persistent behaviour interfering with weight gain, and disturbance in body-weight perception. Subtypes are restricting and binge-eating/purging. Medical sequelae include amenorrhoea, bradycardia, hypotension, lanugo, electrolyte disturbance, and osteopenia; it carries one of the highest mortality rates of any psychiatric disorder. DSM-5 removed the formal amenorrhoea requirement.

Bulimia nervosa is recurrent binge eating with recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviours (self-induced vomiting, laxatives, fasting, excessive exercise), on average at least once a week for three months, with self-evaluation unduly influenced by shape and weight; body weight is often normal, which can mask the disorder. Purging produces hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis, dental erosion, parotid swelling, and Russell's sign (knuckle calluses). Binge-eating disorder, new as a full DSM-5 diagnosis, is recurrent binge eating (at least weekly for three months) with marked distress but without the regular compensatory behaviours of bulimia, hence it is associated with overweight and obesity.

Exam trap

The dividing line between bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder is the presence of compensatory behaviour: bulimia binges and then purges or compensates, while binge-eating disorder binges without compensating. Anorexia is distinguished from both by significantly low body weight. Both bulimia and binge-eating disorder require the behaviour at least weekly for three months.

Taken together, this section's logic is consistent: identify the core mechanism, count the symptoms, check the duration against the precise threshold, and then run the differential against the nearest neighbours. The durations in particular (the psychotic ladder of one and six months, the two-week depressive episode, the one-week manic and four-day hypomanic episodes, the six-month GAD, the one-month PTSD against three-day acute stress, and the three-month adjustment window) are the spine on which most examination questions in abnormal psychology are built.

2 · Biopsychology: Biological Foundations

Biopsychology asks a single deep question: how does the physical nervous system give rise to behaviour, thought, and emotion? For the Amity M.A. Clinical Psychology and the M.Phil entrance this is a heavy-core, high-yield area, and examiners reward candidates who can name the structure, state its function, give the mechanism, and link it to a clinical disorder. This section builds that mastery from the cell upward: first the wiring (neuroanatomy), then the chemistry that runs along the wires (neurochemistry), then the slower chemical messaging of hormones (endocrinology), and finally what happens when the system breaks (neuropathology).

A1. The nervous system: CNS, PNS, and the autonomic divide

The entire nervous system splits into two halves. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord: it integrates information and issues commands. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is everything else, the nerves that carry signals between the CNS and the rest of the body. The PNS is the cabling; the CNS is the processor.

The PNS itself divides into two systems. The somatic nervous system handles voluntary skeletal muscle and carries sensory information inward; this is the system you use to lift a cup. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) runs involuntary, automatic functions: heart rate, digestion, pupil size, glandular secretion. The ANS is where biopsychology meets emotion, because stress and fear live here.

The ANS has two opposing branches that act like an accelerator and a brake. The sympathetic nervous system drives fight-or-flight: it dilates pupils, speeds the heart, dumps glucose, and shunts blood to the muscles, preparing the body for emergency action. The parasympathetic nervous system drives rest-and-digest: it slows the heart, constricts pupils, and promotes digestion and recovery. The two are tonically active and continuously balance one another, a state called autonomic tone.

Definition

Autonomic nervous system: the involuntary branch of the PNS controlling smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands, divided into the sympathetic (fight-or-flight, energy-mobilising) and parasympathetic (rest-and-digest, energy-conserving) divisions.

Exam trap

Sympathetic = arousal and expenditure; parasympathetic = calm and conservation. A classic distractor reverses pupil effects: sympathetic dilates the pupil (more light in to scan for threat), parasympathetic constricts it. Also note sympathetic ganglia sit in a chain near the spinal cord, whereas parasympathetic fibres synapse near or in the target organ.

A2. The neuron and the glia

The neuron is the functional signalling unit. It has four key regions. The soma (cell body) contains the nucleus and keeps the cell alive. Dendrites are branching inputs that receive signals from other neurons. The axon is the single long output fibre that carries the signal away from the soma toward other cells. At the end sit the axon terminals, which release chemical messengers.

Many axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating sheath that dramatically speeds signal conduction. The myelin is not continuous: it has regularly spaced gaps called the nodes of Ranvier. The electrical signal effectively jumps from node to node, a fast mode of conduction called saltatory conduction (from the Latin saltare, to leap). Loss of myelin, as in multiple sclerosis, slows or blocks conduction and is the structural basis of that disease.

Neurons do not work alone. Glial cells outnumber neurons and provide support, insulation, and immune defence. The four to know are: astrocytes (star-shaped cells that supply nutrients, regulate the extracellular chemical environment, and help form the blood-brain barrier); oligodendrocytes (which myelinate axons in the CNS, one cell wrapping many axons); microglia (the brain's resident immune cells, which clear debris and dead cells); and Schwann cells (which myelinate axons in the PNS, one cell per segment).

Exam trap

Myelin source differs by location. Oligodendrocytes myelinate the CNS; Schwann cells myelinate the PNS. An MCQ that says "Schwann cells myelinate brain axons" is wrong. Remember: Schwann = peripheral.

A3. The brain: hindbrain, midbrain, forebrain

Developmentally and functionally the brain organises into three regions. The hindbrain sits at the top of the spinal cord and runs the most basic life-support functions. It contains the medulla oblongata (which controls heart rate, breathing, and blood pressure, the autonomic vital functions), the pons (a bridge relaying signals between cortex and cerebellum, also involved in sleep and arousal), and the cerebellum ("little brain", which coordinates movement, balance, posture, and motor timing, and contributes to motor learning).

The midbrain is a relay and reflex centre. Its structures process visual and auditory reflexes and contain dopamine-producing regions such as the substantia nigra (movement) and the ventral tegmental area (reward), which become important in Parkinson's disease and addiction respectively.

The forebrain is the largest and most evolved region. It includes the cerebral cortex (the wrinkled outer sheet of higher cognition), the limbic system (emotion and memory), the thalamus, the hypothalamus, and the basal ganglia. The forebrain is where most clinically relevant psychology happens.

Exam trap

"Which brain structure, if damaged, is most likely to be fatal?" The answer is the medulla oblongata, because it controls breathing and heartbeat. The cerebellum causes incoordination if damaged but is not immediately fatal; the cortex can sustain large lesions without stopping vital functions. Fatal = medulla.

A4. The four cortical lobes

The cerebral cortex is divided into four lobes per hemisphere, each with characteristic functions. Examiners love lobe-to-function matching and like to test the named language areas.

The frontal lobe is the seat of action and executive control. It contains the primary motor cortex (the precentral gyrus, which sends movement commands), Broca's area (usually in the left frontal lobe, responsible for speech production), and the prefrontal cortex (executive functions: planning, working memory, impulse control, judgement, and personality). Damage to the prefrontal cortex, as in the historical case of Phineas Gage, produces disinhibition and impaired judgement.

The parietal lobe processes bodily sensation and space. It contains the primary somatosensory cortex (the postcentral gyrus, which maps touch, pressure, temperature, and pain across the body) and supports spatial awareness and integration. Damage can cause hemispatial neglect, where a patient ignores one side of space.

The temporal lobe handles hearing, language comprehension, and memory. It contains the primary auditory cortex, Wernicke's area (usually left temporal, responsible for language comprehension), and, on its inner surface, the hippocampus (memory formation). Wernicke's aphasia produces fluent but meaningless speech with poor comprehension.

The occipital lobe at the back is dedicated to vision; it houses the primary visual cortex, which receives and processes visual input. Damage causes cortical blindness or visual field defects despite healthy eyes.

A5. The limbic system

The limbic system is a set of interconnected structures wrapped around the brainstem that govern emotion, motivation, and memory. It is the emotional core of the brain.

The hippocampus is essential for forming new long-term memories, a process called memory consolidation, converting short-term experience into durable storage. It does not store old memories permanently; rather it is the gateway that lays them down. The amygdala is the brain's threat detector, central to fear, emotional learning, and the emotional colouring of memory; it triggers the fear response and tags events as emotionally significant. The hypothalamus is the master regulator of homeostasis and drives: hunger, thirst, temperature, sex, and the stress response, and it links the nervous system to the endocrine system via the pituitary. The cingulate cortex (cingulate gyrus) is involved in emotion regulation, attention, and the experience of pain. The fornix is the main output tract of the hippocampus, carrying its fibres toward the hypothalamus. The thalamus sits in the centre and acts as the great sensory relay station: nearly all sensory information (except smell) passes through the thalamus on its way to the cortex.

High-yield

Memorise the limbic division of labour: hippocampus = memory consolidation, amygdala = fear and emotion, hypothalamus = homeostasis and endocrine link, thalamus = sensory relay. These four matchings appear repeatedly in entrance papers.

Exam trap

The thalamus relays all senses except olfaction (smell), which projects directly to the cortex and limbic areas. A question asking which sense bypasses the thalamus is testing smell.

A6. Basal ganglia, contralateral control, and lateralization

The basal ganglia are deep forebrain nuclei (including the caudate, putamen, and globus pallidus) that regulate the initiation and smoothing of voluntary movement and contribute to habit learning. Their dysfunction produces movement disorders: dopamine loss here causes Parkinson's disease (poverty of movement), while degeneration causes Huntington's disease (excess movement).

Two organising principles cut across the whole brain. Contralateral control means each hemisphere controls and senses the opposite side of the body: the left motor cortex moves the right hand, and a right-sided stroke causes left-sided weakness. Lateralization means the two hemispheres specialise: in most people the left hemisphere is dominant for language and analytic/sequential processing, while the right hemisphere is specialised for spatial processing, faces, and holistic perception. The two halves communicate through the corpus callosum, a thick band of fibres; cutting it (split-brain surgery) reveals the independent capacities of each hemisphere.

Exam trap

A left-hemisphere stroke typically causes right-sided body weakness and, often, language deficits (because language is usually left-lateralised). Candidates frequently match the stroke to the same side; remember control is crossed.

B1. Key neurotransmitters and their clinical links

Neurons communicate chemically. A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger released by one neuron to influence another. For the entrance, master the function and the clinical link of each major transmitter.

Dopamine governs reward, motivation, and the control of movement. Too little dopamine in the basal ganglia (substantia nigra pathway) causes Parkinson's disease: tremor, rigidity, and slowness. Excess dopamine activity in mesolimbic pathways is implicated in the positive symptoms of schizophrenia, which is why antipsychotics block dopamine receptors. Serotonin (5-HT) regulates mood, sleep, appetite, and impulse control. Low serotonergic function is linked to depression and anxiety, and SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) treat depression by blocking serotonin reuptake, leaving more in the synapse. GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is the brain's chief inhibitory transmitter; it calms neural activity. Boosting GABA reduces anxiety, which is how benzodiazepines and alcohol act. Glutamate is the chief excitatory transmitter and is central to learning and memory through long-term potentiation (LTP); in excess it causes excitotoxicity, overexciting and killing neurons (a mechanism in stroke and neurodegeneration). Acetylcholine (ACh) drives muscle activation, attention, and memory; its loss is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease, which is why cholinesterase inhibitors are used. Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) drives arousal, alertness, and the fight-or-flight response and is implicated in mood and anxiety disorders.

B2. The resting and action potential

A neuron at rest maintains a voltage difference across its membrane, the resting potential, of about negative 70 millivolts (inside negative relative to outside). This is maintained by the selective permeability of the membrane and by the sodium-potassium pump (Na/K pump), which actively pushes three sodium (Na+) ions out for every two potassium (K+) ions it pulls in, keeping sodium high outside and potassium high inside. Each ion sits near its electrochemical equilibrium, where the pull of concentration balances the pull of charge.

When inputs depolarise the membrane to a threshold (around negative 55 mV), voltage-gated sodium channels snap open, sodium rushes in, and the cell rapidly depolarises, firing an action potential. This is all-or-none: once threshold is reached the spike fires at full size regardless of how strong the trigger was, and a weaker stimulus simply does not fire. Sodium channels then close and potassium channels open, potassium flows out, and the cell repolarises, often briefly overshooting (hyperpolarisation) before the pump restores the resting state. During the absolute refractory period no new action potential can fire (sodium channels are inactivated), which is why signals travel one way and firing rate is capped; during the relative refractory period a stronger-than-usual stimulus is needed. In myelinated axons the impulse jumps node to node, saltatory conduction, which is far faster than continuous conduction.

B3. Synaptic transmission

Neurons connect at the synapse, a tiny gap between the axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite of the next. Transmission proceeds in steps. The action potential reaches the terminal and opens calcium channels; calcium influx triggers synaptic vesicles (membrane sacs storing neurotransmitter) to fuse with the membrane and release their contents into the synaptic cleft. The transmitter diffuses across and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening channels and either exciting or inhibiting that neuron. The signal is then terminated in two ways: reuptake (the transmitter is pumped back into the sending neuron for reuse, the step SSRIs block for serotonin) or enzymatic degradation (an enzyme breaks the transmitter down, for example acetylcholinesterase destroying acetylcholine).

Drugs and toxins act on these steps. An agonist mimics or enhances a neurotransmitter's action (for example, by activating its receptor or blocking reuptake), while an antagonist blocks or reduces its action (for example, by occupying the receptor without activating it). Antipsychotics are dopamine antagonists; nicotine is an acetylcholine-receptor agonist.

Exam trap

Agonist enhances, antagonist blocks. An SSRI is loosely a serotonin agonist in effect because it increases available serotonin (by blocking reuptake), even though it does not bind the serotonin receptor itself. Watch for the distinction between acting on the receptor and acting on reuptake or breakdown.

C1. The endocrine system and major glands

Alongside the fast electrical nervous system, the body runs a slower chemical messaging network: the endocrine system, a set of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream to act on distant target organs. Hormonal signalling is slower in onset but longer in duration than neural signalling, which makes it well suited to regulating sustained states such as growth, metabolism, reproduction, and the stress response.

The pituitary gland is called the master gland because its hormones control many other glands. It sits just below and is directed by the hypothalamus, which is the true command link between brain and endocrine system: the hypothalamus releases factors that tell the anterior pituitary what to do. The thyroid (in the neck) secretes thyroid hormone, which sets metabolic rate; underactivity causes sluggishness and depressed mood, overactivity causes anxiety and agitation. The adrenal glands (atop the kidneys) have two parts: the adrenal cortex secretes cortisol (the stress steroid) and the adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline (epinephrine) for rapid arousal. The pancreas secretes insulin and glucagon to regulate blood sugar. The gonads (ovaries and testes) secrete the sex hormones oestrogen, progesterone, and testosterone, which influence reproduction, development, and behaviour.

Definition

Hormone: a chemical messenger secreted by an endocrine gland into the bloodstream that travels to and acts on distant target tissues, producing slower but more sustained effects than neurotransmitters.

C2. The stress response: HPA axis and the SAM pathway

Stress triggers two coordinated systems on different timescales. The fast one is the SAM pathway (sympathetic-adrenal-medullary). The sympathetic nervous system fires within seconds and signals the adrenal medulla to dump adrenaline (epinephrine) into the blood, producing the immediate fight-or-flight surge: racing heart, dilated pupils, mobilised glucose. This is the rapid alarm.

The slower, sustained system is the HPA axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal). Under stress the hypothalamus releases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone), which travels to the anterior pituitary and stimulates release of ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) into the blood. ACTH reaches the adrenal cortex and triggers secretion of cortisol, the chief stress hormone, which sustains glucose availability, suppresses non-essential functions (digestion, immunity), and keeps the body mobilised. Critically, cortisol feeds back to the hypothalamus and pituitary to switch off CRH and ACTH, a negative feedback loop that ends the response once the stressor passes. Chronic stress dysregulates this loop, and persistently high cortisol is linked to depression, immune suppression, and hippocampal damage.

D1. Alzheimer's disease and dementias

Alzheimer's disease is the most common neurodegenerative dementia. Its two pathological hallmarks are amyloid plaques (extracellular clumps of beta-amyloid protein between neurons) and neurofibrillary tangles (intracellular twisted fibres of abnormal tau protein). Neuronal death is prominent in the hippocampus early on, explaining the cardinal early symptom of memory loss, and there is marked loss of cholinergic (acetylcholine) neurons, which is why cholinesterase inhibitors are used to boost remaining acetylcholine. The course is insidious and progressive: it begins with subtle short-term memory failures, advances to language and visuospatial decline and disorientation, and ends in global cognitive and functional loss over years.

Vascular dementia, the next most common, is caused by impaired blood supply, typically a series of small strokes or infarcts. Its signature is a stepwise decline: cognition drops abruptly with each vascular event, then plateaus, rather than the smooth gradual slope of Alzheimer's. Risk factors are vascular (hypertension, diabetes, smoking), and deficits depend on which regions lost blood supply.

Exam trap

Plaques = amyloid (beta-amyloid, extracellular); tangles = tau (intracellular). Examiners frequently swap these. Memory hook: "tangles = tau". Also: Alzheimer's declines gradually; vascular dementia declines in steps.

D2. Delirium versus dementia

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness caused by an underlying medical condition (infection, drug toxicity, metabolic disturbance, withdrawal). Its defining features are acute onset (hours to days), a fluctuating course (often worse at night, called sundowning), prominent inattention and clouded consciousness, and crucially that it is usually reversible once the cause is treated. This contrasts sharply with dementia, which is chronic, gradual, and progressive with a clear sensorium until late stages.

D3. Amnesic syndromes and traumatic brain injury

Amnesia comes in two directions defined relative to the precipitating event. Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new memories after the event: the patient cannot lay down new long-term memories. Retrograde amnesia is the loss of memories formed before the event. The landmark case is patient H.M., who had his hippocampi removed surgically to treat epilepsy and was left with profound anterograde amnesia: he could not form new conscious memories, although he could still learn new motor skills, proving the hippocampus is essential for forming new declarative memories specifically.

Korsakoff's syndrome is an amnesic disorder caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, classically from chronic alcoholism. It produces severe anterograde amnesia and confabulation (filling memory gaps with fabricated material) and damages diencephalic structures such as the mammillary bodies and thalamus.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) results from external mechanical force. A coup-contrecoup injury bruises the brain both at the site of impact (coup) and at the opposite side as the brain rebounds within the skull (contrecoup). A concussion is a transient disturbance of brain function from a blow, with brief confusion or loss of consciousness and usually no visible structural damage on routine imaging. Diffuse axonal injury is widespread shearing and tearing of axons from rotational acceleration-deceleration forces and is a major cause of persistent coma and severe disability after high-speed trauma.

Exam trap

Anterograde = cannot form NEW memories (forward in time); retrograde = lost OLD memories (backward in time). H.M. is the classic anterograde case and is the proof that the hippocampus is required to form new declarative memories, not to store old ones. Korsakoff's is the thiamine/alcohol amnesia with confabulation.

High-yield

Tie the section together: structure to function to clinic. Hippocampus (limbic) to memory consolidation to H.M. and Alzheimer's; dopamine to movement and reward to Parkinson's and schizophrenia; HPA axis to cortisol to chronic stress and depression; medulla to vital functions to "fatal if damaged". The entrance rewards exactly these structure-mechanism-disorder chains.

3 · Research Methodology & Statistics

Clinical psychology is evidence-based, and this area is heavy-core because a clinician who cannot read a study, judge its design, or interpret an analysis cannot practice responsibly. This section builds the logic from the ground up: how we design a study to make a causal claim defensible, how we reason from a sample to a population, and how we choose the correct statistical model for the data we have. The recurring exam theme is matching: the design to the question, the test to the level of measurement, the analysis to the assumptions. Get the matching wrong and every number that follows is meaningless.

3.1 Research Design

A research design is the skeleton of a study: who is observed, what is manipulated, what is held constant, and when measurements are taken. Designs differ chiefly in how much control they give the investigator over the independent variable and over participant assignment, and that control is exactly what licenses (or forbids) a causal conclusion.

True experimental designs

A true experiment rests on three pillars. First, manipulation: the researcher actively varies the independent variable (IV) rather than merely observing it, for example giving one group a new therapy and another a placebo. Second, control: a comparison group plus holding extraneous influences constant, so the only systematic difference between conditions is the IV. Third, and decisively, randomisation: participants are allocated to conditions by chance (random assignment), which on average equates the groups on every variable, measured or unmeasured, before treatment begins. Random assignment lets us attribute group differences to the IV rather than to pre-existing differences, and it is the single feature that separates a true experiment from everything else.

Definition

Random assignment distributes participants to conditions by chance and is the engine of internal validity in experiments. Do not confuse it with random selection (sampling), which concerns who enters the study at all and serves external validity. A study can have one without the other.

Quasi-experimental designs

A quasi-experiment manipulates an IV (or exploits a naturally occurring one) but lacks random assignment, usually because random assignment is impossible or unethical. Comparing depression outcomes between a ward that adopted a new protocol and one that did not, or comparing smokers with non-smokers, are quasi-experimental: the groups were formed by self-selection or circumstance, not by the coin. The cost is that any difference might reflect pre-existing group differences (a selection threat) rather than the IV, so causal claims are weaker. Such designs remain valuable in clinical and field settings where true randomisation cannot be achieved.

Cross-sectional, longitudinal, cohort, and sequential designs

Developmental and epidemiological questions turn on the time dimension. A cross-sectional design measures different age or exposure groups at a single point in time: fast and cheap, but it confounds age with cohort (generation), so an apparent age effect may simply be a generational difference. A longitudinal design follows the same individuals over time, separating true developmental change from cohort effects, but it is slow, expensive, and vulnerable to attrition and repeated-testing effects. A cohort design follows a defined group (a birth cohort, a diagnostic cohort) forward. A sequential (cross-sequential) design is a hybrid that follows several cohorts longitudinally at once, allowing the researcher to disentangle age, cohort, and time-of-measurement effects in one study.

Single-case experimental designs (SCED)

Single-case designs are workhorses of clinical research because they establish experimental control within one individual, repeatedly measured, serving as their own control. They are genuine experiments, not anecdotal case studies.

  • ABAB (reversal) design: baseline (A), introduce treatment (B), withdraw to baseline (A), reinstate treatment (B). If behaviour tracks the introduction and withdrawal of treatment, the treatment is the likely cause. The reversal is the proof, but it is unethical when the gain should not be withdrawn or when learning is irreversible.
  • Multiple-baseline design: treatment is introduced at staggered times across different behaviours, settings, or people. When change appears in each baseline only as treatment reaches it, causal control is demonstrated without any withdrawal, solving the ethical problem of ABAB.
  • Changing-criterion design: the performance criterion for reinforcement is raised in steps; if behaviour shifts to meet each successive criterion, the intervention is controlling it. Ideal for gradually shaping a target such as cigarettes per day or exercise minutes.
Memory aid

SCED chooser: cannot ethically withdraw the gain? Use multiple-baseline. Shaping a behaviour up or down in steps? Use changing-criterion. Reversible behaviour and withdrawal is acceptable? ABAB.

Internal and external validity and their threats

Internal validity is the degree to which a study can claim the IV (and not something else) caused the change in the dependent variable (DV). External validity is the degree to which findings generalise beyond the study to other people, settings, and times. The two often trade off: a tightly controlled lab experiment has high internal but possibly low external validity, while a naturalistic field study reverses the balance.

3.2 Fundamentals: Hypothesis Testing, Variables, Sampling, Ethics

Variables and operationalization

The independent variable (IV) is the presumed cause, manipulated or grouped; the dependent variable (DV) is the measured outcome. An extraneous variable is any other variable that could influence the DV; it becomes a confounding variable when it varies systematically with the IV, making it impossible to separate its effect from the IV's. Operationalization means defining an abstract construct in concrete, measurable terms: "anxiety" becomes "score on the Beck Anxiety Inventory." A good operational definition makes the study replicable; a vague one makes it untestable.

High-yield

All confounds are extraneous variables, but not all extraneous variables are confounds. A confound is specifically an extraneous variable that is correlated with the IV. Randomisation neutralises confounds by breaking that correlation.

Hypothesis testing logic

Inferential statistics ask whether a result in a sample is likely to hold in the population or is just sampling noise. We state a null hypothesis (H0) of no effect or no difference, and an alternative hypothesis (H1) of a real effect. A non-directional (two-tailed) alternative predicts only that a difference exists; a directional (one-tailed) alternative predicts the direction (group A > group B). We then compute a test statistic and its p-value: the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as observed if H0 were true. If p is below the preset alpha (conventionally 0.05), we reject H0 and call the result statistically significant.

Exam trap

The p-value is not the probability that H0 is true, nor the probability the result was due to chance, nor the size of the effect. It is the probability of the data given H0. A tiny p with a trivially small effect in a huge sample is statistically significant but clinically meaningless, which is why effect size must be reported alongside p.

Because we decide under uncertainty, two errors are possible. A Type I error (false positive) rejects a true null: we claim an effect that does not exist; its probability is alpha. A Type II error (false negative) retains a false null: we miss a real effect; its probability is beta. Statistical power = 1 - beta, the probability of detecting a true effect, increased mainly by larger samples, larger true effects, and higher alpha. Effect size (Cohen's d, r, eta-squared) quantifies the magnitude of an effect independently of sample size and is what meta-analyses pool.

Research ethics

Human-participant research is governed by four principles. Autonomy respects the participant's right to self-determination, operationalised through informed consent (a competent, voluntary, fully informed agreement, with the right to withdraw). Beneficence obliges the researcher to maximise benefits, and non-maleficence to do no harm (minimise risk). Justice demands fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research across groups. Practically, these are enforced through confidentiality (protecting participant data), debriefing (explaining the study afterward, especially when deception was used), and prior review by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or ethics committee that approves the protocol before data collection begins.

3.3 Descriptive Foundations

Levels of measurement

The level of measurement of the DV dictates which statistics are legitimate, so this is the first question to ask of any dataset. Nominal data are unordered categories (diagnosis, sex); only counts and modes apply. Ordinal data are ordered but with unequal or unknown intervals (Likert ratings, ranks); medians and rank-based tests apply. Interval data have equal intervals but no true zero (temperature in Celsius, many IQ scales); means and standard deviations apply, but ratios do not. Ratio data have equal intervals and a true zero (reaction time, weight, number of errors); all arithmetic, including ratios, is valid.

Memory aid

"NOIR" orders the scales from least to most information: Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio. Parametric tests generally need interval or ratio data; nominal and ordinal data steer you toward non-parametric or categorical tests.

Central tendency, dispersion, the normal curve

Central tendency summarises the typical value: the mean (arithmetic average, sensitive to outliers, for interval/ratio data), the median (middle value, robust, the choice for skewed or ordinal data), and the mode (most frequent, the only option for nominal data). Dispersion describes spread: the range, the variance (mean squared deviation from the mean), and its square root, the standard deviation (SD). The normal curve is the symmetric, bell-shaped distribution where mean = median = mode and where roughly 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of cases fall within one, two, and three SDs of the mean (the empirical rule).

Skew describes asymmetry: in positive (right) skew a long right tail pulls the mean above the median; in negative (left) skew the reverse holds. A z-score standardises a raw score, z = (X - mean) / SD, expressing how many SDs a value lies from the mean and allowing scores from different scales to be compared on a common metric.

Memory aid

Skew direction follows the tail, not the hump. For the mean-median order, the mean chases the tail: positive skew gives mean > median; negative skew gives mean < median.

3.4 Parametric vs Non-Parametric Tests

Parametric tests make assumptions about the population distribution and use the actual numeric values, which makes them more powerful (better at detecting true effects) when their assumptions hold. The core assumptions are: (1) normality, the DV is approximately normally distributed in the population; (2) homogeneity of variance, the groups have roughly equal variances (homoscedasticity); (3) the data are at the interval or ratio level; and (4) independence of observations (except where a design deliberately pairs them). When these assumptions are violated, when the data are ordinal or the distribution is badly skewed or the sample is tiny, we switch to non-parametric tests, which work on ranks or frequencies, make far fewer distributional assumptions, but sacrifice some power.

3.5 t-Tests

A t-test compares means and is used when there are at most two means to compare. There are three forms, distinguished by the design:

  • One-sample t-test: compares a single sample mean against a known or hypothesised population value (e.g. is this clinic's mean BDI score different from the published norm of 10?).
  • Independent-samples t-test: compares the means of two separate, unrelated groups (e.g. therapy group vs control group), assuming independence and homogeneity of variance.
  • Paired-samples (dependent) t-test: compares two related scores, typically the same participants measured twice (pretest vs posttest) or matched pairs. By analysing the within-subject differences it removes between-person variability and gains power.
High-yield

Choose by counting means and checking relatedness: one mean vs a constant gives one-sample; two unrelated groups gives independent; the same people measured twice gives paired. Three or more means demands ANOVA, never repeated t-tests.

3.6 ANOVA: One-Way, Repeated-Measures, Factorial

Analysis of variance compares three or more means by partitioning total variability into between-group variance (differences among the group means, reflecting the IV plus error) and within-group variance (variability inside each group, pure error). The test statistic is the F ratio = between-group variance / within-group variance. If the IV has a real effect, between-group variance inflates and F grows large; an F near 1 indicates the group differences are no bigger than chance.

High-yield

Why ANOVA instead of many t-tests? Because each t-test carries its own alpha, running several across the same data inflates the family-wise (cumulative) Type I error rate. With three groups and three pairwise t-tests at alpha 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive rises well above 5%. One omnibus ANOVA holds alpha at 0.05 across the whole comparison.

A significant omnibus F tells you that the groups differ but not which pairs differ, so it is followed by post-hoc tests that compare pairs while correcting for multiple comparisons: Tukey's HSD and the Bonferroni correction (which divides alpha by the number of comparisons) are the standard choices.

The three ANOVA families the syllabus names are distinguished by how many IVs there are and whether the same participants appear in every condition.

Exam trap

The named contrast: factorial vs repeated-measures ANOVA. The defining difference is not the number of groups. Factorial is about the number of IVs (two or more), and its hallmark is the interaction effect. Repeated-measures is about who provides the data: the same participants measured across all levels of a single IV (within-subjects). A study with two IVs is factorial regardless of participants; a study with one IV measured on the same people over time is repeated-measures. A design can be both at once (a factorial with one within-subjects factor is a mixed-design ANOVA).

Definition

Interaction effect: the effect of one IV on the DV changes depending on the level of another IV. On a line graph, non-parallel (crossing or diverging) lines signal an interaction. A significant interaction must be interpreted before the main effects, because the main effects can be misleading when an interaction is present.

Two extensions appear briefly. ANCOVA (analysis of covariance) is ANOVA with a continuous covariate statistically removed first (e.g. controlling for baseline scores or age), which reduces error variance and adjusts for pre-existing differences. MANOVA (multivariate ANOVA) handles two or more dependent variables simultaneously, controlling Type I error across outcomes and detecting effects on the set of DVs jointly.

3.7 Correlation and Correlation Matrices

Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables. The coefficient ranges from -1 to +1: the sign gives direction (positive = move together, negative = move oppositely) and the absolute value gives strength (0 = no linear relation, 1 = perfect). Pearson r is the parametric coefficient for two interval/ratio variables that are linearly related. Spearman rho is its non-parametric counterpart, computed on ranks, for ordinal data or monotonic-but-non-linear relationships. Point-biserial correlation applies when one variable is genuinely dichotomous (e.g. pass/fail) and the other is continuous.

Squaring r gives the coefficient of determination, r-squared, the proportion of variance in one variable explained by ("shared with") the other. An r of 0.50 looks moderate, but r-squared = 0.25 means only 25% of the variance is accounted for, a sobering correction to over-reading a correlation.

A correlation matrix is a square table listing every variable on both axes, with each cell holding the correlation between the row and column variable. The diagonal is all 1.00 (each variable with itself), and the matrix is symmetric about that diagonal. It lets a researcher scan all pairwise associations at once and is the raw input to techniques such as factor analysis.

Exam trap

Correlation is not causation. A strong r can arise from reverse causation or a third (confounding) variable. Only a true experiment with manipulation and randomisation supports a causal claim. Also distinguish r from r-squared: r is the correlation; r-squared is the variance explained, always smaller for any r between 0 and 1.

3.8 Linear Regression

Where correlation describes the association between variables, regression goes further and uses it for prediction, estimating the value of an outcome from one or more predictors. Simple linear regression uses one predictor and fits the line Y = a + bX, where Y is the predicted outcome, a is the intercept (the value of Y when X = 0), b is the slope or regression coefficient (the change in Y per one-unit change in X), and X is the predictor. Multiple regression uses two or more predictors (Y = a + b1X1 + b2X2 + ...), each coefficient representing that predictor's unique contribution while holding the others constant.

Standardised coefficients, the beta weights, are expressed in SD units so that predictors measured on different scales can be compared for relative importance. The overall R-squared reports the proportion of variance in the outcome explained by the full set of predictors together.

Exam trap

Correlation vs regression: correlation quantifies the degree of association and is symmetric (r of X with Y equals r of Y with X). Regression predicts a specified outcome from predictor(s), is directional (predicting Y from X differs from predicting X from Y), and yields a usable equation. If a stem asks "how strongly are they related," that is correlation; "predict / estimate the outcome from," that is regression.

3.9 Factor Analysis

Factor analysis is a data-reduction technique that takes a large set of correlated observed variables (e.g. many test items) and identifies a smaller number of underlying latent factors (constructs) that explain the pattern of correlations among them. Its founding application was Charles Spearman, who invented factor analysis and used it to argue for a general intelligence factor, g. It remains central to test construction: it shows which items cluster onto which dimension, providing evidence of a scale's construct validity and dimensional structure.

Two forms answer different questions. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is used when you do not yet know how many factors underlie the data and you let the structure emerge from the correlation matrix. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is used when you have a theoretically specified model and you test how well the data fit that pre-specified factor structure (a form of structural equation modelling). EFA explores; CFA confirms.

Key outputs: eigenvalues express how much total variance each factor accounts for, and a common retention rule keeps factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser's criterion), often cross-checked against a scree plot. Factor loadings are the correlations between each observed variable and a factor; high loadings show which items define (load onto) which factor and so name the construct.

High-yield

EFA = exploratory, hypothesis-generating, number of factors unknown. CFA = confirmatory, hypothesis-testing, structure pre-specified. Eigenvalues = variance explained per factor (retain > 1). Loadings = item-to-factor correlations. Spearman invented factor analysis (the g factor of intelligence).

Memory aid

Whole-section test chooser: first fix the level of measurement (NOIR) and the number of groups/means; then check whether parametric assumptions hold (if not, jump to the non-parametric twin); then count IVs (2+ IVs = factorial) and ask whether the same participants recur (yes = repeated-measures/paired). Association = correlation; prediction = regression; latent structure = factor analysis.

4 · Psychological Testing & Psychodiagnostics

Psychological testing is the disciplined craft of turning fuzzy, internal attributes (intelligence, anxiety, memory, a personality trait) into numbers that mean something. A test is a standardized procedure for sampling behaviour and scoring it against a reference. Psychodiagnostics is the clinical application of that craft: choosing the right instrument, administering it under standard conditions, scoring it correctly, and interpreting the score in the context of a person. This section builds the foundations (psychometrics), then names the major cognitive, personality, and neuropsychological instruments at the level of detail an examiner expects you to produce on demand.

High-yield

The four pillars of any usable test are reliability (consistency), validity (does it measure what it claims), standardization (fixed procedure), and norms (a comparison group). Almost every psychometrics question is testing one of these four and the relationships between them.

A. Psychometrics: the foundations of test construction

Test construction begins with a blueprint: define the construct, sample its content, and write items. Item writing demands clear, unambiguous items free of double negatives, leading cues, and cultural bias. Once items are drafted and trialled on a sample, item analysis decides which items survive.

Two statistics govern item analysis. The item difficulty index (p) is the proportion of examinees who answer an item correctly. It ranges from 0 (no one got it) to 1 (everyone got it). Counter-intuitively, a high p means an easy item. For maximum discrimination on a typical ability test, items clustering around p = 0.5 spread people out best (each item then carries the most information). The item discrimination index (D) measures how well an item separates high scorers from low scorers, often computed as the proportion correct in the top group minus the proportion correct in the bottom group, ranging from -1 to +1. A positive D means strong scorers pass the item more often than weak scorers (good). A near-zero or negative D flags a bad item (ambiguous, miskeyed, or measuring something else) and it gets revised or dropped.

Exam trap

A high difficulty index means an EASY item, not a hard one, because p is the proportion who got it right. If 0.90 of people pass an item, it is easy (p = 0.90). Students reverse this constantly. Difficulty index up = easier.

Classical Test Theory vs Item Response Theory

Classical Test Theory (CTT) rests on one famous equation: X = T + E, where an observed score (X) equals the person's true score (T) plus measurement error (E). The true score is the average score you would get over infinite testings; error is the random noise of any single administration. CTT assumes errors are random, uncorrelated with true scores, and average to zero. Reliability in CTT is the ratio of true-score variance to observed-score variance. The major limitation of CTT is that its statistics are sample-dependent: item difficulty and a person's score depend on the particular group tested, so the numbers shift if you use an easier or harder sample.

Item Response Theory (IRT) models the probability of a correct response as a function of the person's latent trait level (theta) and the properties of the item, captured by an item characteristic curve (ICC), an S-shaped logistic curve. IRT uses up to three item parameters: a = discrimination (the steepness of the curve), b = difficulty (the trait level at which a person has a 50 percent chance of answering correctly, the curve's location on the x-axis), and c = guessing (the lower asymptote, the floor probability of a correct answer by chance). The 1-parameter (Rasch) model uses only b; the 2-parameter adds a; the 3-parameter adds c. The decisive advantage is that IRT parameters are sample-independent (invariant): item difficulty is a property of the item itself, and ability is estimated on the same scale regardless of which items were administered. This invariance is what makes IRT the engine behind computer-adaptive testing.

Exam trap

CTT is sample-dependent; IRT is sample-independent (invariant). That single contrast is the most tested CTT-vs-IRT fact. Also remember the equation belongs to CTT: X = T + E. The S-shaped curve with difficulty/discrimination/guessing parameters is IRT (the ICC), not CTT.

Reliability is the consistency or repeatability of measurement: how much of the score is true signal versus random error. It is expressed as a coefficient from 0 to 1. There is no single reliability; you choose the type that matches the kind of error you worry about.

Standardization and norms

Standardization means administering and scoring the test under fixed, uniform conditions so that scores are comparable across people. Norms are the distribution of scores in a defined reference (standardization) sample, against which an individual's raw score is compared and converted into a meaningful relative score.

A norm-referenced test compares a person to others (percentile rank, IQ relative to age peers). A criterion-referenced test compares a person to a fixed standard or mastery cut, regardless of how others did (a driving test, a board exam pass mark). The reliability-validity relationship

This is the single most tested conceptual point in psychometrics. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. A test must be consistent before it can be accurate, but consistency alone does not guarantee it measures the right thing. A bathroom scale that always reads 5 kg too high is perfectly reliable (same answer every time) yet invalid (wrong answer every time). Conversely, a test cannot be valid beyond the limit set by its reliability: the more random error, the lower the ceiling on validity. So a test can be reliable but not valid; a test cannot be valid without being reliable.

Exam trap

Reliable does not equal valid. Reliability is a precondition for validity, not a substitute. The textbook one-liner: "reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity." If a question asks which can exist without the other, the answer is that reliability can exist without validity, but validity cannot exist without reliability.

B. Cognitive assessments

The Wechsler scales

The Wechsler family is the standard for clinical intelligence assessment, built on the deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15) rather than the older mental-age ratio. The three age bands are: WPPSI (Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence) for ages roughly 2.5 to 7; WISC-V (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, 5th ed) for ages 6 to 16; and WAIS-IV (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, 4th ed) for ages 16 to 90. Each yields a Full Scale IQ (FSIQ) built from index scores.

High-yield

Know the band by age: WPPSI = preschool, WISC = children (6 to 16), WAIS = adults. Know the four classic WAIS-IV indices (VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI) feeding FSIQ, and that the IQ metric is a deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15).

Stanford-Binet

The Binet lineage is the origin of modern intelligence testing. Alfred Binet (with Theodore Simon) built the first practical scale in 1905 in Paris to identify children needing educational support, introducing the idea of mental age. Lewis Terman at Stanford adapted it for American use as the Stanford-Binet and popularized the ratio IQ: IQ = (mental age / chronological age) x 100. The current Stanford-Binet, 5th edition (SB5) has abandoned the ratio formula for a deviation IQ and measures five factors (Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing, Working Memory) across verbal and nonverbal domains.

Memory aid

Lineage: Binet (mental age, 1905, France) to Terman (Stanford-Binet, ratio IQ, USA). The ratio-IQ formula (MA/CA x 100) is the historical Stanford-Binet; modern SB5 and all Wechsler scales use the deviation IQ instead.

Raven's Progressive Matrices

Raven's Progressive Matrices is a non-verbal test of abstract reasoning: each item shows a pattern matrix with a missing piece, and the examinee selects the option that completes the pattern. Because it requires no reading, language, or culturally specific knowledge, it is described as culture-fair (culture-reduced) and is a strong marker of fluid intelligence (Spearman's g, specifically fluid g), the capacity for novel problem solving as opposed to acquired knowledge (crystallized intelligence).

Exam trap

Three labels almost always attach to Raven's: non-verbal, culture-fair, and a measure of fluid g (eductive ability). If a question asks for a culture-fair test of fluid reasoning, the answer is Raven's Progressive Matrices. Do not call it a verbal or crystallized-intelligence test.

Memory batteries

The Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS, current WMS-IV) is the most widely used standalone memory battery, yielding index scores for Auditory Memory, Visual Memory, Visual Working Memory, Immediate Memory, and Delayed Memory. It dissociates verbal from visual memory and immediate from delayed recall, which is exactly the pattern needed to characterize amnestic syndromes.

The Indian standard is the PGI Memory Scale, developed by Pershad and Wig at the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGI), Chandigarh. It assesses ten functions including remote memory, recent memory, mental balance, attention and concentration, delayed and immediate recall, retention of similar and dissimilar pairs, visual retention, and recognition. Being normed on an Indian population, it is preferred for Indian clinical practice and is a high-yield "Indian test" name for this exam.

High-yield

Indian-developed tests recur in this exam. Memorize: PGI Memory Scale = Pershad and Wig (PGI Chandigarh). Pair this with other Indian tools (the NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery below; Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence; Malin's Indian adaptation of WISC).

C. Personality inventories

Personality assessment divides sharply into objective (self-report) inventories, with fixed-response items scored by key, and projective techniques, with ambiguous stimuli and open-ended responses.

Objective inventories

The MMPI-2 (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, 2nd ed) is the most researched objective personality/psychopathology inventory: 567 true/false items yielding 10 clinical scales (Hypochondriasis, Depression, Hysteria, Psychopathic Deviate, Masculinity-Femininity, Paranoia, Psychasthenia, Schizophrenia, Hypomania, Social Introversion). Its scales were built by empirical (criterion) keying: items were retained not because they "sounded" relevant but because they statistically distinguished a diagnostic group from controls, regardless of content. Critically, the MMPI has validity scales that detect response distortion, classically L (Lie), F (Frequency/Infrequency), and K (Correction/defensiveness), which flag faking-good, faking-bad, and defensive responding.

The 16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire) by Raymond Cattell measures 16 source traits that Cattell derived by factor analysis of the trait lexicon (distinguishing deep "source traits" from observable "surface traits"). It is a normal-range personality measure scored on the sten scale.

The NEO-PI-R (Costa and McCrae) operationalizes the Big Five / Five-Factor Model, mnemonic OCEAN: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism, each with six facets. It is the dominant dimensional model of normal personality.

Exam trap

Match author to model: Cattell = 16PF (16 source traits, factor analysis); Costa and McCrae = NEO-PI-R / Big Five (OCEAN); MMPI = empirical/criterion keying with validity scales L, F, K. The MMPI's hallmark fact is empirical keying (items kept for how they discriminate groups, not for face content), and that it carries dedicated validity scales to catch lying and defensiveness.

Projective techniques

The projective hypothesis holds that when a person is asked to respond to an ambiguous, unstructured stimulus, they "project" their own needs, conflicts, and defences onto it, revealing material that bypasses conscious self-presentation. The trade-off for this depth is weaker psychometrics.

The Rorschach inkblot test presents 10 cards (5 achromatic, 2 black-and-red, 3 multicoloured) and asks "what might this be?". The dominant scoring system is the Exner Comprehensive System, which codes each response along dimensions including Location (whole blot W, common detail D, unusual detail Dd), Determinants (what drove the percept: Form, Movement, Color, Shading), Content (human, animal, anatomy, etc.), and Popularity (whether the response is one commonly given). The configuration of these codes generates interpretive indices (e.g. for thought disorder, coping style, affect).

The Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), developed by Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan at Harvard, presents ambiguous scenes (people in emotionally suggestive situations) and asks the examinee to tell a story: what is happening, what led up to it, what the characters feel, and how it ends. Interpretation focuses on recurring narrative themes, the identified "hero", and the needs and "press" expressed, tying directly to Murray's theory of psychogenic needs, notably the need for achievement (nAch) later studied by McClelland.

D. Neuropsychological batteries

Neuropsychological assessment maps behaviour to brain function, characterizing deficits across domains (attention, memory, executive function, language, visuospatial ability, and motor/sensory function) to help localize damage and guide rehabilitation. The three named batteries:

The Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery (HRNB) is a fixed battery of standardized tests (Category Test for abstraction/concept formation, Tactual Performance Test, Rhythm Test, Finger Tapping, Trail Making, etc.). It yields an Impairment Index summarizing overall deficit and is strong at detecting and lateralizing brain damage; it is comprehensive but lengthy.

The Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery (LNNB) operationalizes Alexander Luria's qualitative theory of functional brain systems into a standardized, quantified battery. It groups items into clinical scales (Motor, Rhythm, Tactile, Visual, Receptive Speech, Expressive Speech, Writing, Reading, Arithmetic, Memory, Intellectual Processes). It is shorter than the Halstead-Reitan and blends Luria's qualitative process approach with psychometric scoring.

The NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery is the Indian-standardized battery (developed at the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences, Bangalore), normed on the Indian population with age and education stratification. It assesses the core domains: attention (e.g. digit/visual span, color trails), executive function (Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Tower tests, verbal fluency, Stroop), memory, language, visuospatial construction, and motor speed. It is the preferred battery in Indian clinical and research settings precisely because of culturally and educationally appropriate norms.

High-yield

Three batteries, three identities: Halstead-Reitan (fixed battery, Impairment Index, lateralizes damage); Luria-Nebraska (operationalizes Luria's functional-systems theory into clinical scales); NIMHANS battery (the INDIAN battery with Indian norms). All assess attention, memory, executive function, language, visuospatial, and motor domains, and all serve to detect and help localize brain dysfunction.

5 · Psychotherapy & Clinical Interventions

Psychotherapy is the systematic application of psychological principles to relieve distress, modify behaviour, and promote growth through a structured therapeutic relationship. For the RCI-regulated clinical psychology entrance you must know each major school at depth: who founded it, the theoretical model behind it, its signature techniques, a concrete example, and the precise goal of treatment. This section moves from the four mainstream modalities (cognitive-behavioural, psychodynamic, humanistic/existential, systemic/family), through specialized interventions for specific populations, and finally into the Indian law and professional ethics that govern practice. Treat the legal section as memorise-verbatim material: the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 and the Rehabilitation Council of India are heavily examined and easily confused.

Definition

Psychotherapy (the "talking cure", a phrase coined by Freud's patient Anna O.) is a planned, structured intervention delivered by a trained professional that uses psychological methods (not drugs) to treat mental disorders and emotional problems. It is distinguished from counselling by its greater depth, focus on personality and disorder, and grounding in a formal theory of psychopathology.

Overview: the four major schools

Before the detail, fix the master map in your head. Each school answers the question "what causes suffering and what cures it?" differently. CBT says distorted thinking and maladaptive behaviour; the cure is restructuring both. Psychodynamic therapy says unconscious conflict rooted in early experience; the cure is insight. Humanistic therapy says a blocked drive toward self-actualisation; the cure is an accepting relationship. Systemic therapy says a dysfunctional family system; the cure is changing the pattern of relationships.

A1. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

CBT is the most evidence-supported psychotherapy and the most frequently tested. It rests on the principle that thoughts, feelings, and behaviours are interconnected, and that changing maladaptive thoughts and behaviours changes emotion. It is structured, present-focused, time-limited, collaborative, and uses homework. Two founders dominate the exam: Aaron T. Beck (cognitive therapy) and Albert Ellis (rational emotive behaviour therapy, REBT).

Beck's cognitive model. Aaron Beck, originally a psychoanalyst, developed cognitive therapy in the 1960s while studying depression. He proposed a layered architecture of cognition. At the surface are automatic thoughts: rapid, involuntary, situation-specific thoughts ("I'm going to fail this exam") that the person may not even notice. These are shaped by cognitive distortions, systematic errors in reasoning, including all-or-nothing (dichotomous) thinking, overgeneralisation, catastrophising, mental filtering, mind-reading, personalisation, and emotional reasoning. Beneath these lie core beliefs and schemas: deep, absolute beliefs about the self, world, and future formed early in life ("I am unlovable", "I am incompetent"). In depression these cluster into the famous cognitive triad: a negative view of the self, the world, and the future.

Definition

Beck's cognitive triad is the trio of negative views that maintain depression: (1) a negative view of oneself ("I am worthless"), (2) a negative view of the world/current experience ("everything is against me"), and (3) a negative view of the future ("things will never improve"). Memorise it as Self, World, Future.

Ellis's REBT. Albert Ellis founded rational emotive behaviour therapy (originally "rational therapy", 1955), predating Beck. Its centrepiece is the ABC model: an Activating event (A) does not directly cause the emotional and behavioural Consequence (C); rather it is the Belief (B) about the event that causes C. Therapy adds D (Disputing the irrational belief) and E (the new Effect/effective philosophy). Ellis identified core irrational beliefs built on rigid demands ("musturbation"), such as "I must be loved by everyone", "I must succeed at everything", and "the world must treat me fairly". The therapist actively, often forcefully, disputes these beliefs to replace demands with flexible preferences.

Exam trap

CBT = Beck vs REBT = Ellis. Beck created cognitive therapy and the cognitive triad; Ellis created REBT and the ABC model. Ellis came first (1955) and is more confrontational and directive; Beck (1960s) is more collaborative and uses Socratic questioning ("guided discovery"). Do not attribute the ABC model to Beck or the cognitive triad to Ellis.

Core CBT techniques. Cognitive restructuring identifies, tests, and modifies distorted thoughts. Thought records (the dysfunctional thought record) are worksheets logging situation, automatic thought, emotion, evidence for and against, and a balanced alternative thought. Socratic questioning uses guided questions so the client discovers the distortion themselves rather than being told. Behavioural activation treats depression by scheduling rewarding and mastery activities to break the withdrawal-low mood cycle. Exposure (graded, in vivo or imaginal; the basis of systematic desensitisation and flooding) treats anxiety by confronting feared stimuli until anxiety habituates, preventing avoidance. Behavioural experiments test predictions in real life.

Third-wave CBT. The "third wave" shifts from changing the content of thoughts to changing one's relationship with them, emphasising acceptance and mindfulness. Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), developed by Marsha Linehan for borderline personality disorder, balances acceptance with change and teaches four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), developed by Steven Hayes, promotes psychological flexibility through acceptance, defusion, and committed action toward chosen values. Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) (Segal, Williams, Teasdale) combines mindfulness with CBT to prevent depressive relapse.

High-yield

DBT = Linehan, for BPD, four modules (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness). ACT = Hayes, psychological flexibility, values-driven action. MBCT = relapse prevention in recurrent depression. These three are the canonical "third-wave" therapies.

A2. Psychodynamic Therapy

Psychodynamic therapy descends from Freud's psychoanalysis. Its central claim is that current symptoms arise from unconscious conflicts, usually rooted in early childhood relationships, that the person defends against being aware of. The goal is insight: making the unconscious conscious so the person can work it through and gain freedom from old patterns.

Freudian foundations. The unconscious holds wishes, memories, and conflicts kept out of awareness. Free association (the "fundamental rule") asks the patient to say whatever comes to mind without censorship, allowing unconscious material to surface. Dream analysis treats dreams as "the royal road to the unconscious", distinguishing manifest content (what is remembered) from latent content (the hidden meaning). Resistance is the patient's (often unconscious) opposition to the therapy, such as falling silent or arriving late, signalling that something threatening is near. Defence mechanisms (repression, denial, projection, displacement, reaction formation, sublimation, regression) are unconscious strategies the ego uses to manage anxiety.

Definition

Transference is the patient's unconscious redirection of feelings and patterns from important past figures (often parents) onto the therapist. Countertransference is the therapist's emotional reaction to the patient, including feelings the therapist projects back onto the patient. Analysing transference is a central therapeutic tool.

Exam trap

Transference vs countertransference is a classic MCQ. Transference flows FROM patient TO therapist (patient relives old relationships). Countertransference flows FROM therapist TO patient. A simple cue: "trans" = the patient transfers onto the analyst; "counter" = the analyst's reaction back. Mislabelling the direction is the trap.

Object relations theory (Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicott, Margaret Mahler, W.R.D. Fairbairn) shifts the focus from Freud's drives to internalised relationships. "Objects" are mental representations of significant people; early relationships (especially with the mother) form internal templates that shape adult relating. Winnicott contributed the "good-enough mother" and the "holding environment". Brief (short-term) psychodynamic therapy (e.g., Malan, Davanloo, Sifneos) condenses the work into a focused, time-limited format around a central conflict, making the approach more practical.

A3. Humanistic and Existential Therapies

The humanistic "third force" (after psychoanalysis and behaviourism) views people as inherently growth-oriented and trustworthy. Distress arises when the natural drive toward growth is blocked.

Rogers' person-centred therapy. Carl Rogers held that every organism has an actualising tendency, an innate drive to grow and fulfil its potential. Distress results when "conditions of worth" imposed by others create incongruence between the real self and the experienced self. Rogers argued that the therapist need not interpret or direct; instead, three core conditions, if genuinely present, are necessary and sufficient for change. The approach is non-directive: the client, not the therapist, leads.

Core conditionMeaning
Unconditional positive regardWarm, non-judgemental acceptance of the client as a person, regardless of what they say or do
Empathy (empathic understanding)Accurately sensing the client's inner world "as if" it were one's own, and communicating that understanding
Congruence (genuineness)The therapist is real, transparent, and authentic, not hiding behind a professional facade
Exam trap

Rogers' three core conditions are unconditional positive regard, empathy, and congruence (genuineness). "Actualising tendency" is the motivating force, NOT a core condition. Questions often add a distractor like "interpretation" or "transference" (those belong to psychodynamic therapy) or list four items. There are exactly three core conditions.

Gestalt therapy. Founded by Fritz Perls (with Laura Perls), Gestalt therapy emphasises present-moment awareness, the here-and-now, and personal responsibility, aiming to integrate fragmented parts of the self into a whole ("gestalt"). Its signature technique is the empty chair, in which the client speaks to an imagined person (or part of themselves) seated in an empty chair, then switches seats to reply, surfacing and resolving unfinished business.

Existential therapy. Existential therapy confronts the inescapable conditions of human existence. Irvin Yalom named four "ultimate concerns": death (mortality), freedom (and the responsibility it imposes), isolation (existential aloneness), and meaninglessness. Anxiety arises from how we face these givens; therapy helps the person live authentically with them. Viktor Frankl, a Holocaust survivor, founded logotherapy ("logos" = meaning), holding that the primary human drive is the "will to meaning"; finding meaning even in suffering is curative. Techniques include paradoxical intention and dereflection.

High-yield

Yalom's four ultimate concerns: Death, Freedom, Isolation, Meaninglessness (mnemonic "D-FIM"). Frankl = logotherapy = will to meaning, born from his concentration-camp experience (book: Man's Search for Meaning). Perls = Gestalt = empty chair = here-and-now.

A4. Systemic and Family Therapy

Family therapy treats the family as a system rather than locating pathology inside one "identified patient". Drawing on general systems theory, it holds that a change in any part affects the whole, and that symptoms serve a function within the system. Two key ideas: homeostasis, the family's tendency to maintain its existing equilibrium and resist change (so a member's symptom may stabilise the system), and circular causality, the view that family members' behaviours mutually influence one another in loops, replacing the linear "A causes B" with reciprocal feedback.

Definition

Circular causality rejects linear cause-and-effect: instead of "the husband withdraws because the wife nags", the systemic view is that withdrawing and nagging continuously trigger and maintain each other in a feedback loop. The "identified patient" is the symptom-bearer, but the symptom is understood as serving the whole system.

Structural family therapy (Salvador Minuchin). Minuchin focused on the family's organisation: its subsystems (spousal, parental, sibling) and the boundaries between them. Healthy boundaries are clear; problems arise when boundaries are too rigid (producing disengagement, emotional distance) or too diffuse (producing enmeshment, over-involvement). The therapist actively restructures the family, for instance by strengthening a weak parental subsystem or breaking a cross-generational coalition.

Strategic family therapy (Jay Haley, with Cloé Madanes, building on Milton Erickson). Strategic therapy is brief and problem-focused; the therapist designs specific strategies and directives to interrupt the problem sequence, including paradoxical directives (e.g., prescribing the symptom) and reframing.

Bowen family systems therapy (Murray Bowen). Bowen emphasised differentiation of self (the capacity to maintain one's own identity and think clearly while staying emotionally connected to the family), triangles (a two-person tension recruits a third person to stabilise it), and the multigenerational transmission of patterns. Bowen popularised the genogram, a family-tree diagram mapping relationships and patterns across at least three generations.

Exam trap

Match the founder to the concept: Minuchin = STRUCTURAL (boundaries, subsystems, enmeshment/disengagement); Haley = STRATEGIC (directives, paradox); Bowen = differentiation, triangles, genograms. The genogram is Bowen's, not Minuchin's. "Enmeshment" is structural (Minuchin); "differentiation" is Bowen.

B. Specialized Interventions

Play therapy. Because young children cannot easily express conflict verbally, play therapy uses play as the child's natural medium of communication and emotional expression. Virginia Axline developed non-directive (child-centred) play therapy, applying Rogerian principles to children. Her eight basic principles include: develop a warm relationship; accept the child exactly as they are; establish permissiveness so the child expresses feelings freely; recognise and reflect the child's feelings; respect the child's ability to solve their own problems; let the child lead (the therapist follows, not directs); do not hurry the process; and establish only the limits necessary to anchor therapy in reality.

High-yield

Axline = non-directive play therapy = Rogerian principles applied to children = eight basic principles, the child leads. This contrasts with directive play therapy and with Anna Freud's and Melanie Klein's psychoanalytic play techniques.

Art therapy uses creative expression (drawing, painting, sculpting) as a non-verbal route to surface and process emotions and trauma, useful when words are inaccessible or threatening. It is often grouped with the broader "expressive/creative arts therapies".

Biofeedback trains physiological self-regulation by giving the person real-time feedback (via display or tone) of bodily functions that are normally involuntary, so they learn voluntary control. Common modalities: EMG (electromyography) for muscle tension, used in tension headache and relaxation training; EEG (neurofeedback) for brainwave activity, explored in ADHD and epilepsy; HRV (heart-rate variability) and thermal/skin-conductance feedback for stress and anxiety. Biofeedback is behavioural medicine's application of operant principles to autonomic functions.

Definition

Biofeedback is a technique that uses electronic monitoring to convey information about physiological processes (muscle tension, brainwaves, heart rate, skin temperature) so the person can learn to consciously modify them. EMG = muscle, EEG = brain (neurofeedback), HRV = heart-rate variability.

Trauma-informed care (TIC) is not a single therapy but a framework: an organisation and clinician realise trauma is widespread, recognise its signs, respond by integrating this knowledge, and actively resist re-traumatisation. The SAMHSA principles are commonly examined: safety (physical and emotional), trustworthiness and transparency, peer support, collaboration and mutuality, empowerment, voice and choice, and attention to cultural, historical and gender issues. The shift is from "what is wrong with you?" to "what happened to you?".

Specific trauma therapies. TF-CBT (Trauma-Focused CBT, Cohen, Mannarino and Deblinger) is the leading evidence-based treatment for traumatised children and adolescents, combining CBT with trauma-sensitive components (often summarised by the acronym PRACTICE). EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing), developed by Francine Shapiro, has the client recall the traumatic memory while engaging in bilateral stimulation (typically guided eye movements) to reprocess it; it follows an eight-phase protocol. Prolonged Exposure (PE), developed by Edna Foa for PTSD, uses repeated imaginal exposure (revisiting the memory) and in vivo exposure (confronting avoided reminders) to extinguish trauma-related fear.

High-yield

EMDR = Francine Shapiro, bilateral stimulation/eye movements, eight phases. Prolonged Exposure = Edna Foa, for PTSD (imaginal + in vivo). TF-CBT = Cohen, Mannarino, Deblinger, for children. Trauma-informed care principles (SAMHSA): Safety, Trustworthiness, Peer support, Collaboration, Empowerment, Cultural/historical/gender sensitivity.

C2. The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI)

The Rehabilitation Council of India is the statutory body that regulates and standardises training of professionals and personnel working with persons with disabilities, including clinical psychologists. For clinical psychology specifically, the RCI licenses practitioners, accredits training programmes (such as the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology), and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR), the official register of qualified rehabilitation professionals. A clinical psychologist must be RCI-registered (hold a valid CRR number) to practise lawfully; the registration is renewable. The RCI also lays down a Code of Ethics for registered professionals.

Definition

The Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR) is the statutory register maintained by the RCI listing all qualified and registered rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists). Practising or claiming to be a registered professional without being on the CRR is an offence under the RCI Act.

History (memorise the two dates). The RCI was first set up in 1986 as a registered society. It was later given statutory teeth by the Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992, which came into force on 22 June 1993 (amended in 2000 to expand its scope to research and human-resource development).

Exam trap

RCI 1986 vs 1992/1993. 1986 = the year the RCI was first established as a (non-statutory) registered society. 1992 = the RCI Act was passed; 1993 = the Act was enforced (became operational). If a question asks when the RCI became a STATUTORY body, the answer is the 1992 Act (enforced 1993), not 1986. Do not confuse the RCI Act 1992 with the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995 or the MHCA 2017.

C3. Core Professional Ethics

Beyond statute, clinical psychologists are bound by a professional ethical code (RCI's code, informed by APA-style principles). Four pillars recur in examinations.

Informed consent. Before treatment, the client must be given understandable information about the nature, purpose, risks, benefits, and alternatives of the intervention, and must voluntarily agree, with the right to withdraw. Consent must be competent, informed, and voluntary. Special provisions apply for minors and for persons whose decision-making capacity is impaired (where the Nominated Representative and capacity assessment under the MHCA become relevant).

Confidentiality and its limits. What a client discloses is confidential, a duty reinforced by the MHCA's right to confidentiality. However, confidentiality is NOT absolute. It can and must be broken when there is a serious risk of harm to the client (e.g., active suicidality) or to identifiable others. The landmark Tarasoff case (California, 1976) established a clinician's duty to warn/protect identifiable third parties from a client's credible threats of serious harm. Other limits include court orders, mandatory reporting of child abuse, and risk to the public.

Exam trap

Confidentiality has limits. The standard exceptions: (1) harm to self, (2) harm to identifiable others (Tarasoff duty to warn/protect), (3) suspected child abuse, (4) court order/legal mandate. Tarasoff = "duty to protect" originated from Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California. A question asking when a therapist MAY breach confidentiality wants these exceptions, not "never".

Boundaries and dual relationships. The therapeutic relationship must be protected from conflicting roles. A dual (or multiple) relationship exists when the therapist has another role with the client (business partner, friend, relative, teacher) in addition to the professional one. The most serious violation is a sexual relationship with a current client, which is categorically prohibited. Dual relationships are avoided because they impair objectivity, create conflicts of interest, and risk exploiting the client's vulnerability.

Competence. Psychologists must practise only within the boundaries of their competence, based on their education, training, and supervised experience. They must keep their skills current (continuing professional development), refer out when a case exceeds their expertise, and not misrepresent their qualifications. Practising beyond one's competence, or without RCI registration, is both unethical and unlawful.

High-yield

The four core ethics to recall as a set: informed consent, confidentiality (with limits: harm to self/others, Tarasoff duty to warn, abuse, court order), boundaries/dual relationships, and competence. Combine these with the MHCA 2017 rights (dignity, confidentiality, community living, advance directive, nominated representative) and the RCI registration requirement (CRR) for a complete answer on Indian professional ethics and law.

6 · General & Developmental Psychology

This section covers the engine room of psychology: how organisms learn, how memory is built and lost, how the perceptual system organises raw sensation into a coherent world, how emotion is generated in the brain, how the mind develops from infancy to adulthood, and how social context bends individual behaviour. The Amity / RCI / M.Phil entrance leans heavily on named theorists, exact mechanisms, and the fine distinctions between superficially similar theories. Learn the names, the order of stages, and the traps, because that is where the marks live.

A1. Learning: Classical and Operant Conditioning

Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour or behavioural potential that results from experience. The two foundational forms of associative learning are classical conditioning (learning by association between stimuli) and operant conditioning (learning by the consequences of behaviour).

Classical conditioning was discovered by the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov while studying salivation in dogs. Food (the unconditioned stimulus, UCS) automatically and reflexively produces salivation (the unconditioned response, UCR). A neutral stimulus (NS) such as a bell initially produces nothing relevant. When the bell is repeatedly paired with the food, the bell becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that now produces salivation on its own: this learned salivation is the conditioned response (CR). The key processes are: acquisition (the building of the CS to CR link through pairing, strongest when the CS slightly precedes the UCS), extinction (the CR weakens and disappears when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS), spontaneous recovery (after a rest interval, the extinguished CR reappears, weaker, showing extinction suppresses rather than erases), stimulus generalisation (stimuli similar to the CS also evoke the CR, for example a different-pitched bell), and discrimination (learning to respond only to the specific CS and not to similar stimuli).

John B. Watson, the founder of behaviourism, demonstrated classical conditioning of emotion in the Little Albert experiment (1920, with Rosalie Rayner). An 11-month-old infant was conditioned to fear a white rat (originally neutral) by pairing it with a loud, frightening clang (UCS). Albert came to fear the rat (CR) and the fear generalised to other furry white objects (a rabbit, a fur coat, a Santa mask). This showed emotional responses can be learned, supporting Watson's radical environmentalism.

Definition

Unconditioned stimulus (UCS) elicits a response with no learning; conditioned stimulus (CS) elicits the response only after pairing. The same physical stimulus (the bell) is the NS before learning and the CS after learning.

Operant conditioning concerns voluntary behaviour controlled by its consequences. Edward Thorndike's puzzle-box studies with cats produced the Law of Effect: responses followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened and more likely to recur, while responses followed by discomfort are weakened. B. F. Skinner extended this into a full system using the Skinner box, coining the term operant (behaviour that operates on the environment).

Skinner's central tool is the reinforcement/punishment 2x2. Reinforcement always increases behaviour; punishment always decreases it. Positive means a stimulus is added; negative means a stimulus is removed. Combining these gives four cells.

Exam trap

Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment. "Negative" means subtraction of an aversive stimulus, and it still increases behaviour (escape and avoidance learning). Students routinely mislabel negative reinforcement as a form of punishment; it is the opposite in effect.

Schedules of reinforcement determine the pattern and persistence of responding. Continuous reinforcement (reward every response) produces fast learning but fast extinction. Partial (intermittent) schedules produce slower learning but greater resistance to extinction. The four partial schedules: fixed ratio (reward after a set number of responses, for example every 10th), variable ratio (reward after an unpredictable average number, for example slot machines and gambling), fixed interval (reward for the first response after a set time, producing a scalloped response curve), and variable interval (reward for the first response after an unpredictable average time). Shaping is reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour, building complex behaviour step by step.

High-yield

The variable-ratio schedule produces the highest, steadiest rate of responding and is the most resistant to extinction. This is why gambling is so persistent: the unpredictable payoff means the gambler can never be sure the next pull is not the winner. Memorise: variable ratio = most resistant.

A2. Memory: Models, Consolidation, and Interference

The modal model of Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968) describes memory as three stores in sequence. Sensory memory holds a large but very brief sensory trace (iconic visual memory lasts about a quarter to half a second; echoic auditory memory a few seconds). Attended information passes to short-term memory (STM), of limited capacity (Miller's "magical number seven, plus or minus two", roughly 15 to 30 seconds without rehearsal). Rehearsal transfers material to long-term memory (LTM), of effectively unlimited capacity and duration.

Baddeley and Hitch (1974) replaced the passive STM store with an active working memory model. It has a central executive (an attentional controller that allocates resources and coordinates the subsystems), a phonological loop (holds and rehearses verbal and acoustic information, the "inner voice and inner ear"), a visuospatial sketchpad (holds visual and spatial information, the "inner eye"), and, added by Baddeley in 2000, the episodic buffer (a limited-capacity store that integrates information across the subsystems and links to LTM into a unified episode).

Definition

Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart, 1972): memory durability depends not on which store information sits in but on the depth of processing. Shallow structural processing (how a word looks) and intermediate phonemic processing (how it sounds) yield weaker memory than deep semantic processing (what it means). Elaborative encoding produces the most durable traces.

The hippocampus is essential for forming new declarative (explicit) long-term memories. The classic evidence is patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), whose bilateral medial temporal lobe resection left him with severe anterograde amnesia (could not form new long-term declarative memories) while procedural learning and short-term memory remained intact. This supports systems consolidation: memories are initially hippocampus-dependent and are gradually transferred to and stabilised in the neocortex over time. Reconsolidation is the finding that a reactivated (recalled) memory becomes temporarily labile and must be re-stabilised, opening a window in which it can be modified. The cellular basis of memory is long-term potentiation (LTP): the persistent strengthening of synapses following high-frequency stimulation, heavily involving NMDA and AMPA glutamate receptors. LTP is the leading model of the synaptic change underlying learning (Hebb's principle: "cells that fire together wire together").

Interference is a major cause of forgetting. Proactive interference is when old learning disrupts new learning (your old phone number intrudes when recalling the new one). Retroactive interference is when new learning disrupts old learning (learning a new language makes the old one harder to recall).

Memory aid

"PRO = forward, old blocks new; RETRO = backward, new blocks old." Proactive proceeds forward in time (past pushing forward onto the present); retroactive reaches backward (present reaching back to disrupt the past).

A3. Perceptual Organization

Perception is the active construction of a meaningful representation from sensory input. The Gestalt psychologists (Wertheimer, Koffka, Kohler) argued that "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts" and described laws by which we automatically organise elements into wholes. Proximity: items near each other are grouped. Similarity: items that look alike are grouped. Closure: we mentally fill gaps to perceive a complete figure. Continuity (good continuation): we perceive smooth, continuous lines rather than abrupt changes. Figure-ground: we segregate a focal figure from its background (the reversible Rubin vase and faces). Common fate: elements moving together in the same direction are grouped. Overarching all of them is Pragnanz (the law of good form): we perceive the simplest, most stable, most regular organisation possible.

Bottom-up processing builds perception from the raw sensory data upward (data-driven, starting from features). Top-down processing uses prior knowledge, expectation, and context to interpret incoming sensation (concept-driven). Reading messy handwriting using context to guess words is top-down; piecing together an unfamiliar shape feature by feature is bottom-up.

Perceptual constancies keep our perception of objects stable despite changing sensory input: size constancy (a person walking away is seen as the same size though the retinal image shrinks), shape constancy (a door is seen as rectangular though it projects a trapezoid when open), and colour and brightness constancy (a white shirt looks white in shade and in sun).

Depth perception uses two classes of cue. Binocular cues require both eyes: retinal (binocular) disparity (the two eyes receive slightly different images; the brain computes depth from the difference, the larger the disparity the nearer the object) and convergence (the inward turning of the eyes for near objects, the muscle effort signalling distance). Monocular cues work with one eye: relative size, interposition (overlap), linear perspective, texture gradient, aerial perspective, relative height, light and shadow, and motion parallax.

Exam trap

Retinal disparity and convergence are the binocular depth cues. Everything else (interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, relative size, motion parallax) is monocular. A common item asks you to sort one cue: if it needs two eyes, it is binocular; if a one-eyed person could still use it, it is monocular.

A4. The Neuroscience and Theories of Emotion

The limbic system is the emotional core of the brain. The amygdala is central to detecting threat and generating fear; lesions abolish learned fear, and stimulation produces fear and aggression. The amygdala tags stimuli with emotional significance and drives autonomic and behavioural fear responses.

Four classic theories explain the sequence and origin of emotion. The James-Lange theory holds that the bodily response comes first and the felt emotion is our perception of that bodily change: "we are afraid because we tremble" (we do not tremble because we are afraid). The Cannon-Bard theory counters that the physiological arousal and the conscious emotional experience occur simultaneously and independently, both triggered at once via the thalamus, because bodily responses are too slow and too similar across emotions to be the source. The Schachter-Singer two-factor theory holds that emotion requires two ingredients: physiological arousal plus a cognitive label that interprets the cause of the arousal; the same arousal can be felt as anger or euphoria depending on the cognitive interpretation of the context (the adrenaline injection study). The Lazarus appraisal theory holds that cognitive appraisal of the situation comes first and is necessary for emotion; you must first judge an event as relevant and threatening before any emotion or arousal follows.

Joseph LeDoux mapped two neural fear pathways. The low road is a fast, crude, subcortical route: thalamus straight to amygdala, allowing an immediate defensive response before conscious recognition (jumping back from a curved stick before realising it is not a snake). The high road is slower: thalamus to sensory cortex to amygdala, providing a more accurate, evaluated appraisal that can confirm or cancel the alarm.

B1. Piaget's Cognitive Development

Jean Piaget viewed the child as a "little scientist" who actively constructs knowledge. A schema is a mental framework for organising information. Assimilation is fitting new information into an existing schema (a child calls a zebra a "horse"). Accommodation is changing the schema to fit new information (the child creates a new "zebra" schema). Equilibration is the drive to balance assimilation and accommodation, resolving the cognitive disequilibrium that new experiences create, and is the motor of development.

B2. Vygotsky's Sociocultural Theory

Lev Vygotsky argued that cognitive development is fundamentally social: children develop through interaction with more skilled members of their culture, and language is the primary tool of thought. The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can achieve with guidance from a more knowledgeable other (MKO). Scaffolding is the tailored support a teacher or peer provides within the ZPD, gradually withdrawn as competence grows. Private speech (children talking aloud to themselves) is, for Vygotsky, a tool for self-guidance that later becomes internalised as inner speech and silent thought.

C1. Attribution and the Fundamental Attribution Error

Attribution theory concerns how people explain the causes of behaviour. Fritz Heider, the father of attribution theory, distinguished internal (dispositional) causes (the person's traits) from external (situational) causes (the circumstances). Harold Kelley's covariation model says we judge cause by examining three kinds of information across instances: consensus (do others behave this way?), distinctiveness (does this person behave this way only in this situation?), and consistency (does the person behave this way across time?). High consistency with high consensus and high distinctiveness points to a situational cause; high consistency with low consensus and low distinctiveness points to a dispositional cause.

Definition

The fundamental attribution error (FAE) is the pervasive tendency to over-attribute others' behaviour to their disposition (personality, character) while underestimating situational causes. Seeing someone trip, we think "clumsy" rather than "the floor was slippery".

Two related biases: the actor-observer bias is the tendency to attribute our own behaviour to the situation but others' behaviour to disposition (I failed because the exam was unfair; you failed because you are lazy). The self-serving bias is attributing our successes to disposition (my skill) and our failures to the situation (bad luck), protecting self-esteem.

Exam trap

FAE applies to explaining other people (over-blame their character). The actor-observer bias adds the asymmetry: situational for self, dispositional for others. The self-serving bias is specifically about self-esteem protection across success vs failure. An item that describes you excusing your own lateness as "traffic" but calling a colleague's lateness "irresponsibility" is the actor-observer bias.

C2. Cognitive Dissonance

Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance (1957) states that holding two inconsistent cognitions, or acting in a way that contradicts one's attitudes, produces an uncomfortable state of tension (dissonance) that motivates the person to reduce it, often by changing the attitude to match the behaviour. In the classic Festinger and Carlsmith (1959) $1 / $20 study, participants did a boring task, then were paid either $1 or $20 to tell the next participant it was enjoyable. Counter-intuitively, those paid only $1 later rated the task as more enjoyable. The $20 group had ample external justification for lying ("I did it for the money"), so felt little dissonance; the $1 group had insufficient justification, so to resolve the dissonance they changed their actual attitude ("it must have been fun"). Less external justification produces more attitude change.

C3. Conformity and Obedience

Conformity is adjusting one's behaviour or beliefs to match a group. Muzafer Sherif's autokinetic studies (1935) used the illusion of a stationary point of light appearing to move in a dark room; with no objective answer, individuals converged on a shared group norm and kept using it later when alone, demonstrating informational social influence and norm formation. Solomon Asch's line-judgement studies (1951) showed people conforming to an obviously wrong group answer about line lengths roughly a third of the time, even when the correct answer was clear, demonstrating normative social influence (the desire to fit in and avoid disapproval).

Obedience is following a direct order from an authority figure. Stanley Milgram's shock experiments (1963) found that around 65% of ordinary participants continued delivering what they believed were escalating, potentially lethal electric shocks (up to 450 volts) to a "learner" simply because an experimenter in a lab coat instructed them to. Milgram explained this through the agentic state: the person sees themselves as an agent carrying out another's wishes and surrenders personal responsibility to the authority.

C4. Intergroup Dynamics

Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment (1954) divided boys at a summer camp into two groups. Competition for scarce resources generated hostility, supporting realistic conflict theory (intergroup hostility arises from competition over limited resources). Hostility was reduced not by mere contact but by superordinate goals: shared objectives requiring cooperation from both groups (such as restoring the camp's water supply).

Henri Tajfel's social identity theory shows that merely categorising people into groups, even on a trivial or random basis (the "minimal group paradigm"), is enough to produce in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination. We derive part of our self-concept and self-esteem from group membership, motivating us to see our in-group as superior.

Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971) assigned students to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock prison; the guards rapidly became abusive and the study was stopped early. Zimbardo argued that situational forces and assigned roles, aided by deindividuation (a loss of individual self-awareness and personal responsibility in groups, anonymity-driven), can lead ordinary people to harmful behaviour.

High-yield

These name-to-study pairings are the backbone of the social-psychology questions.

7.1 Orientation: the six darshanas and where psychology lives

Classical Indian thought is organised into six orthodox philosophical systems, the shad darshanas ("six visions"). They are āstika ("orthodox") schools because they accept the authority of the Vedas, as opposed to the nāstika ("heterodox") systems such as Buddhism, Jainism, and the materialist Charvaka, which do not. The six are traditionally paired: Nyaya and Vaisheshika (logic and atomism), Samkhya and Yoga, and Mimamsa and Vedanta (ritual interpretation and the philosophy of the Upanishads).

You do not need to master all six. What you need is to know which schools carry the psychological content, the material about the self, mind, consciousness, and personality. That content sits in three. Samkhya gives the metaphysical scaffolding: the two ultimate categories Purusha and Prakriti, and the three gunas that make up everything in nature, including the mind. Yoga (the system of Patanjali) turns Samkhya's map into an applied psychology, a practical method for controlling and stilling the mind. Vedanta (the philosophy distilled from the Upanishads) gives the model of the self in layers (the koshas), the states of consciousness, the inner instrument (antahkarana), and the identity of Atman and Brahman.

So when the paper says "Indian psychology", it does not mean a separate modern discipline. It means the psychological doctrines embedded in these traditions: how they conceive the self, the structure of the mind, the nature of consciousness, and the typology of personality. Everything below is that content, taught from the system it comes from.

7.2 The Trigunas (from Sāṁkhya): the three strands of nature

Samkhya holds that all of manifest nature, Prakriti, is woven from three fundamental qualities or strands called the gunas. The word guna literally means "strand" or "rope", and the image is exactly that: three strands twisted together into the single rope of experienced reality. The three are Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas.

Sattva is the quality of purity, clarity, harmony, balance, and knowledge. When sattva dominates, the mind is calm, luminous, and discerning; it is associated with wisdom, contentment, and lightness. Rajas is the quality of activity, passion, movement, and restlessness. When rajas dominates, there is drive, ambition, desire, agitation, and emotional turbulence; it is the principle of motion and craving. Tamas is the quality of inertia, dullness, heaviness, and darkness. When tamas dominates, there is laziness, sleep, confusion, ignorance, and resistance to change; it is the principle that obscures and weighs down.

The crucial doctrinal point, which the paper loves to test, is that everyone has all three gunas in varying proportion. The gunas are not three types of people in a rigid sense; they are three ingredients present in every mind and every object, and a person's temperament reflects which one currently predominates. They are also in constant flux: a single person is more sattvic in calm clear moments, more rajasic when driven or angry, more tamasic when dull or sleepy. Because the gunas belong to Prakriti, they characterise the body and mind, never the pure witnessing consciousness (this is the link to 7.3 below).

Indian-approaches research has operationalised this into formal psychometrics. The Guna scales (for example, instruments developed in the Indian psychology literature to measure sattva, rajas, and tamas as personality dimensions) attempt to assess a person's guna profile much as Western inventories assess traits. You should recognise that the trigunas are not only a philosophical idea but a measured personality model in the formal study of Indian approaches to personality.

7.3 Purusha vs Prakriti: Sāṁkhya dualism

Samkhya is fundamentally a dualist system. It says reality reduces to two ultimate, irreducible categories. Purusha is pure consciousness, the witness. It is inactive, attributeless, changeless, and merely observes. Importantly, in classical Samkhya Purusha is plural: there are many purushas, one for each conscious being, unlike Vedanta's single universal consciousness. Purusha does nothing; it simply illuminates, the way a lamp lights a room without acting on it.

Prakriti is primordial matter or nature, the active, creative principle. Prakriti is unconscious but dynamic, and it is Prakriti that carries the three gunas described above. In its unmanifest state the three gunas are in perfect equilibrium; when that balance is disturbed (in the presence of Purusha), Prakriti begins to evolve and unfolds into the whole manifest world, intellect, ego, mind, the senses, the elements, the body. Everything you can perceive, including your own mind and personality, is a product of Prakriti.

The manifest world arises from the interaction of these two. Purusha's mere presence, like a magnet near iron filings, disturbs Prakriti's equilibrium and sets evolution in motion; consciousness gets "reflected" in the evolving mind, producing the illusion that the witness is the doer. Bondage is precisely this confusion: pure consciousness mistaking itself for the activities of Prakriti. Liberation (kaivalya, "aloneness/isolation") is achieved through discriminative knowledge (viveka), the clear discernment that separates Purusha from Prakriti, so that consciousness recognises it was never the doer and rests in its own nature.

7.4 The Pancha Kosha: the five sheaths of the self

The doctrine of the five sheaths comes from the Taittiriya Upanishad and is a central Vedantic model of the self. The idea is that the true Self (Atman) is wrapped in five concentric coverings or koshas ("sheaths"), arranged from the grossest and most external to the subtlest and most internal, like the layers of an onion. Spiritual realisation involves seeing through each sheath in turn until the Atman, which is none of them, is realised beyond and through them.

The five, in order from gross to subtle, are: Annamaya kosha, the food sheath, the physical body built from and sustained by food (anna = food). Pranamaya kosha, the vital-energy sheath, the layer of breath and life-force (prana) that animates the body. Manomaya kosha, the mental sheath, the layer of mind and emotions, of sensory processing, feeling, and ordinary thought (manas = mind). Vijnanamaya kosha, the wisdom/intellect sheath, the layer of vijnana, intellect, discrimination, judgement, the higher knowing faculty. Anandamaya kosha, the bliss sheath, the subtlest layer, associated with deep peace and bliss (ananda), the covering closest to the Self.

Note the trajectory carefully: it runs body → bliss, gross → subtle, outer → inner. The physical body is outermost because it is the densest; bliss is innermost because it is the subtlest and nearest to Atman. The Atman itself is not a sixth sheath; it is the consciousness that the sheaths cover, realised when one no longer identifies with any of them.

7.5 The Four Avasthas: states of consciousness

The Mandukya Upanishad, the shortest of the principal Upanishads, sets out four states (avasthas) of consciousness, mapping them onto the sacred syllable AUM (OM). The first three are familiar experiential states; the fourth is the transcendent ground.

Jagrat is the waking state, in which consciousness is turned outward to the gross physical world through the senses. Swapna is the dream state, in which consciousness is turned inward and experiences a subtle world of mental impressions, with no external input. Sushupti is deep dreamless sleep, a state of undifferentiated rest in which there are no dreams and no duality of subject and object, experienced as peace but without awareness. And Turiya, literally "the fourth", is pure consciousness itself, the substratum that underlies and transcends the other three. Turiya is not a fourth experience alongside waking, dreaming, and sleeping; it is the silent awareness that is present in and beyond all of them. In the AUM mapping, the three sounds A-U-M correspond to waking, dream, and deep sleep, and Turiya is the silence after the sound of OM, the soundless awareness in which the syllable arises and subsides.

7.6 Antahkarana: the inner instrument of mind

Antahkarana means the "inner instrument" or inner organ, the internal apparatus of mind in Vedantic (and Samkhya-derived) psychology. It is the mental machinery through which consciousness functions, classically analysed into four functions. The key conceptual point is that the antahkarana is an instrument, a tool of Prakriti, not the conscious Self that uses it.

The four functions are: Manas, the sensory and processing mind, which receives sense impressions, coordinates the senses, and handles doubt, desire, and the back-and-forth of ordinary thinking. Buddhi, the intellect, the faculty of discrimination, judgement, decision, and determination, that which concludes and decides after manas has wavered. Ahamkara, the ego or "I-maker" (aham = I), the function that appropriates experience and generates the sense of "I" and "mine", identifying the Self with the body-mind. Chitta, the memory store or "mind-stuff", the reservoir of impressions (samskaras) where past experiences are stored and from which they resurface.

7.7 Atman and Brahman: the Vedantic identity

Vedanta's central teaching concerns two terms. Atman is the individual true Self, the innermost consciousness, what you really are beneath the koshas and behind the antahkarana. Brahman is the ultimate, infinite reality, the absolute ground of everything. The decisive claim of the Upanishads is that these two are, at the deepest level, identical. This is captured in the great saying (mahavakya) "Tat Tvam Asi", "That thou art", you are That, the individual Self is the universal reality.

The school that takes this most strictly is Advaita Vedanta ("non-dualism"), systematised by Shankara (Adi Shankaracharya). Advaita holds that there is only one reality without a second: Atman is Brahman, and the apparent multiplicity of the world is maya, a superimposition produced by ignorance. Liberation (moksha) is the direct realisation of this non-difference. Note the contrast with Samkhya: Samkhya is dualist (many purushas, separate from prakriti), whereas Advaita Vedanta is strictly non-dualist (one consciousness, Atman = Brahman). Do not let the paper blur these.

Definition

Advaita = "not-two", non-dualism. Atman (individual Self) and Brahman (ultimate reality) are one and the same; the sense of separateness is ignorance. The mahavakya expressing it is Tat Tvam Asi ("That thou art").

7.8 Yoga and Patanjali: the applied psychology of mind-control

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali are the foundational text of the Yoga darshana. Yoga shares Samkhya's metaphysics (Purusha, Prakriti, the gunas) but adds a practical method, which is why it functions as an applied Indian psychology of the mind. Patanjali's most famous definition, in the second sutra, is "yogaḥ citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ": yoga is the stilling (nirodha) of the modifications (vrittis) of the mind-stuff (chitta). The mind is normally a stream of fluctuations, perceptions, thoughts, memories, imaginings; yoga is the systematic quieting of those fluctuations so that the witnessing consciousness rests in its own nature.

The method is the eight limbs (ashtanga): Yama (ethical restraints toward others), Niyama (personal observances/disciplines), Asana (steady posture), Pranayama (regulation of breath), Pratyahara (withdrawal of the senses from objects), Dharana (concentration, fixing attention on a single point), Dhyana (sustained meditation, an unbroken flow of attention), and Samadhi (absorption, the union in which the meditator and object merge and the mind is fully stilled). The eight progress from outer ethical/physical discipline toward inner mental absorption.

Memory aid

Eight limbs in order: "Young Ninjas Always Practise Precise Deep Dedicated Stunts", Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi. The last three (Dharana → Dhyana → Samadhi) are the inner, concentrative trio. And remember the one-line definition: yoga = chitta vritti nirodha, stilling the modifications of the mind.

7.9 The Purusharthas: the indigenous model of motivation and well-being

Indian thought frames human motivation and a well-lived life through the four purusharthas, the legitimate aims of human life. Dharma is duty, righteousness, moral and social order, living in accordance with what is right. Artha is wealth, prosperity, and the material means of life. Kama is pleasure, desire, and the enjoyment of the senses and emotions. Moksha is liberation, release from the cycle of birth and death, the ultimate spiritual goal. Together they form an indigenous model of motivation and well-being: a balanced life pursues prosperity (artha) and pleasure (kama) within the bounds of righteousness (dharma), while orienting ultimately toward liberation (moksha). This contrasts usefully with Western need hierarchies, here the highest aim is explicitly spiritual liberation, not self-actualisation in the secular sense.

Memory aid

Four aims: Dharma (duty), Artha (wealth), Kama (pleasure), Moksha (liberation). Dharma frames the lot; Moksha crowns it.

7.10 RCI history: the tested date pair

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) is the statutory body that regulates and monitors training of rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists) and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register. For Indian clinical psychology this is directly relevant: RCI is the body that licenses and registers clinical psychologists in India, and practising as one requires RCI registration. So its history is fair game, and the paper tests a specific date pair.

The RCI began as a registered society in 1986. At that stage it was a voluntary body without statutory teeth. It was then given statutory status by the Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992, which was enforced/brought into force in 1993. The Act gave RCI the legal authority to regulate training programmes, prescribe standards, register professionals, and take action against unqualified practitioners. (The Act was later amended in 2000 to widen its scope.)
