A. Membrane Dynamics & Electrophysiology

A neuron at rest is not electrically dead; it is a charged battery held away from equilibrium by metabolic work. Understanding the resting potential, how it is built, and how it collapses into an action potential is the foundation for everything else in this section, so we build it ion by ion.

Resting Membrane Potential: the gradients and the driving forces

The resting membrane potential (RMP) is the steady voltage difference across the neuronal membrane when the cell is not signalling, conventionally about −70 mV (the inside is negative relative to the outside). It exists because four ions are unequally distributed across the membrane and because the membrane is selectively permeable to them. The key players, with their characteristic gradients, are:

Two forces act on every ion. The chemical (concentration) gradient pushes an ion from where it is concentrated to where it is dilute. The electrical gradient pulls an ion toward the oppositely charged compartment. The combination is the electrochemical driving force. For potassium, the concentration gradient pushes K+ out (it is concentrated inside), but as positive charge leaves, the inside becomes more negative and the electrical gradient begins pulling K+ back in. The voltage at which these two forces exactly balance, so there is no net flux of that single ion, is its equilibrium (reversal) potential. Because the resting membrane is far more permeable to K+ than to anything else, the RMP sits close to the potassium equilibrium potential, near −90 mV, but is dragged a little more positive (to about −70 mV) by the small resting sodium leak.

The Nernst equation: equilibrium for a single ion

The Nernst equation calculates the equilibrium potential for one ion species from its concentration ratio across the membrane. In the form usually quoted for body temperature it is:

Eion = (61 / z) × log10 ( [ion]outside / [ion]inside ) millivolts,

where z is the ionic charge (+1 for Na+ and K+, −1 for Cl−) and 61 is the constant RT/F converted to base-10 logarithms at 37°C (the value 58 to 62 is used in different textbooks; learn 61). Worked example: for potassium with roughly 5 mM outside and 140 mM inside, EK = 61 × log10(5/140) = 61 × log10(0.0357) = 61 × (−1.447) = about −88 mV. That is why the resting cell, dominated by K+ permeability, rests near −90 mV.

Definition

The equilibrium potential of an ion is the membrane voltage at which the electrical force exactly cancels the chemical force, producing zero net movement of that ion. The Nernst equation gives it for a single ion; the GHK equation gives the resting potential when several ions are permeable at once.

Exam trap

Nernst handles one ion in isolation. If a question gives you permeabilities for several ions and asks for the membrane potential, the correct tool is the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation, not Nernst. Also note: changing intracellular charge does not appear in Nernst; only the concentration ratio and the valence do.

The Na+/K+ ATPase: the electrogenic pump

The gradients above would run down within seconds of firing if nothing restored them. The sodium-potassium ATPase (the sodium pump) is the active transporter that does this, hydrolysing one molecule of ATP to drive an uphill exchange. Its stoichiometry is the single most tested fact about it: for every cycle it extrudes 3 Na+ out of the cell and imports 2 K+ in. Because it moves three positive charges out but only two in, it is a net exporter of positive charge: it is electrogenic, contributing a small (a few millivolts) hyperpolarising offset to the RMP over and above the diffusion potential. The pump cycles through conformational states (E1 facing inward binds Na+ and ATP, phosphorylation flips it to E2 facing outward to release Na+ and bind K+, dephosphorylation returns it to E1 to release K+ inside). It is the major consumer of the brain's metabolic energy, which is why neural tissue is so vulnerable to ischaemia.

High-yield

The Na+/K+ ATPase is 3 Na+ out : 2 K+ in per ATP, and it is electrogenic (net one positive charge exported, slightly hyperpolarising). Cardiac glycosides such as ouabain and digoxin block it. Learn the 3:2 ratio verbatim; reversed ratios (2:3) are a standard distractor.

The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) voltage equation

Real membranes are permeable to several ions simultaneously, so the resting potential is a permeability-weighted blend of their equilibrium potentials. The GHK voltage equation computes this:

Vm = 61 × log10 ( ( PK[K+]out + PNa[Na+]out + PCl[Cl−]in ) / ( PK[K+]in + PNa[Na+]in + PCl[Cl−]out ) )

Note that chloride appears inverted relative to the cations (out on top becomes in on top) because it is negatively charged. The P terms are the relative permeability coefficients. The conceptual lesson is decisive: the membrane potential always slides toward the equilibrium potential of whichever ion is currently most permeable. At rest, PK dominates, so Vm sits near EK. At the peak of an action potential, PNa briefly dominates, so Vm swings toward ENa (+60 mV). GHK explains the resting state; it also explains why opening or closing channels moves the voltage.

The action potential cascade

The action potential (AP) is the all-or-none, self-regenerating reversal of membrane polarity that carries the signal down the axon. "All-or-none" means the AP either fires at its full, fixed amplitude or it does not fire at all; a stronger stimulus does not produce a bigger spike, it produces spikes at a higher frequency. This frequency coding is how the nervous system represents stimulus intensity. The AP is produced by two voltage-gated channels with cleverly different kinetics; the whole trick lies in the fact that the sodium channel opens fast and shuts itself off, while the potassium channel opens late.

  • Voltage-gated Na+ channel: this channel has two gates. The m-gate (activation gate) is closed at rest and opens fast on depolarisation. The h-gate (inactivation gate) is open at rest and closes slowly after depolarisation. The channel conducts only in the brief window when both are open.
  • Delayed-rectifier voltage-gated K+ channel: this has a single n-gate, closed at rest, that opens slowly on depolarisation (hence "delayed").

The cascade, in order:

  1. Resting state: roughly −70 mV. Na+ m-gates closed, h-gates open; K+ n-gates closed.
  2. Threshold: a stimulus depolarises the membrane to about −55 mV. This is the critical point at which enough Na+ m-gates open that the inward sodium current exceeds the outward potassium current, and the process becomes self-sustaining (positive feedback). Below threshold, nothing propagates; at threshold, the AP fires fully. This is the all-or-none law.
  3. Depolarisation / rising phase: m-gates fly open, Na+ rushes in down its steep electrochemical gradient, and the membrane potential rockets upward.
  4. Overshoot: the membrane potential reverses past zero to roughly +30 to +40 mV, approaching (but not reaching) ENa of +60 mV. The interior is now transiently positive.
  5. Repolarisation / falling phase: two things now coincide. The slow Na+ h-gates close (inactivating the sodium channel and stopping the inward current), and the slow K+ n-gates finally open, letting K+ flood out. The membrane potential plunges back down.
  6. Hyperpolarisation / undershoot (after-hyperpolarisation): because the delayed-rectifier K+ channels are slow to close, K+ keeps leaving for a moment too long, and the membrane briefly overshoots past the resting potential to about −80 to −90 mV (toward EK) before the channels close and the RMP is restored.
Memory aid

Sodium has the M for mad rush in and the H for the halt; potassium has the N for the next, slower exit. "Na floods in, K leaks out, late." The overshoot heads toward sodium's +60 (positive ion in), the undershoot heads toward potassium's −90 (positive ion out).

Refractory periods

After firing, the axon cannot immediately fire again, and this is what makes propagation unidirectional and caps firing frequency.

PeriodMechanismCan it fire again?

Exam trap

The absolute refractory period is caused by Na+ channel inactivation (the h-gate), not by the K+ channels. The relative refractory period is the one driven by the lingering open K+ channels and the need for a suprathreshold stimulus. Swapping these two causes is the classic distractor.

Passive cable properties

Before the AP regenerates itself, sub-threshold (graded) potentials spread passively along the axon like current down a leaky cable, and two constants describe how far and how fast.

  • The length (space) constant, lambda, is the distance over which a steady graded potential decays to about 37% (1/e) of its starting amplitude. A larger lambda means signals spread further before fading. Lambda is increased by high membrane resistance (Rm) and decreased by high axial resistance (Ra); formally lambda is proportional to the square root of (Rm / Ra). Fat, well-insulated axons have large lambda.
  • The time constant, tau, is how quickly the membrane voltage responds to a current step, the time to reach about 63% of its final value. Tau = Rm × Cm (membrane resistance times membrane capacitance). A small tau means a fast-responding membrane.
ResistancePathEffect of raising it
Ra (axial/longitudinal resistance)Resistance to current flowing along the axon's core (inversely related to diameter; thick axons have low Ra).Shortens lambda (current leaks out before travelling far). Bad for fast spread.
Rm (transverse/membrane resistance)Resistance to current leaking across the membrane (raised by myelin insulation).Lengthens lambda (current stays in the core). Good for fast spread.

Worked intuition: imagine the axon as a leaky garden hose. Ra is how narrow the hose is (a narrow hose resists flow along its length, so water dribbles out before reaching the end), and Rm is how watertight the walls are (a watertight hose loses nothing through the sides). A wide, watertight hose carries water furthest, which is the electrical equivalent of a thick, myelinated axon carrying the graded potential furthest before it decays. Lambda and tau together determine conduction velocity: a large lambda lets the signal reach the next regeneration point, and a small tau lets the membrane respond quickly there.

High-yield

Large axon diameter lowers Ra; myelin raises Rm. Both increase lambda, so the passive signal reaches the next node strongly enough to re-trigger an AP. This is why thick, myelinated axons conduct fastest. Lambda decays to 37% (1/e) per length constant; tau reaches 63% of final voltage in one time constant.

Myelination and saltatory conduction

Myelin is the lipid-rich insulating sheath wrapped around axons, formed by different glial cells in the two halves of the nervous system:

  • In the central nervous system (CNS), oligodendrocytes form myelin. One oligodendrocyte sends processes to myelinate segments of several different axons.
  • In the peripheral nervous system (PNS), Schwann cells form myelin. One Schwann cell myelinates a single segment of one axon, and it provides the neurilemma that supports peripheral nerve regeneration.

Between myelinated segments lie the Nodes of Ranvier, short bare gaps packed with a high density of voltage-gated Na+ channels. Because myelin insulates the internodes (raising Rm and lowering capacitance), the AP cannot regenerate under the sheath; instead the depolarisation spreads passively (and fast) to the next node, where the dense Na+ channels regenerate a full AP. The impulse thus appears to "jump" from node to node. This is saltatory conduction (from the Latin saltare, to leap), and it makes myelinated axons both much faster and far more metabolically efficient than unmyelinated ones.

Exam trap

Oligodendrocytes = CNS (one cell, many axons); Schwann cells = PNS (one cell, one internode). Demyelination of the CNS is multiple sclerosis; demyelination of the PNS is Guillain-Barre syndrome. The cell that aids peripheral nerve regeneration is the Schwann cell, not the oligodendrocyte.

B. Synaptic Transmission & Vesicular Trafficking

When the action potential reaches the axon terminal, the electrical signal is converted into a chemical one. This subsection follows the neurotransmitter from the calcium trigger that releases it, through the protein machinery that fuses the vesicle, to the recycling of the empty vesicle and the two fundamentally different ways the receiving cell can read the message.

Presynaptic exocytosis and the calcium trigger

The AP depolarises the presynaptic terminal, which opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Three types matter: L-type, N-type, and P/Q-type channels, with N- and P/Q-types being the principal triggers of fast neurotransmitter release at the terminal. Calcium rushes in down its very steep gradient (calcium is kept extremely low inside cells), but it does not flood the whole terminal evenly. Instead it forms transient localised microdomains of high Ca2+ concentration right at the channel mouth, precisely where the release machinery is docked. This spatial coupling is why release is so fast (sub-millisecond) and so tightly linked to the calcium signal.

The SNARE core complex: docking, priming, fusion

Vesicle fusion is executed by the SNARE complex, a bundle of proteins that zips the vesicle membrane against the terminal membrane. The cast list is high-yield:

  • v-SNARE (on the vesicle): Synaptobrevin (also called VAMP).
  • t-SNAREs (on the target/terminal membrane): Syntaxin-1 and SNAP-25.

These three coil together into a four-helix bundle that ratchets the two membranes together (docking, then priming into a fusion-ready state). The final trigger is calcium-dependent. Synaptotagmin, a protein on the vesicle, is the primary Ca2+ sensor: when calcium binds synaptotagmin, it forces the final fusion of the membranes and the vesicle dumps its neurotransmitter into the cleft. So: SNAREs assemble the gun, synaptotagmin pulls the trigger when calcium arrives.

Memory aid

"v" for vesicle and for VAMP/synaptobrevin; the two t-SNAREs are Syntaxin and SNAP-25 ("two S's on the target"). The sensor's name literally contains "tag": synaptotagmin tags the calcium. Botulinum and tetanus toxins are clinically famous precisely because they cleave these SNARE proteins and abolish release.

High-yield

Synaptotagmin is the calcium sensor that triggers fusion. The SNARE core is synaptobrevin (v) + syntaxin-1 + SNAP-25 (t). Calcium enters through N- and P/Q-type voltage-gated channels into high-concentration microdomains.

Endocytosis and vesicle recycling

After release, the empty vesicle membrane must be retrieved so the terminal does not balloon and so vesicles can be refilled. The classic pathway is clathrin-mediated endocytosis: the protein clathrin assembles a polyhedral lattice (a coat) that bends a patch of membrane inward into a pit. The neck of that pit is then severed by dynamin, a GTPase that wraps around the neck like a collar and constricts it (neck fission), pinching off a free vesicle. The retrieved vesicle is then sorted through endosomal sorting networks, refilled with transmitter, and returned to the releasable pool.

Postsynaptic transduction: ionotropic vs metabotropic

The transmitter now binds a receptor on the postsynaptic cell, and there are two fundamentally different receptor architectures. The distinction is one of the most reliable themes in the whole paper, because nearly every receptor you study can be sorted into one of these two boxes, and the box predicts the receptor's speed, its mechanism, and often its clinical drug class.

The heterotrimeric G-protein has three subunits: alpha, beta, and gamma. At rest the alpha subunit holds GDP. When the activated GPCR contacts it, GDP is swapped for GTP, and the G-alpha-GTP dissociates from the beta-gamma dimer. Both pieces can now act on effectors. The two canonical effector pathways are:

  • Adenylate cyclase pathway: G-alpha (of the Gs type) stimulates adenylate cyclase, which converts ATP into the second messenger cAMP. cAMP activates Protein Kinase A (PKA). The Gi type of G-alpha inhibits adenylate cyclase, lowering cAMP.
  • Phospholipase C pathway: G-alpha (of the Gq type) stimulates phospholipase C (PLC), which cleaves membrane PIP2 into two second messengers, IP3 and DAG. IP3 releases Ca2+ from intracellular stores; DAG plus that Ca2+ activates Protein Kinase C (PKC). The rise in calcium also activates Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), central to synaptic plasticity and learning.
Definition

A second messenger is a small intracellular molecule (cAMP, IP3, DAG, Ca2+) generated when a first messenger (the neurotransmitter) binds a metabotropic receptor; it amplifies and relays the signal inside the cell. The kinases (PKA, PKC, CaMKII) then phosphorylate target proteins to change their function.

Memory aid

Two effector enzymes, two messenger sets: Adenylate cyclase makes cAMP to wake PKA ("A, A, A"). Phospholipase C makes IP3 + DAG to wake PKC ("C, C, C") plus CaMKII via calcium.

Signal integration: summation, EPSPs and IPSPs

A single synapse rarely fires the postsynaptic cell. Inputs are integrated at the axon hillock (the trigger zone), where the membrane potential is compared to threshold. The graded potentials that arrive are of two kinds:

  • Excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP): a depolarisation (e.g. Na+ influx) that pushes the cell toward threshold, making firing more likely.
  • Inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP): a hyperpolarisation (e.g. Cl− influx or K+ efflux) that pushes the cell away from threshold, making firing less likely.

These potentials are graded and decremental (they shrink as they spread, per the cable properties above), so to reach threshold they must add up. There are two ways they sum:

  • Spatial summation: several inputs at different locations on the cell fire at the same time, and their potentials add together at the hillock.
  • Temporal summation: a single input fires repeatedly in quick succession, so that each EPSP arrives before the previous one has fully decayed, and they stack in time.

If the summed depolarisation at the hillock reaches −55 mV, an AP is launched. EPSPs and IPSPs converging there are algebraically combined, which is how the neuron computes.

Exam trap

Spatial = different places, same time; temporal = same place, different times (rapid repeats). Candidates routinely reverse these. Also: an IPSP is not "no signal"; it is an active hyperpolarising input that can cancel an EPSP.

C. Extended Neurochemistry & Neuropharmacology

This is the densest subsection and the one most directly linked to clinical psychopharmacology. For each transmitter, learn the synthesis pathway and its rate-limiting enzyme, the breakdown pathway and its metabolite, the anatomical source nuclei, the receptor subtypes with their coupling, and the drugs that act there. The examiners build entire questions out of a single enzyme name or metabolite.

The Dopaminergic (DA) system

Synthesis: L-Tyrosine → L-DOPA (by Tyrosine Hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme) → Dopamine (by DOPA Decarboxylase, also called aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase). Catabolism: dopamine is broken down by Monoamine Oxidase (MAO-A and MAO-B) and Catechol-O-Methyltransferase (COMT), with the final metabolite being Homovanillic Acid (HVA).

The four dopaminergic tracts (each a near-certain exam item):

TractFrom → toFunction / clinical link
MesolimbicVentral tegmental area → limbic system (nucleus accumbens)Reward, salience; overactivity gives the positive (psychotic) symptoms of schizophrenia. Blocking it is how antipsychotics reduce hallucinations.
MesocorticalVentral tegmental area → prefrontal cortexExecutive function; underactivity gives the negative and cognitive symptoms.

Receptors fall into two families, all metabotropic:

  • D1-like (D1 and D5): coupled to Gs, so they stimulate adenylate cyclase and raise cAMP.
  • D2-like (D2, D3, D4): coupled to Gi, so they inhibit adenylate cyclase and lower cAMP. The D2 receptor is the principal target of antipsychotics.

Typical vs atypical neuroleptics: typical (first-generation) antipsychotics (e.g. haloperidol, chlorpromazine) are potent D2 blockers; their high D2 blockade controls positive symptoms but causes extrapyramidal side-effects and raised prolactin. Atypical (second-generation) antipsychotics (e.g. risperidone, olanzapine, clozapine) combine more moderate D2 blockade with strong 5-HT2A blockade, which softens motor side-effects and gives some benefit for negative symptoms. Worked example of how the four tracts predict side-effects: a patient on haloperidol who develops a tremor and stiffness is showing nigrostriatal D2 blockade (extrapyramidal effects); the same patient developing breast discharge is showing tuberoinfundibular D2 blockade (the prolactin brake removed). One drug, two tracts, two predictable consequences, which is exactly the reasoning the paper expects you to reproduce.

High-yield

Tyrosine Hydroxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme of catecholamine synthesis; HVA is dopamine's final metabolite. D1-like = Gs (raise cAMP), D2-like = Gi (lower cAMP). Mesolimbic overactivity = positive symptoms; mesocortical underactivity = negative symptoms. Atypicals add 5-HT2A blockade.

Exam trap

D1 is Gs (stimulatory), D2 is Gi (inhibitory): do not swap them. The rate-limiting step is tyrosine → L-DOPA (tyrosine hydroxylase), not the decarboxylation step. HVA is for dopamine; the noradrenaline metabolite is VMA/MHPG, which is a tempting wrong option.

The Serotonergic (5-HT) system

Synthesis: L-Tryptophan → 5-HTP (by Tryptophan Hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme) → 5-HT/serotonin (by Aromatic L-Amino Acid Decarboxylase, the same decarboxylase used in the dopamine path). Catabolism: MAO-A converts 5-HT to 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic Acid (5-HIAA), the metabolite measured as an index of serotonin turnover (low CSF 5-HIAA is linked to impulsivity and suicide).

Anatomy: serotonergic cell bodies sit in the Dorsal and Median Raphe Nuclei of the brainstem and project diffusely across the forebrain. Receptors to know:

  • 5-HT1A: metabotropic (Gi); functions as a somatodendritic autoreceptor that, when stimulated, slows the raphe neuron's own firing. Buspirone is a 5-HT1A partial agonist.
  • 5-HT2A: metabotropic (Gq); the target of atypical antipsychotics (which block it) and of classical psychedelics such as LSD and psilocybin (which agonise it).
  • 5-HT3: the lone serotonin receptor that is ionotropic (a ligand-gated cation channel), mediating nausea and emesis; ondansetron is a 5-HT3 antagonist antiemetic.
Memory aid

"5-HT3 is the odd one: it is the only ionotropic serotonin receptor, and it makes you 3-times sick (emesis)." 1A is the brake on the cell itself (autoreceptor). 2A is the psychedelic/antipsychotic seat.

The Cholinergic (ACh) system

Synthesis: Choline Acetyltransferase (ChAT) joins Acetyl-CoA and choline to make acetylcholine. Termination: it is rapidly hydrolysed in the cleft by Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) (which is why cholinergic signalling is so brief; AChE inhibitors such as donepezil prolong it in dementia). Receptors are of two families:

  • Nicotinic: ionotropic, a pentameric ligand-gated cation channel; found at the neuromuscular junction and in autonomic ganglia.
  • Muscarinic (M1 to M5): metabotropic GPCRs. M1, M3, M5 couple to Gq (excitatory, PLC); M2 and M4 couple to Gi (inhibitory).

Anatomy: two cholinergic projection sources are heavily tested. The Nucleus Basalis of Meynert (in the basal forebrain) projects to the cortex and is the cell group that degenerates in Alzheimer's disease, accounting for the cholinergic hypothesis of the memory deficit. The Medial Septal Nuclei project to the hippocampus and drive the theta rhythm important for memory.

High-yield

ChAT builds ACh; AChE breaks it down. Nicotinic = ionotropic pentamer; muscarinic M1 to M5 = metabotropic. The Nucleus Basalis of Meynert is the cortical cholinergic source that degenerates in Alzheimer's; AChE inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) are the symptomatic treatment.

Amino-acid systems: glutamate and GABA

These are the brain's main fast workhorses: glutamate is the principal excitatory transmitter, GABA the principal inhibitory one.

Glutamate synthesis: from glutamine by the enzyme Glutaminase. Clearance: glutamate is taken back up, mainly by astrocytes, through Excitatory Amino Acid Transporters (EAATs); failure of clearance leaves glutamate in the cleft and drives toxicity. There are three ionotropic glutamate receptors plus metabotropic ones:

ReceptorKey properties
NMDAIonotropic, but with a voltage-dependent Mg2+ block in its pore that must be expelled by depolarisation before it conducts; requires glycine (or D-serine) as a co-agonist alongside glutamate; highly permeable to Ca2+. This coincidence-detecting, calcium-admitting property makes it the molecular substrate of long-term potentiation and learning.
AMPAIonotropic; mediates fast Na+ influx with rapid kinetics; the workhorse of ordinary fast excitatory transmission. Its depolarisation is what removes the Mg2+ block from neighbouring NMDA receptors.
KainateIonotropic; modulatory roles pre- and post-synaptically.

Excitotoxicity: when glutamate is excessive (as in stroke or trauma), over-activation of NMDA receptors lets too much Ca2+ into the neuron. The calcium overload activates destructive enzymes (proteases, phospholipases, endonucleases) and nitric oxide synthase, producing free radicals and mitochondrial failure, and the cell dies. This calcium-mediated cell death is the molecular cascade of glutamate excitotoxicity.

GABA synthesis: GABA is made from glutamate by the enzyme Glutamic Acid Decarboxylase (GAD), which requires Pyridoxal Phosphate (the active form of Vitamin B6) as a cofactor (a fact often tested, and the reason B6 deficiency lowers GABA and provokes seizures). Its receptors:

  • GABA-A: ionotropic Cl− channel. Opening it lets chloride in, hyperpolarising the cell (inhibition). Crucially, GABA-A carries distinct allosteric binding sites for benzodiazepines, barbiturates, ethanol, and neurosteroids: each binds its own site and enhances the chloride current, which is why all of these are sedating, anxiolytic, and cross-tolerant. Benzodiazepines increase channel opening frequency; barbiturates increase channel open duration.
  • GABA-B: metabotropic heterodimer coupled to Gi; it produces slower inhibition by opening K+ channels and closing Ca2+ channels. Baclofen is a GABA-B agonist.
Exam trap

GABA-A is ionotropic (a chloride channel, the seat of benzodiazepines and barbiturates); GABA-B is metabotropic (Gi). GAD needs Vitamin B6, not B12. NMDA needs glycine as a co-agonist and is blocked by Mg2+ in a voltage-dependent way; AMPA is the one that simply passes Na+. Mixing the Mg2+ block onto AMPA, or the glycine co-agonist requirement onto AMPA, is a standard distractor.

"GAD is GABA's Dad, and Dad needs his B6-pack." NMDA = "N for Needs glycine, Needs depolarisation (to shift Mg), aNd lets calciuM iN."

D. Functional Neuroanatomy & Subcortical Systems Loops

The final subsection maps the cortex layer by layer and area by area, then traces three deep-brain circuits (Papez, the amygdala, and the basal ganglia loops) whose wiring diagrams are recurrent exam fodder.

Cortical cytoarchitecture: the six layers

The neocortex is built from six horizontal cellular layers (numbered I at the surface to VI at the white matter), and two of them are heavily tested because they define the cortex's input and output:

  • Layer IV (Internal Granular Layer): the primary recipient of thalamocortical sensory input. It is thick in sensory ("granular") cortex such as the primary visual cortex and essentially absent in primary motor cortex (which is "agranular").
  • Layer V (Internal Pyramidal Layer): the main output layer, containing the giant Betz cells of the primary motor cortex, whose long axons project down to subcortical and spinal motor targets (the corticospinal tract).
Memory aid

"IV is the In-box (sensory input from thalamus); V is the Ventral output (Betz cells out to the cord)." Sensory cortex is granular (fat layer IV); motor cortex is agranular (fat layer V).

The Papez circuit

James Papez proposed in 1937 a closed loop carrying emotion and memory through the limbic system. Learn the full loop in order; the examiners frequently give it with one station blanked out:

Hippocampus → Fornix → Mammillary Bodies → Mammillothalamic Tract → Anterior Thalamic Nucleus → Cingulate Gyrus → Entorhinal Cortex → (back to) Hippocampus.

The clinical anchor is Korsakoff syndrome, in which thiamine deficiency damages the mammillary bodies (a Papez station), producing dense anterograde amnesia and confabulation.

"He Found Mammary Milk, Ate Cake, Ended Hungry": Hippocampus, Fornix, Mammillary bodies, Mammillothalamic tract, Anterior thalamus, Cingulate, Entorhinal, Hippocampus.

The amygdaloid complex

The amygdala is the limbic hub for fear and emotional salience, and its internal division into input and output nuclei is testable:

  • The Lateral and Basolateral nuclei are the sensory convergence (input) zones: they receive multimodal sensory information (from cortex and thalamus) and are the site of fear conditioning.
  • The Central nucleus is the output hub: it organises the actual fear response by projecting to the brainstem and hypothalamus. Specifically, its projections to the Lateral Hypothalamus drive the autonomic (sympathetic) component of fear (heart rate, blood pressure), and its projections to the Periaqueductal Gray (PAG) drive the behavioural component (freezing, defensive responses).
High-yield

Amygdala: Lateral/Basolateral = sensory input and fear learning; Central nucleus = output, sending to the Lateral Hypothalamus (autonomic fear) and the Periaqueductal Gray (behavioural freezing). Bilateral amygdala damage produces the placidity of Kluver-Bucy syndrome.

The basal ganglia tune movement through two opposing loops that both originate in the striatum and converge on the same output nuclei, the internal globus pallidus (GPi) and substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr). 2 · Advanced Statistics, Psychometrics & Research Methodology

This is one of the three heavy-core sections of the M.Phil entrance, and it is where many strong candidates lose marks because they memorised formulas without understanding when each tool applies and what to do when its assumptions break. The examiner's favourite move is to describe a data situation (small sample, skewed scores, unequal variances, correlated factors) and ask which test or which correction is correct. So this section teaches not only the formulas verbatim, but the why behind every choice: what each method assumes, what happens when that assumption fails, and what you switch to. We move from the theory of measurement (how a test score relates to a true score), through item analysis and modern item response theory, into validity and the logic of experimental design, and finally through the full inferential machinery from t-tests to factor analysis. Treat every number, threshold, and Greek letter here as directly examinable.

A. Classical Test Theory: the foundation of measurement

Classical Test Theory (CTT), also called classical reliability theory or true-score theory, is the framework that underlies almost every psychological test you will encounter. Its core idea is deceptively simple: every observed score a person earns is the sum of two parts, a true component and an error component.

Definition

The classical linear model: X = T + E, where X is the observed score (what the test gives you), T is the true score (the person's actual standing on the trait, defined as the expected value of their observed scores over infinite independent testings), and E is the measurement error (random noise from fatigue, lucky guesses, item ambiguity, scoring slips).

The model rests on three fundamental assumptions about the error term. These three are highly examinable and are often presented as "which of the following is NOT a CTT assumption":

  • The mean of error scores is zero: E(E) = 0. Across the whole population, errors are as often positive as negative and cancel out; error is random, not systematic. (Systematic error is not error in this model; it gets folded into the true score or signals invalidity.)
  • True and error scores are uncorrelated: rhoTE = 0. People with high true scores are not more (or less) prone to error than people with low true scores.
  • Errors on separate tests are uncorrelated: rhoE1E2 = 0. The error you make on one administration tells you nothing about the error you will make on another. This is what allows error to be treated as pure random noise.

From X = T + E it follows that observed-score variance decomposes into true-score variance plus error variance: variance(X) = variance(T) + variance(E). Reliability is then defined as the proportion of observed variance that is true variance: rxx = variance(T) / variance(X). A perfectly reliable test (rxx = 1.0) has no error variance; a test of pure noise has rxx = 0.

The Standard Error of Measurement (SEM)

Reliability is a property of the test in a population; the Standard Error of Measurement translates that reliability into a band of uncertainty around an individual's score, which is what clinicians actually need when reporting an IQ or a symptom-scale score.

Definition

SEM = SD × square root of (1 minus rxx), where SD is the standard deviation of the test scores and rxx is the reliability coefficient. It is the standard deviation of the errors of measurement, and it estimates how much an individual's observed score would bounce around their true score on repeated testing.

Worked example: a Wechsler IQ has SD = 15 and reliability rxx = 0.96. SEM = 15 × square root of (1 minus 0.96) = 15 × square root of 0.04 = 15 × 0.20 = 3.0. A 95% confidence interval is roughly the observed score plus or minus 1.96 × SEM, so an IQ of 100 has a true score very likely between about 94 and 106. This is exactly why test manuals report confidence bands rather than a single point.

High-yield

The two endpoints of SEM are the most-tested facts. If reliability is perfect (rxx = 1), SEM = 0 (no error). As reliability falls, SEM rises. SEM is inversely related to reliability and directly related to the SD. Memorise the formula SEM = SD × sqrt(1 minus rxx) verbatim.

B. Reliability quantifications

Reliability is consistency: the degree to which a test gives the same result under consistent conditions. The four computational tools below each estimate it differently, and the exam tests which one fits which situation.

Spearman-Brown Prophecy formula

Reliability rises as a test gets longer (more items sample the trait more stably). The Spearman-Brown Prophecy formula predicts what reliability a test would have if you lengthened (or shortened) it by a factor of n: rnew = (n × rxx) / (1 + (n minus 1) × rxx). Its classic use is the split-half method: you split the test into two halves, correlate them, but that correlation is for a half-length test, so Spearman-Brown (with n = 2) "steps it up" to estimate the full test's reliability.

Worked example: a split-half correlation of 0.60 gives full-test reliability of (2 × 0.60) / (1 + 0.60) = 1.20 / 1.60 = 0.75. The formula also tells you how many items to add to reach a target reliability.

Cronbach's alpha

Definition

Cronbach's alpha is the most widely reported index of internal consistency: the average of all possible split-half reliabilities. It is computed from the variance-covariance matrix of the items: alpha = (k / (k minus 1)) × (1 minus (sum of item variances / total test variance)), where k is the number of items. It tells you how well a set of items hang together as a measure of a single construct.

Conventional benchmarks: alpha ≥ 0.70 is acceptable for research, ≥ 0.80 good, ≥ 0.90 excellent (and required for high-stakes individual decisions). Alpha rises with more items and with higher inter-item correlations.

Exam trap

A very high alpha (above about 0.95) is not purely good: it often signals item redundancy (several items asking the same thing in slightly different words), which inflates the construct's apparent breadth. Alpha also assumes unidimensionality: a high alpha does NOT prove the scale measures one thing. A multidimensional scale can still post a high alpha simply by having many items. So "alpha proves the scale is unidimensional" is a false statement.

Kuder-Richardson formulas: KR-20 and KR-21

When items are dichotomous (scored right/wrong, 1/0, true/false), Cronbach's alpha reduces to the Kuder-Richardson 20 (KR-20). KR-20 uses each item's proportion correct (p) and proportion incorrect (q = 1 minus p): it sums the p×q products across items. KR-21 is a simplified shortcut that assumes all items have equal difficulty, so it needs only the mean and variance of total scores rather than item-by-item statistics. Because real items differ in difficulty, KR-21 is always a lower-bound (more conservative) estimate than KR-20.

Exam trap

KR-20 and KR-21 are only for dichotomously scored items. For polytomous items (Likert scales, partial-credit), you use Cronbach's alpha, of which KR-20 is the special dichotomous case. A question pairing KR-20 with a 5-point Likert scale is testing this error.

Correction for attenuation

Because no measure is perfectly reliable, the observed correlation between two variables is always weaker than the true correlation between the underlying constructs; measurement error attenuates (shrinks) correlations. The correction for attenuation formula recovers the estimated true correlation: rtrue = rxy / square root of (rxx × ryy), where rxx and ryy are the reliabilities of the two measures. It divides the observed correlation by the geometric mean of the two reliabilities, mathematically "removing" the blurring effect of unreliability.

High-yield

The correction for attenuation always increases the correlation (you divide by numbers less than 1). It estimates what the correlation would be if both measures were perfectly reliable. If you correct for the unreliability of only one variable (the predictor or the criterion alone), you use a single reliability in the denominator under the root.

C. Item analysis parameters

Item analysis is the quality control of test construction: for each item you ask "is it too easy or too hard?" and "does it separate the able from the less able?".

Item difficulty index (p-value)

The item difficulty index is simply the proportion of examinees who answered the item correctly, denoted p. Confusingly, a higher p means an easier item (more people got it right). The range is 0 (nobody passed, impossibly hard) to 1 (everybody passed, trivially easy). The most informative items sit around p = 0.50, because an item passed by exactly half the group maximally discriminates between high and low scorers. For a multiple-choice item, the ideal p is adjusted upward to account for guessing (midway between 1.0 and the chance level).

Item discrimination index (d)

Definition

Item discrimination index: d = P(upper 27%) minus P(lower 27%), the proportion of the top-scoring 27% who got the item right minus the proportion of the bottom-scoring 27% who got it right. It measures how well an item distinguishes high scorers from low scorers.

The 27% figure is Kelley's classic split (it maximises the difference between groups for a normal distribution while keeping enough cases in each group). Interpretation: d ≥ 0.40 is excellent; 0.30 to 0.39 good; 0.20 to 0.29 marginal; below 0.20 poor (revise or drop). A negative d is a red flag: it means low scorers passed the item more often than high scorers, signalling a miskeyed item or a flawed item that penalises the able.

Point-biserial correlation

The point-biserial correlation is the correlation between performance on a single dichotomous item (right/wrong) and the total test score (continuous). It is the standard index of item-total consistency: a high positive point-biserial means people who got this item right also tended to score high overall, so the item belongs with the rest. Like d, a near-zero or negative point-biserial flags a bad item.

Memory aid

"p for pass, d for distinguish." The p-value tells you how many passed; d tells you whether the item distinguishes good from poor students. High p = easy; high d = good discriminator. Aim for moderate p (~0.50) and high d (> 0.30).

D. Item Response Theory (IRT): modern psychometrics

CTT has a crippling limitation: its statistics (difficulty, discrimination, reliability) are sample-dependent. An item looks "easy" in a bright sample and "hard" in a weak one, and a person's score depends on which items they happened to get. Item Response Theory solves this by modelling the probability of a correct response as a mathematical function of a single underlying latent trait, theta (the person's ability or standing on the construct), with item parameters that are (ideally) invariant across samples.

Definition

Latent trait (theta): the unobservable underlying ability or attribute (intelligence, depression severity) that IRT estimates for each person, placed on the same scale as item difficulty so persons and items can be directly compared.

The IRT models build up by adding parameters. Each plots an item characteristic curve (ICC), an S-shaped (logistic) curve of probability of correct response against theta:

2PLb and a (adds discrimination)Adds the discrimination parameter a, the steepness of the ICC. A steeper curve (higher a) means the item sharply separates people near its difficulty.
3PLb, a, and c (adds pseudo-guessing)Adds the pseudo-guessing parameter c, the lower asymptote: the probability a very-low-ability person still gets it right by guessing (about 0.25 for a 4-option MCQ). The curve no longer drops to zero.
Exam trap

Know exactly which parameter each model adds: 1PL = difficulty (b); 2PL adds discrimination (a); 3PL adds guessing (c). The Rasch model is mathematically the 1PL but philosophically distinct: Rasch insists data must fit the model (fixed discrimination), whereas the 1PL estimates a common discrimination from the data. The b parameter is on the same scale as theta; do not confuse b (difficulty) with a (discrimination).

Item and Test Information Functions

In IRT, reliability is replaced by information, which can vary along the trait scale. The Item Information Function (IIF) shows how much measurement precision an item provides at each level of theta; an item gives most information near its own difficulty (b) and for high-a items the information peak is tall and narrow. The Test Information Function (TIF) is the sum of all item information functions: it shows where on the trait continuum the whole test measures most precisely. Crucially, the standard error of measurement in IRT is the inverse square root of information, so it is conditional on theta: a test can be precise for average examinees but imprecise at the extremes. This is the foundation of computer-adaptive testing, which selects items giving maximum information at the examinee's current estimated theta.

Differential Item Functioning (DIF)

Differential Item Functioning occurs when examinees of equal underlying ability but from different groups (gender, language, culture) have different probabilities of answering an item correctly. DIF is the modern psychometric test for item bias: because theta is held equal, a remaining group difference points to something irrelevant in the item (a culturally loaded word, an unfamiliar context). DIF is detected with methods like the Mantel-Haenszel statistic or IRT-based ICC comparisons.

High-yield

The headline advantages of IRT over CTT: (1) item parameters are sample-invariant; (2) person ability and item difficulty are on the same scale; (3) precision (information) varies across the trait, not a single reliability; (4) it enables adaptive testing and rigorous bias detection via DIF.

E. Construct validation and matrix analysis

Reliability asks "is the measure consistent?"; validity asks the deeper question "does it measure what it claims to?". You can be perfectly reliable yet invalid (a precise bathroom scale is a reliable but invalid measure of intelligence).

Lawshe's Content Validity Ratio (CVR)

Content validity is whether a test's items adequately sample the whole domain of the construct. Lawshe quantified expert judgement of it with the Content Validity Ratio: CVR = (ne minus N/2) / (N/2), where ne is the number of expert panellists who rate an item as "essential" and N is the total number of panellists. CVR ranges from minus 1 (no expert calls it essential) to plus 1 (all do); CVR = 0 means exactly half called it essential. Items below a critical CVR value (which depends on panel size) are dropped. Averaging the CVRs of retained items gives the Content Validity Index (CVI).

Standard error of estimate

When you use one test to predict a criterion (an aptitude test predicting job performance), the standard error of estimate (SEest) is the standard deviation of the prediction errors (residuals): it indexes how far, on average, actual criterion scores fall from the regression-predicted values. SEest = SDy × square root of (1 minus r2), where r is the validity coefficient. The higher the validity, the smaller the prediction error. (Note the parallel to SEM, which uses reliability instead of validity.)

The Multitrait-Multimethod (MTMM) matrix

Campbell and Fiske (1959) gave the definitive method for establishing construct validity through its two halves: convergent validity (a measure correlates with other measures of the same trait) and discriminant validity (it does NOT correlate strongly with measures of different traits). To test both at once you measure several traits by several methods and arrange the correlations in the MTMM matrix. Heterotrait-monomethod = method bias (different traits look similar only because the method is shared = bad). Convergent wants HIGH; discriminant wants LOW.

Nomological networks

Cronbach and Meehl's nomological network is the wider theoretical web of lawful relationships ("nomological" = lawlike) linking a construct to other constructs and to observable behaviours. Construct validity is built by showing the measure behaves as the network predicts (depression should correlate positively with hopelessness, negatively with life satisfaction, and rise after loss). It is the overarching framework within which MTMM, convergent, and discriminant evidence all sit.

F. Variable control architecture in experimental design

An experiment's job is to attribute changes in the dependent variable to the independent variable and nothing else, which means controlling extraneous variables. Operationalisation (defining a variable by the concrete operations used to measure it, for example "anxiety = score on the STAI") is the starting point, but it has limits: a single operational definition rarely captures a construct fully, so multiple operationalisations strengthen validity. The control techniques you must name:

  • Elimination: physically remove the extraneous variable (run all trials in a soundproof room to eliminate noise). Clean but not always possible.
  • Constancy (holding constant): keep the variable at one fixed level for everyone (test all participants at the same time of day). It cannot be eliminated, so it is held steady so it cannot vary with conditions.
  • Matching: equate groups on a known nuisance variable by pairing participants with similar values (match each treatment participant to a control of the same IQ). Controls that specific variable but not unknown ones.
  • Randomisation: the most powerful control. Random assignment distributes all extraneous variables, known and unknown, evenly across groups on average. It is the only technique that controls variables you have not even thought of, which is why it is the gold standard.
  • Counterbalancing: in within-subjects designs, systematically vary the order of conditions across participants so that order and practice/fatigue effects cancel out rather than confounding the treatment.

Latin Square designs

When full counterbalancing is impractical (with k conditions there are k-factorial possible orders), a Latin Square provides efficient partial counterbalancing. It is a k × k grid in which each condition appears exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column, so every condition occupies every ordinal position equally often across the rows. This balances simple order (progressive/practice and fatigue) effects without needing every permutation.

Exam trap

Randomisation controls unknown variables; matching only controls the specific variable matched. This is the classic discriminator. Also: a Latin square balances position effects but does NOT fully control differential carryover (asymmetric sequence effects where condition A's after-effect on B differs from B's on A); for that you need a fully counterbalanced or washout design.

G. Causal pathway modelling: mediators vs moderators

This pair is one of the most reliably tested distinctions in the whole research-methods syllabus, because students constantly confuse them.

Definition

A mediator explains the mechanism: it is the intervening variable through which X exerts its effect on Y (X causes M causes Y). It answers how or why. A moderator changes the strength or direction of the X-to-Y relationship depending on its level. It answers when or for whom.

Mediation: Baron and Kenny's causal steps and the Sobel test

Baron and Kenny (1986) gave four steps to establish mediation (using paths labelled c, a, b, and c-prime):

  • Step 1: X significantly predicts Y (path c, the total effect, is significant).
  • Step 2: X significantly predicts the mediator M (path a).
  • Step 3: M significantly predicts Y while controlling for X (path b).
  • Step 4: when M is added, the X-to-Y effect shrinks. If it drops to non-significant, that is full mediation; if it only weakens but stays significant (path c-prime), that is partial mediation.

The indirect effect is the product a × b: the effect of X on Y carried through M. The Sobel test evaluates whether this indirect effect is statistically significant by dividing a × b by its pooled standard error (a function of the standard errors of a and b) to give a z statistic. Because the product a×b is not normally distributed in small samples, modern practice prefers bootstrapped confidence intervals (Preacher and Hayes) over the Sobel test, but you must still know the Sobel logic for the exam.

Moderation: interaction terms

A moderator is tested by entering the product interaction term X × Z into a regression alongside the main effects of X and Z. If the X×Z term is significant, Z moderates the X-to-Y relationship: the slope of Y on X differs across levels of Z. (To reduce multicollinearity, predictors are usually mean-centred before forming the product.) Multi-group structural equation modelling is the alternative when the moderator is categorical.

Memory aid

Mediator = Mechanism (the Middle man, how/why); Moderator = Modifies the strength (when/for whom). A mediator sits between X and Y on the causal chain (X to M to Y). A moderator sits off to the side and changes the X-to-Y arrow's strength. Mediation uses a×b and the Sobel test; moderation uses the X×Z interaction term.

H. Design typologies: factorial, quasi-experimental, single-subject

True factorial designs and higher-order interactions

A factorial design manipulates two or more independent variables (factors) simultaneously, so it yields both main effects (the separate effect of each factor averaged over the others) and interaction effects (the effect of one factor depends on the level of another). A 2 × 3 design crosses one two-level factor with one three-level factor (six cells). Its great efficiency is detecting interactions, which a series of one-variable studies could never reveal. A higher-order interaction (a three-way A×B×C interaction) means a two-way interaction itself changes across the levels of a third factor; these are real but hard to interpret and require large samples.

High-yield

The unique payoff of a factorial design is the interaction effect. When an interaction is significant, you must interpret it before the main effects, because a main effect can be misleading in the presence of an interaction (a "qualified" main effect). On a graph, non-parallel lines = interaction present; parallel lines = no interaction.

Quasi-experimental designs

A quasi-experiment tests a treatment but lacks random assignment to conditions (often because randomising is unethical or impossible, as with comparing existing schools or pre-existing groups). This weakens internal validity, and the three named designs each handle that differently:

  • Nonequivalent control group design: a treatment group and an intact (non-randomised) comparison group are both pre-tested and post-tested. The pre-test checks how comparable the groups were; the threat is selection and selection-by-maturation, since the groups may have differed to begin with.
  • Interrupted time-series design: a single group is measured repeatedly at many points before and after the intervention ("interruption"). A genuine treatment effect shows as a change in level or slope of the series at exactly the intervention point. The chief threat is history (some other event coinciding with the intervention).
  • Regression-discontinuity design: participants are assigned to treatment by a strict cut-off on a continuous assignment variable (everyone scoring below a threshold gets remedial tutoring). A treatment effect appears as a discontinuity (a jump) in the regression line at the cut-off. Because assignment is by a known rule, it has stronger causal warrant than other quasi-designs, approaching a true experiment near the cut-off.

Single-subject (single-case) designs

Single-subject designs study one (or a few) participants intensively across time, using each participant as their own control. They are central to behaviour-analytic and clinical research.

  • AB design: a baseline phase (A) followed by a treatment phase (B). Simplest, but weak: any change at B could be coincidental (history/maturation), so it cannot establish a causal effect alone.
  • ABA (reversal/withdrawal): baseline, treatment, then withdraw treatment back to baseline. If behaviour improves under B and reverts when treatment is removed, the treatment is implicated. Ethical problem: it ends on baseline (no treatment).
  • ABAB: adds a second treatment phase, so it both demonstrates the effect twice (stronger causal evidence) and ends on treatment (ethically preferable). This is the workhorse reversal design.
  • Multiple-baseline design: used when reversal is impossible or unethical (you cannot un-teach a learned skill) or undesirable (withdrawing a helpful treatment). Treatment is introduced in a staggered sequence across several baselines: across subjects, across behaviours, or across settings. Each baseline should change only when its own treatment begins, which rules out history as an explanation without ever withdrawing the treatment.
Exam trap

The reason to choose a multiple-baseline over an ABAB is precisely to demonstrate control without therapeutic withdrawal: it controls the history threat by staggering, not by reversing, so you never take away a working treatment or rely on a behaviour being reversible. ABAB requires the behaviour to be reversible; multiple-baseline does not.

External validity threats

  • Selection × treatment interaction: the treatment works only for the particular (often unrepresentative) sample studied, so it does not generalise to other populations.
  • Setting × treatment interaction: the effect holds only in the specific setting (lab, one clinic) and fails to transfer to other settings.
  • Reactive arrangements / Hawthorne effect: participants change their behaviour simply because they know they are being studied, not because of the treatment. (Named for the Hawthorne Works productivity studies.)
  • Demand characteristics: participants pick up cues about the hypothesis and consciously or unconsciously behave to confirm (or sabotage) it.
  • Experimenter expectancy / Rosenthal effect: the experimenter's expectations unintentionally bias how they treat participants or record data, producing a self-fulfilling result. The double-blind procedure is the control.
Memory aid

Internal-validity threats are about confounds inside the study (was it really the treatment?); external-validity threats are about generalising outside it (does it travel?). For the reactive trio: Hawthorne = being watched changes you; demand characteristics = the participant guesses the hypothesis; Rosenthal/expectancy = the experimenter's bias leaks out. All three are fixed by blinding.

J. Inferential statistics: the hypothesis-gating parameters

Central Limit Theorem

Definition

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT): regardless of the shape of the parent population, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as sample size increases, with the approximation generally adequate at N ≥ 30. Its standard deviation (the standard error of the mean) equals the population SD divided by the square root of N.

The CLT is why parametric tests, which assume normality of the sampling distribution, remain valid even with somewhat non-normal data once N is reasonably large: it is the large-sample licence to use z and t.

A Type I error (alpha) is rejecting a true null hypothesis: claiming an effect that is not real (a false positive). Alpha is set by the researcher, conventionally 0.05. A Type II error (beta) is failing to reject a false null: missing a real effect (a false negative).

Statistical power

Power = 1 minus beta: the probability of correctly detecting an effect that truly exists. The convention is power ≥ 0.80 (so beta ≤ 0.20). Four things raise power:

  • Larger sample size (N): the single most controllable lever; bigger N shrinks the standard error.
  • Larger effect size (gamma): bigger true effects are easier to detect (not under your control, but estimated for sample-size planning).
  • A larger (more lenient) alpha: raising alpha from 0.01 to 0.05 increases power, but at the cost of more Type I errors. This is the fundamental alpha-power trade-off.
  • Lower error variance / one-tailed tests: reducing measurement noise or using a directional test concentrates the test's sensitivity.
High-yield

Power = 1 minus beta. The cleanest way to raise power without inflating Type I error is to increase N. Lowering alpha (say to 0.01) reduces Type I error but reduces power too (raises beta). Alpha and beta trade off; only sample size and effect size buy you out of the trade-off.

K. Effect size formulations

A p-value tells you whether an effect exists; an effect size tells you how big it is, independent of N. Because large samples make trivial effects "significant", effect sizes are now mandatory reporting.

  • Cohen's d: the standardised mean difference, d = (mean1 minus mean2) / SDpooled, using the pooled standard deviation of the two groups in the denominator. Benchmarks: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. (Hedges' g is the small-sample-corrected version.)
  • Eta-squared (eta2): for ANOVA, the proportion of total variance explained by a factor = SSeffect / SStotal. Benchmarks: 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large.
  • Partial eta-squared (partial eta2): SSeffect / (SSeffect + SSerror): it removes other factors' variance from the denominator. In multi-factor designs the partial values are larger than ordinary eta-squared and (a common trap) can sum to more than 1.
  • Omega-squared (omega2): a less biased, more conservative estimate of variance explained, preferred for small samples because eta-squared overestimates the population effect. Omega-squared adjusts for the number of groups and error variance.
Exam trap

Eta-squared overestimates the population effect (it is positively biased, especially with small samples); omega-squared corrects this and is smaller. Do not report partial eta-squared as if it were ordinary eta-squared: partial values do not sum to the total variance explained and are inflated in factorial designs.

L. Parametric assumption testing: the diagnostic toolkit

Every parametric test rests on assumptions; this section is the examiner's goldmine because it asks "which diagnostic, which threshold, and what do you do when it fails?".

Normality

  • Shapiro-Wilk test: the preferred normality test for small samples (N < 50); it is the most powerful normality test for small N.
  • Kolmogorov-Smirnov test: used for larger samples (N ≥ 50); it compares the sample distribution to a reference normal (Lilliefors correction when parameters are estimated).
  • Skewness and kurtosis: as a rough rule, values within roughly plus or minus 1 (some texts say plus or minus 2) indicate acceptable normality. Skewness is asymmetry; kurtosis is peakedness/tail-heaviness.
Exam trap

The N split is highly testable: Shapiro-Wilk for N less than 50; Kolmogorov-Smirnov for N of 50 or more. Also note that with very large N, normality tests become hypersensitive and flag trivial departures as significant, so inspect skew/kurtosis and Q-Q plots too, do not rely on the p-value alone.

Homogeneity of variance

  • Levene's test: the robust default; it tests equality of variances using deviations from the group mean (or median, the "center alignment", which makes it robust to non-normality).
  • Bartlett's test: more powerful than Levene if the data are genuinely normal, but very sensitive to departures from normality (a chi-square based check). Use Bartlett only when normality is well established; otherwise prefer Levene.

Sphericity (repeated measures)

Sphericity is the assumption, specific to repeated-measures ANOVA, that the variances of the differences between all pairs of conditions are equal. It is tested with Mauchly's test. If Mauchly's test is significant, sphericity is violated and the F-test becomes too liberal (inflated Type I error), so you correct the degrees of freedom by multiplying them by an epsilon estimate:

  • Greenhouse-Geisser correction: the conservative correction, used when the estimated epsilon is below 0.75 (more severe violation).
  • Huynh-Feldt correction: the less conservative correction, used when epsilon is above 0.75 (milder violation), because Greenhouse-Geisser would over-correct.
High-yield

Sphericity applies ONLY to repeated-measures (within-subjects) designs. Mauchly's test detects violation; if violated, correct df with Greenhouse-Geisser (epsilon below 0.75) or Huynh-Feldt (epsilon above 0.75). Both shrink the degrees of freedom, which raises the critical F and restores a valid Type I error rate.

Homogeneity of regression slopes (for ANCOVA)

ANCOVA adjusts the dependent variable for a covariate, and it assumes the covariate-to-outcome relationship (the regression slope) is the same in every group: this is homogeneity of regression slopes. It is tested by checking the covariate × group interaction; if that interaction is significant the slopes differ, ANCOVA is invalid, and you should not use it (the covariate adjustment would mislead).

Multicollinearity

In multiple regression, multicollinearity (predictors highly correlated with each other) destabilises coefficient estimates. It is diagnosed with the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF): VIF > 5 signals serious redundancy (some texts use > 10 as the severe cut-off). VIF is the reciprocal of tolerance: VIF = 1 / Tolerance, where tolerance = 1 minus R2 of a predictor regressed on the others. High VIF (low tolerance) means the predictor is largely explained by the other predictors.

M. Hypothesis-testing executions

The t-test family

  • One-sample t-test: compares one sample mean to a known or hypothesised population value.
  • Independent-samples t-test: compares the means of two separate (unrelated) groups.
  • Paired-samples (dependent) t-test: compares two related measurements (pre vs post on the same people, or matched pairs).
  • Welch's t-test: the correction used when the two groups have unequal variances (heterogeneity, flagged by Levene). It adjusts the degrees of freedom downward and does not pool the variances, so it stays valid when the standard t-test's equal-variance assumption fails. Many statisticians now recommend Welch's as the default.
Exam trap

When Levene's test is significant (variances unequal), you do not abandon the t-test; you switch to Welch's t-test (the "equal variances not assumed" row in SPSS output). Switching to a non-parametric test is only required when normality fails, not merely when variances are unequal.

ANOVA and its variants

Analysis of Variance compares means across three or more groups using an F-ratio, avoiding the inflated Type I error of running many t-tests. Its logic is variance partitioning:

Definition

SSTotal = SSBetween + SSWithin. Total variability splits into between-groups variability (differences among group means, the "signal", reflecting treatment) and within-groups variability (variability of scores inside each group, the "noise", error). F = (SSBetween/dfbetween) / (SSWithin/dfwithin) = mean square between over mean square within. A large F means the signal exceeds the noise.

  • Factorial ANOVA: two or more factors, yielding main effects and interactions (see Section H).
  • Repeated-measures ANOVA: the same participants measured under all conditions; more powerful (removes between-subject variance) but assumes sphericity.
  • ANCOVA: ANOVA with a covariate statistically removed first, increasing power by reducing error variance; assumes homogeneity of regression slopes.
  • MANOVA: ANOVA with two or more dependent variables analysed jointly via centroid (mean vector) comparisons, controlling Type I error across the outcomes and detecting effects on the combination of DVs.

MANOVA significance is read from four multivariate test statistics: Wilks' Lambda (the most common; smaller Lambda = bigger effect, since it is the proportion of unexplained variance), Pillai's Trace (the most robust to assumption violations and unequal group sizes, preferred then), Hotelling's Trace, and Roy's Largest Root. For two groups they give identical results.

Memory aid

Wilks' Lambda is "leftover" variance: smaller is better (less unexplained = bigger effect). Pillai's Trace is the robust one: use it when assumptions are shaky or group sizes unequal. Hotelling's Trace is essentially the two-group case extended.

Post-hoc correction protocols

A significant omnibus ANOVA tells you some means differ but not which; post-hoc tests make the pairwise comparisons while controlling the family-wise error rate that would otherwise balloon:

  • Tukey's HSD (Honestly Significant Difference): the standard for all pairwise comparisons; well balanced between power and Type I control when group sizes are equal.
  • Bonferroni correction: divides alpha by the number of comparisons, alphanew = alpha / k. Simple and very conservative (over-corrects with many comparisons, costing power).
  • Scheffé's test: the most conservative; needed for complex contrasts (combinations of means, not just pairs). Use when comparisons are decided after seeing the data or involve groupings of means.
  • Dunnett's test: the specialist test for comparing several treatment groups each against a single control; more powerful than Tukey for that specific job because it makes fewer comparisons.
High-yield

Match the post-hoc to the question: Tukey HSD = all pairwise; Bonferroni = a few planned comparisons (alpha/k); Scheffé = complex contrasts (most conservative); Dunnett = many treatments vs one control. Bonferroni's formula alpha/k is a near-certain MCQ.

Chi-square tests for categorical data

  • Chi-square test of independence: tests whether two categorical variables are associated (in a contingency table) by comparing observed and expected cell frequencies.
  • Chi-square goodness-of-fit: tests whether one categorical variable's observed frequencies match a hypothesised distribution.

The key assumption: expected cell frequencies should be at least 5 (in most cells). When this fails, you must correct or switch:

  • Yates' correction for continuity: applied to 2 × 2 tables to reduce chi-square's overestimation when counts are small (it subtracts 0.5 from each absolute observed-minus-expected difference).
  • Fisher's exact test: the proper test for small samples / small expected frequencies in a 2 × 2 table; it computes the exact probability rather than approximating with chi-square.
  • McNemar's test: for paired nominal data (the same subjects measured twice, pre vs post), testing whether the proportions changed; it focuses on the discordant cells (those who switched).
Exam trap

When expected frequencies fall below 5 in a 2×2 table, use Fisher's exact test (not ordinary chi-square). For paired nominal data (the same people before and after), use McNemar's, not the independence chi-square: McNemar is the nominal cousin of the paired t-test, whereas the chi-square of independence assumes independent observations.

O. Correlation and regression

Bivariate correlation coefficients

  • Pearson's r: for two continuous, linearly related, normally distributed variables; the parametric coefficient.
  • Spearman's rho: the rank-order correlation; non-parametric, for ordinal data or non-linear monotonic relationships (it is Pearson's r computed on the ranks).
  • Kendall's tau: another rank correlation based on concordant vs discordant pairs; preferred over Spearman for small samples or many tied ranks, and gives a more conservative (smaller) value.

Partial vs semi-partial correlation

Partial correlation removes the variance of a third (control) variable from both primary variables, then correlates the residuals: it asks "what is the X-Y relationship with Z held constant for both?". Semi-partial (part) correlation removes the control variable's variance from only one of the two variables. In regression, the semi-partial squared is the unique variance a predictor adds.

High-yield

Partial = remove the covariate from BOTH variables; semi-partial = remove it from only ONE. Because partial cleans both variables, it is usually the larger of the two. This one-word difference ("both" vs "one") is the entire exam point.

Regression models

  • Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression: fits a line minimising the sum of squared residuals (vertical distances of points from the line); the standard linear model.
  • Stepwise regression: predictors enter or leave automatically by a statistical criterion (the data decide the order). Atheoretical and capitalises on chance, so it is criticised.
  • Hierarchical regression: the researcher enters predictors in theory-driven blocks in a predetermined order, examining the change in R2 at each step. This is the preferred, theory-led approach.
  • Adjusted R2: R2 penalised for the number of predictors and sample size, because raw R2 always rises when predictors are added even if useless. Always report adjusted R2 in multiple regression.

Logistic regression

When the outcome is dichotomous (diagnosed vs not, relapsed vs not), OLS is inappropriate (it can predict impossible probabilities); you use logistic regression, which models the log-odds (logit) of the outcome as a linear function of predictors. Key quantities:

  • Odds Ratio (OR) = ebeta: exponentiating a coefficient converts log-odds to an odds ratio. OR > 1 means the predictor raises the odds of the outcome; OR < 1 lowers them; OR = 1 means no effect.
  • Wald statistic: tests whether an individual predictor's coefficient differs significantly from zero (the logistic analogue of the t-test on a regression coefficient).
  • Hosmer-Lemeshow test: the goodness-of-fit test for logistic regression; it compares observed and predicted frequencies across groups. Here a non-significant result is good (it means the model fits well), the reverse of the usual logic.
Exam trap

For the Hosmer-Lemeshow test you WANT a non-significant p-value (good fit). A significant Hosmer-Lemeshow means the model does NOT fit the data. This inverted interpretation is a favourite trick. Also remember OR = ebeta, not beta itself: the raw coefficient is in log-odds units.

P. Factor analytic modelling

Factor analysis reduces many observed variables to a few underlying dimensions (factors). The two foundational distinctions are extraction method and rotation type.

PCA vs Principal Axis Factoring

Definition

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a data reduction technique: it analyses total variance (including unique and error variance) to produce components that summarise the variables as efficiently as possible. Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) is true factor analysis: it analyses only the shared (common) variance to uncover the latent constructs presumed to cause the correlations.

Use PCA when the goal is pure dimension reduction or computing composite scores; use PAF (or maximum likelihood) when the goal is to model latent constructs (the usual clinical aim). PCA puts 1.0 on the diagonal of the correlation matrix (all variance); PAF puts the communalities there (shared variance only).

Factor extraction criteria (how many factors to keep)

  • Kaiser criterion: retain factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 (an eigenvalue is the variance a factor explains; below 1 it explains less than a single variable). Simple but tends to over-extract.
  • Scree plot: plot eigenvalues in descending order and find the "elbow" where the curve levels off; keep the factors above the bend (Cattell's scree test). Subjective but widely used.
  • Horn's parallel analysis: the most accurate modern method: compare your eigenvalues to those from random data of the same size, and retain only factors whose eigenvalues exceed the random ones. It corrects the over-extraction of the Kaiser rule.
Exam trap

The Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1) tends to OVER-extract factors, and the scree test is subjective; Horn's parallel analysis is the recommended, most accurate criterion. A question asking "the most accurate method to determine the number of factors" wants parallel analysis, not Kaiser.

Rotation: orthogonal vs oblique

Rotation makes the factor solution interpretable by maximising high loadings and minimising low ones (simple structure). The fundamental choice is whether factors may correlate:

  • Orthogonal rotation forces the factors to remain uncorrelated (a 90-degree, independent reference frame). Methods: Varimax (the most common; simplifies columns/factors) and Quartimax (simplifies rows/variables). Use when factors are assumed independent.
  • Oblique rotation allows the factors to correlate, reflecting reality (psychological constructs usually overlap, e.g. anxiety and depression). Methods: Promax and Direct Oblimin. Oblique rotations also yield a factor-correlation matrix and separate pattern and structure matrices.
High-yield

Orthogonal (Varimax, Quartimax) = uncorrelated factors, 90 degrees; Oblique (Promax, Direct Oblimin) = correlated factors. For real clinical constructs, which almost always intercorrelate, oblique rotation is usually more appropriate and is the defensible default. Varimax is the single most-named rotation in MCQs.

Loadings, cross-loadings, and communality

A factor loading is the correlation between a variable and a factor; a loading above .32 is the conventional minimum for an item to be considered meaningfully related to a factor (.32 corresponds to about 10% shared variance). A cross-loading occurs when a variable loads above the cut-off on two or more factors, making it ambiguous: such items are usually dropped or revised. The communality (h2) of a variable is the proportion of its variance explained by all the retained factors combined (the sum of its squared loadings across factors); a low communality (say below 0.30) means the factors capture little of that variable, and it is a candidate for removal.

Memory aid

Loading > .32 to keep an item; communality h2 = shared variance an item gives to the factors; cross-loading = an item that cheats on two factors (drop it). Eigenvalue is per-factor variance (keep if > 1); communality is per-variable variance.

High-yield

The "when assumptions fail, what do you switch to" logic is the backbone of this whole section, and the exam tests it constantly. Lock in the chain: variances unequal → Welch's t; normality fails → non-parametric (Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman); sphericity fails → Greenhouse-Geisser / Huynh-Feldt; small expected cell counts in 2×2 → Fisher's exact (or Yates' correction); paired nominal → McNemar; slopes not homogeneous → ANCOVA invalid; high VIF → multicollinearity, drop or combine predictors.

3 · Psychopathology & Clinical Syndromes

This is the highest-yield section in the entire entrance, because clinical psychology papers test it the way an anatomy paper tests bones: by the exact number. A disorder is not a vague mood but an operationalised object with a symptom count, a duration gate, and a near-neighbour it must be told apart from. The single most-tested fact type here is the duration threshold, the precise window of time a presentation must occupy to earn its name, because two disorders can share every symptom and differ only in how long they last. Brief psychotic disorder and schizophrenia look identical in a snapshot; only the clock separates them. This section teaches each disorder from its mechanism up, gives you the exact criteria and durations verbatim in tables you can memorise, and for every entry states the differential, the trick of telling it from the condition it is most often confused with, because that confusion is exactly what the distractor options are built from. We begin with how the two classification systems are architected and what causal models underpin them, then march through the full spectrum disorder by disorder.

A. Diagnostic Frameworks & Comparative Systems

Two nosologies dominate. The American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR, 2022) and the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11), whose mental-and-behavioural-disorders chapter came into force in January 2022. Both are descriptive: they group disorders by observable clinical features rather than by presumed cause, a deliberately atheoretical stance inherited from DSM-III (1980), which broke from the earlier psychoanalytic, aetiology-driven framework. DSM is built for a primarily American clinical and research audience and is famously operationalised: explicit symptom counts and time frames. ICD is a global public-health instrument used for mortality and morbidity statistics across all member states; its Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR) read as flexible prototypes rather than rigid checklists, because they must function across radically different health systems.

Definition

A categorical classification treats a disorder as a discrete kind: a person either meets criteria or does not (DSM's dominant logic). A dimensional classification rates a problem along a continuum of severity or trait level, accepting that pathology shades gradually into normality. The standing critique of pure categorical systems is that they draw artificial boundaries, generate excessive comorbidity (a patient piling up four labels for one underlying problem), and dump residual cases into vague "not otherwise specified" bins.

The three structural differences you must know

The exam loves the points where the two systems diverge in architecture, not just in wording. Three divergences are tested repeatedly.

1. Categorical versus dimensional spine. DSM-5-TR remains predominantly categorical, bolting dimensional elements on (severity specifiers, the Section III alternative model). ICD-11 keeps categories for most disorders but turns strongly dimensional precisely where DSM does not: personality.

2. Acute stress disorder moved out of anxiety. In older frameworks acute and post-traumatic stress reactions sat near the anxiety disorders. ICD-11 carved out a dedicated grouping, Disorders specifically associated with stress, and relocated the trauma reactions there, alongside its new prolonged grief disorder and a reworked adjustment disorder. DSM-5 had already created a separate Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders chapter, so both systems now treat trauma reactions as their own block rather than as a subtype of anxiety. The high-yield phrasing is that ICD-11 deleted acute stress disorder from the anxiety grouping and placed it in a trauma-specific block; ICD-11 also classifies acute stress reaction itself as a normal reaction (a Z-code factor influencing health, not a mental disorder), which is a favourite trick.

3. Abolition of categorical personality disorders. This is the headline difference. The traditional model in both systems was a fixed list of named personality types. DSM-5 kept its ten categorical personality disorders in the main text but parked an Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) in Section III, rating level of personality functioning plus five pathological trait domains. ICD-11 went all the way and abolished the categorical types entirely. It first assigns a global severity (personality difficulty below threshold, then mild, moderate, or severe) and then optionally adds up to five trait domain qualifiers: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, and anankastia, plus an optional borderline pattern qualifier retained for treatment continuity.

Etiological paradigms

Beyond classification, the paper tests the major causal frameworks, because every therapy chapter and every formulation question rests on them. Learn the five paradigms below as a set, because comparison items ("which model explains symptoms by conditional assumptions") are common.

Molecular genetics and behaviour genetics. Heritability is estimated chiefly from twin studies: comparing concordance in monozygotic (identical, sharing ~100% of genes) versus dizygotic (fraternal, ~50%) twins. The heritability coefficient, written as h squared, is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population attributable to genetic variance; for schizophrenia twin-based heritability is roughly 0.8, for bipolar disorder roughly 0.7 to 0.8, both among the most heritable disorders. Linkage analysis tracks whether a disorder co-segregates within families with known genetic markers of identified chromosomal location, while genome-wide association studies (GWAS) scan the whole genome for common variants. The takeaway tested most is that severe disorders (schizophrenia, bipolar, autism) are highly heritable and polygenic: many genes of small effect, not one gene.

Neurofunctional circuitry. This paradigm locates pathology in altered brain circuits and neurotransmitter systems: the dopamine hypothesis of psychosis (mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia driving positive symptoms, mesocortical hypodopaminergia driving negative symptoms), serotonergic and noradrenergic dysregulation in mood and anxiety, and structural or functional changes such as enlarged ventricles in schizophrenia or amygdala hyper-reactivity in anxiety and PTSD.

Psychodynamic structural and object-relational models. The structural model (Freud) explains symptoms as the surface compromise of intrapsychic conflict among id, ego, and superego, managed by defence mechanisms; anxiety signals a threatened breakthrough of forbidden impulses. Object-relations theory (Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, Kernberg) shifts the unit of analysis from drives to internalised relationships: pathology reflects deficient or distorted internal representations of self and others laid down in early caregiving, so a borderline patient is understood through unintegrated "all-good" and "all-bad" object images (the root of splitting).

Cognitive-behavioural vulnerabilities. Behaviourism explains pathology through learning: classical conditioning (a neutral cue paired with fear), operant conditioning (avoidance negatively reinforced because it relieves anxiety), and modelling. The cognitive layer (Beck, Ellis) adds enduring schemas, deep core beliefs about self, world, and future, and the conditional (operational) assumptions built on them ("if I am not perfect, I am worthless"), which, when activated by a stressor, generate the automatic negative thoughts that drive symptoms.

Diathesis-stress integration. The unifying model. A diathesis is a pre-existing vulnerability (genetic, neurobiological, or cognitive) that lies dormant until a sufficient environmental stress triggers it. Neither alone suffices: a high diathesis needs only mild stress to decompensate, a low diathesis needs severe stress. The closely related biopsychosocial model generalises this to the routine interplay of biological, psychological, and social contributors.

Memory aid

Map each paradigm to its signature noun: genetics = heritability, neuro = circuit, psychodynamic = conflict / object, cognitive = schema / assumption, integrative = diathesis + stress. Match the noun in the stem to the paradigm.

B. Schizophrenia Spectrum & Psychotic Disorders

Psychosis is a loss of contact with reality, expressed through delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking and speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, and negative symptoms. Schizophrenia is the prototype. DSM-5 requires at least two of five Criterion A symptoms present for a significant portion of one month (the active phase), at least one being delusions, hallucinations, or disorganised speech; continuous signs of disturbance must persist for at least six months total. The five Criterion A symptoms are delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, and negative symptoms. Brief psychotic disorder and schizophreniform disorder

Brief psychotic disorder is the abrupt onset of one or more positive psychotic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, or grossly disorganised/catatonic behaviour) lasting at least one day but less than one month, after which the person returns fully to premorbid functioning. It is frequently precipitated by a marked stressor (the "with marked stressor" specifier, historically brief reactive psychosis) and may occur with peripartum onset. Schizophreniform disorder is, symptomatically, schizophrenia caught early: the identical Criterion A picture but with a total duration of one to six months. Crucially, it does not require the decline in social or occupational functioning that schizophrenia mandates. If symptoms persist past six months the diagnosis converts to schizophrenia; if they remit before one month the label was brief psychotic disorder.

Exam trap

The schizophreniform window is reckoned on total duration including prodrome and residual, not just the one-month active phase. And it is the one psychotic-spectrum diagnosis that does not require functional decline. Distractors swap these in.

Schneiderian first-rank symptoms

Kurt Schneider (1959) listed symptoms he thought, when present without organic cause, strongly suggested schizophrenia. They are heavily tested by name. The classic eleven, grouped: (1) audible thoughts (thought echo, hearing one's thoughts aloud); (2) voices arguing or discussing the patient in the third person; (3) voices commenting on one's actions (running commentary); (4) thought withdrawal (thoughts removed by an outside force); (5) thought insertion (alien thoughts placed in the mind); (6) thought broadcasting (thoughts diffuse out to others); (7) made (passivity) feelings; (8) made impulses; (9) made volitional acts; (10) somatic passivity (bodily sensations imposed by others); and (11) delusional perception (a normal percept given a private delusional meaning). Note that DSM-5 dropped the old special weighting that a single bizarre delusion or first-rank hallucination alone sufficed for diagnosis; two Criterion A symptoms are now always required.

High-yield

The "made" phenomena (made feelings, made impulses, made acts) plus somatic passivity are delusions of control / passivity: the sense that an external agent is driving one's body or mind. Thought insertion, withdrawal, and broadcasting are the thought-alienation triad. These are the most-asked Schneiderian items.

Positive versus negative symptoms: the five A's

Positive symptoms are pathological additions to normal experience: delusions (fixed false beliefs, e.g. persecutory, grandiose, referential), hallucinations (percepts without stimulus, auditory being most common in schizophrenia), disorganised speech (formal thought disorder: derailment, tangentiality, word salad), and grossly disorganised or bizarre behaviour. Negative symptoms are deficits, the loss of normal function, and they predict poorer prognosis and respond least to antipsychotics. Memorise them as the five A's: avolition (loss of goal-directed drive), alogia (poverty of speech), anhedonia (inability to feel pleasure), asociality (withdrawal from social contact), and affective flattening (blunted/diminished emotional expression).

Memory aid

The five negative symptoms are the 5 A's: Avolition, Alogia, Anhedonia, Asociality, Affective flattening. Positive symptoms ADD to experience; negative symptoms SUBTRACT from it.

Catatonia: 3 of 12 signs

Catatonia is a syndrome of marked psychomotor disturbance that can accompany schizophrenia, mood disorders, or medical conditions. DSM-5 requires at least 3 of 12 signs. Learn all twelve, because the paper asks both the threshold number and the full list: (1) stupor (no psychomotor activity, not actively relating to environment); (2) catalepsy (passive induction of a posture held against gravity); (3) waxy flexibility (slight, even resistance to repositioning, like bending a candle); (4) mutism (no, or almost no, verbal response); (5) negativism (opposition to or no response to instructions); (6) posturing (spontaneous maintenance of a posture against gravity); (7) mannerisms (odd caricatures of normal actions); (8) stereotypies (repetitive, non-goal-directed movements); (9) psychomotor agitation (excessive, purposeless motor activity); (10) grimacing; (11) echolalia (mimicking another's speech); and (12) echopraxia (mimicking another's movements).

High-yield

Catatonia = 3 of 12 signs. Distinguish catalepsy (posture passively induced and held) from waxy flexibility (the even, candle-like resistance felt while repositioning) and from cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion, which belongs to narcolepsy, not catatonia). The catalepsy / cataplexy swap is a classic distractor.

Schizoaffective disorder and delusional disorder

Schizoaffective disorder requires an uninterrupted period during which a major mood episode (depressive or manic) is concurrent with Criterion A schizophrenia symptoms, PLUS delusions or hallucinations for at least two weeks in the absence of a prominent mood episode at some point in the lifetime, AND mood-episode symptoms present for the majority of the total illness duration. The two-week psychosis-without-mood window is the linchpin that separates it from a mood disorder with psychotic features.

Delusional disorder requires one or more delusions for at least one month, with Criterion A schizophrenia otherwise never met, no prominent hallucinations, and functioning not markedly impaired apart from the delusion's direct impact; behaviour is not obviously bizarre. Subtypes name the delusion's theme: erotomanic, grandiose, jealous, persecutory, somatic, mixed.

Exam trap

Schizoaffective vs. mood disorder with psychotic features: if psychosis occurs only during mood episodes, it is a mood disorder with psychotic features; if there is a period of at least two weeks of psychosis with the mood episode absent, it is schizoaffective. That two-week window is the entire differential.

C. Bipolar & Related Disorders

The bipolar disorders are defined by the presence of elevated-mood episodes (manic or hypomanic) on a background that usually also includes depression. The decisive distinctions are the duration and severity of the high.

Definition

A manic episode is a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy, lasting at least 7 consecutive days (or any duration if hospitalisation is required), with at least 3 of the DIG FAST symptoms (4 if mood is only irritable) and marked impairment, psychosis, or hospitalisation. A hypomanic episode has the same symptom set but lasts at least 4 consecutive days, is observable by others, and causes a clear change in functioning without marked impairment, psychosis, or hospitalisation.

DisorderDefining requirement (verbatim)
Bipolar I disorderAt least 1 manic episode (at least 7 days, or any duration if hospitalised); severe socio-occupational impairment. Depressive episodes usual but not required for diagnosis.
Bipolar II disorderAt least 1 hypomanic episode (at least 4 days, clear functional change, no gross impairment or hospitalisation) AND at least 1 major depressive episode. Never a full manic episode.
Cyclothymic disorderAt least 2 years in adults (1 year in children/adolescents) of numerous hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria; symptom-free intervals cannot exceed 2 months.
Rapid cycling (specifier)At least 4 distinct major mood episodes within a 12-month period.

Bipolar I is the only one requiring a full manic episode; a single lifetime manic episode suffices for the diagnosis even with no depression ever. Bipolar II requires both a hypomanic episode and a major depressive episode, and the presence of any full manic episode immediately reclassifies it as Bipolar I. It is a common error to treat Bipolar II as "milder"; its depressive burden is often heavier and more chronic. Cyclothymia is the persistent, sub-threshold chatter of highs and lows, never reaching full episode intensity, over the required two-year span. Rapid cycling is a specifier, not a separate disorder, applied when four or more episodes occur in twelve months.

Memory aid

Mania symptoms = DIG FAST: Distractibility, Insomnia (decreased need for sleep), Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Speech (pressured), Thoughtlessness (risk-taking, e.g. spending, sex). Mania needs 3 of these (4 if mood is only irritable).

Exam trap

The mania duration is 7 days; hypomania is 4 days. The decisive line between them is not just length but impairment: hospitalisation, psychosis, or marked dysfunction automatically makes it mania regardless of duration. A 3-day high that lands the patient in hospital is a manic episode.

D. Depressive Disorders

Definition

Major depressive disorder (MDD) requires at least 5 of 9 symptoms during the same 2-week period, and at least one must be either depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure (anhedonia). The nine: depressed mood, anhedonia, significant weight/appetite change, insomnia or hypersomnia, psychomotor agitation or retardation, fatigue, worthlessness or guilt, impaired concentration, recurrent thoughts of death/suicidality.

DisorderCriteria and duration (verbatim)
Major depressive disorderAt least 5 of 9 symptoms in the same 2-week block, including depressed mood or anhedonia.
Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)Depressed mood for at least 2 years in adults (1 year in children/adolescents); sub-threshold; no symptom-free gap longer than 2 months.
Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD)Severe recurrent temper outbursts (verbal/behavioural) out of proportion, at least 3 times per week, across at least 12 months, with onset before age 10 (diagnosed ages 6 to 18).

Persistent depressive disorder (the DSM-5 merger of chronic MDD and the older dysthymia) is the long, low-grade depression: at least two years (one in youth) of depressed mood plus two of six features (poor appetite or overeating, insomnia or hypersomnia, low energy, low self-esteem, poor concentration, hopelessness), with no break exceeding two months. A patient can carry both diagnoses simultaneously (so-called double depression: a major depressive episode superimposed on dysthymia). DMDD was introduced to curb the over-diagnosis of paediatric bipolar disorder; its core is a persistently irritable or angry mood between the outbursts, distinguishing it from the episodic mood elevation of bipolar disorder.

Specifier mechanics

Specifiers refine an episode and are reliably tested. Melancholic features: profound despondency with loss of pleasure in nearly everything, mood non-reactive (does not lift even briefly to good news), distinct quality of depressed mood, worse in the morning, terminal/early-morning insomnia, marked psychomotor change, anorexia/weight loss, and excessive guilt. Atypical features: the mirror image, mood reactivity preserved (brightens to positives), plus at least two of hyperphagia (increased appetite/weight), hypersomnia, leaden paralysis (heavy, leaden limbs), and a long-standing pattern of interpersonal rejection sensitivity. Peripartum onset: onset of the episode during pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum.

Exam trap

The single cleanest melancholic-versus-atypical discriminator is mood reactivity. Melancholic mood is non-reactive (nothing lifts it) with early-morning waking and weight loss; atypical mood is reactive with oversleeping and overeating. The "leaden paralysis" and rejection sensitivity belong to atypical.

E. Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders share excessive fear (response to real or perceived imminent threat) and anxiety (anticipation of future threat) with associated behavioural disturbance, and are separated largely by the object of the fear and the duration.

DisorderCriteria and duration (verbatim)
Panic disorderRecurrent unexpected panic attacks followed by at least 1 month of persistent worry about future attacks or maladaptive behavioural change.
Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)Excessive, uncontrollable worry about multiple domains for at least 6 months, with at least 3 of 6 somatic/arousal signs in adults (1 in children).
Specific phobiaMarked fear of a specific object/situation, lasting at least 6 months, driving avoidance.
Social anxiety disorderMarked fear of social/performance scrutiny, lasting at least 6 months.
AgoraphobiaMarked fear of at least 2 of 5 situations (public transport, open spaces, enclosed places, queues/crowds, being outside the home alone), lasting at least 6 months.
Separation anxiety disorderDevelopmentally inappropriate fear of separation: at least 4 weeks in children/adolescents, at least 6 months in adults.

A panic attack is an abrupt surge of intense fear peaking within minutes, with at least 4 of 13 somatic/cognitive symptoms (palpitations, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, choking, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, chills/heat, paraesthesias, derealisation, fear of losing control, fear of dying). The attack itself is not a disorder; panic disorder needs the recurrent unexpected attacks plus the one-month anticipatory worry/avoidance. GAD's six somatic signs are restlessness, easy fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance; three of six are needed in adults. Agoraphobia's "2 of 5 situations" and GAD's "3 somatic signs over 6 months" are favourite number facts.

Exam trap

Specific phobia vs. agoraphobia vs. social anxiety: all three run at least 6 months. Specific phobia fears a discrete object; social anxiety fears scrutiny/embarrassment; agoraphobia fears situations from which escape is difficult or help unavailable if panic strikes (needs 2 of 5 listed). Match the feared cue, not just the avoidance.

Memory aid

Separation anxiety durations: think "children are quick, adults are slow," 4 weeks for children, 6 months for adults. The longer adult requirement guards against over-diagnosing normal adult attachment worry.

F. Obsessive-Compulsive & Related Disorders

DSM-5 grouped these as a chapter separate from anxiety because they share repetitive, ritualised behaviour and overlapping circuitry (cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loops).

Definition

Obsessions are recurrent, intrusive, unwanted, ego-dystonic thoughts, urges, or images that cause marked anxiety. Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts the person feels driven to perform to neutralise the anxiety or according to rigid rules, not realistically connected to what they aim to prevent. OCD requires obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming (more than 1 hour/day) or distressing.

OCD carries insight specifiers: good or fair insight (recognises beliefs are probably/possibly untrue), poor insight (thinks they are probably true), and absent insight / delusional beliefs (completely convinced they are true), plus a tic-related specifier. The other disorders in the chapter:

  • Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD): preoccupation with one or more perceived defects in appearance that are unnoticeable or slight to others, with repetitive behaviours (mirror checking, grooming, reassurance seeking) or mental acts (comparing). The muscle dysmorphia specifier covers the belief that one's build is too small/insufficiently muscular.
  • Hoarding disorder: persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value, driven by a perceived need to save and distress at discarding, producing clutter that congests active living spaces and compromises their use.
  • Trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder): recurrent pulling out of one's hair causing hair loss, with repeated attempts to stop.
  • Excoriation (skin-picking) disorder: recurrent skin picking causing lesions, with repeated attempts to stop.

PANDAS (Paediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal infections) and the broader PITAND/PANS spectrum describe an abrupt, dramatic onset of OCD symptoms and/or tics in children following streptococcal (PANDAS) or other infectious or immune triggers, hypothesised to arise from autoantibodies cross-reacting with basal-ganglia neurons. They are the autoimmune etiological network of the OCD chapter.

Exam trap

Obsessions are ego-dystonic (experienced as alien, intrusive, distressing). This contrasts with the ego-syntonic traits of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD), where the perfectionism feels right to the person. OCD is anxiety-driven ritual; OCPD is a rigid personality style. Do not confuse them.

G. Trauma- & Stressor-Related Disorders

These all require a defined stressor as the precipitant, which is what sets the chapter apart.

Definition

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) follows exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence (directly, witnessed, learned of in a close other, or repeated occupational exposure). Symptoms fall into 4 clusters and must persist for more than 1 month.

The four PTSD symptom clusters, in order: (1) Intrusion, the re-experiencing cluster: intrusive memories, nightmares, flashbacks, psychological and physiological distress at reminders; (2) Avoidance of trauma-related thoughts, feelings, or external reminders; (3) Negative alterations in cognition and mood: amnesia for the event, persistent negative beliefs, distorted blame, persistent negative emotional state, anhedonia, detachment, inability to feel positive emotion; (4) Alterations in arousal and reactivity: irritability/aggression, recklessness, hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, concentration problems, sleep disturbance.

DisorderOnset / duration (verbatim)
Acute stress disorderSymptoms lasting from 3 days to 1 month after trauma exposure.
PTSDSymptoms (4 clusters) persisting for more than 1 month.
Adjustment disorderSymptoms emerge within 3 months of a stressor and resolve within 6 months once the stressor or its consequences end.

Acute stress disorder and PTSD share the symptom universe; the distinction is purely temporal. Within the first month it is acute stress disorder; if the disturbance persists beyond one month it becomes PTSD. (Symptoms can also have delayed expression, with full criteria met only six months or more after the event.) Reactive attachment disorder (RAD) and disinhibited social engagement disorder (DSED) both arise from grossly insufficient early caregiving (neglect, repeated changes of caregiver, institutional rearing), but diverge in direction: RAD is the internalising pathway, a child emotionally withdrawn and inhibited who rarely seeks or responds to comfort; DSED is the externalising pathway, a child overly familiar and willing to go off with relative strangers, showing no normal stranger wariness. Adjustment disorder is the residual stressor-linked diagnosis: clinically significant emotional or behavioural symptoms out of proportion to an identifiable, ordinary stressor (not necessarily traumatic), beginning within three months and remitting within six months of the stressor ceasing.

Exam trap

RAD vs. DSED: RAD = withdrawn, inhibited, does not seek comfort (internalising). DSED = disinhibited, over-friendly, approaches strangers (externalising). Same etiology (pathogenic care), opposite social behaviour. The "child who wanders off with strangers" is DSED.

H. Dissociative & Somatic Symptom Disorders

Dissociative disorders involve a disruption of the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, or body representation. Somatic symptom and related disorders involve prominent physical symptoms or health concerns producing distress and impairment.

DisorderCore criterion (verbatim where dated)
Dissociative identity disorder (DID)Disruption of identity with at least 2 distinct personality states plus recurrent gaps in recall (amnesia) for everyday events, personal information, or trauma.
Dissociative amnesiaInability to recall important autobiographical (usually traumatic/stressful) information; localised, selective, or generalised; specifier with dissociative fugue (purposeful travel or bewildered wandering with amnesia).
Depersonalisation/derealisation disorderPersistent or recurrent unreality/detachment from one's own mind/body (depersonalisation) or surroundings (derealisation), with intact reality testing.
Somatic symptom disorderAt least 1 distressing somatic symptom PLUS disproportionate, persistent thoughts/feelings/behaviours about it, lasting at least 6 months.
Illness anxiety disorderPreoccupation with having/acquiring a serious illness; somatic symptoms absent or mild; high health anxiety for at least 6 months.
Conversion disorder (FND)Neurological symptom (motor/sensory) showing clinical incompatibility with recognised neurological/medical disease.

DID (historically multiple personality disorder) hinges on the coexistence of two or more identity states with the marked discontinuity in sense of self and agency, accompanied by amnesia; it is strongly associated with severe, repeated childhood trauma. Dissociative amnesia is memory loss too extensive for ordinary forgetting; its fugue specifier adds purposeful travel or confused wandering. Depersonalisation/derealisation disorder is defined by the preserved insight: the person knows the unreality is a subjective experience, which is what keeps it out of the psychotic spectrum.

On the somatic side, the key DSM-5 change was a shift from "medically unexplained symptoms" to positive psychological criteria. Somatic symptom disorder needs real distressing somatic symptoms plus excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviour about them for six months. Illness anxiety disorder is the worry without (or with only minimal) somatic symptoms: the focus is on the fear of being or becoming ill (formerly hypochondriasis). Conversion disorder / functional neurological symptom disorder rests on demonstrated incompatibility between the presentation and known neurology (e.g. Hoover's sign, tremor entrainment), not merely on the absence of a found cause.

Factitious disorder versus malingering

Factitious disorder is the deliberate falsification or induction of physical or psychological signs of illness in oneself (or another, factitious disorder imposed on another) with deceptive intent in the absence of obvious external rewards; the motive is the internal psychological gain of occupying the sick role. Malingering is the conscious fabrication or exaggeration of symptoms for explicit external (secondary) gain: avoiding work, evading legal consequences, obtaining money, drugs, or disability benefits. Malingering is not a mental disorder at all but a V/Z-code condition that may be a focus of clinical attention.

High-yield

The single discriminator between factitious disorder and malingering is the presence of external (secondary) gain. Malingering = conscious deception for an external reward (money, legal, drugs). Factitious = conscious deception with no external reward, only the internal need to be sick. Conversion disorder differs from both: the symptom is not consciously produced.

I. Feeding, Eating & Sleep-Wake Disorders

DisorderCriteria and duration (verbatim)
Anorexia nervosaRestriction of intake leading to significantly low body weight; intense fear of gaining weight; distorted body image. Subtypes: restricting vs. binge-eating/purging.
Bulimia nervosaRecurrent binge eating PLUS inappropriate compensatory behaviours (purging, laxatives, fasting, excessive exercise), at least once a week for 3 months.
Binge-eating disorderRecurrent binges with distress, at least once a week for 3 months, in the absence of compensatory behaviours.
Insomnia disorderDissatisfaction with sleep quantity/quality, difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, at least 3 nights per week for 3 months.
NarcolepsyRecurrent irrepressible need to sleep / lapses into sleep, at least 3 times per week over 3 months, with cataplexy and/or hypocretin (orexin) deficiency.

Anorexia nervosa is defined by the maintained low weight plus the intense fear of fatness and disturbed body image; the two subtypes are restricting (weight loss through dieting/fasting/exercise) and binge-eating/purging. Bulimia nervosa patients are typically normal or above normal weight and cycle binge then compensate; the once-a-week-for-three-months frequency is the key number. Binge-eating disorder shares the binges but has no compensatory behaviour, distinguishing it from bulimia. Narcolepsy couples the sleep attacks with cataplexy (sudden bilateral loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotion, often laughter) and a deficiency of the hypothalamic neuropeptide hypocretin/orexin; sleep-onset REM and hypnagogic hallucinations are associated features.

Exam trap

Anorexia binge-purge subtype vs. bulimia: both binge and purge. The decider is body weight: if the patient is at significantly low weight it is anorexia (binge-eating/purging type); if at normal or higher weight it is bulimia. Weight, not behaviour, separates them. And binge-eating disorder vs. bulimia: the absence of compensatory behaviour marks binge-eating disorder.

J. Personality Disorders (by Cluster)

A personality disorder is an enduring, pervasive, inflexible pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from cultural expectation, is stable over time, traces back to adolescence or early adulthood, and causes distress or impairment. DSM-5 retains the categorical ten, grouped in three clusters.

Cluster A shades toward the psychotic spectrum (schizotypal is genetically linked to schizophrenia). The differential within it: paranoid disorder is suspicion with social engagement; schizoid is genuine indifference to relationships without the cognitive distortions; schizotypal adds the odd beliefs and perceptual aberrations that schizoid lacks. Exam trap

Avoidant vs. schizoid: both are socially withdrawn. The avoidant person desires relationships but avoids them out of fear of rejection (Cluster C); the schizoid person is genuinely indifferent to relationships (Cluster A). Desire-but-afraid = avoidant; doesn't-care = schizoid. Also remember OCPD is ego-syntonic and has no true obsessions/compulsions, unlike OCD.

High-yield

Antisocial personality disorder: conduct disorder before age 15, pattern since age 15, diagnosed only at age 18 or older. Splitting is the hallmark defence of borderline personality disorder. These two facts are near-certain to be tested.

K. Neurodevelopmental & Childhood Disorders

These onset in the developmental period and are defined by deviation from expected developmental milestones.

Intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder) requires both (a) deficits in intellectual functions (reasoning, problem-solving, abstract thinking) confirmed by clinical assessment and individualised standardised intelligence testing, and (b) deficits in adaptive functioning causing failure to meet developmental and socio-cultural standards for independence across the three domains: conceptual, social, and practical. Severity (mild, moderate, severe, profound) is set by adaptive functioning, not by IQ score alone, a deliberate DSM-5 shift away from IQ-band cut-offs.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) requires both (a) persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts (social-emotional reciprocity, non-verbal communication, developing/maintaining relationships), and (b) restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities (at least 2 of: stereotyped movements/speech, insistence on sameness/rituals, fixated intense interests, sensory hyper- or hypo-reactivity), with onset in the early developmental period. DSM-5 collapsed the older separate labels (autistic disorder, Asperger's, PDD-NOS) into a single spectrum with severity levels 1 to 3 by support needed.

Definition

ADHD requires a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning, with at least 6 symptoms (5 if age 17 or older) from a domain, lasting at least 6 months, with several symptoms present before age 12, across at least 2 settings (e.g. home and school).

DisorderCriteria and duration (verbatim)
ADHDAt least 6 symptoms (inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity) for at least 6 months, several before age 12, across at least 2 settings.
Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD)Angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behaviour, or vindictiveness, lasting at least 6 months.
Conduct disorder (CD)Repetitive violation of others' rights or major norms across 4 clusters, with at least 3 symptoms in the past 12 months.

The disruptive disorders form a developmental gradient. ODD is the milder, earlier pattern of angry/defiant behaviour directed mainly at authority figures, without the serious rights-violations of conduct disorder. Conduct disorder is graver: a repetitive pattern violating the basic rights of others or major age-appropriate norms across four symptom clusters, namely (1) aggression to people and animals, (2) destruction of property, (3) deceitfulness or theft, and (4) serious violations of rules, with at least three symptoms in the past twelve months. Conduct disorder before age 15 is the developmental precursor required for the later diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder, tying the childhood and personality chapters together.

Exam trap

ODD vs. conduct disorder: ODD is defiance, anger, and argumentativeness without serious aggression, property destruction, or rule violations; conduct disorder crosses into violating others' rights across its four clusters. The "child who is argumentative and loses his temper" is ODD; the "child who is cruel to animals, sets fires, and steals" is conduct disorder.

High-yield

Pin the childhood-disorder numbers: ADHD = at least 6 symptoms, at least 6 months, onset before age 12, at least 2 settings. Conduct disorder = 4 clusters, at least 3 symptoms in 12 months. ODD = at least 6 months. Intellectual disability = three adaptive domains (conceptual, social, practical), severity set by adaptive function. DMDD (from the depressive chapter) = outbursts at least 3 times/week for at least 12 months, onset before age 10. These exact figures are the spine of the section.

4 · Psychological Assessment & Psychodiagnostics

Psychological testing is the operational backbone of clinical psychology: it converts vague referral questions ("is this child gifted?", "is this an organic dementia or a depressive pseudodementia?", "is this patient psychotic or malingering?") into reproducible numbers and structured behaviour samples. This section teaches the two great families that dominate the M.Phil paper. First, the intellectual, cognitive and neuropsychological batteries: instruments that quantify general mental ability and then localise deficits to brain systems. Second, the objective and projective personality typologies: structured self-report inventories with built-in lie-detectors, set against the open-ended projective methods that read personality off ambiguous stimuli. For every test you must hold four things in your head at once: what it measures, how it is structured, how it is scored and interpreted, and the named author or theory behind it. The examiner's favourite distractors are exactly the places where two of these slip out of alignment (confusing an index with the full-scale score, attributing Exner's system to Rorschach himself, or naming the wrong validity scale as the malingering detector). Read each test below until you can reproduce its architecture from memory.

A. The Wechsler Intelligence Matrix (WAIS-IV / WISC-V)

David Wechsler built the dominant family of individually administered intelligence tests, defining intelligence as "the global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally and to deal effectively with his environment." His central departure from the original Stanford-Binet was to abandon the mental-age ratio IQ and adopt the deviation IQ: a person's score is expressed as a standard score with a population mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, so an IQ simply states how many standard deviations the examinee sits above or below same-age peers. The adult form is the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, fourth edition (WAIS-IV), for ages 16 to 90; the child form is the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, fifth edition (WISC-V), for ages 6 to 16. Earlier Wechsler scales reported a Verbal IQ and a Performance IQ; the modern editions have abandoned that two-IQ split in favour of a single Full Scale IQ (FSIQ) sitting on top of four (WAIS-IV) or five (WISC-V) index scores.

Definition

The Full Scale IQ (FSIQ) is the single best estimate of general intellectual ability (Spearman's g), derived from the core subtests across all index domains. Each subtest yields a scaled score (mean 10, SD 3); index scores and the FSIQ are composites rescaled to mean 100, SD 15.

In the Verbal Comprehension Index, Similarities asks how two concepts are alike (in what way are a dog and a lion alike?) and taps abstract verbal reasoning; Vocabulary asks for word definitions and is the single best correlate of overall FSIQ and the most resistant to decline (a classic "hold" test); Information samples general fund of acquired knowledge; the supplemental Comprehension probes practical reasoning and understanding of social conventions. In the Perceptual Reasoning Index, Block Design requires the examinee to reproduce a printed geometric pattern using red-and-white blocks under time pressure (it is exquisitely sensitive to right-parietal and constructional dysfunction); Matrix Reasoning presents an incomplete visual pattern matrix to be completed from options (a near-pure fluid-reasoning, g-loaded task); Visual Puzzles asks which three pieces combine to make a target shape (mental rotation and spatial analysis); supplemental Figure Weights uses balance-scale problems for quantitative-analogical reasoning, and Picture Completion asks what important part is missing from a picture. In the Working Memory Index, Digit Span has three parts (forwards, which is simple attention span; backwards, which adds mental manipulation; and sequencing, which requires reordering), Arithmetic presents mentally solved word problems under time pressure, and supplemental Letter-Number Sequencing requires reordering an intermixed string of letters and numbers. In the Processing Speed Index, Symbol Search requires scanning to decide whether target symbols appear in a search group, Coding requires writing the symbol paired with each digit using a key (sensitive to attention, motor speed and incidental learning), and supplemental Cancellation requires marking target shapes among distractors.

Memory aid

For the four indices use "V P W P" = Verbal, Perceptual, Working memory, Processing speed. For VCI core trio: "SVI" (Similarities, Vocabulary, Information). For PRI core trio: "BMV" (Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, Visual Puzzles). For PSI: Symbol Search and Coding are the two pencil-speed tasks.

WISC-V's five-factor structure

The WISC-V split the old Perceptual Reasoning Index into two separate indices, giving a five-factor model: Verbal Comprehension, Visual Spatial, Fluid Reasoning, Working Memory and Processing Speed. The Visual Spatial Index is anchored by Block Design and Visual Puzzles; the Fluid Reasoning Index is anchored by Matrix Reasoning and Figure Weights. This reflects modern Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory, which treats visual processing (Gv) and fluid reasoning (Gf) as distinct broad abilities rather than one "perceptual" lump.

General Ability Index versus Cognitive Proficiency Index

This is the highest-yield conceptual point in the whole Wechsler topic. Beyond the FSIQ, the WAIS-IV provides two ancillary composites that repartition the same subtests in a clinically meaningful way.

Definition

The General Ability Index (GAI) is computed from VCI + PRI only. It deliberately extracts the higher-order reasoning component (verbal and perceptual reasoning) while removing the contributions of working memory and processing speed. The Cognitive Proficiency Index (CPI) is computed from WMI + PSI only: it isolates the efficiency machinery (how quickly and reliably the brain manipulates and processes information).

The clinical logic: working memory and processing speed are the cognitive functions most vulnerable to attentional disorders, anxiety, depression, traumatic brain injury and ADHD. When a clinician suspects that low FSIQ is being dragged down by these efficiency deficits rather than by a true reasoning limitation, the GAI gives a "purer" estimate of intellectual capacity, while a low CPI relative to a high GAI flags exactly where the efficiency bottleneck lies. A large GAI-greater-than-CPI gap is a hallmark of conditions that spare reasoning but impair processing.

Exam trap

Do not confuse GAI with FSIQ. The FSIQ includes all four indices (VCI + PRI + WMI + PSI). The GAI deliberately drops WMI and PSI and equals VCI + PRI only. A distractor item will say "GAI = the full-scale score" or "GAI includes working memory": both are false. Also note GAI = VCI + PRI (reasoning) while CPI = WMI + PSI (efficiency); swapping which composite contains which pair is the second favourite trap.

High-yield

WAIS-IV and WISC-V: deviation IQ, mean 100, SD 15; subtest scaled scores mean 10, SD 3. Four core indices = VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI. GAI = VCI + PRI (capacity, minus efficiency constraints); CPI = WMI + PSI (efficiency). WISC-V splits PRI into Visual Spatial + Fluid Reasoning, giving five indices.

B. Kaufman Assessment Systems

Alan and Nadeen Kaufman built a family of tests explicitly grounded in a neuropsychological and information-processing theory rather than in Wechsler's atheoretical clinical tradition. The flagship is the Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (K-ABC, now KABC-II), for ages 3 to 18. Its theoretical spine is the Luria-Das distinction between sequential and simultaneous processing: sequential processing solves problems by arranging stimuli in serial, temporal order (analogous to Luria's successive processing, anchored in frontotemporal systems), whereas simultaneous processing integrates stimuli all at once into a spatial or gestalt whole (analogous to occipital-parietal systems). The KABC-II can be scored through either a Luria model (which excludes acquired knowledge, useful for children from diverse language backgrounds, yielding the Mental Processing Index) or a Cattell-Horn-Carroll model (which adds crystallised knowledge, yielding the Fluid-Crystallized Index). The deliberate separation of processing ability from acquired knowledge was the Kaufmans' signature contribution, designed to reduce cultural and educational bias. The adult counterpart is the Kaufman Adolescent and Adult Intelligence Test (KAIT), which formalises the fluid/crystallised split.

Memory aid

Kaufman = "processing, not just knowledge." Sequential = one-at-a-time-in-order (Luria successive); Simultaneous = all-at-once-as-a-whole (Luria simultaneous). The dual-model scoring (Luria vs CHC) is the Kaufman signature.

C. Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales, Fifth Edition (SB5)

The Stanford-Binet is the oldest standardised intelligence test in continuous use, descending from Binet and Simon's 1905 Paris scale, imported and renormed at Stanford by Lewis Terman (1916), and the original home of the ratio IQ (mental age divided by chronological age, times 100). The modern fifth edition (SB5, Roid, 2003) covers ages 2 to 85+ and, like the Wechsler, now uses a deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15). Its defining architecture is a five-factor model crossed with two domains, giving a 5 x 2 matrix of ten subtests. The five factors are:

  • Fluid Reasoning: novel problem solving, inductive and deductive reasoning not dependent on prior knowledge.
  • Knowledge: accumulated general information and crystallised learning (the SB5's analogue of verbal comprehension).
  • Quantitative Reasoning: facility with numbers, numerical concepts and quantitative problem solving.
  • Visual-Spatial Processing: ability to see patterns, relationships and spatial orientation among figures.
  • Working Memory: capacity to hold and transform information in immediate awareness.

The genuinely distinctive feature is that each of these five factors is measured in both a verbal domain and a non-verbal domain. So Fluid Reasoning is tested both with verbal analogies and with non-verbal matrices; Knowledge is tested both verbally and through picture absurdities; and so on. This yields a Verbal IQ, a Non-verbal IQ and a Full Scale IQ, plus the five factor indices. The non-verbal pathway makes the SB5 unusually suitable for examinees with limited language, hearing impairment, or English as a second language. It also has excellent floor and ceiling, making it a preferred instrument at the extremes (severe intellectual disability and the assessment of profound giftedness).

High-yield

SB5 five factors: Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing, Working Memory, each crossed with verbal and non-verbal domains (5 x 2 = 10 subtests). It is the home of the original ratio IQ (MA/CA x 100) but the modern edition uses deviation IQ. Strong floor and ceiling: the instrument of choice at the extremes of ability.

Memory aid

SB5 factors: "F-K-Q-V-W" = Fluid reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative reasoning, Visual-spatial, Working memory. Remember the 5 factors x 2 domains grid: that crossing of verbal and non-verbal is what distinguishes SB5 from the Wechsler.

D. Indian Adaptations of Performance Tests

Western verbal IQ tests are heavily culture-loaded and language-bound, so Indian clinical practice relies on performance (non-verbal, motor) batteries that were standardised on Indian populations. Two are examined constantly.

Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests

C. M. Bhatia (1955) assembled a battery of five performance sub-tests standardised on the Indian population, designed for the illiterate and the educationally disadvantaged because it minimises the role of formal schooling and language. The five sub-tests are:

  • Kohs' Block Design Test: reproduce printed coloured designs with cubes whose faces are coloured; taps non-verbal abstract reasoning and visuo-constructional ability (the same task lineage that became Wechsler's Block Design).
  • Alexander's Pass-Along Test: slide coloured blocks along channels in a frame to reproduce a pattern in the fewest moves; taps spatial planning and problem solving.
  • Pattern Drawing Test: copy increasingly complex patterns in a single continuous line without lifting the pencil or retracing; taps visuo-motor planning.
  • Immediate Memory Test: digits forward and digits backward (a digit-span analogue) measuring immediate auditory attention span.
  • Picture Construction Test: assemble cut-up pieces into a meaningful picture; taps perceptual organisation and synthesis.

Scores are summed and converted to an IQ via age-based norms. Bhatia's battery is the standard non-verbal IQ estimate for Indian adults and is widely used clinically when verbal testing is inappropriate.

Memory aid

Bhatia's five: "Kohs, Pass-along, Pattern, Memory, Picture" = K-P-P-M-P. Two are eponymous (Kohs' Block Design, Alexander's Pass-Along): the examiner loves to ask who authored which sub-test.

Seguin Form Board Test

The Seguin Form Board (Edouard Seguin) is a wooden board with ten recessed cut-outs of common geometric shapes; the examinee must place the matching blocks into their slots as fast as possible. It is primarily a test of eye-hand coordination, visuo-motor speed and form perception, used as a non-verbal screen for intellectual ability in children and in adults with limited language. Three variables are recorded across repeated trials:

  • Speed (time): typically the shortest of three trials is taken as the score; faster placement indicates better visuo-motor efficiency.
  • Accuracy: errors in placement and the smoothness of insertion.
  • Learning curve across trials: because the board is administered three times, the drop in completion time from trial 1 to trial 3 indexes the rate of motor learning and the benefit of practice; a flat or erratic curve (no improvement) can itself be clinically informative, suggesting poor learning or attentional fluctuation.
Exam trap

The Seguin Form Board is not a memory test and not a verbal test: its core variable is speed (best of three trials), with accuracy and the across-trial learning curve as the secondary measures. A distractor will frame it as measuring "vocabulary" or "auditory memory": wrong. It measures visuo-motor speed and form perception.

E. Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery (HRNB)

We now move from "how much general ability?" to "which brain systems are intact or damaged?" The Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery was built by Ward Halstead and extended by Ralph Reitan. It is a fixed (standardised) battery: every examinee receives the same comprehensive set of tests regardless of the referral question, which maximises comparability and yields an overall Halstead Impairment Index (the proportion of tests falling in the brain-damaged range; an index of 0.5 or more suggests impairment). High-yield

HRNB is a fixed battery yielding the Halstead Impairment Index. Know the five core subtests and what each taps: Category Test (abstract reasoning, most sensitive to brain damage), Tactual Performance Test (tactile spatial memory and localisation), Seashore Rhythm (auditory attention), Speech Sounds Perception (auditory-verbal perception), Finger Tapping (motor speed, hand comparison for lateralisation).

F. Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery (LNNB)

The Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery (Charles Golden and colleagues) standardised and quantified the qualitative clinical methods of the Russian neuropsychologist Alexander Luria. Where Luria himself worked flexibly, testing hypotheses one at a time, Golden converted his procedures into a fixed battery of roughly 269 items grouped into 11 clinical scales, each item scored 0 (normal), 1 (borderline) or 2 (impaired), so qualitative neurological deficits are captured on a standardised quantitative scale. The 11 clinical scales are:

  • C1 Motor Functions, C2 Rhythm (acoustico-motor), C3 Tactile Functions, C4 Visual Functions, C5 Receptive Speech, C6 Expressive Speech, C7 Writing, C8 Reading, C9 Arithmetic, C10 Memory, and C11 Intellectual Processes.

Profiles are interpreted against critical-level cut-offs (corrected for age and education); elevations on particular scales, and especially the localisation and pathognomonic supplementary scales, point to the affected functional system. The LNNB takes far less time than the HRNB and is more theory-driven, but critics argue its rigid quantification loses some of Luria's flexible, hypothesis-testing genius.

Exam trap

Both Halstead-Reitan and Luria-Nebraska are fixed batteries, but the count and structure differ: LNNB has 11 clinical scales with 0/1/2 item scoring and is built on Luria's theory; HRNB has its five core subtests plus the Impairment Index and is atheoretical/empirical. Do not attribute the 11 scales to Halstead-Reitan, and do not call HRNB "Lurian." Luria's own approach was a flexible qualitative method; the Nebraska version is what made it fixed and quantified.

G. The NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery

This is the most heavily examined neuropsychology topic for Indian entrances because it is the indigenous battery, standardised on the Indian population at the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bangalore. It is a comprehensive profiling tool that samples each major cognitive domain with named, internationally recognised tests rather than relying on a single fixed sequence. Work through it domain by domain.

1. Mental Status Examination and Focal Signs

The battery opens with a Mental Status Examination (orientation, level of consciousness, gross cognitive screen) and a survey of focal signs: lateralised motor or sensory abnormalities, aphasias, apraxias, agnosias and other hard neurological signs that flag a specific lesion site before formal testing begins. This screening tier decides whether and how the detailed battery is administered.

2. Attention

Attention is profiled with cancellation and vigilance tasks. The Colour Cancellation Test requires the examinee to strike out target coloured stimuli scattered among distractors across a page, indexing focused and sustained visual attention and visual scanning (and revealing unilateral neglect if one side of the page is missed). Digit vigilance tasks require cancelling a target digit embedded in rows of random digits within a time limit, measuring sustained attention and speed with an error count.

3. Executive functions ("executive gating")

This is the richest tier and the one most often tested. It uses five classic instruments:

  • N-Back test: a continuous working-memory task in which the examinee must indicate when the current stimulus matches the one presented N steps earlier (1-back, 2-back, and so on). It loads the central executive and is a pure measure of working-memory updating; difficulty scales with N.
  • Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST): the gold-standard test of set-shifting and frontal/executive flexibility, discussed in detail below.
  • Tower of London: a planning task in which coloured beads on pegs must be rearranged to match a goal configuration in the minimum number of moves; it measures forward planning, look-ahead and inhibition of impulsive moves, and is sensitive to dorsolateral prefrontal dysfunction.
  • Stroop Colour-Word Test: discussed below; measures response inhibition and the suppression of an over-learned response.

The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test in detail

In the WCST the examinee sorts response cards under four stimulus cards. They must sort by an unstated rule (colour, form or number), are told only "right" or "wrong" after each placement, and must infer the rule from the feedback. After ten consecutive correct sorts the examiner silently switches the rule, and the examinee must detect the change and shift. The diagnostic scores are:

  • Perseverative errors: continuing to sort by the old rule after it has changed (the signature frontal-lobe failure of cognitive flexibility). A high perseverative-error count is the hallmark sign of dorsolateral prefrontal dysfunction.
  • Categories achieved: the number of rules (out of six) the examinee successfully completed; fewer categories indicates worse abstraction and set-shifting.
  • Failure to maintain set: breaking off a correct run of sorts before completing a category (five or more correct then an error), indicating attentional lapse or distractibility.
Definition

A perseverative error on the WCST is a response that persists with a previously correct sorting principle after the rule has changed. It is the cardinal index of frontal (dorsolateral prefrontal) lobe dysfunction and of impaired cognitive set-shifting.

The Stroop Colour-Word Test in detail

The Stroop effect (J. R. Stroop) exploits the conflict between reading, an automatic over-learned process, and colour-naming, a more effortful one. The examinee is shown colour words printed in incongruent ink (the word "RED" printed in blue ink) and must name the ink colour while suppressing the urge to read the word. The slowing on the incongruent condition relative to baseline is the interference score, and it measures response inhibition and the ability to override a prepotent response, mediated by the anterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex.

4. Memory

Memory is the domain with the most named instruments, so learn each one.

The Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) presents a 15-word list (List A) read aloud over five learning trials; then a distractor 15-word list (List B) is presented once; then immediate recall of List A is tested again, a delayed recall follows after 20 to 30 minutes, and finally a recognition trial requires picking List A words out of a longer list. The components index distinct processes:

  • Immediate recall and the learning curve (trials 1 to 5): the rise across trials shows the rate and capacity of verbal learning; a flat curve indicates poor acquisition.
  • Proactive interference: the degree to which already-learned List A intrudes on the learning of List B (old learning disrupting new).
  • Retroactive interference: the drop in List A recall after List B has been interposed (new learning disrupting old); measured as the post-interference List A trial against trial 5.
  • Delayed recall: words retained after the 20 to 30 minute delay, indexing consolidation and storage.
  • Recognition: identifying List A words among distractors. The crucial differential: if delayed recall is poor but recognition is preserved, the deficit is one of retrieval (a sub-cortical or frontal pattern); if recognition is also poor, the deficit is one of encoding or storage (an amnestic, medial-temporal pattern, as in Alzheimer's disease).
Memory aid

Proactive interference = Past learning Pushes forward onto new material (List A hurts List B). Retroactive interference = new material Reaches back to hurt old (List B hurts List A recall). "Recall poor but recognition spared = retrieval problem; both poor = storage problem."

Other memory instruments in the NIMHANS battery:

  • Visual Retention tests (such as the Benton Visual Retention Test): a design is shown briefly then reproduced from memory, indexing visual (non-verbal) immediate memory and visuo-constructional ability, the visual analogue of the RAVLT.
  • Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS): the standard omnibus memory battery, indexing immediate and delayed memory across auditory and visual modalities (logical memory paragraphs, paired associates, visual reproduction, and so on); Indian adaptations are incorporated into the NIMHANS battery.
  • PGI Memory Scale (Postgraduate Institute Memory Scale, Pershad and Wig): the indigenous Indian memory battery, comprising ten subtests that sample the major dissociable memory functions: remote memory, recent memory, mental balance, attention and concentration, delayed recall, immediate recall, verbal retention for similar pairs, verbal retention for dissimilar pairs, visual retention, and recognition. Its strength is that it dissociates remote from recent memory and separates attention-concentration from pure recall, so the profile localises which memory system has failed.
High-yield

NIMHANS battery domains and signature tests: Attention (Colour Cancellation, digit vigilance); Executive (N-Back, WCST with perseverative errors / categories achieved / failure to maintain set, Tower of London, Stroop interference); Memory (RAVLT with immediate / interference / delayed / recognition; Visual Retention; WMS; PGI Memory Scale). The RAVLT recall-versus-recognition dissociation (retrieval vs storage deficit) is a perennial item.

Exam trap

Match the test to its domain: WCST and Tower of London are executive (frontal) tests, not memory tests; RAVLT is a verbal memory test, not an attention test; the Stroop measures inhibition, not memory. The PGI Memory Scale is the Indian memory scale (Pershad and Wig); do not confuse it with the Wechsler Memory Scale. Perseverative errors belong to the WCST, not the Stroop.

H. Objective Personality: MMPI-2

We now cross into personality assessment. The objective tradition uses fixed-item, fixed-scored self-report inventories (true/false or Likert), so that scoring is independent of the clinician's interpretation. The dominant instrument is the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, second edition (MMPI-2), with 567 true/false items. Its founding genius (Starke Hathaway and J. C. McKinley, 1943) was empirical criterion keying: items were retained on a scale not because they sounded relevant but because they statistically discriminated a known clinical group (say, diagnosed depressives) from normals, regardless of face content. This makes the MMPI relatively resistant to faking by content alone and is why some scale items look unrelated to the construct.

Validity indicators (the lie-detectors)

The MMPI-2's most distinctive feature is its battery of validity scales, which detect how the person approached the test before any clinical scale is interpreted. Learn each, because the examiner tests "which scale catches which response style."

Two-point code-types

The MMPI-2 is rarely interpreted scale by scale; instead the clinician reads the two highest clinical scales together as a "code-type", because empirically the combination predicts the clinical picture better than either scale alone. Know the classic codes:

  • 2-7 / 7-2: anxious depression, with prominent worry, tension, rumination and dysphoria (Depression plus Psychasthenia). A very common and clinically stable code.

  • 1-3 / 3-1, the "Conversion V": scales 1 (Hs) and 3 (Hy) both elevated with scale 2 (D) relatively low, so the profile forms a V shape across the neurotic triad. High-yield

MMPI-2 = 567 true/false items, empirically keyed. Validity scales L/F/K plus Fb/VRIN/TRIN/Fp/S. Ten clinical scales 1-Hs to 0-Si. Code-types: 2-7 anxious depression, 4-9 antisocial/impulsive, 1-3 with low 2 = Conversion V (somatoform/denial), 2-7-8 = severe distress with thought disturbance.

Exam trap

The Conversion V is defined by 1 and 3 high and 2 low (a V dipping at Depression). If 2 is also elevated it is no longer a conversion V. Do not confuse the 2-7 (anxiety/depression, neurotic level) with 2-7-8 (adds the schizophrenia scale, indicating thought disturbance and far greater severity). And remember scale numbering: Scale 4 = Pd (Psychopathic Deviate), Scale 9 = Ma (Hypomania); the 4-9 code is the acting-out profile.

I. Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory (MCMI-IV)

The Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory, fourth edition, built by Theodore Millon, is a shorter true/false self-report inventory (195 items) designed specifically for psychiatric populations rather than the general public, and aligned with the DSM axes (personality patterns on the old Axis II plus clinical syndromes on the old Axis I). Its theoretical foundation is Millon's evolutionary theory of personality, which holds that personality styles arise from how an individual resolves three (later four) fundamental evolutionary polarities: pleasure versus pain (the aim of existence), active versus passive (the mode of adaptation), and self versus other (the strategy of replication). Each personality-disorder scale is derived from a particular configuration of these polarities (for example, the avoidant style as a passive-pain orientation). Because its norms are drawn from clinical samples, MCMI scores are expressed as Base Rate (BR) scores rather than T-scores: BR 75 marks the presence of a trait and BR 85 marks its prominence, calibrated to the actual prevalence of each disorder in psychiatric settings, which improves diagnostic hit rate.

Exam trap

The MCMI is tied to Millon's evolutionary theory (pleasure-pain, active-passive, self-other polarities) and is normed on psychiatric, not general, populations, hence its Base Rate scores rather than T-scores. A distractor will offer "MMPI-style T-scores" or attribute the evolutionary theory to someone else: both wrong. MCMI's strength is personality-disorder assessment; the MMPI is broader-spectrum.

J. Projective Assessment: The Rorschach Inkblot Method (Exner Comprehensive System)

Projective tests present an ambiguous stimulus and assume the examinee "projects" their own needs, conflicts and perceptual style onto it (the projective hypothesis). The Rorschach (Hermann Rorschach, 1921) uses ten standardised inkblots (five achromatic black-and-grey, two with red, three fully chromatic), shown one at a time with the open question "What might this be?" For decades rival scoring systems (Beck, Klopfer, Piotrowski, Rapaport) fragmented the field, until John Exner integrated the empirically defensible elements into a single standardised, psychometrically grounded Comprehensive System. Administration has a Response (Free Association) phase followed by an Inquiry phase, in which the examiner re-presents each blot to establish exactly where on the blot the percept was seen and what features of the blot determined it. Every response is then coded on several axes.

Location

Where on the blot the response was seen:

  • W (Whole): the entire blot is used. A high W rate signals an ambitious, integrative, organising approach.
  • D (Common Detail): a commonly used, obvious part of the blot. The "ordinary" economical perception.
  • Dd (Unusual Detail): a rarely used, small or oddly bounded part; excess Dd can indicate over-particularity or oppositionality.
  • S (Space): the white space is used (usually in combination, coded WS, DS, DdS). Space responses are associated with oppositionality, negativism or autonomy striving.

Developmental Quality (DQ)

This codes the cognitive sophistication of how the percept is organised, independent of where it was located: DQ+ (synthesised: two or more objects in a meaningful relationship, with at least one having a specific form) is the most cognitively mature; DQo (ordinary: a single object with definite form); DQv/+ (synthesised but with vague form); DQv (vague: a formless impression such as "blood" or "clouds") is the least mature. The ratio of DQ+ to DQv indexes the complexity and quality of cognitive mapping.

Determinants

This is the richest and most examined code: what about the blot made it look that way. The determinants are the perceptual features the examinee used.

  • Form (F): the response is based purely on the shape/outline of the blot. Pure-form responses reflect detached, affect-free, controlled cognitive processing; the proportion of pure-F responses feeds the Lambda index below.
  • Movement: M (human movement), the percept involves a human in action (associated with inner life, fantasy, ideation, empathy and abstract thought); FM (animal movement), animals in species-typical action (associated with ungoverned, instinctual need states); m (inanimate movement), forces or objects in motion such as an explosion or dripping water (associated with situational stress and a sense of forces beyond one's control).
  • Chromatic Colour: the use of the coloured areas, graded by how much form controls the colour. FC = form dominant, colour secondary (well-modulated, adaptive affect); CF = colour dominant, form secondary (more labile, reactive affect); C = pure colour with no form (raw, unmodulated, potentially impulsive affect). The FC : CF+C ratio indexes affect regulation.
  • Achromatic Colour (C'): using the black, grey or white as colour ("a black bat," "white snow"). It is associated with constrained, internalised, painful or dysphoric affect (swallowed or contained emotion).
  • Shading: using gradations of light and dark, coded in three families. Texture (T): shading read as a tactile surface (fur, velvet), linked to needs for closeness and attachment. Vista / Dimension (V): shading read as depth or distance, linked to painful self-scrutiny and inferiority. Diffuse shading (Y): shading used without form definition, linked to anxiety and helplessness.
  • Form Dimension (FD): depth or perspective conveyed by the contour/form (not shading), associated with introspection and self-awareness (adaptive in moderation).
  • Pairs (2) and Reflections (rF, Fr): the symmetry of the blot read as two identical objects (a pair) or as a mirror reflection. Reflection responses are strongly associated with narcissism, egocentricity and self-focus; they feed the Egocentricity Index.

Form Quality (FQ)

This codes how well the percept fits the actual contour of the blot, that is, how accurately the person perceives reality:

  • FQ+: superior, highly specified good fit.
  • FQo (ordinary): a common, easily-seen good-fit percept.
  • FQu (unusual): an uncommon percept that nonetheless does not violate the blot's form (a low-frequency but not distorted fit).
  • FQ- (minus): the percept does not fit the blot contour; a distorted, arbitrary perception. A high proportion of FQ- (poor form quality, summarised in indices such as X-% and the WDA%) is the single most important Rorschach sign of a perceptual-thinking disturbance / reality-testing breakdown, central to the psychosis indices.

Content and Populars

Each response is also coded for content (Human H, Animal A, Anatomy An, Blood Bl, and so on), and a Popular (P) response is one of the small set of percepts given very commonly to a particular blot (for example, the bat or butterfly on Card I). A normal number of Populars shows the person perceives the world conventionally; very few Populars suggests idiosyncratic perception or poor conventionality.

The Structural Summary: the interpretive engine

After every response is coded, the codes are tallied and combined into the Structural Summary, a page of ratios, percentages and derived indices that is the actual interpretive output. You must understand these indices as relationships, not as a flat list.

Definition

Lambda is the ratio of pure-Form responses to all other (non-pure-F) responses: Lambda = F divided by (R minus F), where R is the total number of responses. A high Lambda means the person leans heavily on bare form and screens out the emotional, movement and colour features of the blot: a constricted, simplistic, affect-avoidant engagement with the world. A very low Lambda means they are flooded by the complexity and cannot simplify. Lambda is therefore a measure of how openly versus defensively the person processes experience.

Experience Balance (EB) is written as a colon-separated ratio, Sum of human Movement (M) : Weighted Sum of Colour (WSumC). It defines the person's preferred coping style:

  • If M clearly exceeds weighted colour, the style is introversive: the person prefers to solve problems through internal ideation, deliberation and fantasy before acting.
  • If weighted colour clearly exceeds M, the style is extratensive: the person prefers to process problems through interaction with the environment and through affect, trial-and-error and externalised expression.
  • If the two sides are roughly balanced, the style is ambitent: an inconsistent coping style with no settled preference, often associated with less efficient problem solving.
Memory aid

EB = M : WSumC. M (human movement) is the ideational/inner side, so high-M = Introversive (think before acting). Colour is the affective/outer side, so high-colour = Extratensive (engage and react). Balanced = ambitent.

EA versus es compares resources to demands. Experience Actual (EA) = the sum of both sides of EB (M + WSumC); it represents the volume of organised, available psychological resources the person can deliberately deploy. Experienced Stimulation (es) sums the more peripheral, less controlled experience pressing on the person: the non-volitional movement (FM and m) and the shading and achromatic-colour determinants (the stress and affect a person is subjected to rather than directs). When EA comfortably exceeds es, the person has more controlled resource than ambient demand, the sign of stable, available coping capacity. When es exceeds EA, the person is overwhelmed by more internal stimulation and stress than they can organise.

Adjusted es (Adj es) removes the situational components of es, principally the m and the diffuse-shading (Y) elements that reflect transient, here-and-now stress, leaving the more chronic, characterological load. Comparing es with Adj es shows how much of the person's burden is acute situational stress versus enduring strain.

Definition

The D-Score is derived from the standardised difference between EA and es (essentially how far available resource exceeds or falls short of current demand, converted to a standard-score-like metric). It indexes the person's capacity to tolerate stress and maintain controlled, directed behaviour right now. A D-Score of 0 indicates adequate current control; a negative D-Score indicates that demand outstrips resource, predicting impulsivity, disorganisation and a propensity to lose control under stress.

The Adjusted D-Score (Adj D) is the same calculation but using Adjusted es instead of raw es, so it removes the transient situational stress and reveals the person's baseline, characteristic stress-tolerance capacity. The comparison is the whole point:

  • If D is negative but Adj D is zero (or higher), the person's controls are overwhelmed only because of acute, situational stress; their underlying capacity is intact and they should recover as the stressor passes.
  • If both D and Adj D are negative, the deficit is chronic: a stable, characterological vulnerability to disorganisation under even ordinary demand.
High-yield

Exner Structural Summary chain: Lambda (form-dominance / openness) -> EB = M : WSumC (introversive vs extratensive vs ambitent) -> EA vs es (controlled resources vs ambient demand) -> D-Score (current stress tolerance) and Adjusted D-Score (chronic baseline tolerance). The D vs Adj D comparison separates acute (D negative, Adj D normal) from chronic (both negative) stress overload.

The diagnostic constellations

Exner also defined empirically derived constellations: clusters of structural-summary variables that, when enough are positive, flag a specific clinical risk. Know all five by name and target:

  • CDI (Coping Deficit Index): immaturity and inadequacy in coping and interpersonal relations; a person who copes poorly and relates ineffectively, often under-diagnosed because they look "just inadequate."
  • DEPI (Depression Index): a configuration associated with affective (depressive) disturbance; a structural marker of depression that does not depend on the patient reporting it.
  • SCZI (Schizophrenia Index), revised to the PTI (Perceptual-Thinking Index): poor form quality, cognitive slippage and disordered thinking; the marker of psychotic-level perceptual and thought disturbance. The PTI replaced the older SCZI to reduce false positives.
  • HVI (Hypervigilance Index): a guarded, mistrustful, hyperalert, paranoid-style stance toward the environment.
  • S-CON (Suicide Constellation): a set of variables (when 8 or more of 12 are positive) empirically associated with completed suicide; the single most clinically urgent index, screened first in any protocol.
Exam trap

Exner's Comprehensive System is the standardised scoring system; it is not the original test (the inkblots are Hermann Rorschach's, 1921). Do not credit Exner with the blots or Rorschach with the structural summary. Note the SCZI was superseded by the PTI (Perceptual-Thinking Index): an up-to-date answer names the PTI. The S-CON is the suicide constellation (screened first, 8-of-12 cut-off), not a depression index; DEPI is the depression index. Reflection responses, not pairs alone, drive the narcissism/egocentricity interpretation.

K. The Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

The Thematic Apperception Test (Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan, 1935) is the other great projective method. The examinee is shown a series of ambiguous black-and-white picture cards (31 in the full set, around 20 used in practice, some specific to age and sex) and asked to tell a story for each: what is happening, what led up to it, what the characters are feeling and thinking, and how it turns out. The projective assumption is that in interpreting an ambiguous interpersonal scene the storyteller reveals their own dominant motives and conflicts. Murray interpreted the stories through his own personality theory, Need-Press Theory, which is the high-yield content here.

The Hero

The Hero is the character in the story with whom the storyteller is assumed to identify (usually the protagonist, often of the same age and sex as the examinee). Analysis centres on the hero: their needs, what presses upon them, and how the story resolves are read as a projection of the examinee's own psychology.

Internal Needs

A need (n) is an internal drive or motivational force organising behaviour toward a goal. Murray catalogued a long list of psychogenic needs; the examiner expects the canonical examples:

  • nAch (need for Achievement): the drive to accomplish, master, excel and meet a standard of excellence.
  • nAff (need for Affiliation): the drive to form and maintain friendly, warm relationships and to be close to others.
  • nAgg (need for Aggression): the drive to overcome opposition forcefully, to attack, injure or oppose.

The clinician scores which needs appear, how strongly, and in which combinations, in the hero's behaviour across the cards.

External Press

A press (p) is an environmental force that facilitates or obstructs the hero's pursuit of a need: what the world does to the hero (a hostile authority, a loss, an opportunity, an obstacle). Murray drew a crucial distinction between two kinds of press:

Definition

Alpha Press is the objective environment as it actually is: the real, quantifiable situational forces that an outside observer would agree are present. Beta Press is the subjective environment as the hero perceives and interprets it: the personal, often distorted construal of what the situation means. The gap between alpha and beta press (objective reality versus subjective interpretation) is itself diagnostic, because a wide divergence reveals how the person misreads or colours their world.

The Thema

The Thema is the overarching plot or behavioural unit that emerges from the interaction of the hero's needs with the environmental press: the recurring need-press-outcome pattern across the stories. A simple thema is one need meeting one press with one resolution; a complex thema interweaves several. Recurrent themas across many cards reveal the examinee's stable, characteristic conflicts and motivational style (for example, a repeated thema of high nAch repeatedly crushed by hostile beta press would suggest a person who strives but expects defeat).

High-yield

TAT = Murray (and Morgan), interpreted via Need-Press Theory. Hero = the identified-with character; Need = internal drive (nAch achievement, nAff affiliation, nAgg aggression); Press = external force (Alpha = objective reality, Beta = subjective perception); Thema = the recurrent plot born from the need-press collision.

Exam trap

Keep the TAT and Rorschach contributions straight: the TAT samples motivation and interpersonal themes via Murray's Need-Press theory, whereas the Rorschach samples perception, affect regulation and reality-testing via Exner's structural variables. On press, remember Alpha = objective / actual and Beta = subjective / perceived; the favourite distractor reverses them. The Hero is the character of identification, not necessarily the card's central figure if the storyteller clearly invests in someone else.

Memory aid

"Alpha = Actual (objective), Beta = Belief (subjective)." Both start with the same first letters as their meaning. Hero feels Needs; world applies Press; the story they make together is the Thema.

5 · Psychotherapy & Clinical Interventions

Psychotherapy is the planned, systematic application of psychological principles to relieve distress and produce lasting change in how a person thinks, feels, behaves, and relates. For a clinical entrance examination this section is unusually high-value because the questions cluster around a small number of fixed facts: who originated a model, the single sentence that captures why that model says people change, the named technique attached to it, and the look-alike model it is most often confused with. A candidate who can say that flooding is prolonged real-life exposure while implosion is imaginal exposure built on psychodynamic themes, or that Klein gave us the paranoid-schizoid position while Mahler gave us separation-individuation and Winnicott the good-enough mother, has already banked the marks that vaguer candidates lose. This section teaches each approach from its theory of change upward, then the techniques that follow from that theory, and always flags the exact distractor the paper will use. We move through the three great families in the order the syllabus sets them: the cognitive-behavioural and third-wave therapies, the psychoanalytic and humanistic-existential and systemic paradigms, and finally clinical ethics and the Indian mental-health legislation.

A. Traditional Behavioural Interventions

Behaviour therapy rests on a single claim: maladaptive behaviour is learned, and what is learned can be unlearned by the same laws of classical and operant conditioning that produced it. There is no appeal to unconscious conflict; the target is the observable behaviour and the contingencies that maintain it. The two engines are Pavlovian (classical) conditioning, which Wolpe harnessed, and Skinnerian (operant) conditioning, which gave us functional analysis and the reinforcement technologies.

Wolpe: reciprocal inhibition and systematic desensitisation

Joseph Wolpe (1958) proposed the principle of reciprocal inhibition: if a response antagonistic to anxiety can be made to occur in the presence of an anxiety-evoking stimulus, it will weaken the bond between that stimulus and the anxiety. The classic antagonist is deep muscular relaxation, because a body cannot be simultaneously relaxed and anxious; relaxation and anxiety are physiologically incompatible. The theory of change is therefore counter-conditioning: pairing the feared cue with a competing, incompatible state until the cue no longer evokes fear.

Wolpe operationalised this as systematic desensitisation, a three-step protocol. First, the patient is trained in deep relaxation, usually Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation. Second, therapist and patient build an idiosyncratic anxiety hierarchy: a personally tailored, ranked list of feared situations from least to most threatening, each rated on the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), a 0 to 100 self-report scale where 0 is complete calm and 100 is the worst imaginable terror. Third, the patient, while deeply relaxed, is exposed (in imagination first, then in vivo) to the lowest item, moving up the hierarchy only when each rung evokes no anxiety. Exposure is therefore graded and gradual, the opposite of flooding.

Definition

SUDS (Subjective Units of Distress Scale): a 0 to 100 self-rating of present anxiety used to anchor each step of the hierarchy and to decide when the patient is calm enough to advance. Reciprocal inhibition: the weakening of an anxiety response by simultaneously eliciting an incompatible response (relaxation).

High-yield

Systematic desensitisation is graded exposure plus relaxation, built on reciprocal inhibition and ordered by a SUDS-rated anxiety hierarchy. Originator: Joseph Wolpe. Distinguish it sharply from flooding, which uses no relaxation and no hierarchy.

Skinner: operant conditioning and functional analysis

B. F. Skinner's operant conditioning holds that behaviour is shaped by its consequences: reinforcement increases the future probability of a behaviour, punishment decreases it. The clinician's first tool is functional behavioural analysis, the ABC sequence: identify the Antecedent (the trigger or setting event that precedes the behaviour), the Behaviour itself (defined observably and measurably), and the Consequence (what follows and thereby maintains it). Mapping the ABC reveals the function the behaviour serves (escape, attention, tangible reward, or sensory self-stimulation), which is what intervention must target.

A token economy applies operant principles to a ward, classroom, or family: desired behaviours earn tokens (secondary, conditioned reinforcers) that are later exchanged for backup reinforcers (privileges, snacks, activities). It works only if the target behaviours are clearly specified, tokens are delivered immediately and contingently, and the exchange rate is meaningful.

Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA) is the structured application of operant principles, most associated with autism and developmental disability. Its core teaching procedures are:

  • Shaping: reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour, so a behaviour the person cannot yet perform is built up step by step from rough attempts.
  • Chaining: teaching a multi-step skill (for example handwashing) as a linked sequence. In forward chaining the first step is taught first and later steps are prompted; in backward chaining the therapist completes all but the last step and the learner performs the final step (gaining the natural reinforcement of completion immediately), then works backward.
  • The Premack principle: a high-probability (preferred) behaviour can reinforce a low-probability (less preferred) one. Stated as "first eat your vegetables, then you may play": access to the preferred activity is made contingent on completing the non-preferred one. Also called the "grandma's rule" of reinforcement.
Exam trap

Forward versus backward chaining is a favourite reversal. Forward chaining teaches step 1 first; backward chaining has the learner perform the last step first (the therapist having done the rest), so completion-reinforcement is immediate. Shaping reinforces approximations of one behaviour; chaining links several discrete behaviours into a sequence. Do not conflate them.

Memory aid

Premack = "Preferred behaviour Pays for the Painful one." Grandma's rule: dessert is earned by eating dinner.

Exposure paradigms: flooding, implosion, ERP

Exposure therapies all work by confronting the feared stimulus while preventing the escape or avoidance that normally maintains the fear. Where systematic desensitisation is graded and gentle, the flooding family is intensive and immediate.

FeatureFloodingImplosion (implosive therapy)
Mode of exposureIn vivo (real-life), prolongedImaginal (in imagination)

Both flooding and implosion drop the hierarchy and the relaxation of desensitisation and instead expose the patient to high anxiety until it spontaneously subsides; the patient learns, through direct experience, that the feared catastrophe does not occur and that anxiety falls on its own when avoidance is blocked.

High-yield

The cleanest one-line discriminator: flooding = in-vivo (real) prolonged exposure; implosion = imaginal exposure using psychodynamic anxiety themes. Both abandon the graded hierarchy and relaxation that define systematic desensitisation.

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is the gold-standard behavioural treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder. The patient is deliberately exposed to the obsessional trigger (touching a "contaminated" surface) and then prevented from performing the compulsive ritual (hand-washing). The compulsion normally produces negative reinforcement: the ritual lowers anxiety, which strengthens the ritual. ERP breaks this loop by blocking the response so the patient discovers that anxiety habituates on its own and the feared outcome does not materialise. The two mechanisms cited are habituation (within- and between-session decline of the anxiety response with repeated exposure) and, in the modern account, inhibitory learning (the original fear association is not erased but a new, competing "safety" association is learned and comes to inhibit it).

Exam trap

In OCD the compulsion is maintained by negative reinforcement (removal of anxiety), not positive reinforcement. ERP works by preventing the response so the negative-reinforcement loop cannot complete. The modern mechanism is inhibitory learning (a new safety memory inhibits the old fear), not simple erasure of the fear.

Behavioural activation and biofeedback

Behavioural activation targets major depression. Its theory of change is that depression is maintained by a vicious cycle: low mood leads to withdrawal and inactivity, which removes the person from sources of reinforcement (mastery and pleasure), which deepens low mood. Treatment schedules graded, valued, and pleasurable activities to re-establish contact with positive reinforcement, reversing the cycle from the outside in (action before motivation), without requiring any cognitive restructuring. It is one of the most robustly evidenced standalone treatments for depression.

Biofeedback gives the patient real-time information about a physiological process normally outside awareness (muscle tension via electromyography, heart-rate variability, skin conductance, peripheral temperature, or EEG in neurofeedback). By seeing or hearing the signal, the patient learns voluntary control through operant shaping, lowering arousal in anxiety, tension headache, and hypertension. The mechanism is operant: the displayed signal acts as immediate reinforcement for movement in the desired direction.

B. Cognitive Restructuring: Beck and Ellis

Cognitive therapy shifts the target inward: distress is driven not by events themselves but by the interpretation of events. Change the appraisal and you change the emotion. Two figures dominate, and the examiners test the difference between them.

Beck's cognitive model and the cognitive triad

Aaron T. Beck developed cognitive therapy from his work on depression. His cognitive model proposes a three-level architecture of thought. At the deepest level sit core beliefs / schemas, absolute global statements about the self formed early in life ("I am unlovable," "I am incompetent"). Schemas generate intermediate beliefs: the conditional rules, attitudes, and assumptions that operate as if-then contracts ("If I am not perfect, then I am a failure"). When an environmental event activates a schema, it throws up automatic thoughts: the rapid, involuntary, situation-specific cognitions that flit through the mind ("I'll mess this up") and directly drive the emotional and behavioural reaction.

In depression specifically, Beck described the cognitive triad: simultaneous negative views of (1) the self ("I am worthless"), (2) the world / current experience ("everything is against me"), and (3) the future ("nothing will improve"; hopelessness). The triad is the engine of depressive cognition and a near-certain examination item.

Definition

Beck's three levels, deepest to most surface: core beliefs / schemas (global self-statements) → intermediate beliefs (conditional rules and assumptions) → automatic thoughts (moment-to-moment cognitions triggered by events). The cognitive triad = negative views of self, world, and future.

The cognitive distortions are the systematic errors of reasoning by which automatic thoughts get generated. Learn the full list with its signature definition, because items pair the name to a one-line example:

DistortionWhat it is
Arbitrary inferenceDrawing a conclusion with no supporting evidence (jumping to conclusions)
Selective abstractionFocusing on a single negative detail out of context while ignoring the whole
OvergeneralisationDrawing a sweeping rule from one isolated incident
Dichotomous thinkingAll-or-nothing, black-and-white reasoning with no middle ground
PersonalisationAttributing external events to oneself without basis
Magnification / minimisationExaggerating negatives or shrinking positives (the "catastrophising" subtype magnifies threat)

Beck's techniques follow from the model. The therapeutic stance is collaborative empiricism: therapist and patient work as co-scientists, treating thoughts as hypotheses to be tested rather than facts. Socratic questioning / guided discovery uses a chain of careful questions to lead the patient to examine and revise a belief themselves rather than being told. Behavioural experiments (reality-testing) design real-world tests of a prediction ("if I speak up I will be humiliated") to gather disconfirming evidence. The downward arrow technique repeatedly asks "and if that were true, what would it mean about you?" to drill from a surface automatic thought down to the underlying core belief.

Memory aid

The downward arrow points downward: it digs from a surface automatic thought to the deep core belief. The cognitive triad is self, world, future: "me, my surroundings, my tomorrow."

Ellis: Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT)

Albert Ellis founded Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT), the earliest cognitive therapy (1950s). Its theory of change is the ABCDE model. An Activating event (A) does not cause the emotion directly; it is filtered through the person's Belief (B), and it is the belief, when irrational, that produces the disturbed emotional/behavioural Consequence (C). Therapy adds D, active Disputation of the irrational belief (logical, empirical, and pragmatic challenge), which yields E, an Effective new rational philosophy and the healthier emotion that follows from it.

Ellis held that the core pathogenic beliefs are absolutist demands: rigid "musts," "shoulds," and "oughts" imposed on oneself, others, or the world. He coined musturbation for the tyranny of these demands and "the tyranny of the shoulds." Disputation directly attacks the rigid demand and replaces it with a flexible preference ("I would prefer to succeed" rather than "I must succeed").

Exam trap

Beck versus Ellis. Beck speaks of the cognitive triad, schemas, automatic thoughts, and cognitive distortions, and his style is gentle Socratic collaborative empiricism. Ellis speaks of the ABC(DE) model, irrational beliefs, musturbation and shoulds, and his style is direct, forceful disputation. If a stem says "musturbation" or "ABCDE," the answer is Ellis/REBT; if it says "cognitive triad" or "collaborative empiricism," the answer is Beck.

C. Third-Wave Therapies: DBT, ACT, MBCT

The "third wave" (the first being behaviour therapy, the second Beck/Ellis cognitive therapy) does not try to change the content of thoughts so much as the person's relationship to them. The common ingredients are acceptance, mindfulness, and values, layered on top of behavioural and cognitive technique.

Linehan: Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)

Marsha Linehan developed Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) specifically for borderline personality disorder and chronic parasuicidality (recurrent self-harm and suicidal behaviour). Its central insight is a dialectic: the simultaneous holding of two opposites, acceptance (validating the patient exactly as they are) and change (relentlessly working toward new behaviour). Pure change-focused therapy feels invalidating to these patients and they drop out; pure acceptance leaves the life-threatening behaviour untouched. The synthesis of the two is the engine of change.

DBT delivers skills through four modules:

  • Core Mindfulness: the foundational module; observing, describing, and participating without judgement, learning "wise mind" (the synthesis of emotion mind and reasonable mind).
  • Distress Tolerance: surviving crises without making them worse. Key skills are TIPP (Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired/Progressive muscle relaxation) to rapidly drop physiological arousal, and radical acceptance (fully accepting reality as it is, to end the suffering that comes from fighting it).
  • Emotion Regulation: identifying, understanding, and modulating emotions, including opposite action.
  • Interpersonal Effectiveness: asking for what one needs and saying no while keeping the relationship and self-respect, taught through DEAR MAN (Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, stay Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate).
High-yield

DBT: originator Marsha Linehan, for borderline personality disorder / parasuicidality, central acceptance-change dialectic, four modules (Mindfulness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation, Interpersonal Effectiveness). Attach TIPP and radical acceptance to Distress Tolerance and DEAR MAN to Interpersonal Effectiveness.

Hayes: Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

Steven Hayes developed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), grounded in Relational Frame Theory (RFT), a behavioural account of how human language and cognition arbitrarily relate events and so make us suffer over words and thoughts. ACT's single goal is psychological flexibility: the ability to stay in contact with the present moment and act on one's values even in the presence of painful thoughts and feelings. The model is the Hexaflex, six interlocking processes:

  • Acceptance: making room for unwanted internal experiences rather than struggling to change or suppress them.
  • Cognitive defusion: stepping back from thoughts and seeing them as just thoughts, words and pictures, not literal truths or commands.
  • Contact with the present moment: flexible, mindful attention to the here and now.
  • Self-as-context: the stable "observing self" that notices experience but is not defined by any particular thought or feeling.
  • Values: clarifying what truly matters, the chosen life directions.
  • Committed action: taking concrete, persistent, values-guided steps even when it is uncomfortable.
Memory aid

ACT in three words: "Accept, Choose, Take action." The Hexaflex pairs into three dyads: be open (acceptance + defusion), be present (present moment + self-as-context), do what matters (values + committed action).

Exam trap

The theoretical base of ACT is Relational Frame Theory, not Beck's schema theory. ACT does not dispute or restructure the content of thoughts (that is Beck/Ellis); it changes the person's relationship to thoughts via defusion. Mixing up ACT (Hayes, RFT, Hexaflex) with DBT (Linehan, dialectic, four modules) is the commonest error.

MBCT

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) (Segal, Williams, and Teasdale) integrates Beck's cognitive therapy with Jon Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR). Its specific indication is the prevention of depressive relapse in people with recurrent depression. The mechanism is decentring: by teaching mindful awareness, MBCT helps patients notice the early sad-mood-triggered ruminative thinking that reactivates depression and step out of the rumination loop before it pulls them back into a full episode.

High-yield

MBCT = Beck's CBT + Kabat-Zinn's MBSR, aimed squarely at preventing relapse in recurrent depression by interrupting rumination. It is preventive, not primarily for acute episodes.

D. Psychoanalytic and Psychodynamic Paradigms

Psychoanalysis, founded by Sigmund Freud, holds that present symptoms are the surface expression of unconscious conflict, much of it rooted in early childhood and sexuality, kept out of awareness by defence. The theory of change is insight: making the unconscious conscious so that buried conflict can be re-experienced and worked through.

The two models of mind

Freud offered two overlapping maps. The topographical model divides the mind by accessibility to awareness: the Conscious (what is in mind now), the Preconscious (what can be readily brought to mind), and the Unconscious (the vast reservoir of repressed wishes and memories that cannot ordinarily be accessed). The later structural model divides the mind by function into three agencies:

  • Id: present from birth, entirely unconscious, the reservoir of instinctual drives; it runs on the pleasure principle (immediate gratification) and primary process thinking (illogical, wish-fulfilling).
  • Ego: the executive that mediates between id, superego, and reality; it runs on the reality principle, using reality testing and secondary process (logical, planful) thinking to delay gratification.
  • Superego: the internalised moral standards and ideals (conscience plus ego-ideal), formed by identification with parental prohibitions.
Exam trap

Topographical (Conscious / Preconscious / Unconscious) and structural (Id / Ego / Superego) are different models. Pleasure principle and primary process belong to the id; reality principle, reality testing, and secondary process belong to the ego. Items swap these attributes.

Defence mechanisms

Defences are unconscious ego operations that protect against anxiety arising from conflict. Examiners want them grouped by maturity, an arrangement associated with George Vaillant:

LevelMechanismsSignature definition (one example)
MatureSublimation, Humour, Suppression, AltruismSublimation: channelling an unacceptable impulse into a socially valued activity (aggression into surgery or sport)
Neurotic / immatureReaction formation, Projection, Displacement, Intellectualisation, Rationalisation, Regression, Isolation of affectReaction formation: expressing the opposite of a forbidden impulse (excessive kindness masking hatred)
PsychoticDenial, Distortion, Projective identificationDenial: refusing to acknowledge an obvious external reality

Note the fine discriminations the paper exploits: projection attributes one's own unacceptable feeling to another ("he hates me," when I hate him), whereas displacement redirects the feeling onto a safer target (anger at the boss vented on the spouse). Reaction formation converts the impulse into its opposite. Suppression (mature, conscious postponement) must be distinguished from repression (the basic, unconscious banishing of material). Projective identification (a Kleinian, psychotic-level defence) goes beyond projection: the person not only projects a feeling into another but unconsciously pressures that other into actually feeling and enacting it.

Memory aid

Mature defences spell a handy "SASH": Sublimation, Altruism, Suppression, Humour. Suppression is the conscious cousin of repression: suppression you choose, repression happens to you.

The classical techniques

Freudian technique aims to surface the unconscious. Free association asks the patient to say whatever comes to mind without censorship; the gaps and detours reveal conflict. Dream analysis treats the dream as the "royal road to the unconscious," distinguishing the remembered manifest content from the underlying latent content. Transference is the patient's unconscious projection onto the therapist of feelings and patterns originally directed at important childhood figures; it is not noise but the central therapeutic material, because the old conflict is now live in the room to be interpreted. Countertransference is the therapist's own emotional reaction to the patient, to be monitored and managed (and, in modern use, mined for information). Resistance is anything in the patient that opposes the work (lateness, silence, forgetting); analysing the resistance is itself therapeutic.

Definition

Transference: patient's feelings about past figures redirected onto the therapist. Countertransference: the therapist's emotional reaction to the patient. Manifest content is the dream as remembered; latent content is its hidden meaning.

Extended schools

Psychoanalysis branched into four post-Freudian schools that the syllabus names explicitly:

  • Ego psychology (Anna Freud, Heinz Hartmann): shifts focus from the id to the ego and its defences. Anna Freud catalogued the defence mechanisms; Hartmann introduced the "conflict-free ego sphere" and the idea of adaptation to an "average expectable environment."
  • Object relations theory centres on internalised relationships ("objects") rather than drives. Melanie Klein proposed two developmental positions: the paranoid-schizoid position (the infant splits objects into all-good and all-bad to manage persecutory anxiety) and the later depressive position (recognising that the loved and hated object are one person, bringing guilt and the wish to repair). Margaret Mahler described separation-individuation, the process by which the infant emerges from fusion with the mother, through subphases (normal autism, symbiosis, then differentiation, practising, rapprochement, and consolidation). Donald Winnicott gave us the good-enough mother (who need not be perfect, only reliable enough), the transitional object (the blanket or teddy that bridges inner and outer reality), and the true self versus false self (a compliant false self develops when the environment fails to meet the infant's spontaneous gestures).
  • Self psychology (Heinz Kohut) holds that the self is built through selfobject needs met by caregivers: mirroring (being admired and affirmed), idealising (merging with an admired, calm figure), and twinship / alter-ego (feeling essentially alike with others). These needs are met and gradually internalised through "transmuting internalisation," and empathic failures produce narcissistic pathology.
Exam trap

The three object-relations names are the single most confused cluster in this section. Fix them: Klein = paranoid-schizoid and depressive positions (and splitting, projective identification). Mahler = separation-individuation subphases. Winnicott = good-enough mother, transitional object, true/false self. Kohut = self psychology, mirroring/idealising/twinship.

E. Humanistic-Existential Therapies

The "third force" (after psychoanalysis and behaviourism) rejects both the deterministic unconscious and the mechanistic stimulus-response account. People are seen as fundamentally growth-oriented, free, and responsible; the therapeutic task is to provide conditions in which the person's own drive toward self-actualisation can resume.

Rogers: Person-Centred Therapy

Carl Rogers built Person-Centred (client-centred) Therapy on the belief that the client, not the expert therapist, holds the direction of change. Constructive personality change depends on three core therapist conditions:

  • Empathy: accurately sensing and reflecting the client's internal frame of reference, "as if" one were the client.
  • Congruence (genuineness): the therapist being authentic and transparent, not hiding behind a professional facade.
  • Unconditional positive regard: a non-possessive, non-judgemental warmth and acceptance of the client regardless of what they disclose.

When these conditions are reliably present, the client's incongruence between the real self and the ideal self reduces and growth resumes. Rogers held these conditions to be the necessary and sufficient conditions of change.

High-yield

Rogers's three core conditions: empathy, congruence (genuineness), unconditional positive regard. A favourite item asks for "the three Rogerian conditions" or names one (for example unconditional positive regard) and asks for its school: person-centred therapy.

Perls: Gestalt Therapy

Fritz Perls founded Gestalt therapy, which prizes immediate awareness in the here-and-now over insight into the past. Health is good contact at the contact boundary between self and environment; neurosis is unfinished business, incomplete emotional situations that press for closure. Techniques are active and experiential: the empty chair (the client dialogues with an imagined other or a disowned part of the self placed in an empty chair), exaggeration, and internal dialogue games. Its theoretical heart is the paradoxical theory of change (Arnold Beisser): change occurs not when one tries to become what one is not, but when one fully becomes what one is; trying to force change entrenches the status quo, while full acceptance of the present state frees it to shift.

Memory aid

Empty chair = Gestalt = Perls. The paradoxical theory of change: "you change by accepting what you already are, not by chasing what you are not."

Frankl: Logotherapy

Viktor Frankl founded logotherapy ("therapy through meaning"), born of his concentration-camp survival. The central drive is the "will to meaning"; its frustration produces an existential vacuum, a pervasive sense of emptiness and meaninglessness. Two named techniques: dereflection (redirecting attention away from anxious self-monitoring, useful in sexual and sleep problems where excessive self-observation is the trouble) and paradoxical intention (deliberately wishing for or exaggerating the very thing one fears, for example an insomniac trying hard to stay awake, which removes the anticipatory anxiety that maintained the symptom).

Yalom: existential psychotherapy

Irvin Yalom systematised existential therapy around four ultimate concerns of existence that confront every person: death (the inevitability of one's own ending), freedom (the burden of responsibility that comes with having no firm external ground), existential isolation (the unbridgeable gap between oneself and any other), and meaninglessness (the absence of any pre-given meaning to life). Anxiety and symptoms are understood as flowing from how one confronts or evades these givens.

Exam trap

Keep the existential figures distinct: Frankl = logotherapy, existential vacuum, dereflection and paradoxical intention. Yalom = the four ultimate concerns (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness) and the group-therapy factors below. Paradoxical intention (Frankl) is not the paradoxical theory of change (Perls/Gestalt); these two "paradox" items are routinely swapped.

F. Systemic, Marital, and Group Therapies

Systemic therapies relocate the problem from inside the individual to the pattern of relationships in which the person is embedded. The identified patient is a symptom of a dysfunctional system; treat the system and the symptom resolves.

Minuchin: Structural Family Therapy

Salvador Minuchin developed Structural Family Therapy, which maps the family's invisible structure: its subsystems (spousal, parental, sibling), its hierarchies, and above all its boundaries, the rules governing who participates and how. Boundaries lie on a continuum:

  • Rigid boundaries produce a disengaged family: members are isolated, autonomous to a fault, and offer little support.
  • Diffuse boundaries produce an enmeshed family: members are over-involved, over-reactive, and lack individual autonomy.
  • Clear boundaries (the healthy middle) allow both belonging and autonomy.

Techniques restructure these patterns in the room: enactment (having the family play out a problematic interaction live so the therapist can observe and alter it) and reframing (giving a behaviour a new, often more benign or systemic meaning so the family can respond differently).

Memory aid

Pair them: rigid boundaries → disengaged (a wall between members); diffuse boundaries → enmeshed (no walls, everyone tangled together).

Bowen: Family Systems Theory

Murray Bowen built a multigenerational systems theory. Its keystone is differentiation of self: the capacity to separate one's intellectual from one's emotional functioning, and to maintain a clear sense of self while staying connected to the family emotional system. Poorly differentiated people fuse with others and are swept along by group emotion. Multigenerational transmission describes how levels of differentiation are passed down the generations, so chronic anxiety and dysfunction accumulate across a family tree. Triangulation is the move by which a tense two-person relationship pulls in a third party (often a child) to dilute the anxiety, the basic building block of family emotional process.

Exam trap

Minuchin versus Bowen. Minuchin = structural, boundaries (rigid/disengaged vs diffuse/enmeshed), subsystems, enactment. Bowen = differentiation of self, multigenerational transmission, triangulation. The signature word "differentiation of self" is always Bowen.

Yalom: group psychotherapy

Irvin Yalom identified eleven therapeutic factors that produce change in group therapy: instillation of hope, universality (realising one is not alone in one's problems), imparting of information, altruism (gaining from helping others), the corrective recapitulation of the primary family group, development of socialising techniques, imitative behaviour, interpersonal learning, group cohesiveness, catharsis, and existential factors. The group also moves through developmental stages as cohesion builds (an initial orientation/dependency stage, a conflict/storming stage, and a mature cohesive working stage), echoing Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing sequence.

High-yield

Yalom's group therapeutic factors (eleven) are commonly tested by example: "realising other members share your problem" = universality; "benefiting by helping others" = altruism; emotional release = catharsis.

G. Clinical Ethics and Legislative Frameworks

This final block is pure recall of statute and code, and it is reliably tested because the answers are unambiguous. Master the two Indian Acts and the core professional principles.

The Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA), 2017

The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 replaced the Mental Health Act of 1987 and reframed mental healthcare around the rights and autonomy of the person with mental illness. Its tested provisions:

  • Advance Directive: a person may make a written directive, in advance, stating how they wish to be treated or not treated for a mental illness and who their representative will be, to take effect during a future crisis when they may lack capacity.
  • Nominated Representative (NR): every person has the right to appoint a representative to take decisions on their behalf and safeguard their interests during treatment.
  • Authorities and Boards: the Act establishes a Central Mental Health Authority and State Mental Health Authorities (to register and regulate establishments and professionals), and Mental Health Review Boards (MHRBs) to review admissions, advance directives, and complaints, protecting patient rights.
  • Section 115: presumption of severe stress. Any person who attempts suicide is presumed, unless proved otherwise, to be under severe stress, and is not to be tried or punished. This effectively decriminalises attempted suicide (overriding Section 309 of the Indian Penal Code) and obliges the government to provide care, treatment, and rehabilitation to reduce the risk of recurrence.
  • Prohibitions: the Act bans unmodified ECT (electroconvulsive therapy without muscle relaxants and anaesthesia), bans ECT for minors except with Board permission, and prohibits sterilisation of persons with mental illness as a treatment.
High-yield

MHCA 2017, Section 115: presumption of severe stress in attempted suicide, decriminalising the act and mandating state-sponsored rehabilitation. This single section is the most heavily tested fact in the entire ethics block. Also remember: advance directive, nominated representative, Mental Health Review Boards, and the ban on unmodified ECT and sterilisation.

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 expanded the list of recognised disabilities from 7 to 21 and, crucially for clinical psychology, brought mental illness, autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, and chronic neurological conditions under statutory disability classification. It defines benchmark disability as not less than 40% of a specified disability as certified by a competent authority; benchmark-disability status unlocks reservations in education and government employment and other statutory benefits.

Exam trap

The 40% threshold and the term "benchmark disability" belong to the RPwD Act 2016, not the MHCA. The MHCA 2017 deals with treatment rights (advance directives, NR, Section 115); the RPwD Act deals with disability status and the 40% benchmark. Items deliberately attach the 40% figure to the wrong Act.

Professional ethical guidelines

The RCI code and the APA ethical principles converge on a common core:

  • Informed consent: the client must be given, in understandable terms, the nature, purpose, risks, benefits, and limits of the intervention and must agree voluntarily, with special care for children, persons lacking capacity, and diverse populations.
  • Confidentiality and its exceptions: information is private, but confidentiality is limited and must be broken when there is an immediate risk of serious harm to self or others, in cases of child abuse, or under a valid court order. Knowing these exceptions verbatim is the tested point.
  • The Duty to Warn / Duty to Protect: established by Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (California Supreme Court). When a client makes a credible threat against an identifiable victim, the therapist's duty of confidentiality yields to a duty to protect the third party (by warning the victim, notifying police, or other reasonable steps). The court's famous formulation: "the protective privilege ends where the public peril begins."
  • Dual relationships: a therapist must avoid simultaneous or sequential roles (social, business, sexual, familial) that could impair objectivity or exploit the client; boundaries must be maintained.
  • Cultural competence: the duty to understand and adapt to the client's cultural, linguistic, and social context rather than imposing the therapist's norms.
  • Tele-therapy ethics: digital provision adds duties around data security and privacy, verifying client identity and location, managing emergencies at a distance, obtaining consent specific to the remote modality, and confirming licensure across jurisdictions.
High-yield

The Duty to Warn derives from the Tarasoff case. If a stem describes a client threatening an identifiable third party and asks what overrides confidentiality, the answer is the Tarasoff duty to warn/protect. Pair it with the three standard confidentiality exceptions: risk of harm to self or others, child abuse, and court order.

Definition

Dual relationship: any additional relationship with a client (social, financial, sexual, familial) beyond the professional one, prohibited where it risks impaired judgement or exploitation. Benchmark disability: not less than 40% impairment, certified under the RPwD Act 2016.

6 · Foundational Psychology, Lifespan Development & Social Dynamics

This section teaches the engine room of general psychology: how the mind takes in information (memory and perception), how it changes through experience (learning), how it matures across a lifetime (development), how people behave in groups (social psychology), and how stable individual differences are described (personality). For an RCI clinical-psychology entrance, this is the densest single source of factual one-mark questions: examiners love named theorists, exact stage labels and ages, the four reinforcement schedules, attachment phenotypes, and the classic social-psychology studies. The trap is always the near-neighbour: proactive versus retroactive interference, sensitivity versus bias, assimilation versus accommodation, conformity versus obedience. We build each concept from the floor up, name the theorist, give the mechanism and a worked example, then flag the exact distractor the paper will use against you.

A. Memory: Structural Architectures and Forgetting

Memory is the set of processes by which the nervous system encodes information (turns experience into a storable code), stores it (retains it over time), and retrieves it (brings it back into awareness). The dominant teaching model of the twentieth century treated memory not as one thing but as a sequence of separate stores, each with its own capacity, code, and duration.

The Atkinson-Shiffrin Multi-Store Model (1968)

Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin proposed that information flows through three structurally distinct stores in series. This is also called the modal model because it became the standard (modal) account.

  • Sensory memory (sensory register): a very brief, high-capacity, modality-specific buffer that holds a raw, literal copy of incoming sensory input for a fraction of a second. It is pre-attentive: everything that hits the senses is registered, but it decays almost instantly unless attention selects it. Sensory memory is modality-specific, so it has separate sub-stores. Iconic memory is the visual store (the word comes from icon, image); George Sperling's partial-report experiments (1960) showed iconic memory holds roughly nine to twelve items but decays within about 0.25 to 0.5 seconds. Echoic memory is the auditory store (the word comes from echo); it lasts longer, around 2 to 4 seconds, which is why you can still "hear" the end of a sentence you were not attending to.
  • Short-term store (STS): the contents of current awareness. Capacity is famously about seven plus or minus two items (George Miller's "magical number seven", 1956), better described in chunks than raw items. Duration without rehearsal is roughly 15 to 30 seconds. The code is largely acoustic (we tend to confuse similar-sounding items). Maintenance rehearsal (repeating) keeps items in the STS; elaborative rehearsal transfers them onward.
  • Long-term store (LTS): effectively unlimited in capacity and potentially permanent in duration. The code is predominantly semantic (by meaning). Information must pass through the STS to reach the LTS in this model.

Iconic = visual and very brief (about a quarter second); echoic = auditory and longer (a few seconds). The short-term store holds seven plus or minus two items for 15 to 30 seconds. These exact numbers are tested verbatim.

Exam trap

Do not swap iconic and echoic. Iconic sounds like "icon = picture" (visual); echoic sounds like "echo = sound" (auditory). Examiners reverse the durations too: iconic is the shorter store, echoic the longer one.

The Baddeley and Hitch Working Memory Model (1974)

Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch argued the unitary "short-term store" was too simple to explain how we can hold something in mind while manipulating it. They replaced the passive STS with an active, multi-component working memory system: a workspace for temporarily holding and processing information.

  • Central executive: the attentional control system. It is not a store; it allocates attention, switches between tasks, suppresses irrelevant information, and coordinates the subsystems. It is modality-free and has limited capacity.
  • Phonological loop: handles speech-based (acoustic) information. It has two parts: a phonological store (the "inner ear", holding sound traces for about 2 seconds) and an articulatory rehearsal process (the "inner voice", refreshing the trace by sub-vocal repetition). This is the component you use to hold a phone number by saying it to yourself.
  • Visuospatial sketchpad: the "inner eye", handling visual and spatial information (the appearance of objects and their locations). You use it to picture a route or rotate a shape mentally. Baddeley split it into a visual cache (form and colour) and an inner scribe (spatial and movement).
  • Episodic buffer: added by Baddeley in 2000 to fix a gap in the original model. It is a limited-capacity, temporary store that integrates information from the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad, and long-term memory into a single multimodal, coherent episode (a "scene"). It binds what something looks like, sounds like, and means into one representation and provides an interface between working memory and long-term memory.
High-yield

The four components: central executive (boss, attention), phonological loop (sound, inner voice), visuospatial sketchpad (vision and space, inner eye), and episodic buffer (the multimodal integrator, added in 2000). The episodic buffer is the most-tested "new" piece: it binds the other stores plus long-term memory into one episode.

Memory aid

"Central Executive Posts Visuals and Episodes": Central executive, Phonological loop, Visuospatial sketchpad, Episodic buffer. Phonological = phone (you rehearse a phone number aloud).

Tulving's Classification of Long-Term Memory

Endel Tulving subdivided long-term memory by what kind of content is stored and what kind of awareness accompanies retrieval. The top split is declarative versus non-declarative.

  • Declarative (explicit) memory can be consciously brought to mind and "declared" in words. It divides into:
    • Episodic memory: memory for personally experienced events tied to a specific time and place ("what I ate at my last birthday"). Tulving linked it to autonoetic awareness (self-knowing): you mentally travel back and re-experience the event as having happened to you.
    • Semantic memory: general world knowledge and facts, detached from when you learnt them ("Delhi is the capital of India"). It involves noetic awareness (knowing): you know the fact without re-living its acquisition.
  • Non-declarative (implicit) memory cannot be verbalised and is expressed through performance rather than recollection. The core example is procedural memory: motor and cognitive skills (riding a bicycle, typing). Tulving linked it to anoetic awareness (non-knowing): the skill runs off without any conscious recollection of having learnt it. Other non-declarative forms include priming, classical conditioning, and habituation.
Exam trap

Match the three awarenesses to the three memory types: autonoetic to episodic (re-experiencing the self in an event), noetic to semantic (knowing a fact), anoetic to procedural (no conscious knowing, just doing). The paper loves to pair "semantic" with "autonoetic" as a wrong option. Autonoetic belongs to episodic.

Forgetting Mechanics

Forgetting is the failure to retrieve information that was once available. Several mechanisms are distinguished.

  • The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: Hermann Ebbinghaus, using himself as the only participant and learning lists of nonsense syllables, plotted retention over time and found memory drops sharply at first and then levels off. Most forgetting happens within the first hour to day; the residue decays slowly. He also discovered savings (relearning is faster than first learning) and the value of spaced over massed practice (the spacing effect).
  • Proactive interference: old learning disrupts the retention of new material. Pro- means forward: previously stored information moves forward in time to block what comes next. Example: after years of an old phone number, you keep retrieving it instead of your new one.
  • Retroactive interference: new learning disrupts the retention of old material. Retro- means backward: new information reaches back and overwrites the earlier code. Example: learning a new password makes you forget the previous one.
  • The Zeigarnik effect: Bluma Zeigarnik (a student of Kurt Lewin) found that interrupted or incomplete tasks are remembered better than completed ones, because an unfinished task maintains a state of tension (a "quasi-need") that keeps it active in memory. This is why a waiter remembers unpaid orders but forgets them once settled.
Memory aid

For interference, anchor on the direction of the prefix. Proactive = the past (old) interferes; retroactive = the recent (new) interferes by reaching back. Old-blocks-new is proactive; new-blocks-old is retroactive.

Exam trap

The single most-reversed pair in the whole memory unit is proactive versus retroactive interference. If the question stem says "old learning hampers new learning" the answer is proactive; if "new learning hampers old learning" the answer is retroactive. Zeigarnik is incomplete tasks remembered better, not worse.

B. Learning: Conditioning and Cognitive Models

Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour or knowledge that results from experience, distinguished from temporary changes (fatigue, drugs) and from maturation. Two associative paradigms (classical and operant) plus a cognitive tradition dominate the syllabus.

Classical Conditioning: Pavlov and Watson

Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist studying digestion, discovered that a neutral stimulus, repeatedly paired with one that automatically triggers a reflex, comes itself to trigger that reflex. The vocabulary is fixed and tested:

  • Unconditioned stimulus (UCS): automatically elicits a response (food).
  • Unconditioned response (UCR): the automatic, unlearned reaction (salivation to food).
  • Conditioned stimulus (CS): an originally neutral stimulus (a bell) that, after pairing with the UCS, comes to elicit a response.
  • Conditioned response (CR): the learnt reaction to the CS (salivation to the bell).

John B. Watson, the founder of American behaviourism, extended classical conditioning to emotion. In the "Little Albert" study (1920, with Rosalie Rayner), an infant was conditioned to fear a white rat (CS) by pairing it with a loud noise (UCS). The fear (CR) then generalised to other furry white objects, showing emotional responses are learnt.

Key classical-conditioning phenomena:

  • Acquisition: the build-up of the CR as CS and UCS are paired; strongest when the CS slightly precedes the UCS (forward conditioning).
  • Extinction: presenting the CS repeatedly without the UCS weakens and eventually eliminates the CR.
  • Spontaneous recovery: after extinction and a rest period, the CR reappears when the CS is presented again, showing extinction suppresses rather than erases the association.
  • Stimulus generalisation: stimuli similar to the CS also evoke the CR (Albert fearing a rabbit).
  • Stimulus discrimination: learning to respond only to the specific CS and not to similar stimuli.
  • Higher-order (second-order) conditioning: a well-established CS is used to condition a new neutral stimulus, even though that new stimulus is never paired with the original UCS.

The Rescorla-Wagner Model

Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner (1972) formalised classical conditioning mathematically. The crucial insight: conditioning depends not on simple pairing but on surprise. A UCS only drives learning to the extent it is unpredicted. The change in associative strength of a cue on a given trial is:

Delta VA = alphaA × beta × (lambda − VTotal)

Reading the terms:

  • Delta VA is the change in associative strength of cue A on this trial.
  • alpha (the salience of the CS) and beta (the learning rate set by the UCS) are learning-rate parameters between 0 and 1.
  • lambda (the asymptote) is the maximum associative strength the UCS can support: how much learning is "possible" given this outcome.
  • VTotal is the summed associative strength already held by all cues present on the trial: how much is "already predicted".
  • The bracket (lambda − VTotal) is the prediction error or surprise. When the outcome is fully predicted, lambda equals VTotal, the bracket is zero, and no learning occurs. Large surprise drives large learning.

This single equation explains several phenomena that simple-pairing accounts cannot:

  • Blocking (Kamin): if cue A already fully predicts the UCS (VTotal near lambda), then adding a new cue B in compound (AB) leaves nothing to learn (bracket near zero), so B acquires little or no associative strength. The prior learning "blocks" learning about B. This is the model's flagship prediction.
  • Overshadowing: when two cues of unequal salience are conditioned together, the more salient cue (higher alpha) grabs most of the limited associative strength, "overshadowing" the weaker cue.
  • Uncorrelated contingencies: if a CS does not reliably predict the UCS (the UCS is just as likely without it), no net associative strength accrues, because gains on paired trials are cancelled by losses (extinction) on unpaired trials.
High-yield

Rescorla-Wagner says learning is driven by prediction error / surprise, captured by the term (lambda minus VTotal). Its signature explanation is blocking: a stimulus that adds no new predictive information is not learnt about.

The Garcia Effect (Conditioned Taste Aversion)

John Garcia showed that rats which became nauseous hours after drinking a flavoured solution developed a strong aversion to that taste, even though the illness was delayed by hours and was caused independently (by radiation or a drug). This broke two classical assumptions:

  • Equipotentiality (the behaviourist claim that any CS can be associated equally easily with any UCS) is false: taste readily associates with nausea, but a light or tone does not. The pairing is biologically "prepared", reflecting evolutionary readiness to link food cues with sickness.
  • The contiguity requirement (CS and UCS must be close in time) is violated: taste aversions form across delays of hours, in a single trial.
Exam trap

The Garcia effect is the textbook counter-example to equipotentiality and to the need for temporal contiguity. It demonstrates "biological preparedness" and one-trial, long-delay learning. If the stem mentions taste, nausea, or "preparedness", the answer is Garcia.

Operant Conditioning: Thorndike and Skinner

Operant (instrumental) conditioning concerns learning from the consequences of voluntary behaviour, in contrast to classical conditioning's reflexive responses.

Edward Thorndike placed cats in puzzle boxes and timed how long they took to escape. Escape time fell gradually over trials (trial-and-error learning), leading to the Law of Effect (1898): responses followed by a satisfying consequence are "stamped in" (more likely to recur), while responses followed by discomfort are "stamped out". This is the foundation of all reinforcement theory.

B. F. Skinner systematised this with the operant chamber ("Skinner box") and a precise vocabulary of consequences:

  • Positive reinforcement: adding a pleasant stimulus to increase a behaviour (a treat for a lever press).
  • Negative reinforcement: removing an aversive stimulus to increase a behaviour (a rat presses a lever to switch off a shock). Note: it increases behaviour, it is not punishment.
  • Positive punishment: adding an aversive stimulus to decrease a behaviour (a slap).
  • Negative punishment: removing a pleasant stimulus to decrease a behaviour (taking away a toy).
Exam trap

"Positive" and "negative" mean add and remove, not good and bad. Negative reinforcement strengthens behaviour (by removing something aversive) and is constantly confused with punishment. Reinforcement always increases behaviour; punishment always decreases it.

A schedule specifies the rule by which responses are reinforced. The two dimensions are ratio (reinforce after a number of responses) versus interval (reinforce the first response after a period of time), each either fixed (constant) or variable (averaged).

Cognitive Learning

The cognitive tradition argued that learning involves internal representations, not just stimulus-response bonds.

  • Edward Tolman: latent learning and cognitive maps. Rats allowed to explore a maze without reward showed no improvement until a reward was introduced, after which they immediately performed as well as rats rewarded all along. The learning had been latent (present but not expressed) and was revealed only when motivated. Tolman argued the rats had built an internal cognitive map of the maze, demonstrating that learning (acquiring knowledge) is separable from performance (showing it).
  • Wolfgang Kohler: insight learning. Studying chimpanzees (notably Sultan), Kohler observed that they solved problems (stacking boxes or joining sticks to reach a banana) suddenly, after a pause, rather than by gradual trial and error. This sudden reorganisation of the problem field is insight (the "aha" experience), a Gestalt phenomenon contrasting with Thorndike's incremental curve.
  • Albert Bandura: social (observational) learning. Bandura's Bobo doll studies showed children imitate aggression they merely watch, without direct reinforcement. Observational learning runs through four subprocesses:
    • Attention: you must notice and attend to the model's behaviour.
    • Retention: you must store a memory representation of it.
    • Production (motor reproduction): you must be able to reproduce the action.
    • Motivation: you must have a reason to perform it (often via vicarious reinforcement: seeing the model rewarded).
Memory aid

Bandura's four steps in order spell "A R P M": Attention, Retention, Production, Motivation. You can only motivate (perform) what you can produce, and only produce what you retained, and only retain what you attended to.

C. Perception and Attention

Perception is the organisation and interpretation of sensory input into a meaningful experience. Attention is the selective allocation of limited processing resources. The syllabus targets four frameworks.

Signal Detection Theory (SDT)

SDT models how an observer decides whether a faint signal is present against a background of noise. On every trial the signal is either present or absent, and the observer responds "yes" or "no", giving a 2-by-2 matrix:

Observer says "Yes"Observer says "No"
Signal presentHitMiss
Signal absentFalse alarmCorrect rejection

SDT separates two independent things that older "threshold" theories confused:

  • Sensitivity (d-prime, written d′): how well the observer can actually tell signal from noise, a measure of perceptual or discriminative ability. It is the standardised distance between the noise and signal-plus-noise distributions. Higher d-prime means the two are more separable: more hits with fewer false alarms. Sensitivity reflects the observer's capacity and is unaffected by their willingness to say yes.
  • Response bias (beta or C): where the observer sets their decision criterion: how much evidence they demand before saying "yes". A liberal criterion (says yes readily) yields many hits but also many false alarms; a conservative criterion yields few false alarms but more misses. Bias reflects motivation, expectation, and the payoff structure, not perceptual ability.
Exam trap

The central distinction is sensitivity (d-prime) versus response bias (beta or C). d-prime = how good your senses are at discriminating; beta = how cautious or liberal your decision rule is. If a question says payoffs or expectations shifted the responses without changing perceptual ability, that is a change in bias, not sensitivity.

Gestalt Principles of Perceptual Grouping

The Gestalt school (Wertheimer, Koffka, Kohler) held that "the whole is different from the sum of its parts": we perceive organised wholes, not isolated elements. The grouping laws describe how we bundle elements into objects:

  • Proximity: elements close together are grouped (dots near each other seen as a cluster).
  • Similarity: elements that look alike (colour, shape) are grouped.
  • Continuity (good continuation): we perceive smooth, continuous lines rather than abrupt changes.
  • Closure: we mentally fill gaps to perceive a complete figure (seeing a full circle from a broken outline).
  • Common fate: elements moving together in the same direction are grouped (a flock of birds).
  • Pragnanz (the law of good form / simplicity): the overarching principle that we perceive the simplest, most stable, most regular organisation possible. Pragnanz is German for "conciseness" and is the master Gestalt law.
High-yield

Pragnanz (the law of good form / simplicity) is the umbrella Gestalt principle: the mind organises stimuli into the simplest possible form. Common fate is the one based on shared motion. Closure is filling in the gaps.

Depth Perception: Monocular versus Binocular Cues

To perceive a three-dimensional world from two-dimensional retinal images, the brain uses depth cues.

  • Monocular cues require only one eye and convey depth in flat pictures: relative size, interposition (overlap), linear perspective (converging lines), texture gradient, aerial perspective (distant objects hazy), light and shadow, relative height, and motion parallax (nearer objects sweep past faster). These are the "pictorial" cues an artist uses.
  • Binocular cues require both eyes: retinal (binocular) disparity (the two eyes get slightly different images; the brain reads the difference as depth, the basis of stereopsis) and convergence (the inward turning of the eyes for near objects, with the muscular feedback signalling distance).
Exam trap

Retinal disparity and convergence are binocular (two eyes). Everything pictorial (interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, motion parallax) is monocular. The favourite distractor is calling motion parallax binocular: it is monocular.

Attentional Gating: Filter Models

Three classic models debate where in processing unattended information is filtered out.

  • Broadbent's early-selection filter (1958): a single filter selects one input channel early, on the basis of physical features (such as which ear), before meaning is analysed. The unattended channel is blocked entirely, so its meaning is never processed. This is the strict "bottleneck" model.
  • Treisman's attenuation model (1964): the unattended channel is not blocked but attenuated (turned down, not off). Its meaning is still weakly analysed, so highly salient items (your own name, the "cocktail-party" effect) can break through because they have low recognition thresholds. The filter weakens rather than eliminates.
  • Deutsch and Norman late-selection model: all inputs are fully processed for meaning, and selection happens late, at the stage of response or memory, on the basis of relevance. The bottleneck is near the output, not the input.
Exam trap

Order them by where the filter sits. Broadbent = early (filter before meaning, all-or-none). Treisman = attenuation (turns the volume down, meaning still leaks; explains hearing your name). Deutsch-Norman = late (everything processed, selection at the end). The cocktail-party / own-name finding is the killer evidence against Broadbent and for Treisman.

D. Lifespan Development I: Cognitive and Sociocultural

Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development

Jean Piaget viewed the child as a "little scientist" who actively constructs knowledge. The building blocks are schemas (organised mental frameworks for understanding the world). Development proceeds through three functional processes:

  • Assimilation: fitting new information into an existing schema (a child who knows "dog" calls a cat "dog").
  • Accommodation: altering an existing schema, or creating a new one, to fit new information that will not assimilate (learning that the cat is a separate category).
  • Equilibration: the drive to balance assimilation and accommodation. When new experience cannot be assimilated, the child feels disequilibrium and adjusts schemas to restore equilibrium; this tension is the engine of cognitive growth.
Exam trap

Assimilation = fit the world into your head (no schema change). Accommodation = change your head to fit the world (schema change). Calling a cat a "dog" is assimilation; learning cat is a new category is accommodation. Equilibration is the balancing motivation, not a stage.

Piaget proposed four invariant, universal stages:

StageAgeKey achievements and limits
Sensorimotor0 to 2 yearsKnowing through senses and action; reflex modification; primary, secondary, and tertiary circular reactions; attainment of object permanence (objects exist when out of sight)
Preoperational2 to 7 yearsSymbolic function (language, pretend play); egocentrism, animism, centration; cannot conserve
Concrete operational7 to 11 yearsConservation, decentering, reversibility, transitivity, seriation; logic limited to concrete objects
Formal operational11+ yearsAbstract, hypothetical-deductive reasoning; systematic problem-solving

The key concepts within these stages:

  • Object permanence: the sensorimotor milestone that things continue to exist when not perceived. Before it, "out of sight is out of mind"; its absence is tested by hiding a toy.
  • Egocentrism: the preoperational inability to take another's viewpoint, demonstrated by the Three Mountains Task, where the child cannot say what a doll on the opposite side of a model sees and reports their own view.
  • Animism: attributing life and feelings to inanimate objects ("the sun is sleeping").
  • Centration: focusing on one salient dimension while ignoring others, which is why preoperational children fail conservation.
  • Conservation: understanding that quantity stays constant despite changes in appearance (the same water poured into a taller, thinner glass is the same amount). Mastered in the concrete operational stage.
  • Decentering: attending to multiple dimensions at once (height and width), which enables conservation.
  • Reversibility: mentally undoing an operation (the water could be poured back).
  • Transitivity: inferring relations (if A > B and B > C then A > C).
  • Seriation: ordering items along a dimension (arranging sticks by length).
  • Hypothetico-deductive reasoning: the formal-operational ability to generate hypotheses and test them systematically (the pendulum problem).
High-yield

Lock the stage-to-feature pairs: sensorimotor → object permanence; preoperational → egocentrism (Three Mountains) and failure of conservation; concrete operational → conservation, reversibility, seriation; formal operational → abstract hypothetico-deductive reasoning. The ages (0 to 2, 2 to 7, 7 to 11, 11+) are tested verbatim.

Vygotsky's Sociocultural Theory

Lev Vygotsky stressed that cognition is built through social interaction and culture, in contrast to Piaget's solitary little scientist.

  • Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the gap between what a child can do independently and what they can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other. Learning happens in this zone.
  • Scaffolding: the temporary, tailored support an adult or peer provides within the ZPD, gradually withdrawn as competence grows (like a building scaffold removed once the structure stands). The term is Wood, Bruner, and Ross's, but it operationalises Vygotsky's ZPD.
  • Private speech to inner speech: young children talk aloud to themselves to guide action (private speech); this is not egocentric (Piaget's reading) but a tool for self-regulation that is gradually internalised into silent inner speech (verbal thought).
Exam trap

On self-talk, Piaget and Vygotsky disagree: Piaget called children's talking-to-themselves egocentric speech (immature, fading away), whereas Vygotsky called it private speech (a functional self-guidance tool that becomes inner speech). The ZPD is Vygotsky; conservation and object permanence are Piaget.

Memory aid

Virtues mnemonic "Hope Will Purpose Competence Fidelity, Love Care Wisdom": think of a person hoping, then willing, with a purpose and competence, staying faithful, finding love, giving care, ending in wisdom.

Kohlberg's Theory of Moral Development

Lawrence Kohlberg studied moral reasoning (the justification, not the verdict) using moral dilemmas, most famously the Heinz dilemma: should a man steal an unaffordable drug to save his dying wife? He identified three levels, each with two stages.

  • Pre-conventional level (morality is external, based on consequences to the self):
    • Stage 1: Punishment and obedience orientation: right is avoiding punishment ("Heinz should not steal, he will be jailed").
    • Stage 2: Instrumental hedonism (naive egoism): right is what serves one's own needs, with simple exchange ("steal it because he needs his wife", a "you scratch my back" morality).
  • Conventional level (morality is conformity to social roles and rules):
    • Stage 3: Good-boy / nice-girl orientation: right is gaining approval and being seen as nice ("a good husband would steal").
    • Stage 4: Law-and-order orientation: right is upholding laws and social duty ("stealing is against the law").
  • Post-conventional level (morality is based on abstract principles transcending laws):
    • Stage 5: Social contract orientation: laws are social agreements that can be changed for the greater good; rights matter.
    • Stage 6: Universal ethical principles: self-chosen, universal principles of justice and human dignity override unjust laws ("life is worth more than property").

Carol Gilligan's critique: Kohlberg's stages were derived from male samples and rank an abstract ethics of justice (rules, rights) as the highest morality. Gilligan argued women more often reason from an ethics of care (responsibility, relationships, avoiding harm to others), which Kohlberg's scheme wrongly scores as less mature. The difference is one of orientation, not inferiority: a gender bias in the theory, not in women.

Exam trap

Kohlberg scores the reasoning, not the answer: two people who both say "steal" can be at very different stages. Pre-conventional = self (punishment, reward); conventional = others (approval, law); post-conventional = principles. Gilligan's contribution is the ethics of care versus ethics of justice gender critique.

Attachment Theory

John Bowlby argued attachment is an innate, evolved behavioural system: infants are biologically predisposed to form a bond with a primary caregiver because proximity to a protector enhanced survival. From early interactions the infant builds internal working models: mental representations of the self (am I worthy of care?), of others (are they reliable?), and of relationships, which guide expectations for life.

Mary Ainsworth operationalised attachment quality with the Strange Situation, a standardised laboratory procedure of eight episodes involving separations from and reunions with the caregiver, plus a stranger. The infant's behaviour, especially at reunion, classifies the attachment:

Insecure-anxious/ambivalent (resistant)Highly distressedSeeks contact but resists it: clings and pushes away, angry, hard to soothe
Insecure-avoidantLittle or no distressAvoids and ignores the caregiver; treats stranger and caregiver similarly

Exam trap

The reunion behaviour is the key. The avoidant infant shows little distress and ignores/avoids the caregiver at reunion. The ambivalent/resistant infant is very distressed and seeks but angrily resists comfort. Do not swap them. Disorganised is the Main and Solomon addition, linked to trauma.

F. Social Psychology: Attribution, Influence, and Groups

Attribution Theory and Biases

Attribution is how people explain the causes of behaviour, assigning it to internal/dispositional causes (the person's traits) or external/situational causes (the circumstances).

  • Fritz Heider, the founder, described the "naive psychologist" who routinely sorts causes into internal versus external locus.
  • Harold Kelley's covariation model: to attribute a behaviour, people assess whether it covaries with the person, the stimulus, or the occasion across three kinds of information:
    • Consensus: do other people behave this way toward the same stimulus? (high = others do too).
    • Distinctiveness: does the person behave this way only toward this stimulus or toward many? (high = only this one).
    • Consistency: does the person behave this way toward this stimulus across time and situations? (high = always).
    Low consensus, low distinctiveness, high consistency → internal (dispositional) attribution. High consensus, high distinctiveness, high consistency → external (situational) attribution.

The systematic biases:

  • Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE): the tendency, when explaining others' behaviour, to overweight dispositional causes and underweight situational ones (assuming a late colleague is lazy, not stuck in traffic).
  • Actor-observer bias: we attribute our own behaviour to the situation but others' identical behaviour to their disposition (I'm late because of traffic; you're late because you're disorganised).
  • Self-serving bias: we take credit for successes (internal: "I'm skilled") but blame failures on external factors ("the exam was unfair"), protecting self-esteem.
  • Just-world hypothesis: the belief that the world is fair and people get what they deserve, which leads to blaming the victim (assuming a misfortune was earned).
Exam trap

FAE is about explaining others (over-blame disposition). The actor-observer bias adds the self/other asymmetry (situational for self, dispositional for others). Self-serving bias is the success/failure asymmetry (credit me, blame the world). These three are constantly cross-substituted as distractors.

Festinger's Cognitive Dissonance Theory

Leon Festinger (1957) proposed that holding two inconsistent cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, or a belief and a behaviour) produces an uncomfortable state of cognitive dissonance, a drive-like tension people are motivated to reduce. They reduce it by changing one cognition (attitude change), changing the behaviour, or adding consonant cognitions (rationalisation).

The classic demonstration is Festinger and Carlsmith's forced-compliance / one-dollar versus twenty-dollar study (1959). Participants did a boring task, then were paid either 1 dollar or 20 dollars to tell the next participant it was enjoyable. Counter-intuitively, the 1-dollar group later rated the task as more enjoyable. With 20 dollars, the large payment is sufficient external justification for lying, so no dissonance arises. With only 1 dollar, there is insufficient justification, so the dissonance between "I said it was fun" and "it was boring" is resolved by genuinely changing the attitude ("it actually was a bit fun").

High-yield

In the forced-compliance study, the smaller reward (1 dollar) produced the greater attitude change, because insufficient external justification forces internal (attitude) change to reduce dissonance. This counter-intuitive direction is the whole point and is heavily tested.

Social Influence: Conformity and Obedience

Solomon Asch's conformity studies (1951): participants judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard, an easy perceptual task. When confederates unanimously gave an obviously wrong answer, about a third of responses conformed to the wrong majority. Asch showed conformity to group pressure even against clear perceptual evidence. Two mechanisms drive conformity:

  • Informational social influence: conforming because you believe the group is right (you use others as a source of information), leading to genuine private acceptance; strongest in ambiguous situations.
  • Normative social influence: conforming to be liked / accepted and to avoid rejection, leading to public compliance without private change; this drove most of Asch's effect (the task was unambiguous).

Stanley Milgram's obedience studies (1963): participants ("teachers") were ordered by an experimenter to deliver escalating "shocks" to a learner for wrong answers, up to a labelled 450 volts. About 65 percent obeyed to the maximum, demonstrating that ordinary people obey a legitimate authority even against their conscience. Key modifiers reduced obedience:

  • Proximity of the victim (obedience fell as the learner became more present) and of the authority (obedience fell when the experimenter gave orders by phone).
  • Prestige / legitimacy of the setting and authority (obedience fell when moved from prestigious Yale to a run-down office, or when the experimenter wore ordinary clothes).
Exam trap

Asch = conformity to a peer group (no one orders you). Milgram = obedience to an authority figure (a direct command). For Asch's two motives: informational = "they must be right" (ambiguity, private acceptance); normative = "I want to fit in" (public compliance). Do not confuse the two influence types.

Compliance Techniques

  • Foot-in-the-door: get agreement to a small request first, then the larger target request is more likely accepted (consistency with the prior small commitment).
  • Door-in-the-face: make a large request expected to be refused, then the smaller real request looks reasonable by contrast (reciprocal concession).
  • Low-balling: get a commitment to an attractive offer, then reveal a higher cost; people often stay committed despite the worsened terms.
Memory aid

Foot-in-the-door goes small then big (you let the foot in, then the whole person). Door-in-the-face goes big then small (the big request gets the door slammed, the small one then gets in). Low-balling = bait with a low price, then raise it.

Group Performance and Decision Traps

  • Social facilitation: the presence of others improves performance on simple or well-learned tasks (Triplett's cyclists rode faster with others).
  • Social inhibition: the presence of others impairs performance on complex or novel tasks. Zajonc explained both: others raise arousal, which strengthens the dominant response, which is correct on easy tasks and wrong on hard ones.
  • Social loafing: people exert less effort when their contribution is pooled and not individually identifiable (Ringelmann's rope-pulling), through diffusion of responsibility.
  • Groupthink (Janis): in a cohesive group, the desire for harmony and consensus suppresses dissent and critical evaluation, producing poor decisions (symptoms include illusions of invulnerability, self-censorship, and pressure on dissenters).
  • Group polarisation: group discussion shifts members toward a more extreme version of their initial leaning (a cautious group gets more cautious; a risky group takes the "risky shift").
Exam trap

Social facilitation helps on easy/well-learned tasks; the same audience hurts on hard/novel tasks (social inhibition). Social loafing is reduced effort when pooled; groupthink is suppressed dissent for harmony; group polarisation is the shift to extremes. Polarisation makes groups more extreme, not more moderate.

The Bystander Effect

Darley and Latane, prompted by the Kitty Genovese case, found that the more bystanders present at an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help, and the slower the help. Two processes drive it:

  • Diffusion of responsibility: with others present, each person feels less personal obligation to act ("someone else will").
  • Pluralistic ignorance: people look to others to define the situation; if everyone stays calm (each thinking the others know it is not an emergency), the group collectively misreads the event as a non-emergency.
High-yield

The bystander effect: helping decreases as the number of bystanders increases. Mechanisms: diffusion of responsibility (no one feels obliged) and pluralistic ignorance (everyone's calm cues read as "no emergency").

G. Personality: Trait and Social-Cognitive Theories

Personality is the characteristic, relatively enduring pattern of thinking, feeling, and behaving that distinguishes one person from another. Trait theories describe its stable dimensions; social-cognitive theories stress the interaction of person and situation.

Trait Theories

  • Gordon Allport distinguished three levels of traits by how pervasively they shape a person:
    • Cardinal traits: a single dominant trait that organises a whole life (rare; "Machiavellian", "narcissistic"). Most people have none.
    • Central traits: the handful of core characteristics that describe a person (honest, kind, anxious): the basic units of personality.
    • Secondary traits: situation-specific preferences and attitudes that appear only in particular contexts (gets impatient in queues).
  • Raymond Cattell used factor analysis to reduce surface descriptions to underlying dimensions:
    • Surface traits: clusters of observable behaviours that go together.
    • Source traits: the deeper, underlying factors (the "source" of surface traits) isolated by factor analysis. Cattell identified 16 source traits, operationalised in the 16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor) Questionnaire.
  • Hans Eysenck's PEN model proposed a small set of biologically based super-factors:
    • Psychoticism (toughminded, aggressive, antisocial), Extraversion (sociable, outgoing), and Neuroticism (emotionally unstable, anxious).
    • Eysenck rooted Extraversion in cortical arousal: introverts have higher baseline arousal via the Reticular Activating System (RAS), so they are already over-stimulated and seek quiet; extraverts are chronically under-aroused and seek stimulation. Neuroticism he linked to a labile autonomic (limbic) nervous system.
  • Costa and McCrae's Five-Factor Model, measured by the NEO-PI-R, is the modern consensus: five broad dimensions, the OCEAN traits:
    • Openness to experience, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.
Memory aid

The Big Five spell OCEAN (Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism). Eysenck's three spell PEN (Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism). Cattell's number is 16 (the 16PF). Allport's three are Cardinal, Central, Secondary.

Exam trap

Counter-intuitively, Eysenck held that introverts have higher baseline cortical arousal (via the RAS), so they avoid extra stimulation, while extraverts are under-aroused and seek it. Do not say extraverts are the highly aroused ones. Cattell = 16PF and the surface/source trait distinction; Costa and McCrae = the Big Five (NEO-PI-R, OCEAN).

Social-Cognitive Theories

  • Albert Bandura's reciprocal determinism: behaviour is not caused by the person or the situation alone; instead three factors continuously influence each other in interlocking loops: behaviour, personal/cognitive factors (beliefs, expectations), and the environment. Each shapes and is shaped by the others (a triadic, two-way causation).
  • Self-efficacy: Bandura's term for a person's belief in their own capacity to execute the actions needed to achieve a goal. High self-efficacy predicts greater effort, persistence, and resilience; it is built through mastery experiences, vicarious experience (modelling), social persuasion, and managing physiological arousal.
  • Julian Rotter's locus of control: a generalised expectancy about the source of reinforcement. People with an internal locus believe outcomes follow from their own actions and effort; those with an external locus believe outcomes are governed by luck, fate, or powerful others. Internals tend to take more responsibility and cope more actively.
High-yield

Reciprocal determinism = the triadic, mutual causation of behaviour, person (cognition), and environment (Bandura). Self-efficacy = belief in one's capability to succeed (Bandura). Locus of control = internal (I control outcomes) versus external (luck/fate controls them) (Rotter). Keep self-efficacy (Bandura) and locus of control (Rotter) attributed to the right theorist.

Exam trap

Self-efficacy and locus of control sound similar but differ: self-efficacy is task-specific belief in your ability to perform (Bandura); locus of control is a global expectancy about where reinforcement comes from, internal versus external (Rotter). The paper will offer the wrong theorist for each as a distractor.

7.1 Orientation: the six darshanas and where psychology lives

Classical Indian thought is organised into six orthodox philosophical systems, the shad darshanas ("six visions"). They are āstika ("orthodox") schools because they accept the authority of the Vedas, as opposed to the nāstika ("heterodox") systems such as Buddhism, Jainism, and the materialist Charvaka, which do not. The six are traditionally paired: Nyaya and Vaisheshika (logic and atomism), Samkhya and Yoga, and Mimamsa and Vedanta (ritual interpretation and the philosophy of the Upanishads).

You do not need to master all six. What you need is to know which schools carry the psychological content, the material about the self, mind, consciousness, and personality. That content sits in three. Samkhya gives the metaphysical scaffolding: the two ultimate categories Purusha and Prakriti, and the three gunas that make up everything in nature, including the mind. Yoga (the system of Patanjali) turns Samkhya's map into an applied psychology, a practical method for controlling and stilling the mind. Vedanta (the philosophy distilled from the Upanishads) gives the model of the self in layers (the koshas), the states of consciousness, the inner instrument (antahkarana), and the identity of Atman and Brahman.

So when the paper says "Indian psychology", it does not mean a separate modern discipline. It means the psychological doctrines embedded in these traditions: how they conceive the self, the structure of the mind, the nature of consciousness, and the typology of personality. Everything below is that content, taught from the system it comes from.

7.2 The Trigunas (from Sāṁkhya): the three strands of nature

Samkhya holds that all of manifest nature, Prakriti, is woven from three fundamental qualities or strands called the gunas. The word guna literally means "strand" or "rope", and the image is exactly that: three strands twisted together into the single rope of experienced reality. The three are Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas.

Sattva is the quality of purity, clarity, harmony, balance, and knowledge. When sattva dominates, the mind is calm, luminous, and discerning; it is associated with wisdom, contentment, and lightness. Rajas is the quality of activity, passion, movement, and restlessness. When rajas dominates, there is drive, ambition, desire, agitation, and emotional turbulence; it is the principle of motion and craving. Tamas is the quality of inertia, dullness, heaviness, and darkness. When tamas dominates, there is laziness, sleep, confusion, ignorance, and resistance to change; it is the principle that obscures and weighs down.

The crucial doctrinal point, which the paper loves to test, is that everyone has all three gunas in varying proportion. The gunas are not three types of people in a rigid sense; they are three ingredients present in every mind and every object, and a person's temperament reflects which one currently predominates. They are also in constant flux: a single person is more sattvic in calm clear moments, more rajasic when driven or angry, more tamasic when dull or sleepy. Because the gunas belong to Prakriti, they characterise the body and mind, never the pure witnessing consciousness (this is the link to 7.3 below).

Indian-approaches research has operationalised this into formal psychometrics. The Guna scales (for example, instruments developed in the Indian psychology literature to measure sattva, rajas, and tamas as personality dimensions) attempt to assess a person's guna profile much as Western inventories assess traits. You should recognise that the trigunas are not only a philosophical idea but a measured personality model in the formal study of Indian approaches to personality.

7.3 Purusha vs Prakriti: Sāṁkhya dualism

Samkhya is fundamentally a dualist system. It says reality reduces to two ultimate, irreducible categories. Purusha is pure consciousness, the witness. It is inactive, attributeless, changeless, and merely observes. Importantly, in classical Samkhya Purusha is plural: there are many purushas, one for each conscious being, unlike Vedanta's single universal consciousness. Purusha does nothing; it simply illuminates, the way a lamp lights a room without acting on it.

Prakriti is primordial matter or nature, the active, creative principle. Prakriti is unconscious but dynamic, and it is Prakriti that carries the three gunas described above. In its unmanifest state the three gunas are in perfect equilibrium; when that balance is disturbed (in the presence of Purusha), Prakriti begins to evolve and unfolds into the whole manifest world, intellect, ego, mind, the senses, the elements, the body. Everything you can perceive, including your own mind and personality, is a product of Prakriti.

The manifest world arises from the interaction of these two. Purusha's mere presence, like a magnet near iron filings, disturbs Prakriti's equilibrium and sets evolution in motion; consciousness gets "reflected" in the evolving mind, producing the illusion that the witness is the doer. Bondage is precisely this confusion: pure consciousness mistaking itself for the activities of Prakriti. Liberation (kaivalya, "aloneness/isolation") is achieved through discriminative knowledge (viveka), the clear discernment that separates Purusha from Prakriti, so that consciousness recognises it was never the doer and rests in its own nature.

7.4 The Pancha Kosha: the five sheaths of the self

The doctrine of the five sheaths comes from the Taittiriya Upanishad and is a central Vedantic model of the self. The idea is that the true Self (Atman) is wrapped in five concentric coverings or koshas ("sheaths"), arranged from the grossest and most external to the subtlest and most internal, like the layers of an onion. Spiritual realisation involves seeing through each sheath in turn until the Atman, which is none of them, is realised beyond and through them.

The five, in order from gross to subtle, are: Annamaya kosha, the food sheath, the physical body built from and sustained by food (anna = food). Pranamaya kosha, the vital-energy sheath, the layer of breath and life-force (prana) that animates the body. Manomaya kosha, the mental sheath, the layer of mind and emotions, of sensory processing, feeling, and ordinary thought (manas = mind). Vijnanamaya kosha, the wisdom/intellect sheath, the layer of vijnana, intellect, discrimination, judgement, the higher knowing faculty. Anandamaya kosha, the bliss sheath, the subtlest layer, associated with deep peace and bliss (ananda), the covering closest to the Self.

Note the trajectory carefully: it runs body → bliss, gross → subtle, outer → inner. The physical body is outermost because it is the densest; bliss is innermost because it is the subtlest and nearest to Atman. The Atman itself is not a sixth sheath; it is the consciousness that the sheaths cover, realised when one no longer identifies with any of them.

7.5 The Four Avasthas: states of consciousness

The Mandukya Upanishad, the shortest of the principal Upanishads, sets out four states (avasthas) of consciousness, mapping them onto the sacred syllable AUM (OM). The first three are familiar experiential states; the fourth is the transcendent ground.

Jagrat is the waking state, in which consciousness is turned outward to the gross physical world through the senses. Swapna is the dream state, in which consciousness is turned inward and experiences a subtle world of mental impressions, with no external input. Sushupti is deep dreamless sleep, a state of undifferentiated rest in which there are no dreams and no duality of subject and object, experienced as peace but without awareness. And Turiya, literally "the fourth", is pure consciousness itself, the substratum that underlies and transcends the other three. Turiya is not a fourth experience alongside waking, dreaming, and sleeping; it is the silent awareness that is present in and beyond all of them. In the AUM mapping, the three sounds A-U-M correspond to waking, dream, and deep sleep, and Turiya is the silence after the sound of OM, the soundless awareness in which the syllable arises and subsides.

If a question says "the state associated with the silence after OM" or "pure consciousness beyond the other three", the answer is Turiya.

7.6 Antahkarana: the inner instrument of mind

Antahkarana means the "inner instrument" or inner organ, the internal apparatus of mind in Vedantic (and Samkhya-derived) psychology. It is the mental machinery through which consciousness functions, classically analysed into four functions. The key conceptual point is that the antahkarana is an instrument, a tool of Prakriti, not the conscious Self that uses it.

The four functions are: Manas, the sensory and processing mind, which receives sense impressions, coordinates the senses, and handles doubt, desire, and the back-and-forth of ordinary thinking. Buddhi, the intellect, the faculty of discrimination, judgement, decision, and determination, that which concludes and decides after manas has wavered. Ahamkara, the ego or "I-maker" (aham = I), the function that appropriates experience and generates the sense of "I" and "mine", identifying the Self with the body-mind. Chitta, the memory store or "mind-stuff", the reservoir of impressions (samskaras) where past experiences are stored and from which they resurface.

Exam trap

Do not confuse Ahamkara (ego) with Atman (the true Self). Ahamkara is the false "I", the ego-function that mistakes itself for the Self; Atman is the real Self that the ego obscures. The whole point of the antahkarana doctrine is that this fourfold inner instrument is the apparatus of mind, a product of nature, and the Self merely witnesses it. A question may ask "which is the I-maker / ego function?" (answer: Ahamkara) and dangle Atman as a trap, or ask "which is NOT a function of the antahkarana?" with Atman as the correct exclusion. Also keep Manas (processing mind) distinct from Buddhi (deciding intellect), the same mind-vs-intellect split you saw in the koshas.

Memory aid

"Mind Buys A Car": Manas, Buddhi, Ahamkara, Chitta. Manas senses and wavers, Buddhi decides, Ahamkara claims it as "mine", Chitta remembers it.

7.7 Atman and Brahman: the Vedantic identity

Vedanta's central teaching concerns two terms. Atman is the individual true Self, the innermost consciousness, what you really are beneath the koshas and behind the antahkarana. Brahman is the ultimate, infinite reality, the absolute ground of everything. The decisive claim of the Upanishads is that these two are, at the deepest level, identical. This is captured in the great saying (mahavakya) "Tat Tvam Asi", "That thou art", you are That, the individual Self is the universal reality.

The school that takes this most strictly is Advaita Vedanta ("non-dualism"), systematised by Shankara (Adi Shankaracharya). Advaita holds that there is only one reality without a second: Atman is Brahman, and the apparent multiplicity of the world is maya, a superimposition produced by ignorance. Liberation (moksha) is the direct realisation of this non-difference. Note the contrast with Samkhya: Samkhya is dualist (many purushas, separate from prakriti), whereas Advaita Vedanta is strictly non-dualist (one consciousness, Atman = Brahman). Do not let the paper blur these.

Definition

Advaita = "not-two", non-dualism. Atman (individual Self) and Brahman (ultimate reality) are one and the same; the sense of separateness is ignorance. The mahavakya expressing it is Tat Tvam Asi ("That thou art").

7.8 Yoga and Patanjali: the applied psychology of mind-control

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali are the foundational text of the Yoga darshana. Yoga shares Samkhya's metaphysics (Purusha, Prakriti, the gunas) but adds a practical method, which is why it functions as an applied Indian psychology of the mind. Patanjali's most famous definition, in the second sutra, is "yogaḥ citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ": yoga is the stilling (nirodha) of the modifications (vrittis) of the mind-stuff (chitta). The mind is normally a stream of fluctuations, perceptions, thoughts, memories, imaginings; yoga is the systematic quieting of those fluctuations so that the witnessing consciousness rests in its own nature.

The method is the eight limbs (ashtanga): Yama (ethical restraints toward others), Niyama (personal observances/disciplines), Asana (steady posture), Pranayama (regulation of breath), Pratyahara (withdrawal of the senses from objects), Dharana (concentration, fixing attention on a single point), Dhyana (sustained meditation, an unbroken flow of attention), and Samadhi (absorption, the union in which the meditator and object merge and the mind is fully stilled). The eight progress from outer ethical/physical discipline toward inner mental absorption.

Memory aid

Eight limbs in order: "Young Ninjas Always Practise Precise Deep Dedicated Stunts", Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi. The last three (Dharana → Dhyana → Samadhi) are the inner, concentrative trio. And remember the one-line definition: yoga = chitta vritti nirodha, stilling the modifications of the mind.

7.9 The Purusharthas: the indigenous model of motivation and well-being

Indian thought frames human motivation and a well-lived life through the four purusharthas, the legitimate aims of human life. Dharma is duty, righteousness, moral and social order, living in accordance with what is right. Artha is wealth, prosperity, and the material means of life. Kama is pleasure, desire, and the enjoyment of the senses and emotions. Moksha is liberation, release from the cycle of birth and death, the ultimate spiritual goal. Together they form an indigenous model of motivation and well-being: a balanced life pursues prosperity (artha) and pleasure (kama) within the bounds of righteousness (dharma), while orienting ultimately toward liberation (moksha). This contrasts usefully with Western need hierarchies, here the highest aim is explicitly spiritual liberation, not self-actualisation in the secular sense.

Memory aid

Four aims: Dharma (duty), Artha (wealth), Kama (pleasure), Moksha (liberation). Dharma frames the lot; Moksha crowns it.

7.10 RCI history: the tested date pair

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) is the statutory body that regulates and monitors training of rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists) and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register. For Indian clinical psychology this is directly relevant: RCI is the body that licenses and registers clinical psychologists in India, and practising as one requires RCI registration. So its history is fair game, and the paper tests a specific date pair.

The RCI began as a registered society in 1986. At that stage it was a voluntary body without statutory teeth. It was then given statutory status by the Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992, which was enforced/brought into force in 1993. The Act gave RCI the legal authority to regulate training programmes, prescribe standards, register professionals, and take action against unqualified practitioners. (The Act was later amended in 2000 to widen its scope.)
