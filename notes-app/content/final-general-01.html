Leipzig, in the winter of 1879. In a small room of an old university building known as the Konvikt, a doctoral student named Max Friedrich sits in front of a clockwork timer called a Hipp chronoscope, a machine built to measure intervals in thousandths of a second. A stimulus is presented; Friedrich presses a telegraph key. Then the procedure is changed: now he must press the key not the instant he senses the stimulus, but only once he has clearly grasped what it is. The second kind of reaction takes longer, and the extra time, Friedrich and his supervisor reasoned, is the time the mind needs to apperceive, to bring an impression into the clear focus of attention. Trial after trial, the numbers go into a notebook.

His supervisor was a 47-year-old professor of philosophy, Wilhelm Wundt. When Wundt had arrived in Leipzig four years earlier, the university had given him this room to store the apparatus he used in lecture demonstrations. By the winter of 1879 it had become something new: a place where students carried out original experiments on the mind and published them as research. Friedrich's study of the duration of apperception became the first dissertation to come out of that room, and historians who later combed the Leipzig archives dated the start of the work to the last weeks of 1879.

Here is the puzzle that should bother you. Almost nothing in that room was new. The chronoscope had been built for physicists and astronomers. The idea of measuring the time taken by mental acts came from a Dutch physiologist. The word apperception came from a German philosopher who had died in 1716. And at Harvard, in 1875, the same year Wundt got his room, an American named William James had also been given space for psychological apparatus. So why does every textbook in the world, and nearly every entrance paper in India, treat Leipzig in 1879 as the moment psychology was born?

The answer to that question is the subject of this chapter. To find it you need to know where the parts in that room came from: the philosophers who argued for two thousand years about where knowledge comes from, the physiologists who timed nerves and mapped brains, the naturalists who put human beings back among the animals, and the physicists who dared to measure sensation. Then you need to see what Wundt and his students built from those parts, and what the next generation, in Germany and in America, did with it. Remember Max Friedrich pressing his key; we will come back to him at the end of the chapter.

1.1 What Is Psychology?Medium yield · 5 Qs

Ask ten people at a chai stall what psychology is and you will hear ten answers: reading minds, treating madness, understanding why people do what they do. Ask ten textbooks and you will get fewer answers, but you will notice something odd: the textbooks of 1890, 1920 and today do not agree with one another either. The definition of psychology has changed several times, and each change tells you which school was in charge. Entrance papers love these definitions, so we start with them.

From soul to behaviour: a definition that kept changing

The word itself is Greek: psyche (breath, soul) and logos (study or reasoned account). Literally, then, psychology began as "the study of the soul". The Greek philosophers did not use the word as the name of a subject; it first appears in Latin book titles of the sixteenth century (the German scholar Rudolf Goclenius used it in 1590), and the philosopher Christian Wolff (1679 to 1754) made it standard with two books, Psychologia empirica (1732) and Psychologia rationalis (1734). For the Greeks and the medieval scholars, the soul was the principle of life and reason; Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE) wrote the first systematic treatise on it, De Anima (On the Soul), which is why some older Indian textbooks call him the "father of psychology".

From the seventeenth century, philosophers spoke less of the soul and more of the mind: its ideas, its reasoning, its sensations. When psychology became a laboratory science in the nineteenth century, the definition narrowed again. For Wundt and his student Titchener, psychology was the science of conscious experience, studied by trained observers looking inward. William James, in 1890, opened his great textbook with the sentence "Psychology is the Science of Mental Life, both of its phenomena and of their conditions."

Then came the revolt. In 1905 and again in 1908 the British psychologist William McDougall (1871 to 1938) defined psychology as the positive science of the conduct or behaviour of living creatures, and in 1913 John B. Watson declared that psychology should drop consciousness altogether and study only behaviour, by which he meant anything an organism does that can be observed and measured from outside. (Watson and behaviourism are the subject of Chapter 2.) The new definition spread through the textbooks. Robert Sessions Woodworth (1869 to 1962), whom you will meet again in Section 1.7, defined psychology as the scientific study of the activities of the individual in relation to the environment. The definition you will most often find credited in Indian textbooks, "psychology is the scientific study of behaviour", is attributed to the American textbook writer Norman L. Munn.

Watson's definition won battles but lost the war. By the 1960s psychologists were again studying memory, imagery and thinking, and today's standard definition reunites the two halves: psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. Each phrase in that definition is doing work. Behaviour is overt: speaking, walking, blushing, pressing a key. By contrast, mental processes are internal and covert: perceiving, remembering, thinking, dreaming, feeling. You cannot see a student remembering the year of Wundt's laboratory, but you can infer it from what he writes. And scientific means that claims are tested against systematic, public observation that someone else could repeat, not against authority or intuition.

An old classroom quip sums up the whole story: first psychology lost its soul, then it lost its mind, then it lost consciousness, and now it has behaviour, of a sort. Table 1.1 puts the sequence in order.

"In the definition of psychology, mental processes mean ..." The answer is internal, covert processes. "Outward behaviour" and "overt actions and reactions" describe the other half of the definition, and "only animal behaviour" describes neither. A related bank question asks who "first proposed and established psychology as the scientific study of behaviour" and keys N. L. Munn, because Indian textbooks quote that definition from Munn. Historically McDougall (1905, 1908) and Watson (1913) came first; if Watson appears as an option alongside Munn, read the stem closely: "established" behaviourism points to Watson, "the definition" points to Munn. Do not confuse William James (science of mental life) with any behaviour definition.

The four goals: describe, explain, predict, control

Every science tries to do four things with the events it studies, and psychology is no exception. Take one example and push it through all four: a student who "blanks out" in the examination hall at Mysuru even though he knew the material the night before.

  • Describe. First we must say exactly what happens, and to whom: racing heart, sweaty palms, a mind that will not retrieve answers, perhaps in the first ten minutes only. Careful description separates this from simple lack of preparation.
  • Explain. Next we ask why. Perhaps very high arousal and catastrophic thoughts ("If I fail, everything is over") compete with retrieval for limited attention. An explanation is a cause that can be tested.
  • Predict. A good explanation lets us forecast who will blank out and when: students with high scores on a test-anxiety scale, under time pressure, on high-stakes papers.
  • Control (many textbooks now say influence or change). Finally we use the knowledge to change the outcome: practice under timed conditions, relaxation training, rehearsing realistic self-statements. This goal is where clinical psychology lives.

Some textbooks add a fifth goal, improving the quality of life, but the four above are the classic list. Watson put prediction and control at the very centre of his definition, a point we will return to in Chapter 2.

Basic and applied research

Psychologists who study a problem only to understand it are doing basic research; those who study it to solve a practical problem are doing applied research. When Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists of meaningless syllables in the 1880s to discover how quickly memories fade, he was doing basic research; when Lightner Witmer opened a clinic in 1896 to help a boy who could not learn to spell, he was doing applied work. The line is not a wall: Witmer's clinic drew on the basic science of the laboratory, and applied problems constantly raise new basic questions. You will see in Section 1.7 that the split between "pure" and "applied" psychology was one of the main arguments between the first two schools.

The fields of psychology

Modern psychology is a family of specialities. You will study several of them in other books of this series, so here we need only a map (Table 1.2). Two of them deserve a sentence more, because entrance papers test the boundary between them. Put simply, clinical psychology is the assessment, diagnosis and treatment of psychological disorders and distress; its practitioners work with conditions ranging from anxiety to schizophrenia, in hospitals, clinics and private practice. counselling psychology overlaps heavily but has traditionally focused on people in the normal range who face adjustment problems: career choice, relationships, study difficulties, life transitions. In India, the Rehabilitation Council of India licenses clinical psychologists, not counsellors, to work with psychiatric disorders, which is one reason your interviewers will care whether you understand the difference.

The branch that studies the behaviour of animals is comparative psychology: it compares species, often in the laboratory, to understand how behaviour and learning evolved and what is common to humans and other animals. Its biological cousin, ethology, studies animal behaviour in natural settings and is associated with the zoologists Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen. You will see in Section 1.3 that comparative psychology grew directly out of Darwin's work.

Three more names in Table 1.2 are easily confused. The first, evolutionary psychology, explains present-day behaviour and mental mechanisms as adaptations shaped by natural selection, that is, as part of our genetic inheritance from ancestors who faced recurring problems of survival and reproduction. The second, behaviour genetics, asks a narrower question: how much of the variation among people in a trait is due to differences in their genes, studied mainly through twins, families and adoption. The third, differential psychology, is the study of individual differences in abilities and personality; the German psychologist William Stern (1871 to 1938) named it in 1900, but its founder in practice was Francis Galton (Section 1.3).

With a definition and a map in hand, we can ask how this discipline came to exist at all. The German memory researcher Hermann Ebbinghaus put the answer in one famous sentence in 1908: "Psychology has a long past, but only a short history." The long past is two thousand years of philosophy and a century of physiology; the short history begins in rooms like Wundt's. 1.2 Philosophical Roots: A Long PastMedium yield · 5 Qs

Long before anyone measured a reaction time, philosophers had asked the three questions that still organise much of psychology. Where does knowledge come from? How are ideas connected to one another? And how is the mind related to the body? Psychology inherited all three, and the answers given between ancient Athens and eighteenth-century Germany are examined every year in Indian entrance papers. Let us define the main positions first, then meet the people who held them.

On the first question, there are two classic answers. The first, nativism, holds that some knowledge, ideas or abilities are inborn, present before any experience. The second, empiricism, holds that all knowledge comes from experience, delivered through the senses. A third term, rationalism, is often paired with nativism: it holds that reason, not the senses, is the chief source of knowledge, and that the senses can deceive. Keep the two contrasts apart. Nativism versus empiricism is a question about origins (inborn or learned?). Rationalism versus empiricism is a question about the route to knowledge (reasoning or sensing?). In practice the rationalists were usually nativists, and the empiricists were nearly always anti-nativists, which is why exam setters sometimes treat the pairs as one.

Plato and Aristotle

Plato (about 427 to 347 BCE) was a nativist and a rationalist. In his dialogue Meno, Socrates draws a theorem of geometry out of an uneducated slave boy simply by asking him questions, and concludes that the boy was not learning but recollecting knowledge his soul already possessed. For Plato, the senses show us only shifting copies of the true, unchanging Forms; real knowledge comes from reason turning inward. He also divided the soul into three parts (reason, spirit and appetite), an early model of conflict within the mind that you may hear echoed when you study Freud.

Plato's student Aristotle disagreed. In De Anima he treated the soul not as a separate thing trapped in the body but as the form or function of a living body, the way seeing is the function of an eye. And he was an empiricist: the mind at birth, he wrote, is like a writing tablet on which nothing has yet been written, and knowledge begins with the senses. (Hold on to that tablet; John Locke will pick it up two thousand years later.)

Aristotle also gave psychology its first theory of how one idea leads to another. In his short work On Memory and Recollection he observed that when we try to recall something we start from a thing that is similar to it, contrary to it, or was contiguous with it (close to it in time or place). These are the laws of association: similarity, contrast and contiguity, to which later writers added frequency (ideas experienced together often are linked more strongly). Try it on yourself. Hear the name "Sachin" and "Tendulkar" follows by contiguity, because you have always met the two words together; "Virat Kohli" may follow by similarity (another great Indian batsman); and the thought of the slowest batsman you ever watched may follow by contrast.

Exam trap

Aristotle is the empiricist of the pair and Plato the nativist; students often reverse them because Aristotle is called the "father of psychology" (for De Anima) and they assume he must have founded everything. Aristotle's three laws are similarity, contrast, contiguity. David Hume's three laws (below) are resemblance, contiguity in time or place, and cause and effect. If a question lists "contrast", it is Aristotle; if it lists "cause and effect", it is Hume.

Rene Descartes: the body as a machine

As a young man early in the seventeenth century, Rene Descartes (1596 to 1650) walked in the royal gardens at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, near Paris, where hydraulic statues moved, played music and even sprayed water at visitors who stepped on hidden plates in the path. The statues seemed alive, yet they were only pipes, water and valves. Descartes drew a bold conclusion: perhaps the living body is the same kind of machine. Nerves could be tubes, and "animal spirits", a fine fluid stored in the brain's cavities, could flow through them to inflate the muscles.

Using this model, Descartes gave the first clear description of the reflex: an automatic, unlearned response to a stimulus that needs no mind at all. Put your hand near a fire, he said, and the heat moves particles in the skin, which tug on a thread inside the nerve, which opens a tiny valve in the brain, which releases animal spirits that flow back down to the arm muscles and pull the hand away. The details were wrong, but the idea that much behaviour is mechanical stimulus and response would run, through the Russian physiologists, all the way to behaviourism.

The body, then, is a machine, and animals, which Descartes believed had no souls, are nothing but machines (automata). But the human being has something more: a mind, a thinking thing that has no size or location and cannot be doubted. "I think, therefore I am" (in French, je pense, donc je suis), he wrote in his Discourse on Method (1637). This is dualism: mind and body are two different kinds of substance, the thinking substance and the extended (physical) substance. And because the two plainly affect each other (a pinprick causes pain; a decision moves an arm), Descartes was an interactionist. He placed the meeting point in the pineal gland, a small structure deep in the brain, chosen because, unlike most brain structures, it is single rather than paired and sits near the centre of the brain. His last book, The Passions of the Soul (1649), described how bodily events stir the emotions through this gland.

Descartes was also a rationalist and a nativist. He held that certain innate ideas are not learned from experience but are built into the mind: the ideas of God, of the self, of infinity, and the axioms of geometry. You will see why this matters when we reach Kant and, in Chapter 2, the Gestalt psychologists.

Remember

Descartes left psychology four legacies: (1) mechanism and the reflex, which led through reflex physiology to Pavlov and behaviourism; (2) innate ideas, which led through Kant to the nativism of the Gestalt school; (3) dualism, which created the modern mind-body problem; and (4) rationalism, against which the British empiricists reacted. A practice question asks what Descartes' dualism "influenced" and keys behaviourism and Gestalt psychology on exactly this reasoning. "Empiricism and rationalism" is the wrong pairing because Descartes was himself a founder of modern rationalism, and empiricism was the reaction against him.

The British empiricists: Hobbes, Locke, Berkeley and Hume

Across the English Channel a very different tradition grew up, one that insisted the mind contains nothing that did not come in through the senses. Thomas Hobbes (1588 to 1679), best known for his political book Leviathan (1651), was its blunt forerunner. Hobbes was committed to materialism, the view that only matter exists; he held that sensations are motions in the body caused by motions outside it, and that thinking is a "train of thoughts" in which one motion follows another in the order in which they were first experienced. He also argued that human action is driven by the pursuit of pleasure and the avoidance of pain. Many historians call him the founder of British empiricism.

The philosopher who made empiricism a full theory of mind was John Locke (1632 to 1704). Locke tells us that his great book began when a group of five or six friends met in his rooms to discuss a philosophical problem and got nowhere; it struck him that before arguing about what we know, they ought to examine what the human understanding is capable of knowing. Nearly twenty years later he published An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690). Its central claim is that the mind at birth is "white paper, void of all characters", the idea usually summed up by the Latin tabula rasa (blank or scraped tablet). There are no innate ideas. Every idea comes from one of two sources: sensation (experience of the outside world) or reflection (the mind's experience of its own operations, such as thinking and doubting).

Locke divided ideas into simple ideas, which cannot be broken down further (the redness, sweetness and roundness of an apple), and complex ideas, which the mind builds by combining simple ones (the idea "apple" itself). This is the beginning of the "mental chemistry" that runs through the rest of this chapter: complex mental life built from simple elements.

He also distinguished two kinds of qualities in objects. The primary qualities (solidity, extension or size, shape, motion and number) exist in the object itself, and our ideas of them resemble the object. The secondary qualities (colour, sound, taste, smell and warmth) exist only as powers to produce sensations in a perceiver. Locke's own demonstration is worth doing. Put one hand in hot water and the other in cold water for a minute; then plunge both into a bowl of lukewarm water. The same water feels cold to the first hand and warm to the second. Since the water cannot be both hot and cold, warmth must be in the perceiver, not in the water. Its size and shape, by contrast, feel the same to both hands.

Finally, in the fourth edition of the Essay (1700) Locke added a chapter titled "Of the Association of Ideas", giving psychology the phrase. Interestingly, he used association mainly to explain irrational connections: ideas joined by chance or custom (a room where something terrible happened becomes unbearable) that produce prejudice and what he did not hesitate to call a kind of madness.

Indian context

In 1688 the Irish scientist William Molyneux wrote to Locke with a puzzle: if a man born blind, who had learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, were suddenly given sight, could he tell which was which by looking alone? Locke, a good empiricist, said no: the link between seen and felt shape must be learned. For three centuries "Molyneux's problem" could not be tested. Then, in a study published in 2011, Richard Held, Pawan Sinha and colleagues working with Project Prakash in India tested children who had been blind from birth with treatable cataracts. Immediately after surgery the children could see the shapes clearly but could not pick out by sight the shape they had just felt; within days, the link appeared. Locke's answer was largely right, and the learning was fast.

George Berkeley (1685 to 1753), an Irish bishop, pushed Locke's argument to a startling end. If colour and warmth exist only in the perceiver, why not size and shape too? All we ever know are our own perceptions. To exist, for any object, is to be perceived: in Latin, esse est percipi. This position is called subjective idealism or mentalism. Berkeley also made a lasting contribution to the psychology of perception. In An Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision (1709) he argued that we are not born able to see distance; we learn it, by associating visual cues (such as the feeling of the eyes converging on a near object, or blur) with the experience of reaching and walking. This is the first empiricist theory of depth perception, and the nativist reply to it is one of the threads of Chapter 4.

David Hume (1711 to 1776), a Scot, published A Treatise of Human Nature in 1739 and 1740. He divided the contents of the mind into impressions (vivid, immediate experiences) and ideas (faint copies of impressions, as when you remember a taste). Ideas are joined, he said, by three laws of association: resemblance, contiguity in time or place, and cause and effect. He thought of association as a kind of gentle mental force, the mind's equivalent of Newton's gravity. Then he turned the analysis on causation itself and found that we never see one event make another happen; we see only that the two have always been joined (constant conjunction), and the mind forms the habit of expecting one after the other. Even the self, Hume said, is nothing but "a bundle or collection of different perceptions". Hume's scepticism woke the German philosopher Immanuel Kant, as we shall see.

Associationism: Hartley, the two Mills and Bain

Out of British empiricism grew a doctrine with a name of its own. Its name, associationism, refers to the view that complex mental life, including memory, thought and emotion, is built up by linking simple elements (sensations and ideas) according to a few laws, above all contiguity and frequency. Four writers turned it into a system.

David Hartley (1705 to 1757), a physician, published Observations on Man in 1749. He was the first to give association a physiological basis: sensations are vibrations in the nerves and brain, and they leave behind miniature vibrations, which he called vibratiuncles, that are ideas. When two vibrations occur together often, they become linked, so that one later sets off the other. Contiguity, strengthened by repetition, was for Hartley the master law, and he distinguished simultaneous from successive associations. Historians usually credit him with founding associationism as a formal doctrine.

James Mill (1773 to 1836) took the doctrine to its most mechanical extreme in Analysis of the Phenomena of the Human Mind (1829). For him a complex idea is nothing more than the sum of the simple ideas that compose it: the idea of a house is the ideas of bricks, mortar, windows and so on, added together, and the idea of "everything" is all the other ideas at once. This view is called mental mechanics.

His son, John Stuart Mill (1806 to 1873), saw the flaw. In A System of Logic (1843) he argued that ideas can fuse into a whole with properties that none of the parts possessed, just as hydrogen and oxygen combine into water, which is like neither gas. His favourite example was Newton's colour disc: paint the colours of the spectrum on a wheel and spin it, and you see white, which is not experienced as "seven colours added". This is mental chemistry. Think of masala chai. James Mill would say your idea of its taste is tea plus milk plus ginger plus cardamom plus sugar; John Stuart Mill would say the taste of good chai is a new whole that is in none of the ingredients. The younger Mill's idea anticipates both Wundt's creative synthesis (Section 1.5) and the Gestalt slogan that the whole is different from the sum of its parts (Chapter 2).

The last of the associationists, the Scot Alexander Bain (1818 to 1903), brought the tradition to the edge of modern psychology. His The Senses and the Intellect (1855) and The Emotions and the Will (1859) were the standard British textbooks of psychology for half a century, and they tied every mental process to what was then known of the nervous system. Bain described how a random ("spontaneous") movement that happens to bring pleasure is retained and repeated, a trial-and-error account of learning that anticipates Thorndike. In 1876 he founded Mind, the first journal devoted to psychology and philosophy.

Exam trap

Mental mechanics belongs to the father, James Mill; mental chemistry to the son, John Stuart Mill. Do not mix up James Mill with William James, or John Stuart Mill with John Locke. "Tabula rasa" is Locke, not Plato (nativist), Descartes (innate ideas) or Kant (innate categories). If asked the source of knowledge "as per empiricists", the answer is sensory experience; "logical reasoning" and "contemplation" are the rationalists' routes.

The continental rationalists: Leibniz, Herbart and Kant

While the British were building the mind out of sensations, philosophers in Germany insisted that the mind is active, organised and partly unconscious. Their ideas reached Wundt directly, so we need them in some detail.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646 to 1716), co-inventor of the calculus, pictured reality as made of countless monads: simple, indivisible, active units of force, each of which mirrors the whole universe from its own point of view. Monads do not act on one another (they are "windowless"); they are kept in step by a harmony that God arranged in advance. Applied to mind and body, this "pre-established harmony" is a form of parallelism, which we will meet below. More important for psychology was Leibniz's theory of consciousness. He argued that we receive many perceptions too weak to notice individually, which he called petites perceptions (small perceptions). Standing on a beach, you hear the roar of the sea, yet that roar is made of the sounds of thousands of single waves, none of which you could hear on its own. When enough small perceptions add up, they cross into clear awareness, a step Leibniz called apperception. Consciousness is therefore a matter of degree, not all or none, and much mental activity goes on below awareness. Against Locke he wrote, in his New Essays on Human Understanding, that there is nothing in the intellect that was not first in the senses, "except the intellect itself".

Johann Friedrich Herbart (1776 to 1841), who succeeded to Kant's chair at Konigsberg, turned Leibniz's ideas into a kind of mental mechanics with mathematics. Ideas, he said, have strength, and they compete: incompatible ideas push each other down. An idea that is pushed down does not vanish; it sinks below a boundary he called the threshold of consciousness (a limen), where it remains active and strives to return. Fechner would borrow the idea of a threshold for psychophysics, and Freud's repressed ideas that push to return to awareness have a family resemblance to it.

The ideas currently in consciousness form an organised body that Herbart called the apperceptive mass. A new idea is understood, or apperceived, only by being taken up into this mass of existing ideas; an idea that finds nothing related to attach to is not grasped at all. Herbart was also a great educational theorist, and the classroom lesson follows directly: teach new material by linking it to what the child already knows. (His followers later turned this into the "formal steps" of lesson planning, which teacher-training colleges in India still teach.) You have experienced this yourself: the term "apperception" is far easier to learn now, attached to Leibniz's sea and Herbart's competing ideas, than it would be as an isolated word in a list.

Herbart said psychology could be empirical and mathematical, but he did not believe it could be experimental, and he kept it separate from physiology. Within forty years both claims would be overturned in Leipzig.

Exam trap

An IHBAS 2025 question asks what the "apperceptive mass" constitutes and keys pure perception and pre-existing ideas, meaning a new perception taken up into the store of ideas already in the mind. The term is Herbart's; Wundt took over and reworked the idea of apperception, which is why the question names him. The distractors each belong to someone else: "a compound of elemental processes bound by association" is the associationist (and structuralist) picture of the mind; "sensation and pure perception" leaves out the existing ideas that are the whole point; "sensation plus unconscious inference" is Helmholtz's theory of perception (Section 1.3). Apperception as a word was coined by Leibniz.

The most influential German philosopher of all, Immanuel Kant (1724 to 1804), answered Hume in his Critique of Pure Reason (1781). Hume had shown that experience alone can never give us necessary connections such as causality. Kant replied that the mind does not wait passively for experience to write on it; it imposes its own built-in forms on whatever the senses deliver. Space and time are the forms through which we must perceive anything, and categories such as causality, unity and substance are the frames through which we must think about it. We never know things as they are in themselves, only as our minds organise them. This is nativism of a sophisticated kind, and it passed through the perceptual nativists of the nineteenth century into Gestalt psychology.

Kant also issued a challenge. Psychology, he argued, could never be a true natural science, because mental events cannot be measured, cannot be put to experiment, and are distorted by the very act of observing them. The history in the rest of this chapter can be read as a series of replies to Kant: Herbart made psychology mathematical, Fechner made it measurable, and Wundt made it experimental. The mind-body problem

Descartes' dualism created a puzzle that no one has fully solved: if the mind is not physical, how can it move a physical arm, and how can a physical pinprick produce a mental pain? The answers given over the next three centuries form a small set of positions that every psychology student must be able to name (Figure 1.2).

The first, interactionism, is Descartes' own answer: mind and body are different substances that causally affect each other. Its weakness is the one Descartes never solved, namely how something with no physical properties can push on something physical. Some of his followers, notably Nicolas Malebranche, proposed occasionalism: mind and body never act on each other, and God produces the bodily event on each occasion of the mental one, and the reverse.

The second, psychophysical parallelism, holds that mental events and bodily events run side by side, perfectly correlated, but neither causes the other. Leibniz's picture was two clocks that keep identical time because they were set together at the start, not because one drives the other. Parallelism was attractive to the first experimental psychologists because it let them correlate mental events with physiological ones without worrying about causation; Wundt and Titchener both held versions of it.

The third, epiphenomenalism, holds that bodily (brain) events cause mental events, but mental events cause nothing; consciousness is a by-product. Its best-known advocate, the English biologist Thomas Henry Huxley (1825 to 1895), compared consciousness to the whistle of a steam engine: it accompanies the work of the engine but does nothing to drive it.

Finally, monism denies that there are two substances at all. Materialist monism (Hobbes) says only matter exists and mind is a process of the body. Idealist monism (Berkeley) says only minds and their perceptions exist. And double-aspect theory, associated with the Dutch philosopher Baruch Spinoza (1632 to 1677), says there is one underlying reality that shows itself in two ways: seen from the inside it is mind, seen from the outside it is body, as the same curved line is concave from one side and convex from the other. Fechner, the founder of psychophysics, held a view close to this one.

A word on the philosophy of science

Questions about the mind sit inside larger philosophical questions about what exists and how we can know it, and a few terms from the philosophy of science turn up in psychology papers. The term ontology (from the Greek for "being") names the branch of philosophy that deals with existence: what kinds of things are real? Is the mind a thing, a process, or an illusion? By contrast, epistemology deals with knowledge: how do we know what we know, and what counts as good evidence? The nativist-empiricist debate above is an epistemological debate.

In the nineteenth century the French philosopher Auguste Comte (1798 to 1857) founded positivism, the view that genuine knowledge comes only from observable, verifiable facts, and that speculation about unobservable causes should be abandoned. Comte rejected introspection on the ground that the mind cannot observe itself, any more than the eye can see itself, an objection you will meet again when Wundt's method comes under fire. Post-positivism, associated with the twentieth-century philosopher Karl Popper, accepts that all scientific knowledge is fallible and provisional, so that theories are tested by trying to refute them rather than by piling up confirmations. Scientific realism holds that the unobservable entities described by our best theories (electrons, genes, perhaps memory traces) really exist. Of these terms, only ontology is defined by the question of existence.

Philosophy, then, handed psychology its questions and some brilliant guesses. What it could not supply was a way to settle the arguments. For that, psychology had to borrow the methods of a different parent: experimental physiology.

1.3 Physiological and Biological RootsLower yield · 1 Q

By the middle of the nineteenth century, physiologists in Germany, France and Britain were doing something philosophers never had: cutting, stimulating, timing and measuring living nervous systems. Their findings did two things for the future psychology. They showed that the machinery of mind could be studied by experiment, and they gave Wundt, who trained as a physiologist, his methods and his confidence.

Nerves carry the message

The first great discovery was about the wiring. In 1811 the Scottish anatomist Charles Bell (1774 to 1842) described, in a privately printed pamphlet, evidence that the nerve roots entering the spinal cord at the front and at the back have different functions. In 1822 the French physiologist Francois Magendie (1783 to 1855) showed it decisively in experiments on young animals. The result is the Bell-Magendie law: the dorsal (back) roots of the spinal nerves carry sensory information in, and the ventral (front) roots carry motor commands out. The nervous system, in other words, has separate input and output channels, an anatomical basis for the distinction between sensation and movement, and ultimately for the stimulus-response unit. (The two men's quarrel over who deserved credit is a classic example of simultaneous discovery, which we discuss in Section 1.8.)

The most influential physiologist of the age was Johannes Muller (1801 to 1858) of Berlin, whose Handbook of Human Physiology (published in parts from 1833 to 1840) summarised the field for a generation. Muller's best-known idea is the doctrine of specific nerve energies: the quality of a sensation depends not on the stimulus but on which nerve is stimulated. The optic nerve, however it is excited, produces only sensations of light; the auditory nerve only sound. You can test this now: close your eyes and press gently on the outer corner of one eyelid. You will see a patch of light, though no light has entered your eye; pressure on the optic pathway is "read" as light. A blow to the head makes a boxer "see stars" for the same reason. Muller's idea meant that we never perceive the world directly, only the state of our own nerves, a physiological version of the philosophers' point that experience is not the same as the object (the modern form of the idea, "labelled lines", is covered in Chapter 3). Muller's students included Hermann von Helmholtz and Emil du Bois-Reymond, and Wundt himself spent a semester in his Berlin laboratory.

Muller believed that the speed of the nerve impulse was so great that it might never be measured. His student proved him wrong within a decade.

Key study: Helmholtz times the nerve impulse (1850)

Question. Is the nerve impulse effectively instantaneous, like light, or does it take measurable time? Method. Hermann von Helmholtz (1821 to 1894) used a frog's nerve with the muscle still attached. He stimulated the nerve first at a point far from the muscle and then at a point near it, and timed the delay before the muscle twitched. Result. Stimulation farther from the muscle produced a later twitch, and dividing the difference in distance by the difference in time gave a speed of only about 27 metres per second, roughly the speed of a car on a highway. Conclusion. Nervous activity is a physical process that takes time. If nerve conduction takes time, so may perceiving and deciding, and those times can be measured. Caveat. When Helmholtz tried to measure reaction times in human beings, the results varied so much from trial to trial and person to person that he gave up, an early hint of the individual differences that Galton and Cattell would later study.

Helmholtz was perhaps the greatest scientist of the century, and three more of his ideas reach psychology. First, his theory of unconscious inference: perception is not a simple copy of sensation but a conclusion the mind draws, quickly and without awareness, from sensory data and past experience. When you see a distant elephant as huge though its image on your retina is tiny, you are "inferring" its size from its distance. This made Helmholtz an empiricist about perception, and it is the ancestor of modern ideas of perception as hypothesis-testing (Chapter 4). Second, building on a suggestion made by the English physician Thomas Young (1773 to 1829) in 1802, Helmholtz developed the trichromatic theory of colour vision, the Young-Helmholtz theory, which holds that colour vision depends on three types of receptor, most sensitive to different parts of the spectrum. Third, he proposed a resonance (place) theory of hearing. All three are developed in Chapters 3 and 4. For this chapter, remember that Wundt worked as Helmholtz's assistant at Heidelberg for several years.

Exam trap

The just noticeable difference belongs to Weber and Fechner (Section 1.4); the law of effect to Thorndike (Chapter 6). "Proposed the concept of the unconscious" is a trap because Helmholtz did propose unconscious inference, but the concept of the unconscious mind in the psychodynamic sense belongs to Freud (and its philosophical roots to Leibniz and Herbart).

Mapping functions onto the brain

If the nerves are wires, is the brain a single organ that does everything everywhere, or a collection of specialised parts? The modern answer, localisation of function, emerged through a strange mixture of quackery and careful science.

The quackery came first. The Viennese physician Franz Joseph Gall (1758 to 1828) was a skilled brain anatomist, but he is remembered for a bolder claim: that the brain is made up of separate organs for separate mental faculties (such as acquisitiveness, combativeness or love of offspring), that a well-developed faculty means a larger organ, and that a larger organ pushes out the skull above it. By feeling the bumps on a person's head, therefore, one could read his character. Gall's collaborator Johann Gaspar Spurzheim (1776 to 1832) popularised the system under the name phrenology and carried it across Europe and America, where "phrenological parlours" read heads for a fee. Phrenology was wrong in its method (skull shape does not reflect the brain beneath, and Gall chose his evidence selectively), but it was right in its central assumption that different parts of the brain do different things.

Phrenology's first scientific critic was the French physiologist Pierre Flourens (1794 to 1867). In the 1820s he used the method of ablation (also called extirpation): surgically removing a part of the brain of an animal, usually a pigeon or rabbit, and observing what behaviour was lost. He found real localisation at the level of large structures: damage to the cerebellum disrupted the coordination of movement, damage to the medulla stopped breathing and heartbeat, and removal of the cerebral hemispheres abolished perception and voluntary action. But within the cerebral hemispheres he found no faculties in particular places: the effects of damage seemed to depend on how much tissue was removed, and the cortex seemed to act as a whole. Flourens' ablation method became standard in physiological psychology, and his view of the cortex as acting as a whole was revived a century later by Karl Lashley (covered in the Physiological Psychology book).

Localisation within the cortex was finally demonstrated through language. In 1861 the French surgeon Paul Broca (1824 to 1880) examined a patient named Leborgne, known in the hospital as "Tan" because that was nearly the only syllable he could say. Tan understood much of what was said to him but could not produce speech. When he died, Broca's autopsy found damage in the left frontal lobe, in a region now called Broca's area, and by 1865 Broca had concluded that "we speak with the left hemisphere". In 1874 the 26-year-old German neurologist Carl Wernicke (1848 to 1905) described the opposite pattern: patients with damage to the left temporal lobe who spoke fluently but meaninglessly and could not understand speech. Broca's aphasia is effortful, non-fluent speech with relatively preserved comprehension; Wernicke's aphasia is fluent speech with poor comprehension. (The aphasias, including the transcortical and conduction forms, are covered in the Physiological Psychology book.)

Meanwhile, in 1870, the German physicians Gustav Fritsch (1838 to 1927) and Eduard Hitzig (1838 to 1907) applied weak electric currents to the exposed cortex of dogs and found a strip of the frontal lobe where stimulation produced movements of specific body parts on the opposite side of the body: the motor cortex. Electrical stimulation now joined ablation and clinical autopsy as a method of mapping the brain. Table 1.4 brings the localisation story together.

Charles Darwin: the human animal

Physiology showed that the mind had machinery. Biology showed where the machinery came from. When Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882) published On the Origin of Species in 1859, he proposed that species change over generations through natural selection: individuals vary, some variations help their owners survive and reproduce, and those variations become more common in later generations. In The Descent of Man (1871) he applied the theory to human beings, and in The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872) he argued that facial expressions of emotion are inherited and universal, shared with other animals because they once served adaptive purposes (baring the teeth in anger is a remnant of preparing to bite). He even published a diary of his infant son's development (1877), one of the first "baby biographies".

Darwin gave psychology three gifts, and each grew into a branch of the discipline.

  • Continuity between species. If humans evolved from other animals, then animal minds differ from ours in degree, not kind, and studying animals can tell us about ourselves. This is the foundation of comparative psychology and of all later animal research on learning.
  • Function. If a trait exists because it helped survival, then the right question to ask about any mental process is what it is for. This question is the heart of functionalism (Section 1.7), and through functionalism, of today's evolutionary psychology.
  • Variation. Natural selection works on differences between individuals, so those differences are worth measuring. This idea was taken up by Darwin's own half-cousin.

Francis Galton and the measurement of individual differences

Francis Galton (1822 to 1911) shared a grandfather, the physician-poet Erasmus Darwin, with Charles Darwin. Independently wealthy, he never held a university post, and he could not see anything without wanting to count it: he once tried to make a "beauty map" of Britain by secretly tallying attractive and unattractive women he passed in different towns. After reading the Origin, he devoted himself to human variation, and in doing so he founded the study of individual differences.

His first major book, Hereditary Genius (1869), traced the families of eminent judges, statesmen, scientists and others, and found that eminence ran in families far more than chance would predict. Galton concluded that ability is inherited. The flaw is obvious to you now: eminent families also pass on wealth, education and connections, so his data could not separate heredity from environment. Galton saw the problem himself and, in 1875, proposed studying twins to separate the effects of "nature and nurture" (a phrase he popularised). He was the first to use twin studies for this purpose.

Key study: Galton's anthropometric laboratory (1884)

Question. How much do people differ in basic physical and mental capacities? Method. At the International Health Exhibition in London in 1884, Galton set up an anthropometric laboratory where, for threepence, visitors were measured on height, weight, arm span, breathing capacity, strength of grip, swiftness of a blow, keenness of sight and hearing, colour discrimination, reaction time and the highest pitch they could hear (tested with a whistle he designed). More than 9,000 people passed through. Reasoning. Following the empiricist logic that all knowledge enters through the senses, Galton assumed that people with sharper senses and quicker reactions would be more intelligent. Result. He obtained the first large body of data on human individual differences, and to analyse it he had to invent new statistics. Criticism. The assumption linking sensory keenness to intelligence turned out to be wrong, as Cattell's followers discovered (Section 1.7); intelligence testing had to wait for Binet's very different approach (Chapter 9).

The statistics may be Galton's greatest legacy. Studying the heights of parents and children (and, earlier, the sizes of sweet-pea seeds), he noticed that very tall parents tend to have children who are tall but less extreme than themselves, a phenomenon now called regression to the mean (1886). From this he developed the idea of correlation, a measure of how closely two variables vary together (1888); his younger colleague Karl Pearson (1857 to 1936) later produced the formula for the correlation coefficient that you will use in the Research Methodology book. Galton also pioneered the questionnaire (asking people to describe the vividness of their mental image of that morning's breakfast table), and in 1879 he devised the word association test: he read a word and noted the first association it called up, and was startled to find how many of his own associations reached back to childhood and revealed thoughts he would rather not publish. Carl Jung later developed word association into a clinical technique (the Personality book).

There is a darker side. In 1883 Galton coined the word eugenics for the programme of improving the human stock by encouraging the "fit" to have more children and discouraging the "unfit". Eugenics was later used to justify forced sterilisation and, in Nazi Germany, mass murder. Galton's statistics are permanent tools of psychology; his eugenics is a permanent warning.

Animal minds: from anecdote to Morgan's canon

Darwin's idea of continuity invited a new question: how intelligent are animals? Darwin's young friend George John Romanes (1848 to 1894) set out to answer it, and his Animal Intelligence (1882) is usually treated as the first book of comparative psychology. Romanes gathered stories, from naturalists, pet owners and newspaper readers, of animals doing clever things: a cat that opened a door latch, a dog that seemed to plan. This anecdotal method had two weaknesses. Anecdotes are selected (no one writes in to report a dog doing something stupid), and they invite anthropomorphism, reading human thoughts and feelings into animal behaviour.

The correction came from the British psychologist Conwy Lloyd Morgan (1852 to 1936). Morgan's own fox terrier, Tony, learned to open the garden gate by lifting the latch with his head. A visitor who saw only the finished act would have credited Tony with understanding how latches work, but Morgan had watched the learning: it came slowly, through many chance successes while Tony poked his head through the railings. In An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894) Morgan stated the rule now called Morgan's canon: "In no case may we interpret an action as the outcome of the exercise of a higher psychical faculty, if it can be interpreted as the outcome of the exercise of one which stands lower in the psychological scale." It is a principle of parsimony: prefer the simplest explanation that fits the facts.

Key study: Clever Hans (1904 to 1907)

The claim. In Berlin, a retired schoolteacher, Wilhelm von Osten, exhibited a horse called Hans that seemed to add, subtract and answer questions by tapping its hoof. A commission that included the psychologist Carl Stumpf found no trickery in 1904. Method. Stumpf's student Oskar Pfungst then tested Hans systematically, varying whether the questioner knew the answer and whether Hans could see the questioner. Result. Hans answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and was visible. Without meaning to, questioners tensed as the taps began and relaxed slightly, or raised their heads, when the right number was reached, and Hans had learned to stop at that cue. Conclusion (published 1907). The horse was clever, but at reading people, not at arithmetic: a perfect illustration of Morgan's canon. The "Clever Hans effect" is why modern experiments use observers who do not know which condition a participant is in.

Comparative psychology soon moved from anecdote into the laboratory. Edward Thorndike's puzzle-box studies of cats (1898, Chapter 6) and Margaret Floy Washburn's textbook The Animal Mind (1908, Section 1.7) carried it into the twentieth century, and Watson would build behaviourism on it.

Clinical link

Morgan's canon is good clinical sense. A child who will not read aloud in class may have a reading disability, poor eyesight, anxiety or a hearing problem before we conclude that he is "defiant" or "lazy". Test the simpler, lower-level explanations first. You will see in Section 1.7 that the first patient at the world's first psychological clinic turned out to have exactly such a simple problem.

1.4 Measuring the Mind: Psychophysics and Mental ChronometryLower yield · 4 Qs

Kant had said that mental events can never be measured, and for a long time no one could prove him wrong. The proof, when it came, came from two professors at the University of Leipzig, and it took the form of a simple idea: you cannot put a ruler against a sensation, but you can measure the stimuli that produce it and find the rule that links the two. This section covers that idea and the parallel discovery that mental acts take measurable time. The full treatment of thresholds and psychophysical laws is in Chapter 3; here we need only enough to see why they made a science of mind possible.

Ernst Weber and the just noticeable difference

Ernst Heinrich Weber (1795 to 1878) was a Leipzig physiologist who studied the sense of touch. Touch two points of a pair of compasses to your fingertip and you will feel two points even when they are only a few millimetres apart; touch them to the middle of your back and they must be several centimetres apart before you feel two rather than one. This "two-point threshold" was one of Weber's discoveries. The more important one came from lifting weights. Weber asked people to compare a standard weight with slightly heavier ones and found the smallest difference they could detect, the just noticeable difference (JND). The JND was not a fixed amount but a fixed proportion of the standard weight.

A worked example makes the point. Suppose that for lifted weights the proportion is one-fortieth. If you are holding 40 grams, you will just notice the change when one gram is added. If you are holding 400 grams, adding one gram is now undetectable; you need ten. In symbols, the JND (written delta I) divided by the original intensity (I) is a constant, k. Fechner later named this relation Weber's law. You know it from daily life: a single candle lights up a dark room, but carried into a brightly lit wedding hall it makes no visible difference.

Gustav Fechner and the founding of psychophysics

Gustav Theodor Fechner (1801 to 1887) was a professor of physics at Leipzig who suffered a long and severe breakdown in the late 1830s and early 1840s, made worse by damage to his eyes from gazing at the sun through coloured glass while studying afterimages. When he recovered he turned to philosophy, and in particular to the relation between mind and matter, which he believed to be two sides of one reality. On the morning of 22 October 1850, lying in bed, he had the insight that would make his name: the relation between the bodily and the mental could be expressed as a mathematical law. If equal ratios of stimulus intensity produce equal steps in sensation (as Weber's data implied), then sensation must grow with the logarithm of the stimulus. This is Fechner's law, usually written S = k log I, where S is the strength of the sensation and I the intensity of the stimulus.

Push a case through it. Switch on one bulb in a dark room, then a second: the room gets noticeably brighter. To get the same apparent increase again you need not three bulbs but four, and then eight, then sixteen. Sensation climbs in equal steps while the stimulus doubles at each step. That is what a logarithmic relation means.

Fechner spent ten years testing his insight and published the results as Elements of Psychophysics in 1860. He defined psychophysics as the exact science of the functional relations between body and mind, and he gave it three methods that are still taught today: the method of limits, the method of constant stimuli and the method of adjustment (average error), together with the concepts of the absolute threshold and the difference threshold (all in Chapter 3). Researchers in psychophysics still celebrate 22 October as "Fechner Day".

One point about thresholds is worth fixing now, because it matters for perception as much as for psychophysics. Whether a stimulus is perceived depends not only on how strong it is but on how long it lasts. A faint flash of light, or the contour (edge) of a briefly shown shape, is seen only if the stimulation reaches a minimum intensity and is present for a minimum exposure time; for very brief exposures the two trade off, so that a weaker flash must last longer to be seen (Bloch's law, 1885). This is why the early laboratories controlled exposure time so carefully with the tachistoscope. Reaction time, by contrast, measures how quickly a person responds once a stimulus has been perceived (as you will see below); it is not a condition for perceiving anything.

Why does Fechner matter so much to the history? Because he answered Kant. He showed that mental events could be measured, indirectly but precisely, and that measurement could be repeated by anyone. Wundt, Ebbinghaus and G. E. Muller all built on his book. The historian Edwin Garrigues Boring (1886 to 1968) even argued that 1860, not 1879, could be taken as the founding date of experimental psychology. The convention, however, is clear: Fechner founded psychophysics; Wundt founded psychology as an independent experimental discipline.

Exam trap

The "founder of psychophysics" is Fechner, not Weber (whose work Fechner built on and named), not Wundt (who founded the laboratory) and not Ebbinghaus (who applied Fechner's spirit to memory). The JND is Weber's concept; S = k log I is Fechner's law. Keep 1850 (the insight, 22 October) separate from 1860 (the book).

The personal equation and mental chronometry

The second line of measurement began in an observatory. In 1796 the Astronomer Royal at Greenwich, Nevil Maskelyne, dismissed his assistant David Kinnebrook because Kinnebrook's timings of the moment a star crossed a hair line in the telescope were consistently later than his own, by most of a second. Some twenty-five years later the German astronomer Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel compared the timings of several experienced astronomers and found that each had his own systematic error. No one was simply right; every observer took a characteristic amount of time to see and respond. Astronomers called the correction for each observer his personal equation. Here, in effect, was the first measurement of individual differences in reaction time, and the first hint that perceiving takes time.

Helmholtz had shown that nerves take time. The Dutch physiologist Franciscus Cornelis Donders (1818 to 1889) asked whether the time taken by a mental process itself could be measured, and in 1868 he devised a way. His subtraction method compares reaction times in tasks that differ by exactly one mental stage (Figure 1.3). In the a-reaction (simple reaction time), one light appears and the participant presses a key as fast as possible: the task needs only detection and response. In the c-reaction (go/no-go), one of two lights appears and the participant must respond to one of them only: now the stimulus must also be identified. In the b-reaction (choice reaction time), each light has its own key: the stimulus must be identified and a response must be chosen. Subtract the a-time from the c-time and you have the time needed to identify the stimulus; subtract the c-time from the b-time and you have the time needed to choose the response.

Donders had founded mental chronometry, the measurement of the time course of mental processes through reaction time (the interval between the onset of a stimulus and the beginning of the response). Its weak point is marked at the foot of Figure 1.3: the method assumes that inserting a stage leaves the other stages exactly as they were, which is often untrue. A century later the cognitive psychologist Saul Sternberg replaced it with a subtler method that does not need this assumption (Chapter 8). But the idea that the mind can be studied by timing it was immediately taken up in Leipzig. When Max Friedrich pressed his key in the winter of 1879, he was running a version of Donders' experiment, trying to measure the time taken by apperception.

Hermann Ebbinghaus: the higher processes measured

Wundt, as you will see, believed that the higher mental processes such as memory and thought could not be studied by experiment. A young German scholar proved him wrong without ever working in his laboratory. Hermann Ebbinghaus (1850 to 1909) came across a second-hand copy of Fechner's Elements of Psychophysics while travelling in France and England in the late 1870s (accounts differ on whether the bookshop was in Paris or London), and resolved to do for memory what Fechner had done for sensation. To remove the effect of meaning and prior knowledge, he invented nonsense syllables, consonant-vowel-consonant combinations such as DAX or BOK, and memorised lists of them himself, under strictly controlled conditions, for several years. He measured memory by the savings method: how much less time it took to relearn a list than it had taken to learn it the first time. The result, published in On Memory (1885), included the first forgetting curve, showing rapid loss in the first hour and slower loss thereafter.

Ebbinghaus worked alone, at Berlin and later at Breslau, outside any school, and he is the author of the sentence quoted at the end of Section 1.1 ("Psychology has a long past, but only a short history", 1908). You will meet his curve and his methods properly in Chapter 7.

Exam trap

"What do Hermann Ebbinghaus, Francis Galton and Sigmund Freud share in common?" The options are (a) each worked independently and was a pioneer, (b) each founded a formal psychology laboratory, (c) each developed novel methods to study how the mind works, and (d) (a) and (c). Option (b) is false: Freud never founded a laboratory. Both (a) and (c) are true: Ebbinghaus invented nonsense syllables and the savings method, Galton invented the questionnaire, word association and correlation, and Freud invented free association. So the best answer is (d), which is how the Panwar bank keys it. The RML 2020 paper printed the same question and keyed (a). Since (a) alone leaves out a true statement, (d) is the more defensible answer; if you meet this question again, choose (d), and do not be shaken if a key prefers (a).

By 1870, then, all the parts were on the bench. Philosophy had supplied the questions and the idea of mental elements and their association; physiology had supplied the experimental method and the reaction-time technique; biology had supplied the idea of function and the interest in individual differences; and psychophysics had shown that the mind could be measured. What was missing was someone to declare that these belonged together as a new, independent science, and to build an institution for it.

1.5 Wilhelm Wundt and the Founding of Experimental PsychologyMedium yield · 6 Qs

Psychology does have a founder in the way that few sciences do, a single person who set out deliberately to create it. Wundt is remembered as the father of experimental psychology, and also, without qualification, as the father of psychology as a science. To understand why, you need to know what he did and what he believed.

From physiologist to psychologist

Wilhelm Maximilian Wundt (1832 to 1920) was born on 16 August 1832 at Neckarau, near Mannheim in south-western Germany, the son of a Lutheran pastor. He was a lonely, daydreaming schoolboy who did poorly at first, but he became an intensely hard-working student of medicine at Heidelberg, with a semester in Berlin in Johannes Muller's laboratory. After qualifying in medicine he chose research over practice and spent several years at Heidelberg as an assistant to Helmholtz.

During those Heidelberg years his plan took shape. In Contributions to the Theory of Sensory Perception (published in parts from 1858 to 1862) he called for an experimental psychology; in 1862 he began teaching a course on psychology as a natural science; and in 1863 he published Lectures on the Human and Animal Mind. His decisive book was Principles of Physiological Psychology (Grundzuge der physiologischen Psychologie), published in two parts in 1873 and 1874. Its preface announced that the work was "an attempt to mark out a new domain of science". Historians rank it among the most important books in the history of modern psychology, and Wundt revised it through six editions.

After a year at Zurich, Wundt moved in 1875 to Leipzig as professor of philosophy, the post he held for the rest of his career. The room for apparatus followed, then in 1879 the research of students like Max Friedrich, then a journal, Philosophische Studien (Philosophical Studies), founded in 1881 to publish the laboratory's work and usually counted as the first journal of experimental psychology. Wundt also wrote a shorter textbook, Outlines of Psychology (1896), and, in his last twenty years, the ten volumes of his Volkerpsychologie (1900 to 1920). Boring later calculated that Wundt published more than 50,000 printed pages in his lifetime. He died on 31 August 1920, shortly after finishing his memoirs.

Exam trap

Wundt's 1873 to 1874 book is Principles of Physiological Psychology; William James's 1890 book is The Principles of Psychology. Questions asking who wrote "Principles of Psychology" want James. And "physiological psychology" in Wundt's title did not mean the study of the brain. It meant psychology that borrows the experimental methods of physiology. Do not answer a question about Wundt's lab with "brain research".

What Wundt studied: immediate experience and voluntarism

Every science, Wundt said, studies experience, but from different standpoints. The physicist studies mediate experience: experience used as a means of learning about something else, filtered through instruments and concepts. Psychology studies immediate experience: experience as it is directly lived, before interpretation. Look at a red hibiscus. A physicist describes the light it reflects as electromagnetic radiation of a certain wavelength; a psychologist in Wundt's sense describes the experience itself, the quality of the redness, its intensity, how long it lasts, whether it is pleasant.

Wundt's analysis of immediate experience found two kinds of elements. Sensations are the elementary contents produced when a sense organ is stimulated, varying in quality and intensity. Simple feelings are the subjective accompaniments of sensations, such as the pleasantness of a taste or the strain of waiting. Elements combine into compounds: ideas, emotions, acts of will. Psychology's task was to analyse conscious processes into their elements, to discover how the elements are connected, and to state the laws of those connections.

So far this sounds like British associationism in a laboratory coat. But Wundt insisted that the mind is not a passive meeting place where elements stick together by contiguity. It is active, and the activity is that of attention and will. He called his system voluntarism, from the Latin voluntas (will). The central active process is apperception, the term he took from Leibniz and Herbart and made his own. Wundt distinguished the broad field of consciousness from its narrow focus. Contents that merely enter the field are perceived; contents brought into the focus by attention are apperceived. Sit in an examination hall: the whirr of the ceiling fans, a cough two rows back and the invigilator's footsteps are all perceived, at the edge of consciousness; the question in front of you is apperceived, held in the clear focus where you can work on it.

Apperception does more than select; it also combines. Wundt's principle of creative synthesis (also called the law of psychic resultants) states that when elements are combined by apperception, the result has properties that none of the elements possessed. A melody is not just the sum of its notes, since the same notes in a different order make a different tune; the word "SAHIL" is not just five letters. This is John Stuart Mill's mental chemistry placed at the centre of a psychology, and it is why Wundt cannot fairly be called a mere elementist.

Remember

Wundt's own name for his system was voluntarism. Many Indian textbooks list Wundt as the founder of structuralism, because Titchener carried his work to America and gave it that name; in exam questions that pair "structuralism" with Wundt and Titchener, accept the pairing. But if a question asks for the system that stresses will, attention and apperception, the answer is voluntarism, and the person is Wundt.

The tridimensional theory of feeling

One of Wundt's best-known ideas came from listening to a metronome. As he attended to its clicks, he noticed that some rhythmic patterns felt more pleasant than others; that while he waited for the next click he felt a slight strain, which was released when the click came; and that a fast tempo felt stirring while a slow one felt calming. From these self-observations he concluded, in 1896, that simple feelings vary along three independent dimensions: pleasure versus displeasure, tension versus relaxation, and excitement versus depression (sometimes translated excitement versus calm). This is the tridimensional theory of feeling (Figure 1.4). Any feeling can be described as a point in this three-dimensional space, and an emotion is a sequence of feelings moving through it over time. Waiting outside the result board after an examination, for example, might be unpleasant, tense and excited all at once; the moment you find your roll number, the feeling swings to pleasure and relaxation.

The theory was controversial in its day; Titchener's observers at Cornell could find only pleasantness and unpleasantness, and he rejected the other two dimensions. But notice how modern it looks. Twentieth-century research on emotion and on the meanings of words repeatedly found two or three broad dimensions, most often pleasantness (valence) and arousal, very close to Wundt's first and third axes. (Emotion theories are covered in Chapter 11.)

The method: experimental introspection

How do you study immediate experience? You have to ask the person having it. Wundt's method is called introspection (literally "looking within"), but it was very different from the armchair reflection of the philosophers, which Wundt distrusted as much as Comte had. His word for the armchair kind was Selbstbeobachtung (self-observation); the kind he accepted he called innere Wahrnehmung (internal perception), and it was usable only under strict experimental control. (English textbooks often call Wundt's version "experimental self-observation", which is why the terms confuse students; the point is the contrast between uncontrolled and controlled looking within.) Wundt laid down four rules for it:

  1. The observer must be able to tell when the process to be observed begins (the experimenter presents the stimulus at a signalled moment).
  2. The observer must be in a state of readiness, with attention fully concentrated.
  3. Each observation must be repeatable many times under the same conditions.
  4. The conditions must be variable, so that the stimulus can be systematically changed and the effect on experience noted.

Observers were trained, and most of their reports were short and quantitative: whether a stimulus was present or absent, louder or softer, longer or shorter, the same or different, together with reaction times measured by the chronoscope. Wundt did not ask his observers for long descriptions of their inner life; that style of introspection belongs to Titchener and, in a different form, to the Wurzburg school, as Section 1.6 will show.

Exam trap

"Who is most associated with the technique of introspection?" is Wundt. "Which school used introspection as its method of analysis?" is structuralism. In the analogy "Charles Spearman is to factor analysis as Wilhelm Wundt is to ...", the answer is introspection (Spearman, himself a Wundt student, invented factor analysis; psychoanalysis is Freud's, functionalism is James's, humanism is Maslow's and Rogers'). Wertheimer, Watson and James are wrong answers to the first question: Wertheimer founded Gestalt psychology, Watson attacked introspection, and James used casual introspection but is not known for a technique of it.

Volkerpsychologie: the other half of Wundt

Wundt believed that the laboratory could reach only the simpler processes: sensation, feeling, reaction, attention. The higher mental processes, such as thinking, language, morality and religious belief, are shaped by culture and history, and an individual sitting in front of a chronoscope cannot reveal them. They must be studied instead through their collective products: the languages, myths, customs, arts, laws and religions of whole peoples. Wundt devoted the last twenty years of his life to this second psychology, publishing it in ten volumes between 1900 and 1920 as Volkerpsychologie, usually translated as "folk psychology" and better understood today as cultural psychology. A one-volume summary, Elements of Folk Psychology, appeared in 1912.

For most of the twentieth century American textbooks ignored this half of Wundt, partly because few of his American students read the later volumes, and the picture of Wundt as a narrow laboratory elementist owes much to that neglect. Today he is often praised as a forerunner of cultural psychology, which matters for a country like India where psychologists have long argued that Western laboratory findings cannot simply be transplanted.

Exam trap

"Who gave the concept of folk psychology?" (RML 2024) is Wilhelm Maximilian Wundt. The distractors: Freud founded psychoanalysis; Pavlov discovered classical conditioning; Wilhelm Roentgen was a physicist who discovered X-rays in 1895, included only because his first name matches. Be aware that in modern philosophy of mind "folk psychology" means something else (our everyday explanations of behaviour in terms of beliefs and desires); in history questions it is Wundt's Volkerpsychologie.

Leipzig as a training ground

Wundt's laboratory was above all a school. Students came from across Europe and, in great numbers, from America, took doctorates under him, and went home to found laboratories, journals and departments of their own. Wundt supervised well over a hundred doctoral students in psychology, and the first generation of professional psychologists in several countries learned the trade in his rooms. Two stories show the atmosphere. James McKeen Cattell later recalled walking into Wundt's office and announcing that Wundt needed an assistant and that he would be it; Wundt agreed, though he called Cattell's interest in individual differences in reaction time "ganz Amerikanisch" (typically American). And G. Stanley Hall, who studied at Leipzig in 1879 and 1880, liked to call himself Wundt's first American student. Table 1.5 lists the students who matter most for examinations.

1.6 Structuralism and Its European RivalsMedium yield · 6 Qs

Wundt's psychology travelled. The version that reached America first, and that gave the first school its name, was carried by a young Englishman who had spent two years in Leipzig and who spent the rest of his life defending what he believed was Wundt's programme in its pure form.

Edward Bradford Titchener at Cornell

Edward Bradford Titchener (1867 to 1927) was born in Chichester, England, studied at Oxford, and took his doctorate under Wundt in 1892. Finding no opening for an experimental psychologist in England, he accepted a post at Cornell University in New York State that same year and stayed there until his death 35 years later. He was a commanding and formal figure who lectured in his Oxford academic gown, ran his laboratory as an absolute ruler, and translated several of Wundt's books into English. His own books, especially An Outline of Psychology (1896), the laboratory manuals Experimental Psychology (1901 to 1905) and A Text-book of Psychology (1909 to 1910), trained a generation.

In 1904 Titchener formed his own small society, "the Experimentalists", for researchers who shared his approach; it barred women from its meetings until after his death. The irony is sharp, because his first doctoral student was a woman, Margaret Floy Washburn, whom you will meet in Section 1.7. Titchener supervised more than fifty doctoral students in all.

The structuralist programme

Titchener named his approach in an 1898 article, "The Postulates of a Structural Psychology", and the name stuck: structuralism is the school that studies the structure of conscious experience by analysing it into its basic elements. Titchener drew the analogy with chemistry himself. Just as the chemist analyses compounds into elements and arranges them in the periodic table, the psychologist should analyse consciousness into its elements and catalogue them. He defined his subject carefully. Consciousness is the sum of mental processes occurring at any given moment; mind is the sum of mental processes accumulated over a lifetime. And he set psychology three problems:

  1. What? Analyse conscious processes into their simplest elements.
  2. How? Discover how the elements combine, and the laws of their combination.
  3. Why? Connect the elements with the physiological conditions that accompany them. (Titchener was a psychophysical parallelist: nervous processes accompany mental ones but do not cause them, so the "why" was correlation, not causation.)

Titchener found three kinds of elements, one more than Wundt. Sensations are the elements of perceptions; images are the elements of ideas (such as the image of your mother's face when she is not present); and affections are the elements of emotions. Each element has attributes, its basic dimensions of variation: quality (what distinguishes red from cold), intensity (how strong), duration (how long), clearness (how vivid in attention) and extensity (how spread out in space, possessed by sight and touch). Affections, he argued, have quality, intensity and duration but lack clearness: when you try to attend closely to a feeling of pleasantness, it fades. And he recognised only one dimension of affection, pleasantness versus unpleasantness, rejecting Wundt's tension and excitement. Working through his manuals, his laboratory catalogued more than 44,000 distinguishable qualities of sensation, the great majority of them visual and auditory.

Introspection, the stimulus error and the meaning of meaning

Titchener's method was introspection, but in a more demanding form than Wundt's. He defined it as a highly specialised form of self-observation in which the psychologist attempts to study his own consciousness scientifically. His observers were trained for months to report not short judgements but detailed descriptions of their experience in terms of elements and attributes.

The commonest fault of an untrained observer, Titchener said, was the stimulus error: describing the object instead of the experience. Shown an apple, the untrained person says "a red apple". But "apple" is not an experience; it is knowledge about the object. The trained observer reports what is actually in consciousness: a patch of red of a certain brightness and saturation, a bright highlight near the top, a certain extensity, perhaps a faint image of sourness and a mild pleasantness. Try this yourself with any object on your desk, and you will discover how hard it is not to name things.

If consciousness is only elements, where does meaning come from? Titchener's answer was the context theory of meaning. A sensation by itself has no meaning; it acquires meaning from the context of other sensations and images that the mind attaches to it. The core (say, the sound of the word "home") means something because it calls up a context of images: a kitchen, a voice, the smell of food. Often, Titchener thought, the context was kinaesthetic, made of faint sensations of movement and posture. On the same reasoning, he defined perception as a complex of sensory and imaginal processes: sensations from the object plus images supplied by past experience. This definition is the answer to an IHBAS question that asks who defined perception in exactly these terms.

Exam trap

"Introspection is a highly specialised form of ..." is self-observation (Titchener), not self-perception (that is Daryl Bem's later social-psychological theory), not "self-thinking" and not attention. "Perception is a complex of sensory and imaginal processes" is Titchener; Jerome Bruner and Roy Schafer, two of the other names in that question, belong to the "New Look" research on perception of the 1940s and later (Chapter 4), and Gregory Kimble is known for work on learning. The aim of structuralism was to build a "periodic table" of the elements of experience; "to study behaviour and not mind" is behaviourism, so "all of the above" is wrong. "To understand the nature of consciousness" is too general to be the answer: it fits Wundt, James and the Wurzburg school just as well, whereas the periodic table of elements is structuralism's distinctive aim.

The Wurzburg school and imageless thought

The first serious challenge to the structuralist picture came not from America but from one of Wundt's own former assistants. Oswald Kulpe (1862 to 1915) served as Wundt's assistant for several years, then moved to the University of Wurzburg in 1894. Unlike Wundt, he believed that thinking itself could be studied by experiment, and he extended introspection to it. His method, often called systematic experimental introspection, gave the observer a task (for example, to judge, to associate, or to answer a question) and then asked for a full, retrospective description of everything that had passed through consciousness while the task was done.

The results shocked everyone. In 1901 August Mayer and Johannes Orth reported that their observers, giving associations to words, often experienced vague states of hesitation, doubt or certainty that contained no sensations or images at all; they called these "conscious attitudes". Karl Marbe, in the same year, had observers judge which of two weights was heavier and found that they could describe the sensations of lifting perfectly well but could report nothing about how the judgement itself was made; it simply appeared. Karl Buhler later argued that thoughts themselves are elements of experience. The conclusion was imageless thought: thinking can proceed without any sensory or imaginal content in consciousness, which directly contradicted Titchener's claim that consciousness consists only of sensations, images and affections.

The Wurzburgers also discovered how much thinking is directed by the task. Henry J. Watt showed that the instruction or task, which he called the Aufgabe, determines what association a word produces. Narziss Ach (1905) called the unconscious influence that steers thinking toward the goal of the task the determining tendency, and the readiness it creates a set (in German, Einstellung). An example: hear the word "Mysuru" with the task "name a building" and you will think "palace"; hear the same word with the task "name its state" and you will think "Karnataka". The stimulus is identical; the set prepared before it arrived decides the answer, and you are not aware of the set doing its work.

Titchener replied that the Wurzburg observers were poorly trained and had committed the stimulus error; their "imageless" thoughts, he said, were really faint kinaesthetic sensations and images that a trained observer would detect. Wundt, for his part, dismissed the Wurzburg procedures as sham experiments, because the observer, not the experimenter, controlled what was observed. The quarrel, known as the imageless thought controversy, was never resolved, and that was the real damage: two laboratories using the same method reported different contents of consciousness, and introspection had no way to decide between them. Kulpe's influence lived on, however. The concept of set is basic to the study of problem solving, and one of Kulpe's doctoral students at Wurzburg was Max Wertheimer, the founder of Gestalt psychology (Chapter 2).

Act psychology: Brentano, Stumpf and G. E. Muller

A different German-speaking tradition rejected the idea of conscious contents altogether. In 1874, the same year Wundt completed his Principles, the philosopher Franz Brentano (1838 to 1917) published Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint. Brentano argued that psychology should study mental acts, not mental contents. When you see a red flower, the redness is a physical phenomenon; what is mental is the act of seeing it. This is act psychology. Brentano's key idea was intentionality: every mental act is directed at, or refers to, some object beyond itself. You never just see; you see something. You never just love or fear; you love or fear someone or something. He grouped acts into three classes: ideating (sensing, imagining), judging (accepting or rejecting, perceiving, recalling) and loving-hating (feeling, wishing, resolving). His method was careful empirical observation of experience rather than laboratory experiment. Brentano taught in Vienna for two decades, where his students included Carl Stumpf, the philosopher Edmund Husserl (founder of phenomenology), Christian von Ehrenfels (whose "form qualities" anticipated Gestalt psychology) and, as an auditor of his lectures, the young Sigmund Freud.

Carl Stumpf (1848 to 1936), Brentano's student, studied the perception of musical tones in his Psychology of Tone (two volumes, 1883 and 1890) and argued with Wundt over who was the better judge of tones: Stumpf trusted trained musicians, Wundt trusted trained laboratory observers. From 1894 Stumpf directed the psychological institute at the University of Berlin, where he favoured describing experience as it naturally occurs (a phenomenological approach) rather than breaking it into elements. Three of his students, Wolfgang Kohler, Kurt Koffka and Kurt Lewin, became leaders of Gestalt psychology. You have already met Stumpf on the Clever Hans commission.

Finally, Georg Elias Muller (1850 to 1934) ran the second great German laboratory, at Gottingen, for forty years. Muller was a rigorous experimentalist rather than a theorist of acts, and he is grouped here because he stood outside Wundt's system. He refined Fechner's psychophysical methods, extended Ebbinghaus's memory research (with his assistant Friedrich Schumann he developed the memory drum for presenting syllables at a fixed rate), and, with Alfons Pilzecker in 1900, described retroactive interference and proposed that memories need time to consolidate (Chapter 7). His students included Edgar Rubin, whose figure-ground drawings you will meet in Chapter 4.

The decline of structuralism, and what it left behind

Structuralism died with Titchener in 1927; no one carried it on. The reasons for its decline are examined in almost every paper, so it pays to know them as a list.

  • Introspection was unreliable. Trained observers in different laboratories reported different things (the imageless thought controversy), and since each observer's experience is private, there was no way to settle disputes.
  • Observing changes what is observed. As Comte had warned and William James repeated, attending to an experience alters it, and describing it afterwards depends on memory.
  • Elementism missed the whole. The Gestalt psychologists argued that experience comes organised in wholes that analysis destroys.
  • Its subject matter was too narrow. It excluded animals, children, the mentally ill, individual differences and the unconscious, which Freud was making impossible to ignore.
  • It refused to be useful. Titchener regarded applied work as technology, not science, just as American society was demanding a useful psychology.

And yet structuralism mattered. It made psychology a laboratory discipline with standards of training and control, and it gave every later school something definite to argue against. Its topics have also come back. The modern approach most closely aligned with the structuralists in its interest in consciousness, mental imagery and thought processes is cognitive psychology, which studies the same subjects with reaction times, error patterns and brain imaging instead of trained introspection. Neuroscience studies the brain rather than the contents of experience; behaviourism banished consciousness; and functionalism, as you will now see, asked a different question altogether.

Clinical link

Introspection never left the clinic. Every self-report questionnaire, every mental status interview question ("What is going through your mind right now?") and every thought record in cognitive behaviour therapy asks a person to observe and report his own consciousness. Titchener's problems come with it: patients describe interpretations rather than experiences (a cousin of the stimulus error), and their reports are shaped by memory, expectation and the way the question is asked. Good clinicians, like good structuralists, train the observer, which is exactly what learning to separate a situation, a thought and a feeling in therapy does.

1.7 Functionalism and the American PioneersHigh yield · 15 Qs

Structuralism asked what the mind is made of. The question that dominated American psychology from the 1890s was different: what is the mind for? functionalism is the school that studies the mind as it functions in adapting the organism to its environment. Notice the order of words, which a bank question tests: the mind functions in adapting the organism. Functionalists studied mental processes and behaviour not as collections of elements but as activities that help a living creature survive and solve problems.

Why functionalism grew in America

Three forces made America receptive. The first was Darwin, whose theory had become enormously popular in the United States, partly through the evolutionary philosophy of the British writer Herbert Spencer: if traits exist because they are useful, the obvious question about consciousness is what use it is. The second was the American philosophy of pragmatism, which judged ideas by their practical consequences. The third was the character of the country itself: a rapidly growing nation with expanding schools, factories and cities, and universities that had to justify their new psychology departments by doing something useful. Functionalism was never a tightly organised school with a single founder and creed. It was an attitude, shared by people at Harvard, Chicago and Columbia, and it was Titchener, in his 1898 article, who gave it its name, as the opposite of his own "structural" psychology.

William James

William James (1842 to 1910), the elder brother of the novelist Henry James, is the father of American psychology and the inspiration, though not the formal founder, of functionalism. He qualified in medicine at Harvard in 1869 but never practised. Around that time he fell into a deep depression, from which he dragged himself, he said, partly by reading the French philosopher Charles Renouvier on free will and deciding that his first act of free will would be to believe in free will. He began teaching at Harvard, and in 1875 he offered what is usually called the first course in scientific psychology in America and set up a small room of apparatus for classroom demonstrations, the room mentioned at the start of this chapter. James disliked laboratory drudgery and had little patience with Fechner's psychophysics, but his teaching produced the first American doctorate in psychology (G. Stanley Hall, 1878).

In 1878 James signed a contract to write a psychology textbook in two years. It took twelve. The Principles of Psychology (1890), in two volumes, is the most widely read book in the history of the discipline, and its ideas are tested constantly. A shorter version followed in 1892, which students nicknamed "Jimmy" to distinguish it from the full "James". Four ideas from it matter most for you.

The first is the stream of consciousness. Against the structuralists' picture of consciousness as a mosaic of elements, James wrote: "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits. Such words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly ... It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described." He listed its characteristics: thought is personal (always someone's), always changing (no state ever recurs exactly), sensibly continuous (even across gaps such as sleep), concerned with objects outside itself, and selective, choosing among the things it could attend to. The phrase passed into literature, where novelists such as James Joyce and Virginia Woolf adopted a "stream of consciousness" style. Consciousness, for James, was selective precisely because it was useful: it is a fighter for ends, which is a functionalist claim.

The second is habit. James argued that the "plasticity" of nervous tissue lets repeated actions wear grooves in the nervous system, so that they become automatic and free attention for other things. "Habit is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent." His practical advice for forming a new habit is still quoted by therapists: launch the new habit with as strong an initiative as possible, never allow an exception until it is firmly rooted, and seize the very first opportunity to act on a resolution.

The third is the self. James divided the self into the "Me" (the self as known) and the "I" (the self as knower). The Me has three parts: the material self (one's body, clothes, family, home and possessions), the social self (the recognition one gets from others; a man "has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize him") and the spiritual self (one's inner dispositions, faculties and conscience). The I is the pure ego, the sense of personal identity that continues through the stream. He even offered a formula: self-esteem equals success divided by pretensions. Raise your aspirations without raising your achievements and your self-esteem falls; give up a pretension and it rises.

The fourth is his theory of emotion: in an 1884 article James argued that we do not cry because we are sorry; we are sorry because we cry. The Danish physiologist Carl Lange (1834 to 1900) proposed a similar theory independently in 1885, so it is known as the James-Lange theory (Chapter 11).

After the Principles, James turned to philosophy. He developed pragmatism, the doctrine (first stated by his friend Charles Sanders Peirce) that the meaning and truth of an idea lie in its practical consequences, in Pragmatism (1907); he also wrote Talks to Teachers (1899) and The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902).

Exam trap

"Stream of consciousness" is William James (not Wundt, Jung or Skinner). "Functionalism is how people work, play and adapt to their surroundings; this viewpoint was given by ..." is William James: Wundt (misspelt "William Wundt" in one paper) is the voluntarist, Darwin inspired functionalism but was not a psychologist of any school, and Watson founded behaviourism. When a question asks which psychologist functionalism is "most closely associated with", the expected answer is James, not Titchener (structuralism), Watson (behaviourism) or G. Stanley Hall (who was a functionalist in spirit but is examined for institutions and adolescence). If Dewey or Angell appears as an option in a question about the formal founding of the functionalist school at Chicago, choose them.

The Chicago school: Dewey, Angell and Carr

Functionalism became a school, with a home and a programme, at the University of Chicago. Its founding document was a paper by the philosopher John Dewey (1859 to 1952), "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology", published in the Psychological Review in 1896. The reflex arc is the physiologists' unit of behaviour: a stimulus excites a sensory (afferent) nerve, a central process connects it to a motor (efferent) nerve, and a response follows. Psychologists had adopted it as the model for all behaviour: sensation, then idea, then action. Dewey argued that this chops a single, continuous act into three disconnected pieces, "a patchwork of disjointed parts".

He made the argument with an example from James: a child sees a candle flame, reaches for it, is burnt, and pulls back. On the reflex-arc view, two separate arcs have occurred: light-reach, then burn-withdraw. Dewey pointed out that the seeing was never a bare stimulus. It was already part of a coordinated act of looking-in-order-to-reach, and the burn does not end the story; it changes the meaning of the seeing, so that next time the child sees a flame as "a light that burns" (Figure 1.5). Stimulus and response are not separate events but phases of one continuous coordination, in which each response alters the next stimulus. Behaviour, Dewey concluded, must be studied as a whole adaptive act in its context.

Dewey went on to become the most influential American philosopher of education. At Chicago he founded a Laboratory School where children learned by doing, and he served as president of the American Psychological Association in 1899. He left Chicago for Columbia in 1904.

Exam trap

"According to John Dewey, ____ is the connection between sensory stimuli and motor responses." The answer is the reflex arc, the concept his 1896 paper examined and criticised. "Efferent" describes only the outgoing, motor half of the arc (afferent nerves carry signals in, efferent nerves carry them out), so an "efferent arc" cannot connect stimulus to response; "association arc" and "dynamic arc" are invented distractors, the second borrowing a word from Woodworth's Dynamic Psychology. Remember that Dewey attacked the reflex arc as the unit of behaviour; he did not invent it.

The man who turned Dewey's attitude into a working department was James Rowland Angell (1869 to 1949). In his presidential address to the American Psychological Association in 1906, "The Province of Functional Psychology" (published in 1907), Angell gave the school its clearest statement. Functional psychology, he said, is (1) the psychology of mental operations, as opposed to mental elements; (2) the study of the fundamental utilities of consciousness, the mind being what mediates between the environment and the needs of the organism; and (3) psychophysical psychology, which treats mind and body as a single functioning organism rather than two parallel series. Angell's Chicago department trained many students, among them a young man named John B. Watson, who took his doctorate there in 1903. Angell later became president of Yale University.

Under Angell's successor, Harvey A. Carr (1873 to 1954), Chicago functionalism reached its mature form. In Psychology: A Study of Mental Activity (1925), Carr defined the subject as mental activity: the acquisition, fixation, retention, organisation and evaluation of experiences, and their use in guiding conduct. His unit of analysis was the adaptive act, made up of a motive (a stimulus that persists until it is dealt with, such as hunger), a sensory situation, and a response that changes the situation so as to satisfy the motive. By Carr's time functionalism was less a rebel school than the ordinary way American psychologists worked.

The Columbia school: Cattell, Woodworth and Thorndike

At Columbia University in New York, functionalism took a still looser form, focused on measurement and individual differences. Its senior figure was James McKeen Cattell (1860 to 1944), Wundt's self-appointed assistant. After Leipzig, Cattell worked with Galton in England and absorbed his interest in measuring individual differences. In 1888 he became professor of psychology at the University of Pennsylvania, often described as the first such title anywhere, and in 1891 he moved to Columbia, where he stayed until 1917, when he was dismissed for opposing the conscription of soldiers in the First World War.

In an 1890 article in Mind, Cattell coined the term mental test. His tests followed Galton's logic: strength of grip, speed of movement, two-point threshold, sensitivity to pain, discrimination of weights, reaction time to sound, time to name colours, accuracy in bisecting a line, judging a ten-second interval, and the number of letters remembered after one hearing. He hoped such tests would reveal mental ability. In 1901 his student Clark Wissler showed that scores on them bore almost no relation to college grades, and the sensory approach to intelligence collapsed. Cattell's lasting contributions lay elsewhere: in reaction time and reading research (he showed, for example, that a familiar word can be recognised about as quickly as a single letter), in the ranking method of measurement, and above all as an organiser and editor who did more than anyone to make American psychology a profession.

Robert Sessions Woodworth, whom you met in Section 1.1, spent his career at Columbia and became the great systematiser of functionalism. In Dynamic Psychology (1918) he insisted that psychology must study not only the mechanisms of behaviour (how it happens) but also its motivation (why), and he introduced the term "drive" to psychology (Chapter 10). He also proposed adding the organism to the stimulus-response formula. The S-O-R formula (stimulus, organism, response) states that the same stimulus produces different responses depending on the state of the organism: a plate of biryani means one thing to a hungry person and another to someone who has just eaten. Woodworth's Experimental Psychology (1938) was so widely used that students called it "the Columbia Bible", and his Contemporary Schools of Psychology (1931) taught a generation how to compare the systems you are studying now. (His Personal Data Sheet, an early personality questionnaire, belongs to the Assessment book.)

Columbia's third functionalist was Edward Lee Thorndike, who began studying the learning of chicks in William James's basement at Harvard and completed his doctorate under Cattell at Columbia in 1898 with the famous puzzle-box experiments on cats. His law of effect and his work on educational psychology are taught in Chapter 6; for now, notice that he came out of the functionalist tradition, as Watson did.

The builders: Hall, Witmer and Munsterberg

Functionalism's practical spirit produced the institutions of American psychology and its first applied fields. Three Wundt-trained men did most of the building.

Granville Stanley Hall (1844 to 1924) was a founder of almost everything. He received the first American doctorate in psychology (Harvard, 1878), studied in Leipzig in 1879 and 1880, and in 1883 opened at Johns Hopkins University the first American psychological laboratory devoted to research (James's 1875 room had been for demonstration). In 1887 he founded the American Journal of Psychology, the first American psychology journal, and in 1889 he became the first president of the new Clark University in Worcester, Massachusetts. In July 1892 a small group of psychologists meeting at Clark founded the American Psychological Association (APA), with Hall as its first president; its charter members numbered only a few dozen. Hall was also a pioneer of child and developmental psychology: he launched the child-study movement, using questionnaires to learn what children knew and thought, and in Adolescence (1904) he described the teenage years as a period of "storm and stress" (from the German Sturm und Drang). He explained development through recapitulation theory, the idea that each child, in growing up, passes through the stages of the evolution of the human race (a theory now abandoned; the Developmental Psychology book covers adolescence). In 1909 he invited Sigmund Freud and Carl Jung to lecture at Clark, Freud's only visit to America, which did much to spread psychoanalysis there.

Lightner Witmer (1867 to 1956) studied under Cattell at Pennsylvania, took his doctorate under Wundt in 1892, and returned to succeed Cattell at Pennsylvania. In March 1896 he opened there the world's first psychological clinic. At the APA meeting in December of that year he proposed a new field, which he called clinical psychology; in 1907 he founded the journal The Psychological Clinic and, in an article titled "Clinical Psychology" in its first issue, became the first person to use the term in print. His clinic dealt mainly with children who had learning and behaviour problems, and he insisted that clinical work rest on the methods and findings of experimental psychology.

Key study: The first case at the first clinic (1896)

The problem. A schoolteacher brought Witmer a fourteen-year-old boy she described as a "chronic bad speller". He was not lazy and not dull, yet he could not learn to spell. Method. Witmer examined the boy as a scientist would: testing his vision, his memory, his reading and his performance under different conditions, instead of simply labelling him. Finding. Part of the problem turned out to be a defect of vision that made printed words hard to see clearly; with corrected vision and patient, individual teaching, the boy made progress. Conclusion. Witmer took two lessons from the case, and they define the profession you are entering: begin with careful assessment before treatment, and look for the simplest, most concrete cause first (Morgan's canon, applied to a child).

Hugo Munsterberg (1863 to 1916), another Wundt graduate, was invited by William James in 1892 to run the Harvard laboratory. Munsterberg became the great popular champion of applied psychology. In On the Witness Stand (1908) he argued that eyewitness testimony and memory are unreliable and that psychology could help the courts, making him a founder of forensic psychology. In Psychology and Industrial Efficiency (1913) he applied psychological methods to selecting workers (telephone operators, tram drivers) and to advertising, making him a founder of industrial psychology. He also wrote on psychotherapy and on the psychology of film. He died in 1916 while lecturing at Radcliffe College.

Clinical link

Witmer is the father of clinical psychology, and the model of the clinical psychologist that Indian training programmes follow, the scientist-practitioner who assesses carefully, treats on the basis of evidence and evaluates the results, is recognisably his. Notice too what Witmer's clinic did not do: it did not begin with psychoanalysis or with psychiatric diagnosis. Clinical psychology was born out of experimental psychology and education, not out of medicine, and that history explains why assessment remains its distinctive skill.

Exam trap

The first psychological clinic and the term "clinical psychology" are Witmer's (clinic 1896; term proposed 1896, first in print 1907). Freud founded psychoanalysis, Wundt the laboratory, G. Stanley Hall the APA and the first US research laboratory, Cattell the mental test, and Erik Erikson the psychosocial stages. The APA was founded in 1892; 1896 is the year of Witmer's clinic and Dewey's paper, so do not let it pull you. Hall was the APA's first president; Calkins its first woman president.

The women pioneers

Women were excluded from most universities and all of Titchener's meetings, yet several of the founders of American psychology were women, and their stories are examined often.

Mary Whiton Calkins (1863 to 1930) studied psychology at Harvard with William James and Hugo Munsterberg in the early 1890s, attending as a guest because Harvard did not admit women. By 1895 she had completed every requirement for the doctorate, and her examiners recommended the degree, but Harvard refused to award a PhD to a woman; she later declined the offer of a degree from Radcliffe, Harvard's college for women. She taught at Wellesley College, where she founded a psychology laboratory in 1891, invented the paired-associate technique for studying memory (learning pairs of items so that one cues the other), and developed a "self psychology" that treated the self as the centre of all psychology. In 1905 she became the first woman president of the American Psychological Association, and later the first woman president of the American Philosophical Association as well.

Margaret Floy Washburn (1871 to 1939) was the first woman to be awarded a PhD in psychology, at Cornell in 1894, as Titchener's first doctoral student. She taught for most of her career at Vassar College, and her book The Animal Mind (1908) was the standard text of comparative psychology for a quarter of a century, gathering the experimental evidence on the sensory and mental capacities of animals. She also proposed a motor theory of consciousness, in which thought depends on small, incipient movements. In 1921 she became the second woman president of the APA.

Leta Stetter Hollingworth (1886 to 1939), who trained under Thorndike at Columbia, used experiments to demolish two beliefs used to keep women out of education and work. In Functional Periodicity (1914) she tested women on mental and motor tasks across the menstrual cycle and found no impairment at any phase. She also attacked the "variability hypothesis", the claim that men vary more than women and therefore produce more geniuses, showing that the evidence did not support it and that the difference in eminence was explained by social opportunity. She later became a leading authority on gifted children, and worked as one of the early clinical psychologists in New York.

Exam trap

Calkins was the first woman APA president (1905); Washburn the first woman PhD in psychology (1894) and the second woman APA president (1921). Calkins completed every requirement for a Harvard doctorate in 1895 but was refused the degree; Washburn had received hers from Cornell in 1894, so she holds the "first PhD" title. Hollingworth is examined for the psychology of women and the gifted. Karen Horney, sometimes offered as a distractor, was a psychoanalyst who challenged Freud's view of women (the Personality book) and was never APA president.

The legacy of functionalism

Functionalism did not fade like structuralism; it dissolved into the mainstream. Its interests (learning, motivation, individual differences, development, abnormal behaviour, animals) became the ordinary subject matter of psychology, and its willingness to be useful created educational, industrial, forensic and clinical psychology. It also produced its own successors. Watson, Angell's student, took the functionalist interest in adaptive behaviour and animals to its logical end by dropping consciousness altogether; the aim of behaviourism, as Watson put it, was that given the stimulus, psychology should be able to predict the response, and given the response, to specify the stimulus that caused it (Chapter 2). And the functionalist question, what is a mental process for, inherited from Darwin, is the founding question of today's evolutionary psychology. Table 1.6 sets the two first schools side by side.

1.8 Reading the History: Zeitgeist, Great Persons and "Fathers"Lower yield · 4 Qs

You now know where every part in Max Friedrich's room came from. The chronoscope and the habit of timing observers came from physics and astronomy; the idea that a mental act takes measurable time came from Helmholtz and Donders; the word apperception came from Leibniz and Herbart; the conviction that the mind can be measured came from Fechner. So the puzzle from the start of the chapter can now be put sharply. If nearly everything in that room was borrowed, in what sense did Wundt found anything, and why do we give the credit to one man, one city and one year? Historians answer questions of this kind in two broad ways. The answers matter for more than history, because they decide which names end up in textbooks as "fathers", and so which names end up in your question paper.

Two ways of explaining history

The first way puts individuals at the centre. In a series of lectures given in London in 1840 and published the next year as On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History, the Scottish writer Thomas Carlyle (1795 to 1881) declared that "the history of the world is but the biography of great men". This is the great-person theory of history, still often called by Carlyle's own phrase, the great-man theory: progress happens because exceptional individuals, through genius and force of will, change the course of events, and without them it would not have happened, or would have happened very differently. Applied to our story, psychology began in Leipzig because Wundt was Wundt. It took a man who would publish more than 50,000 printed pages, supervise well over a hundred doctorates and work at his desk for more than sixty years to turn a scattered set of techniques into a discipline. Historians call this the personalistic view of history.

The second way puts the times at the centre. Zeitgeist is German for "the spirit of the times" (Zeit, time; Geist, spirit or mind): the whole climate of ideas, knowledge, techniques, values and social conditions of a period. On the Zeitgeist view, a discovery is made when the time is ripe for it, and if one person had not made it, someone else soon would have. Individuals still matter, but as the agents through whom their age does its work; the times choose the person more than the person chooses the times. Historians call this the naturalistic view. Its great champion in psychology was Edwin G. Boring, whose A History of Experimental Psychology (1929; second edition 1950) shaped how generations of students, and of examiners, learned the story you have just read. For Boring the founding of psychology in the 1870s was no accident. You saw at the end of Section 1.4 that by 1870 all the parts were already on the bench; someone was bound to assemble them.

What evidence could decide between the two views? The strongest argument for the Zeitgeist is the frequency of multiple discovery: the same discovery made independently by two or more people at about the same time. You have already met several in this chapter. Charles Bell (1811) and Francois Magendie (1822) both separated the sensory and motor roots of the spinal nerves, and quarrelled for years over the credit. William James (1884) and the Danish physiologist Carl Lange (1885) proposed the same theory of emotion without knowing of each other's work. The most famous case of all is evolution itself. In 1858, while Darwin was still writing his great book, he received from the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace (1823 to 1913), then collecting specimens in the Malay Archipelago, an essay setting out the same theory of natural selection, and papers by both men were read together at a meeting of the Linnean Society in London that July. Outside the life sciences, Newton and Leibniz each invented the calculus. In 1922 two American sociologists, William Ogburn and Dorothy Thomas, listed 148 such cases, and the sociologist of science Robert K. Merton later argued that "multiples" are the normal pattern of science, not the exception. If great discoveries depended entirely on unique geniuses, such coincidences should be rare. That they are common suggests that the state of knowledge makes certain discoveries almost inevitable.

The other side of the same coin is the discovery made too early. In 1866 the monk Gregor Mendel published the laws of heredity he had worked out by breeding pea plants in his monastery garden at Brno. Almost nobody noticed. In 1900 three botanists, working separately, arrived at the same laws and only then found Mendel's paper: a multiple discovery that rescued a premature one. The Zeitgeist also works against ideas. When two Cambridge scholars proposed a laboratory for psychophysics in 1877, the university turned them down; one objector is said to have complained that it would "insult religion by putting the human soul in a pair of scales". At that very moment the same science was flourishing in Leipzig. The ideas were identical; the climate was not.

The spirit of the times includes forces that have little to do with ideas, which historians call contextual forces, and you have met three of them already. The first is economic opportunity: American universities, schools and factories wanted a useful psychology, and functionalism, mental testing and the first clinic grew to meet the demand (Section 1.7). The second is war: Cattell lost his Columbia chair in 1917 for opposing conscription, while the same war gave American psychologists their first mass testing programme, the Army Alpha and Army Beta tests of 1917 (Chapter 9). The third is prejudice: Mary Whiton Calkins was refused the doctorate she had earned because she was a woman, Titchener's Experimentalists barred women from their meetings, and Leta Hollingworth spent the first part of her career disproving claims that were used to keep women out of education and work. Who becomes a "great person" depends partly on who is allowed into the room.

Most historians now conclude that neither view is enough on its own, and the clearest way to see why is to push one event through both. Take Lightner Witmer's clinic of 1896. The great-person version is easy to tell: a schoolteacher brought a boy who could not spell, and Witmer, unlike his colleagues, decided that a psychologist ought to try to help him. Now tell it the Zeitgeist way. American states were making schooling compulsory, so classrooms were filling with children who could not keep up; G. Stanley Hall's child-study movement had made children a respectable subject of research; Cattell had just introduced mental tests; and functionalism had made usefulness a scientific virtue. Someone was likely to open a clinic for schoolchildren around the turn of the century. Both versions are true. The Zeitgeist explains why a clinic appeared in America in the 1890s; the person explains why it appeared in Philadelphia in 1896, and why it took the shape it did, built on careful assessment rather than on medicine or hypnosis. A useful summary: the Zeitgeist decides what can happen; persons decide when, where and how it does.

Exam trap

Zeitgeist means "spirit of the times". It is not "spirit of the place", not "worldview" (that is Weltanschauung) and not "form" or "organised whole" (that is Gestalt, Chapter 2). The historian who championed the Zeitgeist view of psychology's history is Boring; the "great man" view goes back to Carlyle (1841). If a question asks which view best explains simultaneous discoveries, such as Darwin and Wallace (1858), Bell and Magendie, or James and Lange, the answer is the Zeitgeist or naturalistic view. The great-person view is the personalistic one.

Why Germany, why Wundt, why 1879?

The Zeitgeist explains a good deal about where psychology was born. Why Germany, and not Britain, France or America, all of which had brilliant scientists? Three features of German academic life made the difference. First, the German universities, reformed on the model of the University of Berlin (founded in 1810), were built around research as well as teaching. Professors were expected to discover things and enjoyed Lehrfreiheit, the freedom to teach whatever their research led them to; students enjoyed Lernfreiheit, the freedom to move between universities in search of the best teachers. Because the German states had many universities competing for prestige, a new field could find a chair, a room and a budget somewhere. Second, the German word for science, Wissenschaft, covered every kind of systematic knowledge, history and philology included, so a "science of mind" sounded natural rather than contradictory. Third, as Section 1.3 showed, experimental physiology was stronger in Germany than anywhere else; Muller, Helmholtz, Weber and Fechner were all German, and their methods were exactly the ones psychology needed.

Britain, by contrast, had brilliant individuals but no institutional home for them. Darwin and Galton, the two British giants of this chapter, were men of independent means who never held university posts, and the ancient universities, as the Cambridge refusal shows, were slow to accept new sciences. France had a powerful clinical tradition, and French psychology grew up in the hospital and the school rather than the laboratory: first in the study of hysteria and hypnosis, and then in the work of Alfred Binet (1857 to 1911), who with Theodore Simon produced the first practical intelligence test in 1905 (Chapter 9). America had energy and money, but in 1879 it had no research laboratory, and its universities were still learning the German model; G. Stanley Hall, like so many others, crossed the Atlantic to study in Leipzig.

The Zeitgeist cannot, however, explain why it was Wundt rather than one of the many Germans who had the same knowledge. Here historians draw a useful distinction between a founder and a precursor (or pioneer). Weber, Fechner, Helmholtz and Donders all did experiments that we now count as psychology, but none of them set out to create a new discipline. Weber was a physiologist of the senses; Helmholtz thought of himself as a physicist and physiologist; Donders was an eye specialist; Fechner was trying to prove a philosophy of mind and matter. Wundt alone did all four of the things that turn a set of methods into a science. He declared it, in print, in the preface to his Principles (1873 to 1874). He gave it a place, the Leipzig laboratory. He gave it a voice, the journal Philosophische Studien (1881). And he gave it people: the stream of students who carried it to Wurzburg, Cornell, Columbia, Philadelphia, Harvard and, as you will see shortly, Calcutta. A founder is not the first person to have an idea. He is the person who turns the idea into an institution that outlives him.

That leaves the year. Why 1879, when James had a room of apparatus at Harvard in 1875 and Fechner's book had appeared in 1860? Because 1875 was a room for showing known effects to students, and 1860 was a book. The winter of 1879 is the first time that students did original, publishable research in a room set aside for psychology. The date is a convention, fixed by later historians (the university did not formally recognise the laboratory as one of its institutes until some years afterwards), but it is a sensible convention, and it is the one every question paper expects.

Exam trap

"Who established the first laboratory for experimental psychology?" is Wundt (Leipzig, 1879). Each distractor is tied to a different "first" or to no laboratory at all. Galton opened the first anthropometric laboratory (London, 1884), a centre for measuring individual differences, not a research school. Weber was a physiologist whose experiments on touch and lifted weights came before any psychology laboratory existed. Jung turned Galton's word association test into a clinical tool in Zurich (the Personality book). James had a demonstration room at Harvard in 1875. If the question asks for the city, it is Leipzig: not Heidelberg (where Wundt assisted Helmholtz), not Zurich (where he spent one year), not Vienna (Brentano and Freud), not Paris (Charcot's clinic and Binet's tests), and not Cambridge, which is both the town in Massachusetts that is home to Harvard and the English university that refused a psychophysics laboratory in 1877. The first American research laboratory is Hall's (Johns Hopkins, 1883).

Fathers, founders and firsts

Indian entrance papers have a favourite question form: "Who is the father of ...?" The difficulty is that "father" is used loosely, to mean at least four different things. It can mean the founder of an institution: Wundt founded the laboratory, Witmer the clinic. It can mean the person who named a field or school: Titchener named both structuralism and functionalism, though he founded only the first; Witmer named clinical psychology; William Stern named differential psychology. It can mean the most influential early figure, as James is for American psychology. And it can mean the first person to do something in one country, as Sengupta is for India. Two habits will protect you. Read the qualifying word in the stem ("experimental", "American", "modern", "formal", "in India"), because it often decides between two good candidates. And when two candidates are both defensible, choose the one that standard textbooks name, which is the one given in Table 1.7.

Names attached to discoveries can mislead in a second way. Hyphenated names are often a fossil record of multiple discovery: the Bell-Magendie law, the James-Lange theory, the Young-Helmholtz theory (Thomas Young's suggestion of 1802, developed by Helmholtz half a century later) and, in Chapter 11, the Cannon-Bard theory. And some names were given by other people: "Weber's law" was named by Fechner, and Morgan's canon restates, for animal psychology, the much older rule of parsimony usually credited to the fourteenth-century philosopher William of Ockham ("Ockham's razor").

If psychology has fathers, it also has a family tree, and seeing the tree whole is the quickest way to remember who trained whom (Figure 1.6). Notice how much of it grows from one root. Wundt's students founded the Wurzburg school, structuralism, the first American research laboratory, the Columbia school, the first psychological clinic and applied psychology at Harvard. Their students, in turn, include the founder of Gestalt psychology, the first woman to earn a doctorate in psychology, the first woman to lead the APA and the founder of modern psychology in India. A Zeitgeist historian would add that a network like this is exactly how the spirit of an age travels: not through the air, but through people who learned in the same rooms.

Psychology comes to India

Reflection on the mind in India is far older than anything in this chapter. The Upanishads, the Samkhya and Yoga systems, Buddhist analyses of consciousness and the medical tradition of Ayurveda all contain detailed accounts of perception, attention, emotion and the self, and they are the subject of Sections 2.8 and 2.9. What reached India in the early twentieth century was something narrower and newer: the laboratory psychology of Leipzig and its American descendants. It arrived, as so much of modern Indian science did, through the University of Calcutta (now Kolkata).

The first step was a syllabus. In 1905, by the usual account, the philosopher Brajendranath Seal (1864 to 1938) drew up a course in experimental psychology for the University of Calcutta, taught within its philosophy curriculum. The person who turned the syllabus into a science was Narendra Nath Sengupta (1889 to 1944; also spelt Sen Gupta). After his studies in Calcutta, Sengupta went to Harvard, where he trained in experimental psychology under Hugo Munsterberg, himself a Leipzig graduate, so that the line in Figure 1.6 runs unbroken from Wundt's room to Bengal. Back in Calcutta, he set up in 1915 the first laboratory of experimental psychology in India, and in 1916 the University of Calcutta opened the first independent department of psychology in the country, with Sengupta as its moving spirit. (Some sources date the laboratory to 1916 as well; the department is 1916, and the place is always Calcutta.) His early research followed the Leipzig agenda of attention and perception, but he also turned to society: with the sociologist Radhakamal Mukerjee he wrote Introduction to Social Psychology: Mind in Society (1928), one of the first Indian books in the field. He took the lead in founding the Indian Psychological Association in 1925, and later moved to Lucknow University, where he built another department. He is the person Indian papers call the founder, or father, of modern psychology in India.

The second founder came to psychology from medicine. Girindrasekhar Bose (1887 to 1953; also written Girindra Shekhar Bose) qualified as a doctor, then took a master's degree in the new Calcutta department, and in 1921 earned what is usually counted as the first doctorate in psychology awarded by an Indian university, for a thesis published as The Concept of Repression (sources differ by a year on the date of the degree). He had been treating patients by psychoanalytic methods, and in 1921 he wrote to Freud, beginning a correspondence that lasted many years. In 1922 Bose founded the Indian Psychoanalytic Society in Calcutta, one of the earliest psychoanalytic societies outside Europe and North America, and it was soon affiliated to the International Psychoanalytical Association. Bose did not simply copy Freud. In his theory of opposite wishes he argued that every wish is accompanied by its opposite (the wish to beat by the wish to be beaten, for example), and he drew on Indian philosophy to explain how such conflicts resolve; Freud read his ideas with interest but was not persuaded. Bose later headed the Calcutta department, and in 1947 he founded the journal Samiksa. He is the father of psychoanalysis in India. (His theory is taken further in Section 2.8 and in the Personality book.)

Other centres followed. In 1923 psychology was given its own section, together with educational science, at the annual Indian Science Congress. In 1924 M. V. Gopalaswamy, who had trained in London, started the department of psychology at the University of Mysore. In 1925 the Indian Psychological Association was founded, and in 1926 it launched the Indian Journal of Psychology. Over the following decades departments opened at universities across the country. Clinical psychology in India has founding events of its own, and interviewers for clinical programmes expect you to know them. Reading old history with care

One last lesson concerns the reader rather than the past. It is tempting to judge old ideas by what we know now: to laugh at phrenology, to dismiss introspection as naive, to treat Wundt as a dull cataloguer of sensations. In 1965 the historian George Stocking made the standard name for this habit familiar to historians of the behavioural sciences: presentism, interpreting and judging the past by the standards and concerns of the present. Its opposite, historicism, tries to understand past thinkers on their own terms: what questions they were asking, what evidence they had, what their contemporaries believed. Presentism is not always wrong (we do want to know which old ideas turned out to be true), but a purely presentist history becomes a story of heroes and fools, and it gets the facts wrong. Phrenology, judged historically, was a serious theory that got localisation right and method wrong; introspection was abandoned because it could not settle disputes, not because its users were foolish.

The fate of Wundt's reputation is the classic case. For much of the twentieth century American students met Wundt mainly through Titchener, who translated him, presented his own structuralism as the faithful continuation of Wundt's programme, and was far more of an elementist than Wundt ever was. Few Americans read the ten volumes of the Volkerpsychologie, and the most influential history of the period was written by Boring, who had taken his doctorate under Titchener at Cornell. The result was a Wundt who was narrow, mechanical and introspective in Titchener's style. Only in the 1970s did historians who went back to Wundt's own German texts, among them Arthur Blumenthal in "A Reappraisal of Wilhelm Wundt" (1975), recover the Wundt you met in Section 1.5: a voluntarist who stressed attention, will and creative synthesis, who limited introspection to short, controlled judgements, and who wrote a whole second psychology of culture. Translation was part of the problem, which is why the German terms in Table 1.10 are worth knowing exactly. Examiners like to put them side by side as options.

The case of Max Friedrich's key, revisited

Go back to the winter of 1879 and look again at the doctoral student pressing his telegraph key. You can now name the ancestry of almost everything in that room. The clockwork chronoscope belonged to the physicists, and the very idea that observers differ in how long they take to respond had been forced on the astronomers by the personal equation. The logic of the experiment, comparing a simple reaction with one that requires an extra mental act and subtracting the times, was Donders' subtraction method of 1868. The mental act being timed, apperception, had been named by Leibniz and given its psychological meaning by Herbart. The belief that a mental process could be measured at all was Fechner's answer to Kant, and the assumption that each mental event has a bodily partner running alongside it was the psychophysical parallelism that Wundt inherited from Leibniz. Even the plan of asking a trained observer to report under controlled, repeatable conditions was laid down in Wundt's four rules of experimental introspection.

So what was new? Not the parts but the purpose. For the first time, a student was doing original research on the mind, in a room set aside for it, under a professor who had announced a new science and would soon give it a journal, a textbook tradition and a stream of students. That is the whole answer to the puzzle the chapter began with, and it needs both theories of history from Section 1.8: the Zeitgeist had put every part on the bench, and one determined person assembled them into an institution. Friedrich's own measurements are long forgotten, and the hope of timing apperception by simple subtraction did not survive the problem of pure insertion described in Section 1.4. But the room outlived its first experiment. Within a generation its graduates had carried it to Wurzburg, Cornell, Columbia, Pennsylvania and Harvard. In Philadelphia in 1896 one of them, Lightner Witmer, brought the same patient, measuring attitude to a boy who could not spell, and in 1915 a student of another, Munsterberg, carried the laboratory to Calcutta. The clinical psychology you are preparing to study is a direct descendant of that winter in Leipzig.

Chapter summary

1.1 What Is Psychology? The word comes from the Greek psyche (soul) and logos (study); Christian Wolff made it standard in the 1730s, and Aristotle's De Anima was the first treatise on the soul. The definition has changed with each school: the soul, then the mind, then conscious experience (Wundt and Titchener; James's "science of mental life", 1890), then behaviour (McDougall, 1905 and 1908; Watson, 1913; Woodworth; the definition Indian textbooks credit to N. L. Munn). Today psychology is the scientific study of behaviour, which is overt, and mental processes, which are internal and covert. Its four goals are to describe, explain, predict and control (or influence). Basic research seeks understanding for its own sake; applied research solves practical problems. Clinical psychology assesses and treats psychological disorders, while counselling psychology serves people in the normal range with adjustment problems. Comparative psychology studies animal behaviour across species, ethology studies it in natural settings, evolutionary psychology explains behaviour as the product of natural selection, behaviour genetics asks how much variation is due to genes, and differential psychology studies individual differences.

1.2 Philosophical Roots: A Long Past. Nativism holds that some knowledge is inborn and empiricism that all knowledge comes from experience; rationalism makes reason the chief route to knowledge. Plato was a nativist and rationalist who saw learning as recollection; Aristotle was an empiricist who gave the first laws of association (similarity, contrast, contiguity). Descartes treated the body as a machine, described the reflex, held innate ideas and proposed dualism with interaction through the pineal gland. The British empiricists followed: Hobbes the materialist; Locke with the tabula rasa, sensation and reflection, simple and complex ideas, and primary and secondary qualities; Berkeley with esse est percipi and a learned theory of depth; Hume with impressions, ideas and the laws of resemblance, contiguity and cause and effect. The associationists Hartley, James Mill (mental mechanics), John Stuart Mill (mental chemistry) and Bain built the mind out of linked elements. On the continent, Leibniz proposed monads, petites perceptions and apperception, Herbart the threshold of consciousness and the apperceptive mass, and Kant innate categories and the claim that psychology could never be a science. The mind-body positions are interactionism, psychophysical parallelism, epiphenomenalism (Huxley), materialist and idealist monism, and Spinoza's double-aspect theory. Ontology concerns existence and epistemology knowledge; Comte's positivism rejected introspection.

1.3 Physiological and Biological Roots. The Bell-Magendie law (1811, 1822) separated sensory dorsal roots from motor ventral roots. Johannes Muller's doctrine of specific nerve energies held that the quality of a sensation depends on the nerve stimulated. Helmholtz measured the speed of the nerve impulse in 1850 (about 27 metres per second) and proposed unconscious inference and the trichromatic theory. Localisation of function came through Gall and Spurzheim's phrenology (right idea, wrong method), Flourens' ablation studies, Broca's patient Tan (1861), Wernicke's sensory aphasia (1874) and Fritsch and Hitzig's electrical mapping of the motor cortex (1870). Darwin's natural selection (1859) and his book on emotional expression (1872) gave psychology continuity between species, the question of function and an interest in variation. His half-cousin Galton founded the study of individual differences with Hereditary Genius (1869), twin studies, the anthropometric laboratory (1884), regression, correlation, word association and the questionnaire, and coined eugenics (1883). Romanes' anecdotal method invited anthropomorphism, which Lloyd Morgan's canon of parsimony (1894) corrected, as Pfungst's study of Clever Hans (1907) showed.

1.4 Measuring the Mind: Psychophysics and Mental Chronometry. Weber found that the just noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the standard stimulus (Weber's law). Fechner, after his insight of 22 October 1850, proposed that sensation grows with the logarithm of stimulus intensity (S = k log I), published Elements of Psychophysics in 1860, gave the three classical psychophysical methods and is the founder of psychophysics; Boring argued that 1860 could even be taken as psychology's founding date. The astronomers' personal equation revealed individual differences in reaction time. Donders' subtraction method (1868) compared simple, go/no-go and choice reactions to time the stages of identification and choice, founding mental chronometry, though it assumes "pure insertion" of stages. Ebbinghaus extended measurement to memory with nonsense syllables, the savings method and the forgetting curve (On Memory, 1885).

1.5 Wilhelm Wundt and the Founding of Experimental Psychology. Wundt (1832 to 1920), trained in medicine and physiology and once Helmholtz's assistant, published Principles of Physiological Psychology (1873 to 1874), moved to Leipzig in 1875, began laboratory research in 1879 and founded Philosophische Studien in 1881. Psychology for Wundt studied immediate experience, analysed into sensations and simple feelings. His system was voluntarism: the mind actively organises experience through attention and will, apperception brings contents into the focus of consciousness, and creative synthesis produces wholes with new properties. His tridimensional theory of feeling (1896) described feelings as pleasure-displeasure, tension-relaxation and excitement-depression. His method was controlled internal perception (innere Wahrnehmung), not armchair self-observation (Selbstbeobachtung), governed by four rules. Higher mental processes he studied through language, myth and custom in the ten-volume Volkerpsychologie (1900 to 1920). His students included Hall, Cattell, Munsterberg, Kulpe, Titchener, Witmer, Kraepelin, Spearman and, briefly, Bekhterev.

1.6 Structuralism and Its European Rivals. Titchener carried a stricter version of Wundt's programme to Cornell and named it structuralism (1898). He sought the elements of consciousness (sensations, images and affections) and their attributes (quality, intensity, duration, clearness, extensity), kept only the pleasant-unpleasant dimension of feeling, and trained observers to avoid the stimulus error. Meaning came from context, and perception was a complex of sensory and imaginal processes. Kulpe's Wurzburg school found imageless thought and showed how the Aufgabe, the determining tendency and set (Einstellung) direct thinking; the resulting controversy exposed the unreliability of introspection. Brentano's act psychology (1874) studied mental acts and their intentionality, Stumpf studied tone and trained the Gestalt founders, and G. E. Muller extended psychophysics and memory research at Gottingen. Structuralism declined because introspection could not settle disputes, observation altered experience, elementism missed wholes, its subject matter was narrow and it refused application; cognitive psychology later revived its topics.

1.7 Functionalism and the American Pioneers. Functionalism studied the mind as it functions in adapting the organism to its environment, drawing on Darwin, pragmatism and American practicality. James's Principles of Psychology (1890) described the stream of consciousness, habit, the material, social and spiritual selves and the pure ego, and the James-Lange theory of emotion. Dewey's reflex arc paper (1896) founded the Chicago school, Angell's "Province of Functional Psychology" (1906) defined it and Carr's adaptive act matured it. At Columbia, Cattell coined "mental test" (1890), Woodworth gave the S-O-R formula and dynamic psychology, and Thorndike began the study of animal learning. Hall took the first American doctorate (1878), opened the first American research laboratory (1883), founded the American Journal of Psychology (1887) and the APA (1892), and described adolescence as storm and stress. Witmer opened the first psychological clinic (1896) and named clinical psychology; Munsterberg founded forensic and industrial psychology. Calkins became the first woman APA president (1905), Washburn the first woman PhD in psychology (1894), and Hollingworth disproved claims about women's inferiority. Functionalism dissolved into the mainstream and led to behaviourism, applied psychology and evolutionary psychology.

1.8 Reading the History: Zeitgeist, Great Persons and "Fathers". The great-person (personalistic) theory, stated by Carlyle in 1841, explains history through exceptional individuals; the Zeitgeist (naturalistic) theory, championed by Boring, explains it through the spirit of the times, citing multiple discoveries (Bell and Magendie, Darwin and Wallace, James and Lange), premature discoveries such as Mendel's, and contextual forces such as economic opportunity, war and prejudice. Psychology began in Germany because of its research universities, its broad idea of Wissenschaft and its experimental physiology; it began with Wundt because he, unlike the precursors, declared the science, gave it a laboratory, a journal and students; and 1879 marks the start of original student research. "Father" can mean founder, namer, chief influence or first in a country, so the qualifier in the question decides. In India, Seal drew up a syllabus in 1905, Sengupta set up the first laboratory (1915) and the first department (1916) at Calcutta and is the founder of modern psychology in India, Bose earned the first Indian doctorate (1921) and founded the Indian Psychoanalytic Society (1922), Mysore's department began in 1924 under Gopalaswamy, and the Indian Psychological Association followed in 1925. Presentism judges the past by present standards and distorted Wundt's reputation until historians returned to his own texts.

From the past papers

  1. Who was the first to propose and establish Psychology as the scientific study of behaviour? (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) R.S. Woodworth (b) J.B. William (c) Wilhelm Wundt (d) N.L. Munn

    Answer: (d) N. L. Munn. "Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour" is the definition Indian textbooks quote from Munn, and that is what the key rewards. Woodworth defined psychology as the study of the activities of the individual in relation to the environment, and Wundt as the science of immediate conscious experience; "J. B. William" is not a recognised name (perhaps a misprint for J. B. Watson). Historically McDougall (1905, 1908) and Watson (1913) made behaviour the subject first, so read such stems closely.

  2. Which branch of psychology focuses on the study of animal behavior? (RML 2025)

    (a) Social psychology (b) Abnormal psychology (c) Comparative psychology (d) Differential psychology

    Answer: (c) Comparative psychology. It compares behaviour and learning across species, a branch that grew out of Darwin's idea of continuity and Romanes' Animal Intelligence (1882). Social psychology studies people in relation to one another, abnormal psychology studies disorders, and differential psychology studies individual differences among people.

  3. What does clinical psychology primarily focus on? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Industrial applications (b) Assessment and treatment of mental illness (c) Behavioural changes in animals (d) Organizational management

    Answer: (b) Assessment and treatment of mental illness. Clinical psychology, named by Lightner Witmer, assesses, diagnoses and treats psychological disorders. Industrial applications and organisational management belong to industrial-organisational psychology, and behavioural change in animals to comparative psychology.

  4. In the nature versus nurture debate, which early philosopher argued that the mind was a "tabula rasa" or blank slate at birth? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Plato (b) Rene Descartes (c) John Locke (d) Immanuel Kant

    Answer: (c) John Locke. In his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690) Locke described the mind at birth as "white paper", with every idea coming from sensation or reflection. Plato thought learning was recollection of inborn knowledge, Descartes believed in innate ideas, and Kant held that the mind imposes innate forms and categories on experience; all three are on the nativist side.

  5. ____ is a source of knowledge as per Empiricists. (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Logical reasoning (b) Theories (c) Sensory experiences (d) Contemplations

    Answer: (c) Sensory experiences. Empiricism holds that all knowledge comes from experience delivered through the senses. Logical reasoning and contemplation are the rationalists' routes to knowledge (Plato, Descartes, Leibniz), and theories are products of knowledge rather than its source.

  6. According to Wundt 'apperceptive mass' constitutes ___________ . (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Compound of elemental processes bound by association (b) Sensation and pure perception (c) Pure perception and preexisting ideas (d) Sensation plus unconscious inference

    Answer: (c) Pure perception and preexisting ideas. The apperceptive mass is the organised body of ideas already in the mind, into which a new perception is taken up and understood; the term is Herbart's, and Wundt reworked the idea of apperception. Option (a) is the associationist and structuralist picture of the mind, option (b) leaves out the existing ideas that are the whole point, and option (d) is Helmholtz's theory of perception.

  7. Herman von Helmholtz (1821-94): (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) He was a proponent of the just noticeable difference (b) He gave the trichromatic theory of colour vision (c) He proposed the concept of the unconscious (d) He gave the law of effect

    Answer: (b) He gave the trichromatic theory of colour vision. Building on Thomas Young's suggestion of 1802, Helmholtz developed the Young-Helmholtz theory of three receptor types. The just noticeable difference is Weber's and Fechner's, the law of effect is Thorndike's, and although Helmholtz proposed unconscious inference in perception, the concept of the unconscious mind belongs to Freud and, before him, to Leibniz and Herbart.

  8. What do Hermann Ebbinghaus, Francis Galton and Sigmund Freud share in common? (RML 2020)

    (a) Each of them worked independently and were pioneers in the field of psychology (b) Each of them founded a formal psychology laboratory (c) Each of them developed novel observational methods to study how the mind works (d) (a) and (c)

    Answer: (d) (a) and (c). All three worked largely alone and were pioneers, and all three invented methods: Ebbinghaus nonsense syllables and the savings method, Galton the questionnaire, word association and correlation, Freud free association. The RML 2020 key printed (a), but since (c) is also true, (d) is the more defensible answer and is the one the Panwar bank keys. Option (b) is false because Freud never founded a laboratory.

  9. Who established the first laboratory for experimental study of psychology? (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Galton (b) Weber (c) Wundt (d) Jung

    Answer: (c) Wundt. Wundt's Leipzig laboratory began research in 1879, the conventional founding date of psychology as an independent science. Galton's laboratory (London, 1884) was anthropometric, a centre for measuring individual differences; Weber was a physiologist who founded no psychology laboratory; Jung developed word association as a clinical tool in Zurich.

  10. Who gave the concept of 'Folk psychology'? (RML 2024)

    (a) Wilhelm Maximilian Wundt (b) Sigmund Freud (c) Ivan Pavlov (d) Wilhelm Roentgen

    Answer: (a) Wilhelm Maximilian Wundt. His ten-volume Volkerpsychologie (1900 to 1920) studied the higher mental processes through language, myth and custom. Freud founded psychoanalysis and Pavlov discovered classical conditioning; Roentgen was the physicist who discovered X-rays, there only because he shares Wundt's first name.

  11. Charles Spearman is to Factor Analysis as Wilhelm Wundt is to: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Psychoanalysis (b) Introspection (c) Functionalism (d) Humanism

    Answer: (b) Introspection. The analogy pairs each pioneer with his signature method: Spearman (himself a Leipzig doctorate) invented factor analysis, and Wundt made controlled introspection the method of the first laboratory. Psychoanalysis is Freud's, functionalism is associated with James, and humanism with Maslow and Rogers.

  12. Who among the following has defined perception as a complex of sensory and imaginal events? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Kimbel (b) Titchener (c) Bruner (d) Schafer

    Answer: (b) Titchener. For the structuralist Titchener, a perception is built from sensations supplied by the object plus images supplied by past experience, which is also the heart of his context theory of meaning. Bruner and Schafer belong to the "New Look" research of the 1940s, which showed that needs and values shape perception (Chapter 4), and Kimble is known for work on learning; none of them held the structuralist definition.

  13. The current approach to psychology most closely aligned with the structuralists in its interest in topics like consciousness, mental imagery and thought process is: (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Neuroscience (b) Cognitive psychology (c) Behaviourism (d) Functionalism

    Answer: (b) Cognitive psychology. It studies the structuralists' topics, consciousness, imagery and thought, with reaction times, error patterns and brain imaging instead of trained introspection. Neuroscience studies the brain rather than the contents of experience, behaviourism banished consciousness altogether, and functionalism asked what the mind is for rather than what it is made of.

  14. Functionalism, an early school of thought in psychology, was most closely associated with which psychologist? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Edward Titchener (b) John Watson (c) William James (d) G. Stanley Hall

    Answer: (c) William James. James's Principles of Psychology (1890) inspired the functionalist attitude, and Dewey and Angell turned it into a school at Chicago. Titchener founded the rival structuralism, Watson founded behaviourism, and Hall, though functionalist in spirit, is examined for institutions (the APA, the first American research laboratory) and for adolescence.

  15. Functionalism is how people work, play, and adapt to their surroundings. This viewpoint was given by: (Amity 2023)

    (a) William James (b) William Wundt (c) Charles Darwin (d) John B. Watson

    Answer: (a) William James. The functionalist question of how the mind helps us adapt is James's. "William Wundt" is Wilhelm Wundt misnamed, the voluntarist of Leipzig; Darwin inspired functionalism but belonged to no psychological school; Watson founded behaviourism.

  16. The aim of Functionalism was to study ______ as it functions in adapting the ______ to its environment. (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Organism, Mind (b) Mind, Organism (c) Thinking, Organism (d) Attention, Individual

    Answer: (b) Mind, Organism. The functionalists studied the mind as an instrument that adapts the whole living organism to its environment, the Darwinian question of what consciousness is for. Option (a) reverses the order, and options (c) and (d) narrow the subject to single processes, which is not how the school defined itself.

  17. The term "stream of consciousness", referring to the flow of thoughts in the human mind, was coined by which psychologist? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Carl Jung (b) Wilhelm Wundt (c) William James (d) B.F. Skinner

    Answer: (c) William James. In the Principles (1890) James argued that consciousness "is nothing jointed; it flows", against the structuralists' picture of separate elements. Jung is known for analytical psychology and the collective unconscious, Wundt analysed consciousness into elements, and Skinner, a radical behaviourist, did not study consciousness at all.

  18. The first person to use the term clinical psychology in print was (RML 2020)

    (a) Erik Erikson (b) Sigmund Freud (c) Lightner Witmer (d) Cattell

    Answer: (c) Lightner Witmer. Witmer opened the first psychological clinic in 1896, proposed the new field that year, and used the term in print in the first issue of his journal The Psychological Clinic (1907). Erikson is known for the psychosocial stages, Freud for psychoanalysis, and Cattell for coining "mental test".

  19. American Psychological Association (APA) was founded in: (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) 1892 (b) 1893 (c) 1896 (d) 1897

    Answer: (a) 1892. The APA was founded at Clark University in July 1892, with G. Stanley Hall as its first president. 1896 is the tempting distractor because it is the year of Witmer's clinic and Dewey's reflex arc paper.

  20. Who among the following was the first female president of the American Psychological Association (APA)? (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Margaret Floy Washburn (b) Leta Stetter Hollingworth (c) Karen Horney (d) Mary Calkins

    Answer: (d) Mary Calkins. Mary Whiton Calkins became APA president in 1905. Washburn was the first woman to earn a PhD in psychology (1894) and the second woman president (1921); Hollingworth is known for the psychology of women and of gifted children; Karen Horney was a psychoanalyst who was never APA president.

  21. Who is often referred to as the "Founder of Modern Psychology in India"? (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Girindra Shekhar Bose (b) Narendra Nath Sen Gupta (c) Amitosh Mukherjee (d) M.V. Gopalswamy

    Answer: (b) Narendra Nath Sen Gupta. Trained under Munsterberg at Harvard, Sengupta set up India's first laboratory of experimental psychology (1915) and its first department of psychology (1916) at Calcutta. Bose is the father of psychoanalysis in India (1922), Gopalaswamy began the Mysore department (1924), and "Amitosh Mukherjee" is not a founder of Indian psychology (perhaps a garbling of Sir Ashutosh Mookerjee, the vice-chancellor who backed the Calcutta department, an administrator rather than a psychologist).

  22. Which of the following Indian psychologists played a key role in establishing the first psychology laboratory in India? (Panwar Systems & Theories bank)

    (a) Anand C. Paranjpe (b) Uma Chaudhuri (c) Sudhir Kakar (d) G. Misra

    Answer: (b) Uma Chaudhuri, as keyed; historically, none of the four. The first laboratory was N. N. Sengupta's, at Calcutta in 1915, and he is not among the options. Uma Chaudhuri of Calcutta is known for the Indian modification of the Thematic Apperception Test (1960), and she is the name the key expects; Paranjpe, Kakar and Misra are later leaders of indigenous Indian psychology (Section 2.8). If Sengupta is ever offered, choose him.
