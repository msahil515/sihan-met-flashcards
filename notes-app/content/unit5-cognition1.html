Unit 5 — Attention, Perception & Memory

The cognitive core of the syllabus, built from Solso-style cognitive psychology and the classic learning systems: how attention selects a trickle from the flood of sensation (Broadbent, Treisman, Deutsch-Norman, Kahneman), how organisms learn by association and consequence (Pavlov, Skinner, Thorndike, Guthrie, Hull, Spence, Tolman, Bandura), how memory encodes, stores and retrieves (Atkinson-Shiffrin, Baddeley, Tulving, Craik & Lockhart), why we forget (decay, interference, retrieval failure, repression, motivated forgetting), and how the visual system builds a stable percept of form, depth and pattern (Gestalt, template/feature/prototype, Biederman, Selfridge) under uncertainty (signal detection theory). Each model is taught for its mechanism and its exam relevance, with theorists, dates and the traps that examiners love.

1. Attention: forms & models

Solso defines attention as "the concentration of mental effort on sensory or mental events." More fully (Duncan 1999; Motter 1999; Posner & Fernandez-Duque 1999), attention is the means by which we actively process a limited amount of information from the enormous amount available through our senses, stored memories and other cognitive processes. It is a concentration of mental activity that includes both conscious and unconscious processes.

Definition

Attention = selective concentration of finite mental resources on some stimuli at the expense of others. The central problem it solves is that the sensory world delivers far more information than the system can fully process.

Forms of attention

  • Divided attention — attending to several messages simultaneously, responding to each as needed. Performance typically suffers unless the tasks are well practised; practice alters the limits of attentional capacity.
  • Selective attention — focusing on one task/stimulus while disregarding the others. It simplifies our lives because attentional capacity is limited. The Stroop effect is the classic demonstration of the cost of selecting against a dominant response.
  • Sustained attention (vigilance) — the maintenance of selective attention to infrequent events over long periods. There is a reliable vigilance decrement: performance declines as time-on-task wears on, affected by the neurological and physiological state of the person.

Filter / bottleneck models — early selection

Everyday phenomena like the cocktail-party effect (you can follow one conversation in a noisy room, yet your own name spoken across the room captures you) are explained by filter theories. Early-selection means attention operates early in the information-processing sequence: only one source (the attended one) reaches meaningful processing; unattended information is actively filtered/blocked before it can compete for scarce higher-order resources.

Broadbent's switch (filter) model (1958)

Broadbent proposed a simple on-off switch: attention directs processing to one input channel, which is fully analysed for meaning and made available to consciousness. Because the switch is all-or-none, additional messages are completely blocked. Attention is attracted and maintained by sensory/physical attributes of messages (e.g., the voice qualities at a party). The switch does not allow unconscious processing: unattended signals left in sensory memory rapidly decay and never receive the extra processing needed for longer-term retention. A subject should therefore be able to report only the physical features of the unattended message, not its meaning.

Cherry's dichotic listening & shadowing (1953)

Colin Cherry created a paradigm to test early selection: dichotic listening (different messages to each ear at once) combined with shadowing (repeating one message aloud as it occurs). Shadowing forces the subject onto one message so they cannot switch. Findings that strain the strict switch: when the meaning of the shadowed sentence switches to the non-shadowed ear, shadowing is disrupted (Treisman 1960) — the subject follows the meaning rather than the physical cue of ear location, allowing determination of when attention shifts.

Treisman's attenuator model (1964)

Anne Treisman proposed a more flexible early-selection theory using a different mechanical metaphor: instead of an off/on switch, attention works like an attenuator (regulator) that allows gradations of energy through. Most of the attended message passes through; some unattended information may also reach the level of pattern recognition. Attention thus becomes a matter of degree. Physical cues tune the attenuator, but it can also be influenced by the meaning of previously analysed material, so control is exerted by both sensory and semantic information. It remains an early-selection theory because filtering still occurs prior to full meaningful analysis; partial analysis of all signals occurs.

Late-selection models — Deutsch & Deutsch (1963), Norman (1968)

Late-selection models propose that all information is recognised: every sensory input activates its corresponding meaning in long-term memory, but attention then selects among the activated meanings, allowing only a limited amount to reach consciousness. Selective attention operates on response output, not input. The non-shadowed information, requiring no overt response, may be rapidly forgotten before testing. The activation of meaning is unconscious; awareness itself is treated as a response. The activity decays quickly unless the activated meaning is selected for conscious processing.

High-yield

Order the bottleneck: Broadbent (early, all-or-none switch, physical cues) → Treisman (early, attenuator, partial/graded analysis, sensory + semantic) → Deutsch-Deutsch / Norman (late selection, full recognition, filter at response). "Where is the filter?" is the single most-tested question in this topic.

Capacity models — Kahneman (1973)

Daniel Kahneman recast attention not as a filter but as mental effort — a pool of cognitive resources to be allocated. Different tasks demand different amounts of capacity; the number of activities done simultaneously is set by how much capacity each requires. Attention is the process of allocating capacity to inputs (input side) and activities (output side). The figure's central element is the allocation policy, controlled by:

  • Enduring dispositions — involuntary attention (e.g., capacity automatically allocated to the mention of your name).
  • Momentary intentions — voluntary goals (e.g., "listen to the right ear").
  • Evaluation of demands — if two activities need more capacity than is available, perform one and delay the other.
  • Arousal — total available capacity varies with arousal state (links to Yerkes-Dodson).

Automatic vs controlled processing — Schneider & Shiffrin

Walter Schneider and Richard Shiffrin proposed two levels of processing relevant to attention:

AutomaticControlled
Used forEasy tasks, highly familiar itemsDifficult tasks, unfamiliar items
ModeParallel (two or more items at once)Serial (one item at a time)

In their study, participants saw a rapid series of 20 frames; each of four locations could hold a number, letter or dots. Exposure time was 40 or 800 ms. In the consistent-mapping condition (targets never become distractors) the task became so easy that people used automatic processing: only frame-exposure time affected accuracy — neither target-set size nor frame size mattered. In the varied-mapping condition (a target on one trial could be an irrelevant item on the next), accuracy depended on exposure time and people were more accurate searching for one target than four, and with one item per frame than four.

Feature integration theory — Anne Treisman (1986, 1998)

FIT explains how separately-coded features become a single perceived object, in two stages:

  1. Pre-attentive stage — an object is automatically analysed into separate features (e.g., a rolling red ball → colour red, shape round, movement to the right). Each feature is processed in a separate brain area and they exist independently at this stage; no attention required.
  2. Focused-attention stage — attention binds the features together so we perceive the whole object. The observer's attention plays the crucial role in combining features.
Trap

FIT's "pre-attentive" stage is parallel and effortless; the binding stage requires focused attention. Without attention, features can be wrongly recombined — the classic illusory conjunction. Do not confuse Anne Treisman's FIT (perception/binding) with her earlier attenuator model (auditory attention) — same author, two distinct contributions.

2. Classical & instrumental conditioning

Classical (Pavlovian) conditioning

Ivan Pavlov discovered the conditioned reflex. His Nobel-winning digestive work (The Work of the Digestive Glands, 1897; Nobel Prize 1904) led him to investigate "psychical secretion": dogs salivated to artificial/neutral stimuli, not just to food. This conditioned reflex is the simplest form of learning (an acquired behaviour).

The process begins with a natural unconditioned reflex (innate): food (UCS) → salivation (UCR), a pre-programmed survival measure. When a neutral stimulus (a sound, e.g., a metronome) coincides with or immediately precedes the UCS over a few pairings, it becomes an active conditioned stimulus (CS) capable of eliciting salivation on its own (the CR). Pavlov first called this newly acquired habit psychic secretion.

Other Pavlovian phenomena

  • Conditioned alpha blocking — any arousing stimulus paired with a neutral stimulus can produce a CR; any form of activation acts as a UCS capable of generating a conditioned reflex.
  • Law of summation of conditioned stimuli — a stimulus's strength is limited; beyond a level, more intensity is less effective. But two weak CSs combined within the limit produce roughly double the effect (arithmetical summation).
  • Inhibition (extinction) — Pavlov called extinction inhibition: presenting the CS in the absence of the UCS. Four types of internal inhibition: (1) extinction/experimental extinction (CS without reinforcement); (2) retardation/inhibition of delay (withholding reinforcement beyond the bonding time destroys the CR); (3) conditioned inhibition (combining the CS with other stimuli makes it lose effect); (4) differential inhibition (extinguish peripheral CRs by reinforcing only the original stimulus, preventing spread).
  • Irradiation (generalization) — responding to stimuli similar to the CS; Pavlov called it irradiation, believing neuronic action spreads to neighbouring brain areas. Concentration (discrimination) is the careful elimination of unwanted responses, using extinction.
  • Reciprocal induction — combining excitatory and inhibitory conditioning to produce contrasting effects; a method of strengthening/establishing what is learned.
  • First & second signal systems — the first signal system = sensations from the outside world (shared with animals); the second signal system (uniquely human) = stimuli that reach us as speech. Verbal conditioning and language acquisition belong here; Pavlov called the second signal system the "signal of signals."
  • Orienting reflex — Pavlov's investigatory reflex; an innate response to investigate novel surroundings, adaptive by preparing for emergencies and conserving energy. Habituation = decreasing sensitivity with repeated stimulation ("pseudolearning"). Sensitization (dishabituation) = increased response, the disruption of established habituation.

Instrumental / operant conditioning — Skinner

Skinner distinguished respondent behaviour (elicited by a known stimulus) from operant behaviour (not elicited but simply emitted). Correspondingly:

  • Type S (respondent) conditioning = identical to classical conditioning; strength measured by the magnitude of the CR.
  • Type R (operant) conditioning = named for the emphasis on the response; strength measured by response rate.

Core principles of Type R: any response followed by a reinforcing stimulus tends to be repeated; a reinforcing stimulus is anything that increases the rate of an operant response. Skinner even defined culture as a set of reinforcement contingencies.

Apparatus & techniques

  • Skinner box — a small test chamber (direct descendant of Thorndike's puzzle box) with grid floor, light, lever and food cup; lever press fires the feeder, releasing a pellet.
  • Cumulative recording — time on the x-axis, total responses on the y-axis; the trace rises when behaviour occurs and runs parallel to the x-axis when it does not.
  • Lever-press conditioning — steps: (1) deprivation (place animal on a deprivation schedule); (2) magazine training (the feeder makes a loud click before delivering a pellet, away from the cup, so the click becomes a secondary reinforcer via association with food); (3) the animal eventually presses the lever, firing the magazine, whose click reinforces the press and signals the animal to the cup.
  • Shaping — a faster route using two components: differential reinforcement (some responses reinforced, others not) and successive approximation (only responses increasingly similar to the target are reinforced).
  • Extinction & spontaneous recovery — removing the reinforcer flattens the cumulative record to the x-axis (extinction); the response rate returns to the operant level (its rate before reinforcement). After a rest in the home cage, the animal will press again briefly without retraining — spontaneous recovery.

Stimulus control, reinforcement & chaining

  • Discriminative operant — if reinforcement is given only when a light is on, the light becomes SD (discriminative stimulus signalling reinforcement); light-off = SΔ. A discriminative operant is a response given to one set of circumstances but not another.
  • Secondary reinforcement — a neutral stimulus paired with a primary reinforcer (food/water) acquires reinforcing properties of its own. It is independent and non-specific: it will condition new, unrelated responses. A generalized reinforcer is a secondary reinforcer paired with more than one primary reinforcer (e.g., money).
  • Chaining — one response brings the organism into contact with stimuli that act as SD for the next response, and so on; in the Skinner box the chain is held together by the food pellet (a primary positive reinforcer).
  • Reinforcers vs punishment — a positive reinforcer (primary or secondary) increases response probability when added; a negative reinforcer increases it when removed. Punishment removes something positive or adds something negative; it merely suppresses behaviour — when the threat is removed, the behaviour returns to its original level.
Trap

Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment. Negative reinforcement increases a behaviour by removing an aversive stimulus; punishment decreases behaviour. Examiners exploit this constantly.

Schedules of reinforcement

An organism reinforced on every correct response (continuous) extinguishes faster than one reinforced for only a percentage of responses — the partial reinforcement effect (PRE): partial reinforcement leads to greater resistance to extinction. Skinner and Ferster wrote Schedules of Reinforcement.

ScheduleRule
Continuous (CRF)Every correct response during acquisition is reinforced.
Fixed interval (FI)Reinforced for a response made only after a set interval of time.
Fixed ratio (FR)Reinforced when every nth response is made.
Variable interval (VI)Reinforced for responses at the end of time intervals of variable duration.
Variable ratio (VR)Reinforced on the average of every n responses (e.g., VR5 ≈ one in five on average); may get two in a row or none for ten to fifteen.

Verbal behaviour & related findings

  • Verbal behaviour — Skinner argued language is explained within reinforcement theory, classifying verbal responses by their relation to reinforcement: mand (a demand; reinforced when the need is met), tact (naming objects/events appropriately; reinforced by others' confirmation of the match), echoic behaviour (reinforced when someone's response is repeated verbatim), autoclitic behaviour (verbal behaviour based on/depending upon other verbal behaviour; qualifies responses, expresses relations, provides grammatical framework).
  • Premack principle — David Premack: any response of high frequency can reinforce a response of lower frequency; all responses are potential reinforcers.
  • Instinctive drift — the Brelands (1961) found innate behaviour patterns gradually displace learned behaviour; learned behaviour drifts toward instinctive behaviour.
  • Autoshaping — with non-contingent reinforcement (a pigeon reinforced at intervals regardless of behaviour) plus a disc illuminated just before food, the pigeon learns to peck the disc even though pecking is never reinforced — the animal shapes its own behaviour.

3. Fundamental learning theories

Connectionism — Edward L. Thorndike (1874–1949)

Thorndike called the association between sense impressions and impulses to action a bond or connection — the neural connection between stimuli (S) and responses (R). His connectionism combines associationism, Darwinism and the methods of science.

  • Selecting and connecting — the most basic learning is trial-and-error (originally "selecting and connecting"). Using puzzle boxes, he plotted time-to-escape against trials; the time fell gradually with successive trials, so he concluded learning is incremental, not insightful.
  • Learning is not mediated by ideas — learning is direct, not mediated by thinking/reasoning (parsimony: reject reason in favour of direct S-R connection).
  • All mammals learn alike — including humans, by the same laws.

Laws of connectionism

LawStatement
ReadinessWhen a conduction unit is ready to conduct, conducting is satisfying; not conducting is annoying. When not ready but forced to conduct, conducting is annoying.
ExerciseConnections strengthen with use (law of use) and weaken with disuse (law of disuse).
EffectA response followed by a satisfying state strengthens the connection; one followed by an annoying state weakens it. He posited a neurophysiological confirming reaction (the organism is not conscious of it) to explain how effect acts after the response.

Secondary concepts

  • Multiple response (varied reaction) — the first step in learning: if the first response fails, try others.
  • Set or attitude — dispositions/pre-adjustments the learner brings (cultural/genetic heritage; temporary states like deprivation, fatigue, emotion). Satisfiers and annoyers differ among individuals.
  • Pre-potency of elements — "the partial or piecemeal activity of a situation"; we respond to some elements and not others. How we respond depends on what we attend to and the responses attached to it.
  • Response by analogy — we respond to a novel situation as to a related familiar one; transfer is set by the number of identical elements the two situations share (identical-elements theory of transfer of training). He urged school curricula to resemble real-life tasks.
  • Associative shifting — begin with an S-R connection, then gradually drop original stimulus elements and add new ones; as long as enough original elements remain, the same response is given.

Revised laws (post-1930)

In September 1929, before the International Congress of Psychology (New Haven), Thorndike opened his address with "I was wrong."

  • Revised law of exercise — mere repetition (use) does not strengthen, and disuse does not weaken much; he discarded the entire law of exercise after 1930.
  • Revised law of effect — only half true: reinforcement increases connection strength, but punishment does nothing to it.
  • Belongingness — associations form more readily when elements "belong" together (subject & verb > the last word of one sentence and the first of the next).
  • Spread of effect — a satisfying state increases the probability not only of the reinforced response but also of surrounding responses; neutral responses close (in similarity or time) to the reinforced one increase in frequency.

Guthrie — contiguity / one-trial learning

  • Law of contiguity — "A combination of stimuli which has accompanied a movement will on its recurrence tend to be followed by that movement" — explicitly without confirmatory waves, reinforcement or pleasant effects.
  • One-trial learning — "A stimulus pattern gains its full associative strength on the occasion of its first pairing with a response."
  • Recency principle — whatever we did last in a given set of circumstances is what we tend to do again when those circumstances recur.
  • Movement-produced stimuli — movements of the body (muscles, tendons, joints) produce stimuli distinct from external stimulation; responses can be conditioned to them.
  • Movements vs acts vs skills — movements are simple muscle contractions; acts are made of many movements (defined by what they accomplish/change in the environment); a skill is made of many acts. Practice does not improve movements but improves their coordination into acts and skills.
  • Reinforcement — merely a mechanical arrangement explained by his one law: reinforcement changes the stimulating conditions and thereby prevents unlearning.
  • Forgetting — like learning, occurs in one trial: all forgetting involves new learning — an alternative response occurs in the presence of a stimulus pattern.
High-yield

Contrast the founders by their core mechanism: Thorndike = law of effect (consequences); Guthrie = contiguity / one-trial (no reinforcement needed); Pavlov = stimulus substitution (CS–UCS); Skinner = operant contingency (response–reinforcer); Hull = drive reduction (need-based).

4. Hull, discrimination, latent & observational learning

Clark Hull — systematic behaviour theory

Hull's last book, A Behavior System, has a logical structure of postulates and theorems "much like Euclid's geometry."

  • Postulate 1 — sensing the external environment & the stimulus trace: a stimulus trace (S) continues for a few seconds after the stimulus event ends.
  • Postulate 2 — interaction of sensory impulses: behaviour is seldom a function of only one stimulus.
  • Postulate 3 — unlearned behaviour: the organism is born with a hierarchy of responses (unlearned behaviour) triggered when a need arises; if one fails to satisfy the need, more than one reaction may occur.
  • Postulate 4 — contiguity + drive reduction as necessary conditions: if a stimulus leads to a response and that response satisfies a biological need, the S-R association is strengthened — the habit grows, i.e., habit strength (SHR). Primary reinforcement = need satisfaction (drive reduction); a secondary reinforcer is a stimulus closely and consistently associated with need diminution.
  • Postulate 5 — stimulus generalization: a stimulus similar to the training stimulus can elicit a conditioned response, by similarity; Hull called this generalized habit strength (SH̄R).
  • Postulate 6 — drives: a biological deficiency produces a drive (D), each associated with specific drive stimuli (e.g., hunger → stomach pangs; thirst → dry mouth, lips, throat).
  • Postulate 7 — reaction potential (SER): the likelihood of a learned response at any moment = SER = SHR × D.
  • Postulate 8 — reactive inhibition (IR): responding causes fatigue; IR (related to the muscular work of responding) operates against eliciting the CR and dissipates when the organism rests. It explains the reminiscence effect (improved performance after a rest).
  • Postulate 9 — conditioned inhibition (SIR): because IR is a negative drive state, not responding is reinforcing (it lets IR dissipate, reducing the negative drive of fatigue); the learned response of not responding becomes conditioned inhibition (SIR). Subtracting IR + SIR from SER gives effective reaction potential (SĒR) = SHR × D − (IR + SIR).
  • Postulate 10 — oscillation effect (SOR): an "inhibitory potentiality" varying moment to moment, subtracted to give momentary effective reaction potential (SĖR) = [SHR × D − (IR + SIR)] − SOR.
  • Postulates 11–16: SĖR must exceed a reaction threshold (SLR) before a response can occur (11–12); the higher SER, the shorter the latency (StR) (13); SER determines resistance to extinction (14); response amplitude (A) varies directly with SER (15); when incompatible responses compete, the one with the greatest SER occurs (16).

1952 revisions

  • Incentive motivation (K) — in 1943 Hull treated magnitude of reinforcement as a learning variable; research disagreed. Crespi (1942, 1944) and Zeaman (1949) showed performance changed radically when reinforcement magnitude changed after learning was complete — the Crespi effect. Organisms learn as fast for small as for large incentives but perform differently as incentive (K) varies.
  • Stimulus-intensity dynamism (V) — an intervening variable that varies with the intensity of the external stimulus; greater intensity → greater probability the learned response is elicited.
  • Drive-stimulus reduction — Hull shifted from drive reduction to drive-stimulus (SD) reduction: learning follows the reduction of drive stimuli (e.g., water quickly reduces dry-mouth stimulation). Sheffield and Roby (1950) found hungry rats reinforced by non-nutritive saccharine, challenging pure drive reduction.
  • Revised formula: SĖR = [SHR × D × V × K − (IR + SIR)] − SOR.
  • Fractional antedating goal response (rG) — stimuli experienced just before primary reinforcement become secondary reinforcers (via classical conditioning). They reinforce the responses that bring the organism to them and elicit rGs. The rG is some fraction of the goal response (RG) — e.g., minute chewing/salivation — and produces proprioceptive stimulation symbolised sG; the rG–sG mechanism is the "mental" component of chaining.
  • Habit family hierarchy — for any sG there are many possible overt responses; the most likely is the one reaching reinforcement most rapidly and with least effort (the goal-gradient hypothesis, a 1952 corollary, with delay of reinforcement J).

Discrimination learning — K. W. Spence

Spence's continuity theory (1936) describes discrimination learning as a cumulative process: the excitatory tendency of the positive cue is built up by successive reinforcements, while the negative cue's tendency is lowered by an active negative process (inhibition/frustration) that adds algebraically to the positive tendencies. Reinforced responses raise the correct cue's excitatory tendency; unrewarded responses lower the negative cue's. It accounts for transposition — selecting between two new stimuli on the basis of a previously reinforced relationship. (Gestalt theorists explain transposition by saying one learns relations among stimuli, not absolute elements.)

Latent learning — Tolman

Latent learning is learning not translated into performance; it can stay dormant before being shown in behaviour. Tolman and Honzik (1930) ran three groups of rats in a maze: never reinforced; always reinforced; not reinforced until day 11. The third group was key — once reinforcement began on day 11, they soon performed as well as the continuously reinforced group, showing they had been learning all along. For Tolman, learning occurs through observation, independent of reinforcement; what the animal learns is to expect reinforcement if a response is made, an expectation confirmed during acquisition. Latent extinction: a rat placed directly into an empty goal box stops traversing the maze on later trials — extinction without non-reinforced performance of the learned response. (S-R theorists like Spence explain latent extinction motivationally.)

Observational learning

  • Thorndike & Watson — concluded learning results only from direct, not vicarious, experience.
  • Miller & Dollard (1941) — accepted observational learning but explained it within Hullian theory: if imitative behaviour is reinforced it strengthens like any behaviour; imitative learning is a special case of instrumental conditioning. Three categories: same behaviour (two or more individuals respond to the same situation the same way), copying behaviour (one person's behaviour guided by another, e.g., an art instructor's corrective feedback), matched-dependent behaviour (an observer reinforced for blindly repeating a model's actions; characteristic of adults in unfamiliar situations). Imitation itself can become a habit — generalized imitation. For them, imitative learning = observation + overt responding + reinforcement.
  • Skinnerian analysis — observe model's behaviour → match the response → matching response is reinforced; maintained by a natural schedule. The model's behaviour acts as a discriminative stimulus indicating which actions will be reinforced; imitation is "nothing more than a discriminative operant."
  • Bandura — what one learns by observing is information, processed cognitively and acted on advantageously. Both Tolman's and Bandura's theories are cognitive, not reinforcement theories. The learning-performance distinction is central. Bandura (1965): children watched a film of a model hitting/kicking a large doll; one group saw the model reinforced, one saw it punished, one saw neutral consequences. Children who saw reinforcement were most aggressive (vicarious reinforcement facilitated), those who saw punishment least (vicarious punishment inhibited). Contrary to Miller & Dollard, observational learning occurred without the observer's own overt response being reinforced. In phase two, all children offered an incentive reproduced the behaviour — all had learned it; they differed only in performance.
  • Reciprocal determinism — Bandura (1986): a three-way interaction of person (P), environment (E), behaviour (B); behaviour influences person and environment as much as they influence behaviour. "Though the potential environment is identical for all animals, the actual environment depends upon their behaviour" (1977).

5. Memory processes & systems

Cognitive psychologists describe three common operations of memory: encoding (transforming a physical/sensory input into a representation that can enter memory), storage (retaining encoded information), and retrieval (gaining access to stored information).

Encoding

Encoding can be acoustic, visual, semantic or another modality, and the principle differs for STM and LTM.

  • Short-term storage — Conrad (1964) showed the importance of an acoustic code in STM. Baddeley (1966) argued STM relies primarily on an acoustic (not semantic) code: performance was much worse for visually-presented acoustically similar words, with little effect of semantic similarity. STM encoding is primarily acoustic, with some secondary semantic encoding; visual encoding occurs but is even more fleeting.
  • Long-term storage — primarily semantic. Levels of processing also influences LTM encoding: more information moves into LTM with a semantic strategy than a non-semantic one. LTM is not exclusively semantic — there is evidence for visual and acoustic encoding too; storage is flexible.

Transfer to LTM, consolidation & rehearsal

  • Entry into long-term declarative memory occurs via deliberately attending to comprehend, and making associations between new and known information — integration called consolidation. Metamemory strategies (reflecting on one's own memory to improve it) can preserve memories during consolidation.
  • Rehearsal — repeated recitation (overt/covert). Elaborative rehearsal elaborates items so they are meaningfully integrated or connected (more memorable, transfers to LTM); maintenance rehearsal simply repeats items, holding them in STM without transferring to LTM.
  • Spacing effect — distributed practice (sessions spaced over time) yields better memory than massed practice; the greater the distribution, the more is remembered over long periods (ideally spaced over months). It may occur because each session's encoding context varies, enriching schemas.
  • Sleep & consolidation — REM sleep matters; more REM after learning improves it. Insomniacs have trouble with consolidation.
  • Neuroscience — the hippocampus is important for memory; it shows low levels of acetylcholine during the activation associated with consolidation.
  • Reconsolidation — once a memory is recalled it may return to an unstable state, again vulnerable to interference/decay; reconsolidation re-fixes the previously-encoded information.

Organizing information

Stored memories are organized: participants show consistent word-order patterns in recall even when words are unrelated. Mnemonic devices organize lists to aid memorization; even music can serve as a mnemonic and a retrieval cue.

Retrieval

Sternberg's memory-scanning task suggested we retrieve serially and exhaustively. Categorization dramatically affects retrieval (cued recall > free recall). Material processed in certain cortical areas during perception activates those same areas again during LTM recall (recall of spatial positions activates parietal/precentral cortex; recall of faces activates the left prefrontal/temporal cortex and posterior cingulate). Recall tests test for accessibility (whether we can gain access) not availability (whether the information is stored). Initial recall is mediated by processing speed; longer-term retention is mediated by other cognitive processes (e.g., rehearsal strategies) — initial recall and actual long-term learning differ.

Stages of memory — modal model (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968)

Based on the information-processing approach; information is processed serially through stages: sensory memory → short-term store → long-term store.

Sensory memory

Closely tied to perception — "a record of our percepts." It is modality-specific (visual store holds visual info, auditory store auditory info, etc.). Capacity is relatively large but duration is very short (much less than a second); the information is relatively unprocessed (physical, not meaningful). The two best-studied stores are the icon (visual) and the echo (auditory).

  • Icon — Neisser (1967) named the brief visual store the icon, holding unprocessed (uncategorized) visual information for up to ~1 second. It can be masked/"erased" by a later stimulus (circles after letters erase the letter trace). Partial reports can be cued by colour/brightness but not by category or phonological sound — the icon's information is purely visual. Modern views see the icon lasting only ~150–200 ms before recoding into a more meaningful code (Neath & Surprenant 2003 found some category cueing was possible).
  • Echo — Neisser (1967) named the auditory store the echo. Recall can be cued by category to some degree (it works somewhat differently from the icon); the echo has a larger capacity than the icon. The suffix effect: an auditory recall cue (a spoken word) after a list seriously hinders recall of the last few items; a mere beep/tone or visual stimulus has little effect; the more auditory similarity between suffix and list, the greater the effect.

Short-term memory (STM)

A system holding a limited amount of information for brief periods; viewed as a "workbench for consciousness" — hence the alternative term working memory. Capacity ≈ 7 ± 2 independent units (Miller's magic number). If unrehearsed, information is lost in as little as 20 seconds (its retention duration). Evidence for STM as a distinct store:

  • Serial position curve — greater recall accuracy for words early (primacy) and late (recency) in a list than in the middle.
  • Word-length effect — memory span is greater for short than long words.
  • Word-similarity effect — span is greater for words that do not sound alike than for those that do.

Working memory — Baddeley & Hitch (1974)

Atkinson & Shiffrin's short-term store (STS) does more than hold ~7 items: it activates relevant information from LTS into STS, was equated with consciousness, and houses control processes (rehearsal, coding, integration, decision making). Baddeley & Hitch (1974) argued for a working memory = a limited-capacity workspace divided between storage and control processing. Baddeley (1981, 1986, 1990) proposed three components:

  • Central executive — directs the flow of information, choosing what is operated on, when, and how.
  • Visuospatial sketchpad — maintains visual material through visualization.
  • Phonological loop — carries out subvocal rehearsal to maintain verbal material.

Baddeley (1992) regarded WM as an evolution of the STM idea (moving away from a passive, temporary, limited-capacity storehouse), not a competitor.

Trap

The word-length and word-similarity (phonological-similarity) effects are the signature evidence for the phonological loop: short words rehearse faster, and acoustically distinct words resist confusion in the loop.

Long-term memory (LTM)

By the memory-systems view (Tulving, 1983), LTM is not unitary but made of subsystems. Squire (2004) draws the major distinction:

  • Declarative memory — retention of factual information and personally-experienced episodes; two types:
    • Episodic — memory for personally-experienced events, including contextual elements (time and place).
    • Semantic — knowledge about the world without contextual elements (where/when it was learned).
  • Procedural memory — "knowing how" to do something; unlike declarative memory, difficult to verbalize.

Retrieval in LTM

Retrieval depends on cues — stimuli associated with stored information that aid its retrieval.

  • Context-dependent memory — information entered in a particular context is easier to recall in that (or a similar) context.
  • State-dependent retrieval — easier retrieval when the internal state at retrieval matches the state at encoding.
  • Mood-dependent memory — what we remember in a mood is partly determined by what we learned in that same mood; the mood-congruence effect is the tendency to notice/remember information congruent with the current mood.

Levels-of-processing — Craik & Lockhart (1972)

Deep, meaningful processing yields more permanent retention than shallow, sensory processing. People analyse stimuli at various levels: shallow levels analyse physical/sensory characteristics (brightness, pitch); deep levels analyse meaning. A by-product of analysis is a memory trace: shallow analysis → fragile, quickly forgotten trace; deep analysis → durable, well-remembered trace. They distinguished maintenance vs elaborative rehearsal.

  • Craik & Tulving (1975) — people were ~3× more likely to recall a word if they had answered questions about its meaning than about its physical appearance.
  • Parkin (1984) — semantic judgments (category/synonym) yielded better surprise-recall than non-semantic ones.
  • Face recognition reviews — deep processing generally produces higher recall than shallow.
  • Compatibility (transfer-appropriate processing) — Moscovitch & Craik (1975) and Bransford et al. (1979) noted retrieval conditions should duplicate encoding conditions for deep processing to be highly effective; deep semantic processing may not be ideal unless retrieval conditions match encoding. When tested on rhyming patterns, people did better if they had done a rhyming encoding task and worse if they had done a sentence (semantic) task.
  • Why deep processing works — Craik & Lockhart (1986) credited distinctiveness and elaboration. Hunt & Elliot (1980): distinctive short/tall letter sequences (lymph, khaki, afghan) are recalled better than common orthographic sequences (leaky, kennel, airway).

6. Theories of forgetting

Logarithmic function of forgetting

Hermann Ebbinghaus studied memory/forgetting and plotted forgetting as a function of time, finding the forgetting curve is approximately logarithmic (rapid initial loss, then a slow tail). Rubin and Wenzel (1996) confirmed this with their logarithmic-loss law.

Interference theory

Our ability to remember current learning can be disrupted by earlier or future learning.

  • Proactive interference — earlier learning interferes with memory of later learning.
  • Retroactive interference — later learning disrupts memory for earlier learning.

Both are maximal when two different responses are associated with the same stimulus, intermediate when two similar responses share a stimulus, and minimal when two different stimuli are involved. Evidence for retroactive interference: eyewitness-testimony studies (post-event questioning interferes with the memory) and paired-associate lists; it is assumed to occur because two different responses become associated to the same stimulus. Bower, Thompson-Schill & Tulving (1994) found similarity of response categories is crucial. Underwood (1957) showed proactive interference's importance: ~80% forgotten in a day if participants had previously learned 15+ lists, vs ~20% if none — though proactive interference may account for less forgetting than Underwood assumed. Interference theory also explains the serial position effect (primacy and recency) and the fan effect (Anderson 1974): the more facts learned about a concept, the longer it takes to retrieve any one of them.

Decay theory

Earliest theorists held forgetting occurs by decay — "forgetting due to a gradual loss of the substrate of memory." Hardt et al. (2013) argued we form numerous trivial memories daily, requiring a decay process to remove them, especially active in the hippocampus. Evidence exists for both interference and decay (at least in STM), but the evidence for decay is "not airtight."

Cue-dependent forgetting / retrieval failure

Tulving (1974) distinguished two reasons for forgetting:

  • Trace-dependent forgetting — the information is no longer present in memory.
  • Cue-dependent forgetting — the information is present but not accessible (a retrieval failure).

Tulving & Psotka (1971) compared cue-dependent forgetting with interference using three recall conditions: original learning, total free recall, and total free cued recall. Retroactive interference appeared in total free recall (interference theory would say earlier lists were "unlearned"), but there was essentially no interference when category names were available (total free cued recall) — so the forgetting was basically cue-dependent retrieval failure. The encoding specificity principle states: "the probability of successful retrieval of the target item is a monotonically increasing function of informational overlap between the information present at retrieval and the information stored in memory." Prediction: when retrieval context differs from the encoded context, memory performance is worse. Kenealy (1997) manipulated context with happy/sad music at learning and retrieval (route-learning to >80% criterion), tested next day with free and cued recall.

Consolidation theory

Consolidation fixes information in LTM over hours (sometimes years), in two phases: the first phase (hours) focused on the hippocampus; the second phase (days to years) involving interactions among the hippocampal region, adjacent entorhinal cortex and the neocortex. Memory traces are most vulnerable shortly after learning, so people should be more susceptible to retroactive interference early in the retention interval. Evidence: retrograde amnesia patients show impaired memory for events before onset; because the hippocampus is central to the first phase, the most recently formed memories should be most impaired. Haist, Gore & Mao (2001): selective hippocampal activation for famous faces appeared only for those famous in the 1990s, while greater entorhinal-cortex activation (connected to widespread neocortex) appeared for faces famous in the 1980s than the 1990s.

Repression — Freud

Freud emphasised emotional factors in forgetting: very threatening/anxiety-provoking material is often unable to reach conscious awareness — repression. "The essence of repression lies simply in the function of rejecting and keeping something out of consciousness." Material can be repressed even when available to consciousness if it produces no emotional reaction. Evidence comes from normal "repressors": Weinberger, Schwartz & Davidson (1979) classified people as truly low-anxious, high-anxious, defensive-anxious, or repressors. Myers & Brewin (1994) found repressors were much slower to recall negative childhood memories and reported the most indifference/hostility from their fathers.

Motivated / directed forgetting

Directed forgetting = impaired LTM triggered by instructions to forget. Studied with the item method: each word is followed by a remember/forget instruction; recall/recognition is worse for to-be-forgotten words. Causes: the forget instruction probably causes selective rehearsal of remember items, plus inhibitory processes (successful forgetting is associated with activation in the right frontal cortex, linked to inhibition).

Motivated forgetting (inhibition) — Anderson & Green (2001) developed the think/no-think paradigm: participants learn cue–target pairs, then for each cue are told to think the target (respond) or prevent it coming to mind (suppress); some cues are not shown (baseline). Anderson & Huddleston (2012) meta-analysed 47 experiments: recall is lower in the suppress than the respond condition, and lower in suppress than baseline. Strategies of inhibition: thought substitution (associating a different word with the cue) and direct suppression (focusing on the cue while blocking the target). People with the greatest activation in bilateral dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (executive-control areas) were most successful at memory inhibition.

High-yield

Five families of forgetting: decay (trace fades), interference (proactive vs retroactive), cue-dependent retrieval failure (Tulving; encoding specificity), repression (Freud; unconscious, emotional), and motivated/directed forgetting (deliberate inhibition; think/no-think). Tulving & Psotka is the classic study showing cue-dependent forgetting can masquerade as interference.

7. Perception: approaches, illusions & pattern recognition

Frameworks & approaches

James Gibson (1966, 1979) framed perception via: distal (external) object → informational medium → proximal stimulation → perceptual object.

Physiological approach

The first step in perception is the stimulation of receptors by environmental stimuli; light reflected from an object stimulates visual receptors, triggering electrical signals toward the brain. Cortical neurons that respond best to simple shapes (lines/bars of specific orientation) are feature detectors; perceiving a whole object depends on activity beyond the visual cortex, but feature-detector response is the first step.

  • Sensory adaptation — receptor cells adapt to constant stimulation by ceasing to fire until stimulation changes. Because of retinal adaptation, the eyes make tiny rapid movements (saccades) to keep changing the image's location; constant stimulation makes a stabilized image appear to disappear.
  • Perceptual constancies — recognizing that physical objects are consistent even though our sensory input changes; the system adjusts perception of the proximal stimulus to perceive the constant distal object.
    • Size constancy — an object keeps the same perceived size despite changes in the proximal image size. The retinal image size depends on distance; two factors account for constancy: the size–distance invariance principle (we consider both the retinal image and apparent distance) and relative size (comparison to objects of known size).
    • Shape constancy — perceived shape does not change as the retinal image changes (e.g., through rotation), even though parts seem to change differentially in distance; localized to the extra-striate cortex (around primary visual cortex).
    • Brightness/colour constancy — objects and surroundings are usually lit by the same source, so lighting changes affect both simultaneously and the neural message stays unchanged. Constancy breaks down when lighting changes are unequal for object and surround.

Information-processing approach

Dominant in the 1960s–70s, it draws an analogy between human cognition and computerized processing: information is received, stored, recoded, transformed, retrieved and transmitted in stages, stored in specific places. People are "general-purpose symbol manipulators" achieving feats with a few mental operations; coding/storage greatly affects later use. Detection/recognition occur early; recoding/retrieval concern storage; reasoning/concept formation put information together in new ways. The tradition is rooted in structuralism.

Ecological perspective & Gibson's direct perception

The central tenet: cognition does not occur in isolation from cultural context; all cognition is shaped by culture and context (Lave, Murtaugh & de la Rocha 1984 found calculation methods varied with context). Gibson's direct perception is an ecological theory: in the real world, sufficient contextual information over time exists to make perceptual judgments without higher-level intelligent processes. We observe depth cues such as texture gradients to perceive proximity/distance directly.

  • Optic array — the structural pattern of light reaching the eye; it contains all the visual information from the environment.
  • Invariant information — the optic array provides unambiguous information via texture gradients, optic-flow patterns (changes in light pattern when the observer or environment moves) and affordances.
  • Affordances — "opportunities for action that exist in the environment and do not depend on the animal's mind … they do not cause behaviour but simply make it possible." Gibson (1979) claimed potential uses of objects are directly perceivable.

Gestalt organization

Max Wertheimer developed an influential approach to form perception. Gestalt means "form"/"whole form"; the aim is to explain how the visual system combines elements in the visual field — perceiving is more than the summation of sensations. Two aspects of organizing elemental sensations: the figure–ground relationship and the grouping of elements (Gestalt laws).

  • Figure–ground — the figure is the dominant object; the contour belongs to the figure; the figure has shape and meaning. The ground is the background, relatively disorganized. Factors favouring figure perception: orientation (vertical/horizontal > diagonal), relative size (smaller/thinner > larger), symmetry, and inclusion/surroundedness (an image inside another).
  • Gestalt grouping laws — proximity, similarity, good continuation, closure, Prägnanz (good form/simplicity), and common fate (common motion). Modern Gestalt principles add connectedness and common region.

Illusions

Illusion = an instance in which perception yields a false interpretation of physical reality. Types: those due to physical processes (e.g., mirages) and those due to cognitive processes (most fall into illusions of size and illusions of shape/area).

  • Illusions of size — horizontal–vertical illusion, Ponzo illusion, Müller-Lyer illusion, and the moon illusion (the moon looks ~30% bigger at the horizon than at its zenith; at the horizon we see it is farther away than trees/houses, cues lacking overhead).
  • Illusions of shape/area — e.g., the Poggendorff illusion.
  • Non-visual illusions — illusion of touch/temperature: a hand from hot water feels lukewarm water as cool, while a hand from cold water feels the same lukewarm water as hot.
  • Causes of size illusions — the theory of misapplied constancy: cues suggest some parts are farther away, and our strong size-constancy tendency then distorts perceived line length. Learning: in the Müller-Lyer, experience tells us the corner with open arrowheads is larger; many illusions decline (but do not vanish) with extended exposure.

Pattern recognition

Two broad processing directions:

  • Bottom-up (data-driven) — start with small bits of environmental information and combine them into a percept (edges → shapes → "a door and hallway"); relatively uninfluenced by expectation or prior learning.
  • Top-down (theory-driven / conceptually driven) — expectations, theories or concepts guide selection and combination of information.

Bottom-up models

  • Template matching — percepts are compared to stored templates (like stencils) requiring a near-perfect 100% match. Limitations: would need an impossibly large number of templates; must explain how templates are created/tracked; and people recognize many varied patterns as the "same thing" despite large differences.
  • Featural analysis — break stimuli into components (features) and use recognition of parts to infer the whole. Neurophysiological evidence: frog retina cells fired to specific stimuli (edge detectors, "bug detectors"); Hubel and Wiesel (1962, 1968) found visual-cortex cells in cats/monkeys responding selectively to moving edges/contours of a particular orientation (separate "horizontal-line" and "vertical-line" detectors), supporting the featural-analysis model.
  • Recognition-by-components (Biederman 1987) — people segment objects into simple geometric components called geons ("geometrical ions"); a total of 36 primitives can construct mental representations of a large set of common objects. We attend not just to which geons are present but to their arrangement (the same two geons combined differently yield very different objects). Neisser (1963) visual-search studies provide evidence for feature processing.
  • Pandemonium model (Selfridge 1959) — letter perception via featural analysis using "demons" that "scream." Image demons convert the proximal stimulus into representations; feature demons each look for a particular feature (curve, vertical line) and scream louder/softer by confidence; letter demons listen only to the feature demons (not the stimulus) and scream when convinced their letter is present; a single decision demon listens to all the screaming and decides which letter is presented.

Prototype models

Like templates, prototype models match input to a stored representation — but the stored representation is a prototype, an idealized representation of a class of objects/events, requiring only an approximate match (allowing discrepancies). Unlike template and featural models, they do not require any one specific feature; the more features an object shares with a prototype, the higher the probability of a match. Prototype models also account for the relationships among features, not just the features themselves.

Top-down processes

All bottom-up models struggle to explain how viewers "make meaning." Two of the biggest problems they address with top-down processing: context effects (context sets up expectations about which objects will occur) and expectation effects (objects like food/utensils are recognized faster in a coherent kitchen scene than in the same scene jumbled). Top-down processes must interact with bottom-up ones — otherwise you could never perceive anything unexpected.

8. Distance & depth perception

Two pathways handle perception of location and form in the occipital lobe: the dorsal stream ("where"/location) and the ventral stream ("what"/form). Perception of location involves 2-dimensional location and distance/depth perception.

Classifying depth cues

Depth cues can be categorized along several dichotomies: absolute vs relative distance; visual vs nonvisual; pictorial vs kinematic; and binocular vs monocular.

Binocular cues (two eyes)

  • Convergence — for a far object the angle between the focal point and the eye is small; for a close object the convergence angle is larger. Based on kinesthetic information from receptors in the extraocular muscles; most effective for near objects (< ~6 m).
  • Retinal disparity — because the eyes are separated, each eye gets a slightly different view, so two images are formed of a given object. Perceiving depth from binocular cues is also called stereoscopy (or binocular parallax); the device simulating depth with two different images (one per eye) is a stereoscope. Related concepts: the horopter, and crossed vs uncrossed disparity.

Monocular cues (one eye)

  • Accommodation — changes in the shape of the optic lens to focus near/far objects; cues come from the ciliary muscles.
  • Linear perspective — distant parallel objects seem to converge.
  • Familiar size — known sizes of familiar objects are compared to judge relative distance.
  • Texture gradient — details of far things are less distinct, providing distance information.
  • Occlusion / interposition — objects that block others are perceived as nearer.
  • Relative height — farther objects are closer to the horizon.
  • Relative brightness / aerial perspective — distant objects/scenery appear duller and foggier.
  • Motion parallax — when the observer moves, nearer objects appear to move faster than distant ones.
Trap

Convergence and accommodation are both oculomotor/muscular cues but split by eye count: convergence = binocular (extraocular muscles, both eyes turning in), accommodation = monocular (ciliary muscle, lens shape). Retinal disparity is the other binocular cue; everything else listed (linear perspective, texture gradient, occlusion, relative height, aerial perspective, motion parallax, familiar size) is monocular.

9. Form perception

Edges & contours

Form perception begins with detecting edges and contours.

  • Subjective contours — it is possible to perceive contours without any physical edges (illusory/emerging contours). Their detection depends mostly on the presence of an edge caused by heterogeneity in the stimulation, and requires that variations of this stimulation occur on the retina — made possible by eye microsaccades.

Lateral inhibition

Lateral inhibition is a basic visual mechanism for increasing brightness variations that generate a border: when light reaches the retina at a point, what is located just next to it undergoes inhibition. The mechanism by which the activity of certain nerve cells affects that of its neighbours is lateral inhibition, first reported by Keffer Hartline and Floyd H. Ratliff.

  • Lateral inhibition in crabs — crabs have small elementary eyes called ommatidia (each comparable to a ganglion cell of the human visual system). If light is maintained on cell A and another light excites neighbour B, the electrical activity earlier seen on fibre A is decreased. The strength of inhibition depends on the proximity between the nerve cells and the strength of stimulation on the inhibitory cell.
  • Mach bands — revealed by Ernst Mach and explained by lateral inhibition. Near the midpoint where luminance is uniformly black, points get similar inhibition from left and right neighbours, so brightness is the same; at a point X where luminance change begins, inhibition from left and right cells is unequal, producing the illusory bright/dark bands at edges.

Factors influencing contour perception

  • Contours are perceived more easily when visual acuity is greater — greater at the fovea.
  • Requires a minimum of stimulation intensity and a minimum of exposure time.
  • The interaction between intensity (I) and exposure time (T) yields a constant visual effect (C) — a reciprocity between intensity and duration.

10. Signal detection theory

Thresholds in psychophysics

  • Absolute threshold — the smallest magnitude of a stimulus that can be reliably discriminated from no stimulus at all 50% of the time.
  • Difference threshold — the amount of change in a physical stimulus necessary to produce a just noticeable difference (jnd) in sensation.

The theory

Signal detection theory holds that human decisions about the presence/absence of a stimulus are influenced not only by signal strength but also by the nature of the task and the subject's knowledge of results. The probability of detecting a signal can be manipulated by, for example, providing a payoff for a correct identification (a "hit"). SDT postulates links between the stimulus energy of the external world, its detection, and the internal representation that results from both the stimulus and previous knowledge. Beyond stimulus magnitude, two further factors affect the decision: observer expectancy that the signal will/will not appear, and whether reward or punishment can be expected from the decision. The other factor is signal strength — a powerful/unambiguous signal is more readily identified than a weak/ambiguous one.

Signal, noise & the decision matrix

Two fundamental concepts: signal (S) — the stimulus one tries to detect, with precise and stable characteristics — and noise (N) — a random variable that constantly changes. In a typical SDT task the observer decides: was it noise only (N) or noise plus signal (S + N)? The observer adopts a decision criterion, often indexed by beta (β). The criterion generates four outcomes:

Observer says "yes"Observer says "no"
Signal presentHit (correct identification)Miss (failed to detect a present signal)
Signal absentFalse alarm (reported a signal that was not there)Correct rejection (correctly reported only noise)

Observers who wait for high certainty before reporting a signal are conservative (strict criterion, fewer false alarms but more misses); those who report readily are lax/liberal (more hits but more false alarms).

d′ (sensitivity) vs criterion

  • d′ (d-prime) — the difference between the means of the N and S+N distributions, divided by the standard deviation of the noise distribution. It is a pure index of detectability, not affected by the observer's criterion: d′ = Z(Hit) − Z(False alarm).
  • β / criterion — captures the observer's response bias, independent of sensitivity.
High-yield

SDT's great contribution is separating sensitivity from bias. d′ = how well you can tell signal from noise (criterion-free). β / criterion = how willing you are to say "yes" (bias). Manipulating payoffs shifts the criterion (β) without changing d′. Memorize the 2×2: hit, miss, false alarm, correct rejection.

Subliminal perception & priming

  • Subliminal perception — perceptual processing below conscious awareness that can nevertheless influence behaviour (the presumed ability to perceive a stimulus below the threshold for conscious experience). It captured public attention in the 1950s when a marketing executive claimed to have embedded messages like "Eat popcorn" and "Drink Coke" into a film. Compared with supraliminal messages, the effects of subliminal messages were truly tiny.
  • Two thresholds of awareness — the subjective threshold (defined by failure to report conscious awareness) and the objective threshold (defined by inability to make accurate forced-choice decisions, e.g., guess above chance whether it is a word).
  • Semantic priming — the processing of one stimulus enhances the speed of processing a related stimulus (seeing "cat" speeds identifying "dog" but not the unrelated "doll"). Primes presented below an objective threshold failed to influence performance, so the reality of subliminal perception depends on how the threshold of awareness is defined. Even where present, the long-term effects of subliminal stimuli are limited and depend on top-down processes such as motivation and expectation.
