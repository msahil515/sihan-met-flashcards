1. Intelligence: definitions & theories

The one problem behind the whole field. Everyone agrees intelligence exists; nobody agrees what it is. So the topic is really two arguments: (a) how to define intelligence, and (b) how it is structured: one general ability, a handful of group factors, or many independent ones. The factor theories below are best learned as a single conversation in which each thinker reacts to the last.

Galton and sensory keenness (the false start)

The first theories came from the "Brass Instruments" era at the turn of the 20th century. Galton and Cattell promoted a sensory keenness theory: smarter people have sharper senses and faster reactions. This proved a psychometric dead end. (Curiously, Jensen (1980) later reported reaction-time and movement-time indices correlating as high as .50 with traditional IQ tests, partly rehabilitating the idea of mental speed.)

Spearman and the g factor

Charles Spearman proposed that intelligence consists of two kinds of factors: a single general factor g plus numerous specific factors (s1, s2, s3…). To test this he helped invent factor analysis: a statistical technique to discern how many underlying factors must exist to account for the observed correlations among many tests. Performance on any homogeneous test is determined mainly by two influences: g (pervasive) and s (specific to that test). Because s differs for every test and is usually weaker than g, Spearman cared little about it.

Spearman (1923) held that individual differences in g show up in three principles of cognition:

  • Apprehension of experience
  • Eduction of relations (given two ideas, find the relation between them)
  • Eduction of correlates (given an idea and a relation, find the matching idea)

Eduction simply means the process of figuring things out. The hardest problem for his two-factor theory was the stubborn existence of group factors (clusters bigger than s but smaller than g), which the next thinkers seized on.

Definition

g = a single, pervasive general intelligence loading on all cognitive tasks. Factor analysis = the statistical tool Spearman built to find the latent factors behind a correlation matrix.

Thurstone and the Primary Mental Abilities (PMAs)

L. L. Thurstone (1931) refined factor analysis to search correlation matrices for group factors and concluded intelligence is not one thing but about a dozen relatively independent Primary Mental Abilities:

  • Verbal Comprehension (best measure: vocabulary; also reading comprehension, verbal analogies)
  • Word Fluency (anagrams; quickly naming words in a category)
  • Number (speed/accuracy of simple arithmetic)
  • Space (visualizing a 3-D object rotated or disassembled)
  • Associative Memory (rote paired-associate learning)
  • Perceptual Speed (clerical checking of visual detail)
  • Inductive Reasoning (finding a rule, e.g. number-series completion)

P. E. Vernon (1950) reconciled Spearman and Thurstone with a hierarchical group-factor theory: g sits at the top; below it two major group factors, verbal-educational (v:ed) and practical-mechanical-spatial-physical (k:m); below those, minor group factors resembling Thurstone's PMAs; specific factors at the bottom.

Trap

Spearman = one general factor (g + specifics). Thurstone = many coequal group factors (the PMAs), no dominant g. Vernon = a hierarchy that contains both (g on top, group factors beneath).

Cattell–Horn–Carroll: fluid & crystallised, then CHC

Raymond Cattell (1941, 1971) split g into two broad abilities:

  • Fluid intelligence (Gf): on-the-spot reasoning independent of acquired knowledge: drawing inferences, forming concepts, generating and testing hypotheses, understanding implications, inductive and deductive reasoning. Peaks young, declines with age.
  • Crystallised intelligence (Gc): the breadth and depth of acquired cultural knowledge: language, facts, concepts, dependent on exposure, formal and informal education. Holds up or grows across life.

John Horn (1968–1994) extended the theory by adding several broad factors: visual processing (Gv), auditory processing (Ga), quantitative processing (Gq), processing speed (Gs), reading/writing (Grw), short-term memory (Gsm), and long-term storage and retrieval (Glr). John Carroll (1993) then synthesised hundreds of datasets into the three-stratum theory (specific abilities → broad abilities → g). The merger of Cattell-Horn and Carroll is the CHC model, today's dominant framework underlying most modern IQ batteries.

High-yield

Gf = novel reasoning, declines with age; Gc = stored knowledge, stable/rising. CHC = Cattell-Horn (Gf/Gc + broad factors) fused with Carroll's three-stratum hierarchy: the modern standard.

Guilford's Structure-of-Intellect (SOI) model

J. P. Guilford (1967, 1985) continued Thurstone's search and concluded the number of mental abilities far exceeds seven. His Structure-of-Intellect (SOI) model crosses three dimensions: operations × contents × products. In the 1985 version: 5 operations × 5 contents × 6 products = 150 factors, each unique combination being a distinct ability.

Guilford's lasting contribution was flagging divergent production: generating numerous appropriate responses to one stimulus is itself an essential ingredient of intelligent (and creative) behaviour, a bridge straight into the creativity section.

Gardner's Multiple Intelligences (MI)

Howard Gardner (1983, 1993) argued intelligence is not unitary but a set of relatively autonomous intelligences, based loosely on brain–behaviour study. An ability earns "intelligence" status if it meets criteria such as: potential isolation by brain damage; existence of exceptional individuals (savants); identifiable core operations; distinctive developmental history (critical periods, milestones); evolutionary plausibility; support from experimental psychology; support from psychometric findings; and susceptibility to symbol encoding. The seven originally confirmed intelligences:

  • Linguistic
  • Logical-mathematical
  • Spatial
  • Musical
  • Bodily-kinesthetic (athletes, dancers, mime artists, typists, "primitive" hunters)
  • Interpersonal (understanding others)
  • Intrapersonal (access to one's own feeling life)

(Gardner later proposed an eighth, naturalistic, intelligence.) He concedes the exact nature, extent and number of the intelligences are not yet definitively established.

Sternberg's Triarchic Theory

Robert Sternberg emphasised successful intelligence: "the ability to adapt to, shape, and select environments to accomplish one's goals and those of one's society and culture." It has three aspects:

  • Componential / analytical: the internal mental mechanisms (the metacomponents, performance components, and knowledge-acquisition components) responsible for intelligent behaviour. This is what classic IQ tests measure.
  • Experiential / creative: dealing effectively with novel tasks and automatizing familiar ones.
  • Contextual / practical: "mental activity involved in purposive adaptation to, shaping of, and selection of real-world environments" (street smarts).
Mnemonic

Sternberg's three = A-C-P: Analytical (componential, school smarts), Creative (experiential, novelty), Practical (contextual, street smarts).

Jensen's Level I / Level II

Arthur Jensen accepted Spearman's g but split intelligence into two levels: Level I = memory and simple associative learning; Level II = abstract reasoning and conceptual thought. (His controversial racial-difference claims about Level II are noted in the source but are scientifically and ethically contested.)

The PASS theory (Naglieri & Das, after Luria)

PASS theory (Naglieri & Das, 1990) is a modern, neuropsychological, information-processing model extending Aleksandr Luria's work. Luria, from case studies of brain-injured soldiers, proposed three functional units of the brain, with processing flowing from lower to higher units:

  • First unit (subcortical: brain stem, midbrain, thalamus): attention/arousal, selective attention, resistance to distraction.
  • Second unit (posterior cortex: parietal, temporal, occipital): receives, codes, and stores information via simultaneous and successive processing.
  • Third unit (frontal lobes): planning and programming of behaviour; motor output.

From this, Das and Naglieri built the four PASS processes:

  • Planning: selection, use, and monitoring of effective solutions; impulse control; the last stage of processing; frontal lobes.
  • Attention: selectively attending to some stimuli while ignoring others; vigilance over time. Difficulty here underlies ADHD.
  • Simultaneous processing: integrating stimuli into spatial, whole groups (e.g. drawing a cube).
  • Successive processing: integrating stimuli into a temporally ordered series (e.g. repeating digits, imitating hand-movement sequences fist-palm-fist).
Mnemonic

Luria's three units = arousal → coding → planning (back-to-front, subcortex → posterior cortex → frontal). PASS = Planning, Attention, Simultaneous, Successive.

Goleman's Emotional Intelligence

Daniel Goleman (1995) popularised emotional intelligence: a form of intelligence about the emotional side of life, the ability to recognise and manage one's own and others' emotions, motivate oneself, restrain impulses, and handle relationships. Five major parts: (1) knowing our own emotions, (2) managing emotions, (3) motivating ourselves, (4) recognizing emotions of others (empathy), (5) handling relationships.

Piaget's cognitive-development frame

For Piaget, intelligence develops through interaction with the environment, not by maturation or learning alone. New experience demands cognitive reorganisation in a mental structure called a schema (infants begin with simple schemata such as sucking and grasping). Learning runs on two operations: assimilation (fitting new information into existing structures) and accommodation (changing structures to fit new information). He posited four invariant stages of development.

2. Creativity

The defining idea. Creativity is "a cognitive activity that results in a new or novel way of viewing a problem or situation": the process of producing something that is both original and worthwhile (novelty alone is not enough; it must also be appropriate/useful).

The nature of creativity

  • Creativity uses both convergent and divergent thinking (it is not purely divergent).
  • It engages many regions of both hemispheres, left and right (not a "right-brain" myth).
  • It can occur under focused (conscious) attention as well as defocused attention (altered states).

Wallas's four stages of the creative process

Graham Wallas (1926) described creativity as four sequential stages:

  • Preparation: formulating the problem and making initial attempts to solve it.
  • Incubation: leaving the problem and turning to other things.
  • Illumination: achieving insight (the "aha"); the bits and pieces fall into place and irrelevant thoughts are discarded.
  • Verification: testing and carrying out the solution. This "mopping-up" stage may be brief (rechecking a calculation) or take a lifetime of testing and retesting.

Three hypotheses about incubation: it lets us recover from problem-solving fatigue; interruption lets us forget inappropriate approaches; and we may work on the problem unconsciously. Because dormant periods occupy most of life, creative acts simply tend to follow them. Note: incubation does not always lead to illumination, but when it does the sensation is unmistakable.

Mnemonic

Wallas: P-I-I-V = Preparation, Incubation, Illumination, Verification.

Levels of creativity: the 4-C model

The original split was Big-C (eminent, history-changing) vs little-c (everyday) creativity. Beghetto & Kaufman (2007) added mini-c ("the novel and personally meaningful interpretation of experiences, actions, and events"); Kaufman & Beghetto (2009) added Pro-C, giving the Four-C model:

Mini-cNovel, personally meaningful interpretationA musical note or combination of notes

Big-CEminent creativityA national anthem

Rhodes's 4 P's of creativity

Rhodes (1961/1987) said creativity can be studied along four dimensions, the 4 P's:

  • Person: the traits of creative people: broad interests, appreciation of complexity, tolerance of ambiguity, self-confidence, independence, sensible risk-taking; Runco (2004) adds flexibility and reactivity.
  • Process: the special abilities, e.g. restructuring problem information and connecting remote possibilities, plus ordinary cognition like attention and memory. Sternberg & Davidson (1983; Davidson 1995) cite three insight processes: selective encoding (relevant vs irrelevant information), selective combination (combining information in new, productive ways), and selective comparison (relating new information to old in novel ways).
  • Press: the contextual factors and external pressures creativity is subject to.
  • Product: the outcome (painting, poem, design, technology).

Criteria for creative products

Jackson & Messick (1968) applied four criteria: Novelty (new, or a new application of the familiar); Appropriateness (fits the context, not merely novel); Transcendence of constraints (goes beyond the traditional); and Coalescence of meaning (full significance may emerge only over time).

Guilford and Torrance: divergent thinking

Guilford (1950) was among the first to define creativity in terms of the person ("creativity refers to the abilities most characteristic of creative people") and the product. His factor analyses produced divergent thinking: the production of many varied responses, virtually the opposite of convergent thinking (producing the single correct answer fixed by facts and reason). His 1959/1985 SOI model parcelled intelligence into 150 factors along operations, contents, and products.

E. P. Torrance built the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT) loosely on Guilford's model. He was more concerned with the interest level of his measures than with their factorial purity; the tests aim to assess a global cognitive construct of creativity (a "style of thinking" believed essential to creative achievement).

Trap

Divergent = many answers to one stimulus (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration). Convergent = one correct answer. Creativity needs both: diverge to generate, converge to select.

Sternberg's Investment & Confluence theory

Sternberg (2006) proposed that creativity requires a confluence of six interrelated resources: intellectual abilities, knowledge, styles of thinking, personality, motivation, and environment. The "investment" metaphor: creative people "buy low and sell high" in the world of ideas.

Creativity and intelligence: the threshold hypothesis

A high IQ is insufficient for creativity. Terman's (1925) famous longitudinal study of 1,528 gifted children (mean IQ 151) produced great occupational success but no noteworthy creativity. Torrance (1977) reported the median IQ–TTCT correlation across 178 coefficients was only about .20. Guilford (1967) proposed a threshold hypothesis: a minimal IQ (often arbitrarily set at 120) is necessary but not sufficient for creativity, with achievement essentially impossible below that level. He predicted IQ-vs-creativity scatterplots should be triangular (the "triangularity hypothesis") with no points in the low-IQ/high-creativity quadrant.

High-yield

Threshold hypothesis: above ~IQ 120 the IQ–creativity correlation drops near zero (so among smart people IQ barely predicts creativity); below threshold, high creativity is rare. Terman's geniuses + Torrance's r ≈ .20 are the supporting evidence.

3. Nurturing creativity at the workplace

Applied creativity (Merlyn Fernandes). Creativity at work is the ability to combine and connect ideas, generate original possibilities, and produce something novel. Its benefits: encourages problem solving, fosters innovation, increases staff morale and employee engagement, and improves team collaboration.

What kills creativity

  • External factors: bureaucracy; shooting down ideas ("we've tried that before", "this is not going to work out"); rigid working space; toxic leadership (dictatorship, no autonomy, micromanagement, overwork).
  • Internal factors: a fixed mindset, the belief that one is "not creative", risk-aversion, lacking knowledge, fear of challenging the status quo, and lack of motivation.

How to nurture it

  • Flatter hierarchies and more autonomy.
  • Create fun, varied spaces and give workers the right training and tools (diverse office spaces, work-life balance, "headspace to think through problems").
  • Encourage risk-taking and experimentation (e.g. the "20 Circles" divergent-thinking exercise). As Brené Brown puts it, when self-worth is not on the line people risk sharing their gifts.
  • Use Transactional Analysis (ego states) and build a psychologically safe workplace: people give and receive feedback without fear, agree and disagree, challenge assumptions, admit errors, and raise concerns.
  • Allow unfocussed time and cultivate a beginner's mindset.

A design-thinking workflow

The notes use the design-thinking arc: Empathize → Ideate (and beyond):

  • Brainstorming rules: defer judgment; encourage wild ideas; build on others' ideas; go for quantity; diverge and converge; brainstorm often.
  • Mash-up: connect two unrelated ideas into one (the IDEO "node" school desk).
  • Reframing the problem: e.g. "the elevator is too slow" (solution: new lift/upgrade motor) reframed as "the wait is annoying" (solution: play music, put up TV screens). Reframing as "How might we…?" questions (e.g. "How might we make bathrooms safe for the elderly?") opens new solution spaces.

4. Thinking

Thinking is the manipulation of mental representations of information. The representations may be words, visual images, sounds, or abstract symbols; thinking transforms them into new forms to reach goals, solve problems, reason, and decide.

Mental images

Thought can use mental images: representations in the mind that resemble the object or event represented. Classic findings (Kosslyn; Shepard & Metzler) show mental images behave like physical ones: the eye takes longer to scan a large mental image just as it does a large real one, and we can mentally rotate images, with larger rotations taking proportionally longer (mental-rotation experiments). This implies mental images preserve spatial, analog properties of the real thing.

Concepts

A concept is a mental category that groups objects, events, or people sharing common properties. Concepts let us organise complex phenomena into simpler, usable categories so we don't treat every new instance as wholly unique. Some concepts can be defined by a clear set of properties (e.g. geometric shapes such as a square: a figure with four equal sides and four right angles summing to 360°), but most everyday concepts are fuzzier and are better captured by prototypes.

Prototypes

A prototype is the most typical, representative example of a concept. Because real categories vary so much (think of the variation among "dogs", from a chihuahua to a Great Dane: both have fur, four legs, a tail, but the similarities end there), we judge category membership by closeness to the prototype rather than by a rigid definition.

Propositions

A proposition is a meaningful unit of knowledge that expresses a single idea or relationship and that can be judged true or false. Much of our knowledge is stored as networks of propositions linking concepts. Propositional thought is typically expressed in language.

Language and thinking

The relationship between language and thought is a central question. The linguistic-relativity (Whorfian) hypothesis holds that the language we speak shapes how we perceive and think about the world (e.g. the number of colour terms a language has may influence how speakers categorise colour). The stronger claim, that language wholly determines thought, is largely rejected; the milder claim, that language can influence thought, retains support. Either way, language is the chief vehicle through which propositional thinking is encoded and communicated.

5. Concepts & categorization

The fundamental unit of symbolic knowledge is the concept: an idea about something that provides a means of understanding the world. We use stored concepts to think, solve problems, and make decisions. Categorization is the process by which concepts are organised: a category is itself a concept that points out equivalences among other concepts based on shared features or similarity to a prototype. A category is a class of similar things that share either an essential core or some similarity in perceptual, biological, or functional properties.

The classical (feature-based) view

The classical view holds that all instances of a concept share fundamental defining features, and those features are individually necessary and collectively sufficient for membership. Its assumptions: concepts are lists of features; membership is clear-cut (in or out); and all members are created equal. This view fits scientific taxonomies and artificial concepts: it lets us correctly categorise atypical objects (bats as mammals, tomatoes as fruits, coconuts as nuts).

Definition

Necessary and sufficient features: a feature is necessary if every member must have it; a set is sufficient if having all of them guarantees membership. The classical view says category features are both.

Why the classical view fails: graded structure

Semantic memory generally does not use necessary-and-sufficient rules. Real categories are loose and fuzzy, and members vary in how well they fit (graded membership). Related principles:

  • Central tendency: there is a mental core/centre where the best members live.
  • Typicality effect: items differ in how typical they are judged to be (a robin is a "better" bird than a penguin). Novick (2003) found airplanes' rated typicality as vehicles spiked for about a month after the 9/11 attacks, then returned to baseline, showing typicality is context-sensitive.
  • Family resemblance (Wittgenstein): members share some features without any one feature being shared by all.
  • Correlated attributes: real-world features co-occur (things with wings often also have beaks).
Trap

Artificial vs natural concepts. Artificial concepts can be clearly defined by rules (a tomato is a fruit by botanists' criteria). Natural concepts have no fixed defining features, are "fuzzy around the edges", yet more accurately reflect the messy natural world.

Probabilistic theories: prototype vs exemplar

Probabilistic theories assume categories form by accumulating probabilities and likelihoods across experience:

  • Prototype theory: a category is summarised by a prototype, an average of all your experiences with members: a central, idealised representation that may match no individual member. A typical example is judged quickly because it is highly similar to the prototype.
  • Exemplar theory: we store many specific exemplars (individual encountered instances). A typical example is judged quickly because it resembles many stored exemplars.

The two often make the same predictions and can be hard to distinguish. Chin-Parker & Ross (2004) oriented learners to either "diagnostic" features (must-have, classical-flavoured) or "prototypical" features (typically-present); people later showed sensitivity only to the feature type they had learned with, suggesting both kinds of representation are used depending on the task.

High-yield

Prototype = one averaged ideal you compare against. Exemplar = many remembered specific instances you compare against. Both are probabilistic and both explain typicality effects.

Problems with probabilistic theories

  • Circularity problem: how does memory know which experiences to average into a category without already knowing what the category is?
  • Ad hoc categories (Barsalou): people generate categories on the fly without prior experience (e.g. "things to grab from a burning house"). These show graded structure, have a prototype, and exhibit typicality effects, yet are conjured by context (gray is more like white in the context of hair colour but more like black in the context of clouds), revealing how flexible semantic memory is.

Explanation-based (theory) view and essentialism

The explanation-based view says semantic categories are essentially theories of the world we create to explain why things are the way they are: structures we impose on the world, which may or may not reflect how the world actually is. (A shoe and a brick can share a category, "things to pound a nail with if you don't have a hammer", while a shoe and a sock fall in different categories.) People use causal relations among members to infer the internal structure of new members.

  • Embodied cognition (Borghi, Glenberg & Kaschak, 2004): perspective shapes concept use. People told "you are driving a car" responded faster to interior parts (speedometer); those told "you are washing a car" responded faster to exterior parts (trunk).
  • Psychological essentialism: people treat category members as if they share an underlying, often invisible essence, and categorise based on beliefs (right or wrong) about that essence, even where no real essence exists (e.g. "TOOL").

6. Reasoning

Reasoning draws conclusions from premises or evidence. It is not encapsulated (enclosed/isolated): it is woven through a wide array of cognitive functions, and even visual perception contains elements of reasoning.

Two forms of reasoning

  • Deductive reasoning: from general premises to a necessarily valid (or invalid) conclusion, assuming the premises are true. Top-down; truth-preserving.
  • Inductive reasoning: testing the truth of general statements from specific observations. Bottom-up; the engine of theory formation; conclusions are probable, not certain.

Syllogisms

A syllogism is a three-statement logical form: two premises taken to be true, then a conclusion based on them. The two premises state a class-inclusion (subset–superset) relation among abstract elements A, B, C; they are the major premise and minor premise; the third statement is the conclusion. Abstract form: All A are B; All B are C; Therefore all A are C.

Crucially, the truth of the premises is separate from the validity of the argument. A syllogism can be valid yet have a false premise (and thus a false conclusion). Applying syllogisms to the real world is a two-step process: (1) determine whether the syllogism is valid; (2) if valid, determine the empirical truth of the premises. The four types of premises are: universal affirmative (All A are B), universal negative (No A are B), particular affirmative (Some A are B), and particular negative (Some A are not B).

Definition

Valid = the conclusion follows logically from the premises (structure). True = the statement matches reality (content). A valid argument can be untrue; a true statement can sit in an invalid argument.

Belief bias

Belief bias is the tendency to accept believable conclusions and reject unbelievable ones, irrespective of logical validity. Evans et al. (1983) showed that accepting invalid conclusions as valid was dramatically more common when the conclusion was believable than when it was unbelievable. Example trap: "All poodles are animals; all animals are wild; therefore all poodles are wild", logically valid in form, but rejected because the premise is false and the conclusion unbelievable.

Euler circles and how to test a syllogism

Euler circles (diagrams showing class relations) help determine whether a syllogism holds. Performance improves when people draw diagrams or generate specific examples, but only if they actively try to find an interpretation that makes the syllogism false. Formally, evaluating a conclusion by the predicate calculus requires three steps: (1) consider all possible interpretations of premises and conclusion; (2) consider all possible combinations of meanings of the major and minor premises; (3) check whether all possible meanings of the conclusion are consistent with all possible combinations of the premises. If a single interpretation of the conclusion fails to follow from a single possible combination of premise meanings, no valid conclusion may be drawn.

Common errors and their causes

Ceraso & Provitera (1971): people correctly identify a valid conclusion only about three-quarters of the time, and often endorse a conclusion as valid when one cannot say for certain. Systematic patterns:

  • When the major premise is a universal affirmative (all) and the minor is a particular affirmative (some), people overwhelmingly accept a "some" conclusion (and likewise when both are "some").
  • When both premises are universal negatives, people often accept a "no" conclusion.

Three reasons for these errors: cognitive constraints (memory limits), illicit conversion (illegitimately reversing the terms of a premise), and meaning & knowledge (belief bias).

Conditional reasoning

A conditional-reasoning problem has two parts: a conditional clause ("if P then Q") and some evidence about P or Q. The "if" clause is the antecedent (P); the "then" clause is the consequent (Q). The task: decide what follows logically. For "If it rains, the streets will be wet", the four cases are P, not-P, Q, not-Q:

ConditionalEvidence givenConclusionValidityName
If P, then QP (affirm antecedent)Therefore QValidModus ponens
If P, then QNot P (deny antecedent)No conclusionInvalid(denying antecedent)
If P, then QQ (affirm consequent)No conclusionInvalid(affirming consequent)
If P, then QNot Q (deny consequent)Therefore not PValidModus tollens

People handle modus ponens easily but find modus tollens (denying the consequent) much harder. Example of the affirming-the-consequent error: "If Susan is angry, then I am upset; I am upset; therefore Susan is angry" (invalid). Errors fall into three broad categories: form errors (illicit conversion / reversing conditions), search errors (confirmation bias: searching only for positive, confirming evidence), and memory-related errors (memory limits; we tend to use mental models rather than formal logic).

High-yield

Only two valid conditional inferences: modus ponens (affirm antecedent → affirm consequent) and modus tollens (deny consequent → deny antecedent). The two invalid forms are affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent.

7. Problem solving

Problem solving is the process used when you want to reach a specified goal but the solution is not immediately obvious, because you are missing information and/or it is unclear how to reach the goal (Bassok & Novick, 2012). Every problem has three components: the initial state (the situation at the start), the goal state (the situation when solved), and the obstacles (restrictions that make it hard to move from initial to goal state).

Understanding the problem (representation)

Constructing a well-organised mental representation of the problem is crucial. Understanding involves: paying attention to the relevant information; using various methods of representing the problem (diagrams, symbols); and using situated and embodied cognition. (Classic catch: the "bus driver" word problem that buries the simple question, "What is the driver's name?", under irrelevant boarding figures, illustrating how attention to the right information governs success.)

Algorithms vs heuristics

  • Algorithm: a method that will always produce a solution, though it can be inefficient (e.g. exhaustive search). Guarantees success but can be slow.
  • Heuristic: a general rule that is usually right. Faster but not guaranteed.

Example: the anagrams LSSTNEUIAMYOUL and IPMHYLOD can be cracked by exhaustive search (guaranteed but slow) or by a heuristic (reject implausible consonant clusters like LT, TN). The heuristic is quick but may misfire: in the second anagram, ignoring the "LY" combination would be a mistake.

Trap

Algorithm = always works, may be slow. Heuristic = usually works, faster, not guaranteed. Exhaustive search is the prototypical algorithm.

Specific heuristics

  • Analogy: use the solution to a similar earlier problem to solve a new one (the Wright brothers modelled airplane wings on bird wings). The big challenge is to find the problem isomorph, the abstract puzzle underneath, a set of problems with the same underlying structure and solution but different surface details. People fail when they fix on surface features rather than structural features; they use analogy correctly more often after solving several structurally similar problems first.
  • Means-ends analysis: identify the "ends" then the "means" to reach them. Two components: break the main problem into subproblems (each with a defined initial and goal state), and work to reduce the distance between initial and goal state for each subproblem. Sometimes solving requires temporarily increasing the distance (moving "away" from the goal), which people are reluctant to do.
  • Hill-climbing: consistently choose the alternative that appears to lead most directly toward the goal. Useful when you can see only the immediate next step, but it can lead you astray, because it ignores indirect routes with greater long-term payoff (e.g. taking a job right after college for immediate salary when a graduate degree would yield more in the long run).

Gestalt insight, functional fixedness, and mental set

The Gestalt tradition emphasised sudden insight: a problem is restructured and the solution appears whole. Two classic blocks (covered in detail in section 8) are functional fixedness (Duncker's candle problem) and mental set (Luchins' water-jar problem), both arising from over-reliance on top-down processing.

8. Factors influencing problem solving

Effective problem solving needs an ideal blend of top-down (knowledge-driven) and bottom-up (data-driven) processing. Experts use top-down processing well, leaning on knowledge, memory, and strategy. But mental set and functional fixedness interfere precisely because they rely too heavily on top-down processing. Three factors dominate: expertise, mental set, and functional fixedness.

Expertise

An expert shows consistently exceptional skill on representative tasks in an area, with superior long-term memory and a detailed conceptual structure. Experts differ from novices on several dimensions:

DimensionExperts vs novices
Knowledge baseBroader, deeper; detailed, specific schemas.
MemoryBetter but very domain-specific: chess experts recall ~50,000 "chunks" (familiar piece arrangements) and far outperform novices on real game positions, but are only slightly better on random arrangements.
StrategiesApproach novel problems systematically (novices are haphazard); use the means-ends heuristic effectively; emphasise structural similarity in analogies (novices are distracted by surface similarity).
Speed & accuracyMuch faster and more accurate; operations become automatic; on tasks like anagrams they may use parallel rather than serial processing.
MetacognitionBetter at judging problem difficulty, allocating time, and recovering from errors, but they underestimate how long novices will need (novices judge their own difficulty more accurately).

Mental set

Mental set (Luchins' water-jar problem): individuals keep using the same solution that worked on previous problems even when an easier method exists; they close the mind prematurely. Top-down processing is over-active. EEG work shows people have greater changes in event-related potentials (ERPs) on trials where they break a set. Mental set is linked to Carol Dweck's (2006) mindset concept: a fixed mindset (intelligence is fixed; effort won't help; give up on improving) hampers flexible problem solving, whereas a growth mindset (abilities can be cultivated; seek challenges) supports it.

Functional fixedness

Functional fixedness (Duncker's candle problem) is the tendency to assign fixed functions to objects, so we fail to see other useful features. Like mental set, it occurs when top-down processing is over-active, but it concerns physical objects rather than problem-solving strategies. Objects normally have fixed functions (a screwdriver tightens screws; a coin buys things), and in daily life, with many tools available, fixedness rarely handicaps us. It bites when we apply a fixed function too rigidly: if you have no screwdriver, a coin can substitute, but functional fixedness keeps you from seeing it.

Trap

Mental set vs functional fixedness. Mental set = stuck on a strategy/method (water-jar). Functional fixedness = stuck on an object's usual function (candle/box of tacks). Both stem from excessive top-down processing.

9. Decision making

Decision making vs problem solving (Gilhooly, 1996): the two overlap (decision making requires some problem solving, and problem solving requires deciding which strategy to use), but differ in emphasis. In problem solving the focus is on selecting actions and generating options; in decision making the options are usually already presented and you choose among them without the load of option-generation.

Classical (normative) decision theory

Early "economic man/woman" models assumed decision makers are: fully informed about all options and outcomes, infinitely sensitive to subtle distinctions, and fully rational. The refined version, subjective expected utility (SEU) theory, predicts people reach well-reasoned decisions by: considering all known alternatives; using the maximum available information; carefully (if subjectively) weighing costs/risks against benefits; calculating the probability of outcomes; and applying sound reasoning to maximise utility (subjective value) given probability.

Definition

Expected utility = the subjective value (utility) of an outcome weighted by its probability. Normative theory says a rational chooser picks the option with the highest expected utility.

Bounded rationality and satisficing

In the 1950s Herbert Simon (1957) challenged unlimited rationality. We are not irrational, but show bounded rationality: rational within limits of information, time, and cognitive capacity. His key strategy is satisficing: rather than computing the option that maximises gains across the whole universe of options, we consider options one by one and pick the first that is "satisfactory", just good enough to meet a minimum level of acceptability.

Elimination by aspects

Amos Tversky (1972) extended bounded rationality with elimination by aspects, used when there are far more alternatives than we can reasonably weigh: focus on one aspect (attribute), set a minimum criterion, and eliminate every option failing it; pick a second aspect, set a criterion, eliminate more; continue this sequential process until one option remains. In practice we often combine elimination-by-aspects or satisficing to narrow the field to a few options, then apply more thorough strategies. (The notes also reference Galotti's general phases of decision making.)

High-yield

SEU (maximise utility × probability) is the normative ideal; satisficing (first "good enough" option) and elimination by aspects (sequential attribute cut-offs) are Simon's and Tversky's descriptive, bounded-rationality alternatives.

10. Decision-making styles

Rowe & Boulgarides (1983) proposed a Cognitive-Contingency model of decision styles, combining a person's cognitive complexity with their value orientation (concern for task vs people). It is distinct from leadership/managerial style and is measured by the Decision Style Inventory (DSI): 20 questions (five per each of four factors), ~10 minutes, self-scoring, with over 90% face validity and over 70% test-retest reliability, taken by 7,000+ people.

The four-force model and the decision process

The four-force model gives the contextual variables (echoing Lewin): the individual's reaction to the work environment, the people in the organisation, the task/function, and the individual's personal needs. The simplified decision process has three phases: (1) perception/filtering of stimuli (the "information style", subject to overload and bias); (2) cognition (problem solving, negotiation, heuristics, analysis, evaluation, risk-taking); and (3) action (where managerial/leadership behaviour is most overt; power, persuasion, motivation).

The two axes and four styles

The DSI matrix crosses cognitive complexity (vertical: low = few/rigid rules, high need for structure; high = integrates diverse cues, high tolerance for ambiguity) with value orientation (horizontal: task/technical focus = "left brain"; people/social focus = "right brain"). This yields four styles:

StyleComplexity × valueCharacteristics
DirectiveLow complexity, task-orientedLow tolerance for ambiguity; focus on technical problems; high need for power; uses limited information and few alternatives; emphasises speed and action; aggressive, authoritarian, short-range, tight controls; very effective at getting results.
AnalyticHigh complexity, task-orientedAbstract thinker; high tolerance for ambiguity; uses considerable information; careful examination of alternatives; optimises solutions; enjoys challenges; innovative; prefers written reports; seeks variety.
ConceptualHigh complexity, people-orientedWants elaborate, broad treatment of problems; considers many alternatives; systems perspective; future-oriented; highly creative; values quality, openness, shared goals; needs praise; independent; prefers loose control.
BehaviouralLow complexity, people-orientedDeep concern for fellow employees; strong need for affiliation; supportive, good listener, receptive, communicates easily; warm; accepts loose control; prefers meetings to reports; uses little data; short-range, people-oriented.

Individuals have one or more dominant styles and one or more back-up styles; few people change their basic style over time, but shifts occur under stress or information overload. The signature finding: under stress all styles tend to shift toward Directive (e.g. high analytics shift to directive). The directive style "explodes" when stressed; the analytic is the "cool cucumber" who turns directive; the conceptual becomes erratic (diverges rather than converges); the behavioural avoids the issue. Style also shifts with age/maturity (older managers more considerate; younger people lower on directive, higher on behavioural). Applications: training, reorganisation/job matching, personnel selection, career planning, and creativity development.

Mnemonic

The four DSI quadrants: D-A-C-B = Directive (task, simple), Analytic (task, complex), Conceptual (people, complex), Behavioural (people, simple). Under stress, everyone drifts toward Directive.

11. Heuristics in decision making

Daniel Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize in economics for this work. With Amos Tversky he proposed that a small number of heuristics guide human judgment under uncertainty. The classics: representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment, plus the framing effect.

The representativeness heuristic

We judge that a sample is likely if it is similar (representative) to the population it came from. Asked which coin-toss sequence is more likely, T H H T H T or H H H T T T, most pick the first because it "looks random", though both are equally probable. The danger: it is so persuasive that people ignore statistics they should use, especially sample size and base rates.

  • Small-sample fallacy (Kahneman & Tversky, 1972): asked whether a small hospital (~15 births/day) or large hospital (~45 births/day) is more likely to report a day with >60% boys, most say "equal", but a small sample more easily yields extreme proportions. We forget that deviations from population proportions are likelier in small samples, which fuels stereotyping from a handful of group members. A remedy: meet many members of the target group.
  • Base-rate fallacy (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973): we ignore how often something occurs in the population. Given a description matching the stereotype of a computer-science student (Tom W), people judge by representativeness and ignore the base rate even when it is provided.
  • Conjunction fallacy (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983): by the conjunction rule the probability of two events together cannot exceed the probability of either alone. Yet people judge "bank teller AND feminist" more probable than "bank teller", because "feminist" is representative of the described person (single, outspoken, philosophy major, social-justice activist). Traced directly to representativeness.
High-yield

Representativeness breeds three errors: the small-sample fallacy (ignoring sample size), the base-rate fallacy (ignoring population frequency), and the conjunction fallacy (rating A&B more likely than A).

The availability heuristic

We estimate frequency or probability by how easily relevant examples come to mind. Easy retrieval feels like high frequency, even when retrieval ease is uncorrelated with true frequency, biasing decisions. Influencing factors:

  • Recency: recent items come to mind faster, so we overrate them. MacLeod & Campbell (1992): people made to recall pleasant past events later judged pleasant future events more likely; the reverse for unpleasant events.
  • Familiarity: familiar items inflate frequency estimates. Brown & Siegler (1992): during a period when El Salvador was constantly in the news and Indonesia rarely, students estimated similar populations for the two countries, although Indonesia's population was ~35 times larger.
  • Illusory correlation: believing two variables are related (e.g. a group has certain traits) when accurate tabulation shows no significant relationship, a stereotyping mechanism driven by what's available in memory.
  • A helpful counterpart is the recognition heuristic, which can actually improve accuracy (recognised options are sometimes the better bet).

The anchoring and adjustment heuristic

We start from a first approximation (the anchor) and adjust insufficiently from it. The anchor restricts our search for relevant information to numbers near it, even when the anchor is arbitrary. Tversky & Kahneman's product demonstration: estimating 8×7×6×… vs 1×2×3×… (descending vs ascending) gave median estimates of 2,250 vs 512, driven purely by the first (anchor) number. Anchoring also corrupts confidence intervals: people's 98% intervals contain the true answer only ~60% of the time (over-confidence), as Slovic et al. (1974) illustrate with a chained estimate of US car imports anchored on rough early figures. Englich & Mussweiler (2001): experienced trial judges' sentences were swayed by an obviously unqualified "prosecutor's" demand, 28 months after a 12-month demand vs 36 months after a 34-month demand.

The framing effect

The framing effect shows that the outcome of a decision depends on (a) the background context and (b) the wording of the question. Tversky & Kahneman (1981) "Asian disease" problem: framed as lives saved, 72% chose the certain Program A (saving 200 for sure) over the risky Program B (one-in-three chance of saving 600), people were risk-averse for gains. Framed as deaths, only 22% chose the certain Program C (400 die for sure) and 78% chose the risky Program D, people were risk-seeking for losses, even though the four programs are statistically equivalent. Background context matters too: 88% would re-buy a lost theatre ticket when a $10 bill was lost (different mental account), but only 46% when the $10 ticket itself was lost. This asymmetry, risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses, plus reference-dependence and loss aversion, is the heart of Kahneman & Tversky's prospect theory, the descriptive alternative to expected-utility theory.

High-yield

Prospect theory / framing: identical outcomes drive opposite choices depending on wording. People are risk-averse for gains ("lives saved" → take the sure thing) and risk-seeking for losses ("deaths" → gamble). Losses loom larger than equivalent gains (loss aversion).

Trap

Don't conflate the heuristics. Representativeness = "is it similar to the category?" (ignores base rates). Availability = "how easily do examples come to mind?" (recency/familiarity). Anchoring = "start from a number and adjust too little." Framing = "the wording/reference point flips the choice."
