1. Psychology as a science: methods & perspectives

What the field claims. Social psychology is the scientific study of how the thoughts, feelings and behaviour of individuals are influenced by the actual, imagined or implied presence of others (Allport's classic definition). The key word is scientific: it settles questions by study, not intuition. The governing insight, repeated throughout, is the power of the situation: ordinary people help, conform, obey and aggress far more because of where they are and who is around than because of fixed traits. That theme, situation over disposition, returns in obedience (Milgram), bystander apathy (Latané & Darley) and attribution (the fundamental attribution error is precisely our failure to see it).

The role of theory. A theory is an integrated set of principles that explains and predicts observed events. Good science runs a loop: a theory generates a falsifiable hypothesis; a study tests it; results refine or refute it. A theory is never "proven", only supported or disconfirmed.

The core research methods

MethodWhat you doStrength / limit
ExperimentalManipulate one factor (the independent variable, IV), hold all else constant, measure the effect on the dependent variable, DV. Use random assignment to conditions.The only method that establishes cause and effect. Limit: artificiality, low external validity if over-controlled.
CorrelationalMeasure two or more variables as they naturally occur and see if they covary (correlation coefficient, −1 to +1).Good for real-world variables you cannot ethically manipulate. Limit: correlation ≠ causation (direction problem + third-variable problem).
SurveyAsk a representative sample questions to describe a population's attitudes/behaviour.Cheap, broad. Limit: sampling bias, wording effects, social-desirability responding.
Systematic / naturalistic observationWatch and record behaviour as it occurs without intervening (e.g. counting jaywalkers).High realism. Limit: no control, observer effects, no causal claim.
High-yield

The IV is the cause you dial (the knob), the DV the effect you read off (the dial). Random assignment is what lets you claim the knob, not pre-existing differences between people, moved the dial; correlational work has no knob, so it cannot tell you which dial drives. A mediator is the mechanism between cause and effect, explaining why (frustration → anger → aggression: anger mediates). A moderator changes the strength or direction of a relationship, telling you when / for whom it holds (heat raises aggression more for provoked people). A meta-analysis statistically combines many studies to estimate the true effect size.

Ethics, deception and replication

Because the true purpose can change behaviour, social psychology often uses deception (a cover story), permitted only under strict safeguards: informed consent, the right to withdraw, minimising harm, and a thorough debriefing that reveals the true purpose and removes any after-effects. An Institutional Review Board / ethics committee must approve the study first. The field also takes its replication crisis seriously: a finding is trusted only when independent labs replicate it, which is why classics (Milgram, marshmallow studies) are now discussed alongside their replications and failures.

The four perspectives Baron uses

PerspectiveCore claim
CognitiveTo explain social behaviour, study how people think: notice, store, recall, interpret. The dominant modern lens (schemas, heuristics, attribution live here).
EvolutionarySome tendencies (mate choice, helping kin, fear of strangers) were shaped by natural selection for survival/reproduction.
Social neuroscienceLinks social behaviour to brain and biology (fMRI, hormones): amygdala in out-group fear, oxytocin in trust.
Cultural / multiculturalMany findings hold differently across cultures; must test whether an effect is universal or culture-bound (individualist vs collectivist).
High-yield

The researcher is also a thinker prone to the very biases the field studies, so methods (random assignment, double-blind procedures, pre-registration, replication) exist partly to fence out the researcher's expectancy bias / experimenter effects. NIMHANS asks methods crisply: name the only method that proves causation (experimental), say why correlation cannot (third-variable + directionality), and distinguish mediator (the why) from moderator (the when/for whom); also the purpose of debriefing after deception and what an ethics board protects. Lock: Allport's definition = "actual, imagined or implied presence of others"; IV = manipulated, DV = measured; a theory is supported or disconfirmed, never proven; four perspectives = cognitive · evolutionary · social neuroscience · cultural.

2. Sensation & perception

Sensation is detecting raw stimulus energy at the receptors; perception is the brain organising and interpreting it. Transduction converts stimulus energy into neural signals.

Psychophysics (thresholds)

ConceptMeaning
Absolute thresholdSmallest intensity detectable 50% of the time
Difference threshold (JND)Smallest detectable change between two stimuli (50% of the time)
Weber's lawThe JND is a constant proportion of the stimulus, not a fixed amount
Fechner's lawSensation grows with the logarithm of stimulus intensity
Stevens' power lawSensation is a power function of intensity (often fits better than Fechner)
Subliminal / primingStimuli below the absolute threshold can still prime responses

Signal detection theory

Detection is a decision under uncertainty, not a fixed threshold. Four outcomes: hit, miss, false alarm, correct rejection. d-prime (d′) measures true sensitivity; beta is the response criterion (willingness to say "yes"). Motivation and expectation shift beta, not sensitivity.

Theories of perception

High-yield

The two camps: Gibson's direct / bottom-up theory (the stimulus itself carries enough information; perception is data-driven) versus Gregory's constructivist / top-down theory (perception is an active hypothesis built from prior knowledge and inference). Most modern views combine both.

Gestalt organisation, depth & constancy

"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts." Gestalt laws: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity (good continuation), common fate, figure-ground, prägnanz (simplicity). Founders: Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler.

  • Binocular depth cues: retinal disparity, convergence.
  • Monocular cues: relative size, interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, motion parallax, aerial perspective.
  • Perceptual constancies: size, shape, colour, brightness, the object looks stable despite a changing retinal image.
  • Feature integration theory (Treisman): a preattentive stage detects individual features in parallel, then focused attention binds them into a whole object; failed binding produces illusory conjunctions.

Pattern recognition & perceptual set

  • Template, prototype and feature-detection theories of recognition; Biederman's recognition-by-components (geons) builds objects from basic 3D primitives.
  • Perceptual set / expectancy: context, motivation, culture and prior knowledge bias what we perceive (top-down).
  • Illusions: Müller-Lyer, Ponzo, Ames room, moon illusion, Kanizsa triangle (illusory contours), the McGurk effect (vision alters what we hear). Illusions reveal the brain's interpretive shortcuts.

3. Attention

Attention is the selective allocation of limited processing capacity. The big debate: where in the stream does selection happen (early vs late), and how much capacity is there (bottleneck vs flexible resources)?

Bottleneck / filter models (where does selection happen?)

ModelClaim
Broadbent (1958, early selection)A single filter blocks unattended input early, by physical features (pitch, location), before meaning. Based on Cherry's dichotic-listening / shadowing work.
Treisman (1964, attenuation)The filter attenuates (turns down) rather than fully blocks; high-priority words with low thresholds (your name, "fire") still break through.
Deutsch & Deutsch / Norman (late selection)All input is fully processed for meaning; selection happens late, at the response/awareness stage.
Johnston & Heinz (multimode)Selection is flexible, early or late depending on task demands, but later selection costs more capacity.

Capacity / resource models (how much is there?)

ModelClaim
Kahneman (1973, single capacity)One limited pool of effort allocated across tasks; arousal changes the pool size; an allocation policy decides where it goes.
Wickens (multiple resources)Several separate pools (visual vs auditory, verbal vs spatial); two tasks interfere most when they draw on the same pool (why you can drive and listen but not read and listen).
Norman & BobrowData-limited (poor stimulus quality) vs resource-limited (available attention) performance.
Lavie (perceptual load theory)High load → early selection (no spare capacity, distractors filtered); low load → spare capacity spills onto distractors (late). Reconciles the early/late debate.

Spatial / visual attention & control

  • Spotlight model (Posner): attention is a movable spotlight enhancing processing wherever it points. Zoom-lens (Eriksen): it widens or narrows, trading area for resolution.
  • Posner cueing paradigm: a valid cue speeds detection, an invalid one slows it. Endogenous (voluntary, goal-driven, central cue) vs exogenous (reflexive, stimulus-driven, peripheral cue) orienting; phases: disengage → move → engage.
  • Feature integration theory (Treisman): preattentive parallel feature registration, then focused attention binds features; feature search ("pop-out") is parallel and fast, conjunction search is serial; failed binding = illusory conjunctions. Guided search (Wolfe) refines FIT: top-down knowledge guides attention to likely targets.
  • Automatic vs controlled (Schneider & Shiffrin): automatic is fast, parallel, effortless, hard to suppress (Stroop happens because reading is automatic); controlled is slow, serial, effortful, flexible; practice automatises a skill.
  • Supervisory Attentional System (Norman & Shallice): routine action runs on automatic schemas via contention scheduling; the SAS (frontal) intervenes only for novel/risky situations; failures → action slips / capture errors.
  • Types: selective (one source), divided (across tasks), sustained (vigilance), alternating/switching (toggle, with a switch cost).

Key phenomena

  • Cocktail-party effect (Cherry): follow one voice yet still detect your own name in an ignored stream.
  • Stroop effect: naming the ink colour of a colour-word is slowed because reading is automatic.
  • Inattentional blindness (Simons & Chabris invisible-gorilla) and change blindness: without attention, even large changes go unnoticed.
  • Attentional blink: in rapid serial visual presentation a second target is missed ~200–500 ms after the first.
  • Vigilance decrement (Mackworth's clock test): sustained-attention performance declines over time, faster when signals are rare.
  • Psychological refractory period: a second response is delayed when two stimuli arrive close together (evidence for a central bottleneck).
  • Spatial neglect: right-parietal damage leaves patients ignoring the left side of space (a clinical breakdown of spatial attention).
Trap

The cocktail-party effect (hearing your name in an unattended conversation) supports late/attenuation selection, NOT Broadbent's early filter, the most common attention MCQ. Broadbent = early/physical; Treisman = attenuation; Deutsch-Norman = late/meaning; Lavie = depends on load. Feature search pops out (parallel); conjunction search is serial.

4. Memory

Multi-store model (Atkinson-Shiffrin)

Sensory memory → short-term/working memory → long-term memory, with attention and rehearsal as the gates.

StoreCapacity / duration
Sensory: iconic (visual)Large; ~0.25–1 s (Sperling's partial-report)
Sensory: echoic (auditory)~2–4 s
Short-term memory7 ± 2 items (Miller), ~15–30 s; chunking expands it
Long-term memoryEffectively unlimited, durable

Working memory (Baddeley & Hitch)

Four parts: central executive (control), phonological loop (verbal/acoustic), visuospatial sketchpad (visual/spatial), and the episodic buffer (added 2000, integrates information across systems).

Types of long-term memory

  • Explicit / declarative (conscious): episodic (events) and semantic (facts, meanings).
  • Implicit / non-declarative (unconscious): procedural (skills), priming, classical conditioning.

Processes, forgetting & distortion

  • Encoding → storage → retrieval. Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart): deep semantic encoding beats shallow.
  • Forgetting: decay; interference (proactive = old disrupts new; retroactive = new disrupts old); cue-dependent / retrieval failure; encoding failure.
  • Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: rapid early loss then levelling; spacing and overlearning slow it.
  • Serial position effect: primacy (LTM) + recency (STM); a delay wipes recency, not primacy.
  • Reconstructive memory: Bartlett (schemas distort recall, "War of the Ghosts"); Loftus (misinformation effect, leading questions reshape memory).
  • Amnesia: anterograde (cannot form new memories, e.g. H.M.) vs retrograde (cannot recall old). Korsakoff = thiamine deficiency + confabulation.

Retrieval, cues & enhancement

  • Encoding specificity (Tulving): retrieval is best when recall cues match encoding cues.
  • Context-dependent (same place) and state-dependent (same mood/physiological state) memory; mood-congruent recall.
  • Recall vs recognition vs relearning (savings) as measures of retention; recognition is usually easier than recall.
  • Flashbulb memories: vivid, confident memories of shocking events, felt accurate but still prone to distortion.
  • Tip-of-the-tongue = retrieval failure with partial access. Spacing and testing effects: spaced practice and active retrieval beat massed re-reading.
  • Consolidation: the hippocampus binds new memories; sleep aids consolidation; reconsolidation makes reactivated memories briefly editable.

5. Learning

Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

A neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus (UCS) becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) eliciting a conditioned response (CR). Phases: acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation, discrimination, higher-order conditioning. Watson's Little Albert = conditioned fear. Garcia effect = taste aversion learned in one trial over a long delay (biological preparedness).

Operant conditioning (Thorndike, Skinner)

Thorndike's law of effect: responses followed by satisfaction are stamped in. Skinner formalised it.

Add (positive)Remove (negative)
Increase behaviour (reinforcement)Positive reinforcement (give a reward)Negative reinforcement (remove an aversive)
Decrease behaviour (punishment)Positive punishment (add an aversive)Negative punishment (remove a reward)
High-yield

Schedules of reinforcement: Fixed Ratio, Variable Ratio, Fixed Interval, Variable Interval. Variable Ratio gives the highest, steadiest rate and the most resistance to extinction (gambling). Ratio beats interval; variable beats fixed.

Shaping = reinforcing successive approximations. Premack principle = a high-frequency behaviour reinforces a low-frequency one ("eat your vegetables, then dessert").

Cognitive & observational learning

  • Tolman: latent learning + cognitive maps (rats learned the maze without reinforcement).
  • Köhler: insight learning (chimps solved problems suddenly, the "aha").
  • Bandura: observational learning / modelling (Bobo doll); steps = attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
  • Seligman: learned helplessness; biological constraints (preparedness) limit what can be conditioned.
High-yield

Rescorla-Wagner model: conditioning depends on surprise / prediction error, not mere pairing; the CS must reliably predict the UCS. Explains blocking (a new cue learns nothing if an existing cue already predicts the UCS) and the contingency (not just contiguity) requirement, Rescorla showed the CS must signal information.

More terms: stimulus generalisation vs discrimination; spontaneous recovery of an extinguished CR; overshadowing (a salient cue overshadows a weak one); latent inhibition (a pre-exposed stimulus is harder to condition). Operant: escape vs avoidance learning, discriminative stimulus (signals when a response pays off), extinction burst, and the partial-reinforcement extinction effect (intermittently reinforced behaviour resists extinction).

6. Cognition: thinking, language & social cognition

Thinking, problem-solving & decision making

  • Concepts & categories: classical (defining features) vs prototype vs exemplar theories; we categorise by comparison to a best example (prototype); basic-level categories (Rosch).
  • Problem solving: algorithms (guaranteed, slow) vs heuristics (fast rules of thumb); strategies = means-end analysis, working backward, analogy; insight vs incremental search; incubation. Barriers: fixation, mental set, functional fixedness.
  • Decision making: System 1 (fast, intuitive) vs System 2 (slow, deliberate).
  • Reasoning: deductive (general → specific, logically certain) vs inductive (specific → general, probabilistic); confirmation bias and belief bias distort it.
  • Mental imagery: Shepard & Metzler's mental rotation, imagery is analog and spatial.
  • Metacognition: thinking about and monitoring one's own thinking.

Language

Structure: phonemes → morphemes → syntax → semantics → pragmatics. Stages: cooing, babbling, one-word (~12 mo), telegraphic (~24 mo). Chomsky: language acquisition device + universal grammar (nativist). Skinner: language learned by reinforcement (behaviourist), demolished by Chomsky's review. Whorf: linguistic relativity (language shapes thought). Critical/sensitive period (Lenneberg); the case of Genie.

Social cognition: how we think about the social world

The problem. The social world throws more information at us than we can fully process, so the mind takes shortcuts. We are "cognitive misers", spending mental effort sparingly, defaulting to fast, cheap strategies that are usually good enough but sometimes badly wrong. Underlying everything is the dual-process view (System 1 vs System 2): automatic processing is fast, effortless, unconscious, emotional; controlled processing is slow, effortful, deliberate, logical. This same automatic/controlled split reappears in persuasion (peripheral vs central route) and prejudice (implicit vs explicit attitudes).

Heuristics and the schemas they run on

TermWhoWhat it is
Heuristics (availability, representativeness, anchoring & adjustment)Tversky & KahnemanMental shortcuts: judge by ease of recall (availability) / resemblance to a prototype (representativeness) / clinging to a first value then adjusting too little (anchoring).
SchemaBartlett (social use: Fiske & Taylor)Mental framework guiding what we notice, encode and recall about people/situations.
Priming—A recently activated schema shapes the next interpretation, even unconsciously.
High-yield

Availability: you fear plane crashes more than car crashes because crashes make the news and spring to mind easily. Representativeness: told someone is quiet and loves poetry you guess "librarian" over "salesperson" because they fit the prototype, ignoring that salespeople vastly outnumber librarians (base-rate neglect). Anchoring: the first value you hear drags your estimate toward it. A schema is the bigger structure these run on; priming switches a schema on so it colours what comes next. Schemas bias three things, attention, encoding (usually schema-consistent info, though wildly inconsistent info also grabs attention) and retrieval, and they show the perseverance effect (beliefs survive even after the evidence that created them is discredited; first impressions are sticky).

Affect and cognition cut both ways. Mood shapes thought (positive mood broadens thinking, increases heuristic use; we recall mood-congruent material) and thought shapes mood (appraisal of an event determines how you feel). A good mood makes you a lazier, more shortcut-prone thinker.

Trap

Two near-twins: availability = judged by how easily examples come to mind (recent, vivid, emotional); representativeness = judged by how well it matches a prototype (neglects base rates). "Easy to recall / vivid" → availability; "fits the stereotype/category" → representativeness. All the heuristics-and-biases items trace to Tversky & Kahneman.

High-yield

NIMHANS / clinical map. Wegner's rebound effect is the model behind why "just stop thinking about it" fails in OCD, PTSD intrusions and insomnia, and why acceptance-based therapies (ACT, mindfulness) outperform suppression; pair it with the perseverance effect, which explains why a discredited self-belief ("I'm a failure") persists in depression. Negativity bias + counterfactual thinking underlie depressive rumination ("if only I had…"); depressive realism / pessimistic explanatory style is the opposite of optimistic bias; magical thinking is a named symptom in OCD and schizotypal personality. Cognitive therapy is essentially retraining these automatic biases.

7. Emotion

TheorySequence
James-LangeStimulus → body arousal → you label the emotion ("I tremble, therefore I am afraid")
Cannon-BardStimulus → arousal AND emotion at the same time (thalamus routes both)
Schachter-Singer (two-factor)Arousal + a cognitive label = emotion; same arousal, different label, different emotion
Lazarus (appraisal)Cognitive appraisal comes first, then emotion
ZajoncSome emotional reactions occur before/without cognition ("preferences need no inferences")
Facial-feedback hypothesis (Tomkins; Ekman)Facial expressions feed back and intensify/trigger the felt emotion

Ekman: six basic universal emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust). Components of emotion: physiological arousal, expressive behaviour, conscious experience. Yerkes-Dodson law: performance peaks at moderate arousal (inverted U); harder tasks peak at lower arousal.

  • Plutchik's wheel: eight primary emotions in opposing pairs blending into secondary emotions (joy + trust = love).
  • Dimensional models: Russell's circumplex maps emotion on valence (pleasant–unpleasant) × arousal (high–low).
  • LeDoux's two roads to fear: a fast "low road" (thalamus → amygdala) for quick reaction, a slow "high road" (via cortex) for accurate appraisal.
  • Cognitive appraisal (Lazarus): primary appraisal (is it relevant/threatening?) then secondary (can I cope?).
  • Display rules: culturally learned norms for when/how to show emotion; basic expressions universal, display rules vary.
  • Emotional intelligence (Salovey & Mayer; popularised by Goleman): perceiving, using, understanding, regulating emotion.
Trap

James-Lange = body first; Cannon-Bard = simultaneous; Schachter-Singer = body + label. This ordering is the single most common emotion-theory exam item.

8. Motivation

  • Instinct theory: behaviour driven by innate biological programs.
  • Drive-reduction (Hull): a need creates a drive; behaviour reduces it to restore homeostasis.
  • Arousal theory: we seek an optimal level of arousal (links to Yerkes-Dodson).
  • Incentive theory: external rewards pull behaviour.
  • Maslow's hierarchy: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualisation (later: cognitive, aesthetic, transcendence).
  • Intrinsic vs extrinsic: intrinsic = for its own sake; extrinsic = for a reward. The overjustification effect: paying for an already-enjoyed activity can reduce intrinsic motivation.
  • Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan): autonomy, competence, relatedness.
  • McClelland: needs for achievement, affiliation, power (measured via the TAT).
  • ERG theory (Alderfer): Maslow condensed to Existence, Relatedness, Growth; allows frustration-regression.
  • Expectancy theory (Vroom): motivation = expectancy × instrumentality × valence.
  • Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham): specific, challenging, accepted goals drive performance.
  • Approach-avoidance conflict (Lewin): approach-approach, avoidance-avoidance, approach-avoidance conflicts.
  • Specific drives: hunger (lateral hypothalamus starts eating, ventromedial stops it; set-point/leptin); arousal/sensation-seeking; achievement motivation (need for achievement vs fear of failure).

  • Heritability & environment: twin/adoption studies; the Flynn effect and stereotype-threat findings show environment matters; reliability/validity and cultural bias are key test-construction concerns.

10. Personality

Psychodynamic (Freud)

  • Structure: id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle), superego (morality/conscience).
  • Topographic: conscious, preconscious, unconscious (the iceberg).
  • Psychosexual stages: oral → anal → phallic (Oedipus/Electra) → latency → genital; fixation shapes the adult personality.
  • Defence mechanisms: repression, denial, projection, displacement, reaction formation, rationalisation, sublimation, regression (Anna Freud catalogued these).

Neo-Freudians

Jung: collective unconscious, archetypes, introversion/extraversion. Adler: inferiority complex, striving for superiority, birth order. Horney: basic anxiety, womb envy (countered Freud). Erikson: psychosocial stages (see Development).

Trait theories

TheoristModel
AllportCardinal, central, secondary traits
Cattell16 Personality Factors (16PF), via factor analysis
EysenckThree dimensions: extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism (PEN)
Costa & McCraeBig Five / OCEAN: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism

Humanistic & social-cognitive

  • Rogers: self-concept, congruence (real vs ideal self), unconditional positive regard, the fully functioning person.
  • Maslow: self-actualisation (see Motivation).
  • Bandura: reciprocal determinism (behaviour ↔ person ↔ environment), self-efficacy.
  • Rotter: locus of control (internal vs external).
High-yield

Assessment: projective tests (Rorschach inkblots, TAT) tap the unconscious; objective inventories (MMPI, 16PF, NEO-PI) are self-report and standardised.

12. Consciousness & sleep

  • Sleep stages: NREM 1 (theta, hypnagogic) → NREM 2 (sleep spindles, K-complexes) → NREM 3 (delta, deep/slow-wave) → REM (paradoxical: brain active, body paralysed, dreaming); ~90-minute cycles, REM lengthens toward morning.
  • Circadian rhythm: ~24-hour cycle, governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus; melatonin from the pineal gland.
  • Dream theories: Freud (wish fulfilment, manifest vs latent content); activation-synthesis (Hobson & McCarley, brain makes sense of random REM activity); information-processing/consolidation.
  • Sleep disorders: insomnia, narcolepsy, sleep apnoea, night terrors (NREM) vs nightmares (REM), somnambulism.
  • Altered states: hypnosis, meditation, psychoactive drugs (depressants, stimulants, hallucinogens, opioids) altering consciousness via neurotransmitters.
  • Theories of hypnosis: Hilgard's neodissociation ("hidden observer") vs the social-cognitive/role-enactment view.
  • Functions of sleep: restoration, energy conservation, memory consolidation; sleep deprivation effects.
  • Drug concepts: tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, the reward pathway (dopamine, nucleus accumbens).
  • Consciousness debates: the "hard problem"; the cognitive unconscious; global workspace theory.

13. Stress, coping & health

  • Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome: alarm → resistance → exhaustion.
  • Lazarus & Folkman: primary appraisal (is it a threat?) then secondary appraisal (can I cope?). Problem-focused coping (change the stressor) vs emotion-focused coping (manage the feeling).
  • Physiology: the HPA axis and sympathetic "fight-or-flight" (Cannon); cortisol as the chronic-stress hormone.
  • Type A (competitive, hostile, time-urgent) vs Type B; hostility links to heart disease.
  • Buffers: social support, perceived control, optimism, hardiness (Kobasa: commitment, control, challenge).
  • Stressors: Holmes & Rahe Social Readjustment Rating Scale (life-change units); daily hassles vs uplifts (Lazarus); acute vs chronic stress.
  • Tend-and-befriend (Taylor): an alternative to fight-or-flight, oxytocin-linked, more often described in women.
  • Health: psychoneuroimmunology (stress suppresses immunity); the biopsychosocial model; learned optimism (Seligman).

14. Social perception & attribution

The problem. To navigate other people we must answer two questions: what are they feeling now (read from nonverbal cues) and why did they do that (attribution). Social perception is how we form impressions and infer causes from limited information.

Nonverbal communication

Before a word is spoken we read nonverbal channels: facial expressions (Ekman's six basic emotions are largely universal), eye contact (gaze), body language and posture, touch, and paralanguage (tone, pace, pauses). These leak information the person may not mean to send, which is why we often trust the nonverbal over the verbal. Baron's cues to deception: microexpressions (fleeting true feelings flashing before they are masked), interchannel discrepancies (the face says one thing, the hands or voice another), and changes in eye contact and linguistic style. Despite all this, people detect lies barely above chance, because liars control the easy channels and we over-trust the face. Nonverbal "leakage" (Ekman & Friesen): true feelings escape through the channels we control least (legs, feet, voice pitch) while we manage the face.

Attribution: explaining behaviour

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Attribution theoryFritz HeiderWe explain behaviour as internal (disposition) or external (situation); the person as "naïve psychologist."
Correspondent inference theoryJones & DavisWe infer a trait from acts that are freely chosen, unexpected and low in social desirability.
Covariation modelHarold KelleyAttribution uses consensus, consistency & distinctiveness; high all three → situation/stimulus; low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency → the person. High-yield

Walk Kelley's three Cs. Your friend laughs at a comedian. Consensus: does everyone laugh? Distinctiveness: does she laugh only at this comedian? Consistency: does she laugh every time? High on all three → it's the comedian (external); low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency → it's her (internal). Heider gave the basic internal/external split; Jones & Davis asked when an act reveals a true trait; Kelley added the data-driven rule. The two-step attribution model (Gilbert): we first automatically make a dispositional inference, then effortfully correct for the situation, and the correction fails when we are busy or tired, which is why the FAE is so robust.

Impression formation & management

Solomon Asch showed first impressions form fast and that some traits are central (swapping "warm" for "cold" transforms the whole impression, while "polite/blunt" barely moves it). The primacy effect means early information weighs most. Baron contrasts Asch's configural / gestalt view (traits fuse into a whole) with Norman Anderson's information-integration / averaging model (we mentally average the weighted values of each trait). On the flip side, impression management (self-presentation) is our effort to control the impression others form of us, a theme Goffman framed dramaturgically (life as theatre, front-stage vs back-stage); tactics include self-promotion (look competent), ingratiation (flattery, agreement) and self-deprecation.

Trap

The attribution swap: FAE (Ross) = judging others (blame the person, ignore their situation); actor–observer (Jones & Nisbett) = the gap between explaining your own acts (situation) vs others' acts (character); self-serving = explaining your own outcomes (credit success, dodge blame for failure). Read whose behaviour and whose outcome the stem is about. Separately, Asch (configural, central traits reorganise the whole) vs Anderson (averaging the weighted trait values).

High-yield

NIMHANS / clinical map. Depression's negative (pessimistic) attributional style (internal, stable, global for failures) is the mirror image of the self-serving bias, which depressed people lose. The just-world belief explains victim-blaming in trauma work. The FAE is exactly why we pathologise individuals while underrating their situations, the bias clinical formulation tries to correct. Attribution models in order: Heider (naïve psychologist, internal/external) → Jones & Davis (correspondent inference) → Kelley (covariation three Cs).

15. The self

The problem. The self is both the thinker and the thing thought about. How do we build a self-concept (content, "who am I"), set our self-esteem (evaluation, "how do I feel about who I am"), and protect that esteem? The recurring finding is that the self is fundamentally social: we know ourselves largely by comparison to, and reflection in, others.

The self across cultures (high-yield NIMHANS contrast)

Hazel Markus & Shinobu Kitayama drew the field's biggest cultural distinction: the independent self-construal (individualist/Western, the self defined by internal traits, separate, self-promoting) versus the interdependent self-construal (collectivist cultures including India, the self defined by relationships, roles and group harmony). This explains cultural differences in the FAE (weaker in collectivist cultures), self-serving bias, self-enhancement (lower in East Asian samples) and conformity. India is the textbook collectivist/interdependent case.

Trap

Two self-discrepancy directions (Higgins): falling short of your ideal self (hopes, what you want to be) → dejection (sad). Falling short of your ought self (duties, what you should be) → agitation (anxious). Ideal→sad, ought→anxious. And dissonance vs self-perception: Festinger involves arousal and a strong prior attitude; Bem (this section) involves no arousal, just calm inference from a weak attitude.

High-yield

NIMHANS / clinical map. Self-efficacy (Bandura) and self-esteem are workhorses of CBT. Self-discrepancy theory (Higgins) models depression (ideal-self gap) vs anxiety (ought-self gap). Self-verification (Swann) explains why people with low self-esteem resist positive feedback and stay in confirming (even abusive) relationships, a real treatment obstacle. Terror management connects to existential anxiety. Most of these terms are esteem-protecting manoeuvres rather than neutral descriptions; the cooler cognitive exceptions are social comparison (Festinger) and self-perception (Bem).

16. Attitudes & persuasion

The problem. An attitude is an evaluation of an object along a like–dislike dimension. The classic ABC structure has three components: Affective (feelings), Behavioural (action tendencies), Cognitive (beliefs). The two big questions: do attitudes predict behaviour? and how do you change them?

Where attitudes come from, and the implicit/explicit split

Attitudes form mostly via low-level learning: classical conditioning (an object paired with positive/negative stimuli, including subliminal conditioning and mere exposure), instrumental conditioning (attitudes earning approval are reinforced), and observational learning / social comparison (adopting the attitudes of models and reference groups). Twin studies show a partly heritable component.

TermWhat it is
Explicit attitudesConscious, reportable, controllable evaluations.
Implicit attitudesAutomatic, often unconscious evaluations, measured by the Implicit Association Test (IAT) via reaction times; can diverge from what we say, and predict spontaneous behaviour.

Functions of attitudes (Daniel Katz): knowledge (organise the world), utilitarian/instrumental (gain reward, avoid punishment), ego-defensive (protect self-esteem), and value-expressive (state who we are). Changing an attitude requires addressing its function (the same surface attitude may be ego-defensive in one person and value-expressive in another).

The attitude–behaviour link

For decades the embarrassing answer was "barely". The famous demonstration is the LaPiere study: a researcher toured 1930s America with a Chinese couple and was served almost everywhere, yet most of those same establishments later said by mail they would refuse Chinese guests. The resolution came from Fishbein & Ajzen: attitudes predict behaviour best through intentions, shaped by attitudes plus subjective norms plus (in the planned-behaviour update) perceived behavioural control. Attitudes also predict better when strong, specific, accessible and based on direct experience.

The dual route, intuitively. When you care and can concentrate (buying a house) you take the central route, weighing real arguments, and the attitude is durable. Cognitive dissonance & consistency theories

The most-tested idea in the chapter. When two cognitions clash, or behaviour contradicts an attitude, we feel an unpleasant tension (dissonance) and are driven to reduce it, usually by changing the attitude to fit the behaviour.

Theory / termWhoWhat it is

Insufficient justification / induced complianceFestinger & CarlsmithThe $1 group changed their attitude more than the $20 group (less external reward = more dissonance = more internal attitude change).
Effort justificationAronson & MillsThe harder/more embarrassing the initiation, the more we value the group we joined.

High-yield

Why $1 beat $20. Participants did a boring task then were paid to tell the next person it was fun. Those paid $20 had a big external reason to lie ("I did it for the money"), so no dissonance, attitude unchanged. Those paid $1 had no good external reason, so the only way to resolve "I said it was fun but I had no reason to lie" was to decide it actually was fun. Less reward, more attitude change. Same logic in effort justification. NIMHANS / clinical: effort justification is one reason expensive/effortful therapy can be valued more (and underlies hazing/cult commitment); reactance (Brehm) explains why heavy-handed advice backfires and is the rationale for motivational interviewing; dissonance is harnessed in attitude-change health interventions.

Trap

Dissonance vs self-perception: both predict attitude follows behaviour. Festinger (dissonance) = there is arousal/discomfort, applies when the attitude was strong/clear and the behaviour contradicts it. Bem (self-perception) = no arousal, you simply infer the attitude by watching yourself, applies when the attitude was weak or unclear. The deciding clue is whether the stem mentions tension/discomfort (Festinger) or a calm inference (Bem).

17. Stereotyping, prejudice & discrimination

The three words kept straight. A stereotype is the cognitive component (a belief about a group's traits). Prejudice is the affective component (a negative feeling toward a group). Discrimination is the behavioural component (unequal treatment). They are the ABC of intergroup bias.

The three levels. Conformity = changing to match a group with no direct request. Compliance = changing in response to a direct request (the sales techniques). Obedience = changing in response to a direct order from an authority. The levels rise from implicit group pressure to explicit command.

That's-not-all techniqueJerry BurgerAdd a bonus or drop the price before the person decides → boosts compliance.
Pique technique—An unusual request ("can you spare 37 cents?") disrupts automatic refusal and raises compliance by making people think.
Disrupt-then-reframeDavis & KnowlesA confusing twist followed by a reframe ("it's 300 pennies… that's a bargain!") boosts compliance.
Norm of reciprocityAlvin GouldnerWe feel obliged to return favours (the engine behind door-in-the-face and free samples).
Agentic vs autonomous stateStanley MilgramPeople shift from acting on their own values (autonomous) to seeing themselves as an authority's tool, not responsible (agentic state), the mechanism of obedience.
Trap

Foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face: FITD = small → big (Freedman & Fraser, via consistency); DITF = big → small (Cialdini, via reciprocal concession). Lowballing = get the yes, then change the deal. That's-not-all = sweeten the offer before the yes. Examiners love swapping the direction.

High-yield

NIMHANS. Milgram and Asch are perennial: lock the headline numbers (Milgram ~65% to maximum voltage; Asch ~33% conformity per trial, ~75% conformed at least once) and the situational factors that reduce obedience (authority less close/legitimate, peers who rebel, victim more present). Minority influence (Moscovici) via consistency is the standard contrast to majority conformity. Clinically, these explain compliance pressures in institutions and the value of even one dissenting ally.

20. Prosocial behaviour (helping)

The problem. Why do we help, when do we fail to, and is helping ever truly selfless? The chapter splits into situational work (the bystander effect: why help fails) and motivational work (egoism vs altruism: why we help at all).

When help fails: the bystander effect

TermWhoWhat it is
Bystander effect & 5-step decision modelLatané & DarleyMore witnesses → less help. Five steps: notice → interpret as emergency → take responsibility → know how to help → decide to act. (Sparked by the Kitty Genovese case.)
Diffusion of responsibilityDarley & Latané"Someone else will do it"; responsibility spreads thin in a crowd (fails step 3).
Pluralistic ignorance—Everyone stays calm because no one else looks worried, so no one reads it as an emergency (fails step 2).
High-yield

Why a crowd freezes you. The five-step model is a chain, and the crowd snaps it at two links. At step 2 (interpret) you look around, see calm faces, and conclude it must not be an emergency, that is pluralistic ignorance. At step 3 (take responsibility), with many present, each assumes someone else will act, that is diffusion of responsibility. More witnesses make both failures more likely, so paradoxically you are less likely to be helped in a big crowd. Expect a scene with a "which step failed" stem.

Why we help: egoism vs altruism

TermWhoWhat it is
Empathy–altruism hypothesisC. Daniel BatsonFeeling empathy for the victim produces genuinely selfless helping, aimed at their welfare, not our own relief.
Negative-state relief modelRobert CialdiniWe help to relieve our own bad mood; helping is ultimately egoistic.
Arousal: cost–reward modelPiliavin et al.A bystander's unpleasant arousal at seeing distress motivates help; we help when costs of helping are low and costs of not helping are high.
Empathic joy hypothesisSmith et al.We help partly for the pleasure of having a positive impact; we want feedback that our help worked.
Kin selection / reciprocal altruismHamilton; TriversEvolutionary: help relatives (shared genes, kin selection) or those likely to return the favour (reciprocal altruism).
Norm of social responsibility—A social norm that we should help those who depend on us, regardless of return.
Prosocial / altruistic personality—A cluster (empathy, just-world belief, social responsibility, internal locus, low egocentrism) predicting consistent helping.
Volunteerism & the functional approachClary & SnyderSustained helping is driven by the personal functions it serves (values, understanding, career, social, esteem, protective).
Threat-to-self-esteem / reactions to receiving helpNadler & FisherReceiving aid can feel demeaning and imply inferiority, so recipients sometimes refuse or resent help, especially from a similar other.

What boosts helping: similarity to the victim, seeing a helpful model, feeling gratitude, being in a good mood (the "feel-good, do-good" effect; guilt also raises helping), and the victim being not responsible for their plight. Helping drops with a hurry (the Good Samaritan study, Darley & Batson: seminary students late for a talk stepped over a slumped man), diffuse responsibility, and ambiguity. Overjustification (over-rewarding helping can undermine the intrinsic motive); defensive helping (helping an out-group condescendingly to maintain the in-group's status).

Trap

Why we help, egoism vs altruism: Batson (empathy–altruism) = there is true altruism, born of empathy and aimed at the other's welfare. Cialdini (negative-state relief) and Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward) = we ultimately help to fix our own discomfort, so helping is egoistic. That contrast (selfless vs self-serving motive) is the question.

21. Aggression

The first distinction. Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another who does not want to be harmed. Types: hostile aggression (driven by anger, the goal is to hurt, often impulsive) vs instrumental aggression (a cold means to another end, money, status), plus relational aggression (harming through gossip, exclusion, reputation). The chapter traces an arc: from a simple drive theory (frustration always causes aggression), through its cognitive reformulation (frustration only readies aggression, cues pull the trigger), to social learning and integrative models.

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Frustration–aggression hypothesisDollard, Doob, Miller et al.Original strong form: frustration (a blocked goal) always breeds aggression, and aggression always traces to frustration.
Reformulated frustration–aggressionLeonard BerkowitzFrustration creates only a readiness (negative affect); aggression follows when aggressive cues are present.
Weapons effectBerkowitz & LePageThe mere presence of a weapon increases aggression, because the weapon is an aggressive cue.
Cognitive-neoassociationLeonard BerkowitzAversive events (heat, pain, frustration) trigger negative affect that automatically primes a network of anger and fight/flight responses.

Catharsis hypothesis(Freud / Lorenz roots)The idea that venting "drains" aggression. Largely unsupported; venting tends to raise later aggression.
High-yield

The arc in one breath. Dollard et al. said frustration → aggression, full stop, too rigid. Berkowitz rescued it: frustration only builds a charge of negative affect (readiness); whether it discharges depends on aggressive cues, hence the weapons effect (a gun on the table makes you more aggressive). Bandura's Bobo doll added that we also just copy aggressive models. And the popular belief in catharsis is a myth: venting fuels rather than drains aggression, a high-yield "which is false" item.

Reducing aggression. Catharsis does not work. What does: punishment only if prompt, certain and proportionate (or it backfires); incompatible responses (humour, empathy, mild non-aggressive feelings that can't co-exist with anger); apology and mitigating information; cognitive interventions and exposure to non-aggressive models; and self-regulation training. Frustration (Freud/Lorenz also gave the older instinct view of aggression).

Trap

Two Berkowitz ideas kept apart: reformulated frustration–aggression (frustration → readiness, cue → act) and cognitive-neoassociation (aversive events prime an anger network) are both his. Do not confuse excitation-transfer (Zillmann), where arousal carries over from a prior unrelated event, with the weapons effect, where an external cue triggers aggression. And the catharsis hypothesis is largely disproven, examiners frame it as the false option.

High-yield

NIMHANS. Determinants of aggression as a list: biological (low serotonin, high testosterone, amygdala/prefrontal function, alcohol disinhibition) and situational/social (heat, provocation, crowding, frustration, media/violent models, culture of honour). The catharsis myth and the weapons effect are classic single-best-answer traps. Dodge's hostile attribution bias is core to the clinical model of conduct disorder and is itself a clean attribution-error item.

22. Groups & individuals

The problem. What does the mere presence of, or membership in, a group do to the individual, to effort, self-restraint and decision quality? The chapter pairs opposing effects (presence can boost or sap effort; groups can decide better or worse), and the deciding variable is usually whether you are individually identifiable. A group's structure has four ingredients: roles (expected behaviours for a position), status (rank), norms (shared rules), and cohesiveness (the forces binding members).

The first experiment. Triplett (1898) noticed cyclists rode faster racing others than alone, arguably social psychology's first experiment. Facilitation vs loafing: social facilitation = others present make you try harder (you are watched and evaluated individually); social loafing = others present make you try less (your effort is pooled, your individual output hidden). 23. Landmark studies, walked through

Social psychology is taught through its classic experiments, and NIMHANS/MET stems often describe a study and ask you to name it, its author or its conclusion. Each landmark below follows the same shape: the question, what they did, what they found, why it matters, and the trap. Learn the design, not just the headline number, because examiners test the procedure ("judged the length of lines" vs "a dot of light that appeared to move") as the giveaway.

Triplett (1898) — the first social-psychology experiment

Q: does the presence of others change performance? What: Triplett noticed cyclists posted faster times racing others than the clock, then had children wind fishing reels alone vs alongside a co-actor. Found: children wound faster with a co-actor. Matters: widely cited as the first published social-psychology experiment, the seed of social facilitation. Trap: Triplett observed facilitation; Zajonc later explained it (dominant response); Cottrell refined the cause to evaluation apprehension.

Sherif (1936) — autokinetic norm formation

Q: how do group norms form when reality is ambiguous? What: in a dark room a stationary pinpoint of light appears to move (the autokinetic effect). Alone, people gave wildly different estimates; in groups they converged on a shared figure, and each kept using it even when later tested alone. Found: a private, internalised norm forms and persists. Matters: the model case of informational social influence. Trap: Sherif = ambiguous stimulus, real internal conversion (informational); Asch = unambiguous, public compliance (normative). The "dot of light" is the tell for Sherif.

Asch (1951) — conformity on the line task

Q: will people conform to a group that is clearly wrong? What: a single real participant judged which comparison line matched a standard, seated among confederates giving the same wrong answer on critical trials. Found: about 75% conformed on at least one trial; the rate per critical trial was about 33–37%. Conformity rose with group size (up to 3–4, then plateaued) and unanimity, and collapsed the moment a single ally also dissented. Matters: normative pressure can override the plain evidence of one's senses. Trap: lock the two numbers separately and remember one dissenting ally frees the participant, not the size of the majority.

Milgram (1963) — obedience to authority

Q: how far will ordinary people go in harming another when ordered? What: the "teacher" delivered escalating shocks (15V to 450V, labelled "XXX") to a "learner" (a confederate, never actually shocked) while a calm experimenter issued four scripted prods ("the experiment requires that you continue"). Found: 65% went all the way to 450V. Matters: situational pressure, not sadism, produced obedience. Factors that REDUCED obedience: authority physically absent / by phone; legitimacy undercut (ordinary office vs Yale); victim more present (same room, touch-proximity); and crucially peers who rebelled. Obedience rose when responsibility could be diffused. Trap: headline ~65%; know at least two reducing factors; the agentic state (seeing oneself as the authority's instrument) is the explanatory mechanism.

Zimbardo (1971) — the Stanford Prison Experiment

Q: do situations and roles, not dispositions, drive cruelty? What: college men were randomly assigned "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock basement prison, meant to run two weeks. Found: guards rapidly became abusive, prisoners passive/distressed; halted after six days. Matters: headline demonstration of deindividuation and the power of assigned roles. Trap: Festinger coined "deindividuation"; Zimbardo demonstrated it. Modern critique (demand characteristics, coached guards, low replicability) is now part of the discussion, a stem may ask what it is criticised for.

Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) — $1 vs $20

Q: when does doing something for too little reward change your attitude? What: participants did an excruciatingly boring task, then were paid $1 or $20 to tell a waiting participant it was enjoyable, then privately rated their actual enjoyment. Found: the $1 group rated it more enjoyable. Matters: classic cognitive dissonance via insufficient justification. Trap: the counter-intuitive direction (less reward → more attitude change) is the whole point; $20 gave an external justification, so no dissonance.

Latané & Darley (1968–70) — the bystander effect

Q: why do witnesses fail to help? What: two famous variants. In the smoke-filled room study, participants saw smoke seep in; alone, most reported it quickly, but among passive confederates most sat in the haze (pluralistic ignorance). In the seizure study, the more bystanders a participant believed were on an intercom, the slower and less likely they were to help (diffusion of responsibility). Found: more witnesses → less helping. Matters: formalised as the five-step model. Trap: match the failure to the step; the Kitty Genovese case was the journalistic spark, though its "38 witnesses" detail was later shown to be exaggerated.

Darley & Batson (1973) — the Good Samaritan study

Q: does being in a hurry suppress helping? What: seminary students walked to give a talk (some on the parable of the Good Samaritan) and passed a man slumped, coughing, in a doorway; the experimenters varied how rushed the student was. Found: being in a hurry was the strongest predictor of not helping; the talk topic barely mattered. Matters: the power of the situation over personal values even in prosocially primed people. Trap: "hurry > piety," situational time pressure beats disposition.

Bandura (1961–63) — the Bobo doll

Q: is aggression learned by imitation? What: children watched an adult model either attack or ignore an inflatable Bobo doll, then were mildly frustrated and left alone with it. Found: children who saw aggression imitated it (including novel acts and verbal aggression); seeing the model rewarded increased imitation, seeing it punished decreased performance but not learning. Matters: founded social learning theory and the learning/performance distinction. Trap: children learned the aggression regardless of the model's consequences; consequences only changed whether they performed it.

Tajfel (1971) — the minimal group paradigm

Q: how little does it take to produce in-group bias? What: strangers were split on a trivial basis (Klee vs Kandinsky paintings, or a coin toss), then allocated points to anonymous in-group and out-group members. Found: people favoured their own group though groups were arbitrary, with no prior contact, self-interest or conflict, and would even sacrifice absolute in-group gain to maximise the difference. Matters: the empirical bedrock of social identity theory. Trap: minimal group = bias with no real competition; contrast with Sherif's realistic conflict.

Sherif (1954) — the Robbers Cave study

Q: what creates and cures intergroup hostility? What: at a boys' summer camp, two groups (Eagles, Rattlers) were put into competition for prizes, breeding hostility, then given superordinate goals requiring cooperation (a broken water supply, a stuck truck). Found: competition manufactured prejudice; shared goals dissolved it; mere contact alone did not. Matters: the field demonstration of realistic conflict theory and why the contact hypothesis requires cooperation toward common goals. Trap: it was the superordinate goal, not contact by itself, that reduced hostility.

Dutton & Aron (1974) — the shaky-bridge study

Q: can arousal from one source be mislabelled as attraction? What: men crossed either a high, swaying suspension bridge (fear arousal) or a low stable bridge, where an attractive female interviewer gave them her number. Found: men from the scary bridge were far more likely to call and rated her more attractive. Matters: misattribution of arousal, an application of Schachter & Singer's two-factor theory of emotion to attraction. Trap: the arousal was really fear; participants relabelled it.

LaPiere (1934) — attitudes vs behaviour

Q: do stated attitudes predict actual behaviour? What: LaPiere toured the US with a young Chinese couple and recorded their treatment at ~250 hotels/restaurants; they were refused only once. Months later he wrote asking whether the same establishments would serve Chinese guests; over 90% said no. Found: a glaring gap between expressed prejudice and real behaviour. Matters: set off decades of work resolved by Fishbein & Ajzen (attitudes predict behaviour through intentions). Trap: LaPiere = the attitude–behaviour gap; the resolution (intentions, specificity) is the theory-of-reasoned-action line.

25. The MCQ traps, collected

  • Method core: experiment = the only design proving causation (manipulate IV, measure DV, random assignment). Correlation ≠ causation (third-variable + directionality). Mediator = the why; moderator = the when/for whom.
  • Attention selection: Broadbent = early/physical; Treisman = attenuation; Deutsch-Norman = late/meaning; Lavie = depends on load. Cocktail-party effect supports late/attenuation, not Broadbent. Feature search pops out (parallel); conjunction search is serial.
  • Memory: serial position = primacy (LTM) + recency (STM); a delay wipes recency. Anterograde (no new memories, H.M.) vs retrograde (no old). Encoding specificity = Tulving.
  • Conditioning: variable-ratio = highest, most extinction-resistant. Rescorla-Wagner = prediction error, explains blocking. Bobo doll = learning ≠ performance.
  • Emotion order: James-Lange (body first) · Cannon-Bard (simultaneous) · Schachter-Singer (body + label).
  • Availability = ease of recall · representativeness = fit to a prototype (neglects base rates) · anchoring = stuck on a first value. All = Tversky & Kahneman.
  • The attribution swap: FAE (Ross) = over-blame others' character · actor–observer (Jones & Nisbett) = my acts = situation, your acts = character · self-serving = my success = me, my failure = the situation. Models in order: Heider → Jones & Davis → Kelley (consensus, consistency, distinctiveness).
  • Self-discrepancy (Higgins): ideal-self gap → dejection (sad); ought-self gap → agitation (anxious).
  • Self cluster: social comparison = Festinger · self-perception = Bem · self-monitoring = Snyder · self-efficacy = Bandura · self-handicapping = Berglas & Jones · self-verification = Swann · self-affirmation = Steele · spotlight = Gilovich · BIRGing = Cialdini · TMT = Greenberg, Pyszczynski & Solomon.
  • Dissonance vs self-perception: Festinger = arousal/discomfort, strong prior attitude; Bem = no arousal, mere inference, weak attitude.
  • $1 > $20 (insufficient justification, Festinger & Carlsmith): less reward = more attitude change. Effort justification = Aronson & Mills. Balance theory = Heider.
  • Persuasion routes: central/systematic vs peripheral/heuristic. ELM = Petty & Cacioppo; HSM = Chaiken; Yale "who-what-whom" = Hovland; inoculation = McGuire; reactance = Brehm; attitude–behaviour gap = LaPiere; intention model = Fishbein & Ajzen; sleeper effect & functions of attitudes = Hovland / Katz.
  • Prejudice sources: social identity / minimal group = Tajfel (& Turner) · realistic conflict / Robbers Cave = Sherif · authoritarian personality (F-scale) = Adorno. Reduction: contact hypothesis (four conditions) = Allport; jigsaw classroom = Aronson; recategorisation = Gaertner & Dovidio.
  • Stereotype cognition: illusory correlation = Hamilton & Gifford · out-group homogeneity · stereotype threat = Steele & Aronson · stereotype content model (warmth × competence) = Fiske.   • The "who studied liking" cluster: Byrne = similarity · Zajonc = mere exposure · Festinger = propinquity · Sternberg = triangular love · Rusbult = investment model · Dutton & Aron = misattributed arousal (bridge). Evolutionary mate preferences = Buss (vs Eagly & Wood); dissolution phases = Duck.
  • Conformity: Asch = unambiguous line task (normative) · Sherif = autokinetic, ambiguous (informational) · Deutsch & Gerard named the two motives · Milgram = obedience (~65%, agentic state) · Moscovici = minority influence via consistency.
  • Foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face: FITD = small→big (Freedman & Fraser, consistency); DITF = big→small (Cialdini, reciprocal concession); lowballing = change the deal after the yes; that's-not-all = sweeten before the yes; reciprocity norm = Gouldner.
  • Helping: bystander effect & 5-step model = Latané & Darley; crowd failures = pluralistic ignorance (interpret) + diffusion of responsibility (take responsibility). Batson = true altruism (empathy); Cialdini (negative-state relief) and Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward) = egoistic. Kin selection = Hamilton, reciprocal altruism = Trivers.
  • Aggression: frustration–aggression = Dollard et al.; reformulation + weapons effect = Berkowitz (& LePage); social learning/Bobo = Bandura; GAM = Anderson & Bushman; excitation-transfer = Zillmann; cognitive-neoassociation = Berkowitz; threatened egotism = Baumeister & Bushman; hostile attribution bias = Dodge; culture of honour = Nisbett & Cohen; catharsis is a myth.
  • Groups: facilitation (Triplett/Zajonc, dominant-response) vs loafing (Latané/Ringelmann, hidden effort); evaluation apprehension = Cottrell; deindividuation = Festinger coined / Zimbardo demonstrated; group polarisation (Moscovici & Zavalloni) generalises risky shift (Stoner); groupthink = Janis; tragedy of the commons = Hardin; GRIT = Osgood; distributive (outcome) vs procedural (process) justice; transactional vs transformational leadership = Bass.
