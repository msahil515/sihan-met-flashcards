1. Before the Schools: Roots and the Big Questions

Psychology did not appear from nowhere in 1879. For more than two thousand years its questions belonged to philosophy, and its tools, later, to physiology. The schools you are about to study are best understood as rival answers to a handful of very old questions that psychology inherited and then tried to settle with experiment.

The philosophical inheritance

  • Mind and body — are they two different substances or one? René Descartes' dualism (interactionism via the pineal gland) said two; the monists said one. This is the mind-body problem.
  • Where knowledge comes from — the rationalists (Descartes, Leibniz, Kant) held that the mind brings innate structure to experience; the British empiricists (John Locke's tabula rasa, the blank slate, plus Berkeley, Hume, and the Mills) held that all knowledge is written by experience. The empiricists also gave psychology associationism, the idea that complex ideas are built by associating simple ones, which became the raw material of both structuralism and behaviorism.
  • Nature versus nurture — the same debate in modern dress, still alive in every school.

The physiological inheritance

In the nineteenth century, physiology made the mind measurable for the first time. Johannes Müller's doctrine of specific nerve energies, Hermann von Helmholtz's measurement of the speed of the nerve impulse and his work on vision and hearing, Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner's psychophysics (Fechner's 1860 Elements of Psychophysics gave the first true mental measurement, relating stimulus intensity to sensation), and Paul Broca and Carl Wernicke's localisation of speech in the brain all showed that mental events have lawful, measurable physical correlates. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution (1859) supplied the other great engine: it made function and adaptation central and licensed the study of animals, infants, and individual differences (taken up by his cousin Francis Galton, founder of mental testing and the study of individual differences).

High-yield

Fechner (psychophysics, 1860) and Helmholtz made the mind measurable; Darwin (1859) made it functional and adaptive; Wundt (1879) made it a laboratory science. These four names are the bridge from philosophy to scientific psychology, and they explain why the very first quarrel, structuralism versus functionalism, was about structure versus adaptation.

Memory hook

Read the schools as a chain of rebellions: Structuralism (study the contents of consciousness) provokes Functionalism (no, study its purpose) and Gestalt (no, study organised wholes); Behaviorism throws consciousness out entirely; Psychoanalysis dives under it to the unconscious; Humanism answers both Freud and the behaviorists with the whole, free person; and the Cognitive revolution brings the mind back as an information processor. Indian psychology runs alongside the whole story with a different goal: not explaining the mind but transcending it.

2. Structuralism

Psychology as we know it began on a single, much-tested date: 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt founded the first formal psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig, Germany. This act is the conventional birth of psychology as an independent science: it stepped out from under philosophy (which had asked about the mind for centuries through reason and argument) and from under physiology (which studied the body but not experience as experience). Wundt's claim was bold: the contents of conscious experience could be studied with the same controlled, measured, repeatable methods that physics and physiology used. He thereby created experimental psychology and trained the first generation of psychologists, many of whom carried the lab model across Europe and to America.

Wundt's program was to study the structure of conscious experience: to break down the mind into its basic elements and discover the laws by which they combine. His chosen method was introspection, but a disciplined, laboratory version of it. He distinguished immediate experience (the raw, direct datum of consciousness as it is happening, the proper object of psychology) from mediate experience (experience used as a means to know something else about the outside world, which is what other sciences study). A psychologist, for Wundt, reports the immediate experience itself, not the object it points to.

Definition

Introspection is the systematic self-observation and reporting of one's own conscious experience (sensations, images, feelings) under controlled conditions. In Wundt's lab it required trained observers giving standardized reports to a measured stimulus, not casual self-reflection.

It is a common error to label Wundt a "structuralist." Wundt's own position is better called voluntarism: he held that the mind is active, not a passive mosaic of elements. Through a central process he called apperception, the mind selects, attends to, and creatively organizes elementary contents into a meaningful unified experience, an act of will (voluntas). Pure, systematic structuralism, the doctrine that consciousness is to be analyzed into static elements and nothing more, is really the creation of Wundt's student, Edward Bradford Titchener.

Beyond his lab method, Wundt's experimental work included mental chronometry: using reaction-time measurements (an idea he took from the astronomer's "personal equation" and from Donders) to time mental operations such as discrimination and choice. Importantly, Wundt did not think introspection could reach the higher mental processes, language, myth, religion, social custom. These, he argued, must be studied historically and culturally in a second great enterprise he called Völkerpsychologie (folk, cultural, or "people's" psychology), to which he devoted ten volumes late in life.

Titchener and the systematizing of Structuralism

Titchener, an Englishman, took the Leipzig training to Cornell University in the United States, where he coined the term structuralism and radicalized the method into analytic introspection. Trained observers learned to report the raw elements of an experience while strictly avoiding the stimulus error, the mistake of describing the meaningful object ("I see an apple") rather than the bare sensory contents (a red, round, bright patch). For Titchener the mind contained three irreducible elements:

  • Sensations, the elements of perceptions (from sense organs);
  • Images, the elements of ideas (representing experiences not present);
  • Affections, the elements of emotions (feelings such as pleasantness/unpleasantness).

Each sensation could be described by its attributes: quality, intensity, duration, and clarity (attensity), with extensity added for some modalities. Titchener also offered a context theory of meaning: meaning is nothing mystical but simply the context of associated sensations and images that a core sensation calls up.

The discipline set itself three questions: the what (analyze consciousness into its elements), the how (specify how those elements combine, via the laws of association and synthesis), and the why (explain the experience). Wundt allowed a causal "why," and physiological explanation entered through reaction time; Titchener was stricter, treating physiology only as the parallel substrate and resisting a functional "why."

Why Structuralism died

Structuralism was attacked and ultimately collapsed for several reasons. Its method was the central weakness: introspection proved unreliable and unreplicable, because different trained labs reported different elements for the same stimulus. The decisive blow was the imageless thought controversy: the Würzburg school under Oswald Külpe reported that thinking can occur without any sensory image, directly contradicting Titchener's claim that every conscious content reduces to sensations and images. Critics also found the school far too narrow: by limiting psychology to the introspection of the normal adult human mind, it excluded animals, children, and the abnormal, and it produced little of practical use, earning the charge of being sterile. When Titchener died in 1927, structuralism essentially died with him, but it had done its historic job: it established psychology as an experimental laboratory science.

Memory hook

"Wundt Won the lab; Titchener Titled the school." Wundt won psychology its first lab (1879); Titchener gave structuralism its title (and its three elements: Sensations, Images, Affections = "SIA").

3. Functionalism

While German laboratories dissected consciousness into elements, a very different psychology grew up in the United States as a reaction against structuralism. Where structuralism asked what consciousness is made of, functionalism asked what consciousness is for: what does the mind do, and how does it help the organism survive and adapt? This shift was no accident. The deepest influence was Charles Darwin: if bodily organs evolve because they serve a function in adaptation, then so might mind and consciousness. The practical, problem-solving temper of American life reinforced the move; American psychologists wanted a psychology that worked, one applicable to schools, clinics, and industry.

Trap

The single most tested contrast in this unit: structuralism studies the WHAT (the structure / contents / elements of consciousness, analysis), whereas functionalism studies the WHY / WHAT-FOR (the function, purpose, and adaptive use of mental processes). Structure vs function; contents vs operations. Do not reverse them.

William James, the forerunner

William James is called the "father of American psychology." His monumental Principles of Psychology (1890) framed the questions a whole generation would pursue. James argued that consciousness is not a string of static elements but a stream of consciousness: continuous (no gaps), constantly changing, personal (belonging to a self), and above all selective, attending to some things and ignoring others, which is precisely its adaptive function. Among his lasting ideas are the James-Lange theory of emotion (we feel afraid because we tremble and run; bodily change precedes the felt emotion), his analysis of the self into the I (the knower) and the Me (the known, with its material, social, and spiritual aspects), his classic treatment of habit as the "flywheel of society," and his philosophy of pragmatism (an idea's truth lies in its practical consequences). James, however, founded no formal school; he was more an inspiring precursor than a system-builder, and he largely abandoned psychology for philosophy.

The Chicago school, the formal founding

Functionalism as an organized movement is usually dated to John Dewey's 1896 paper, "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology," the field's recognized founding document. Dewey attacked the reflex arc (the stimulus-response unit) for being artificially "chopped up" into separate stimulus, central process, and response. In real behavior, he argued, these form one continuous coordinated act: perception and movement are bound together in a single adaptive circuit, and the "stimulus" and "response" are only roles within an ongoing whole. James Rowland Angell then organized and championed the Chicago school; his 1906 APA presidential address, "The Province of Functional Psychology," laid out its program explicitly. Harvey Carr later consolidated and matured the school.

The Columbia school

A second center grew at Columbia University, more applied and individual-differences oriented. James McKeen Cattell (who had studied under Wundt yet rejected his approach) pioneered mental tests, in fact coined the term "mental test," and championed the study of individual differences. Robert S. Woodworth developed dynamic psychology and inserted the organism into the equation, expanding S-R into S-O-R (Stimulus-Organism-Response): the organism's drives and state determine the response. Edward L. Thorndike studied animal learning, formulated connectionism and the law of effect (responses followed by satisfaction are stamped in), and in doing so built a clear bridge to behaviorism.

The functionalist program, taken as a whole, studied mental operations rather than mental contents, treated the mind-body as one functioning whole serving adaptation, and prized individual differences, applied psychology, education (Dewey's lasting influence on schooling), and mental testing. Because it asked what mind is for rather than what it is made of, it had no reason to exclude anyone: it threw psychology's doors open to the study of animals, children, and the abnormal, and to the applied fields, clinical, educational, and industrial psychology, that define so much of the discipline today.

High-yield

Pin the two anchors: William James = forerunner, Principles of Psychology (1890). John Dewey = "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology" (1896) is functionalism's founding paper. Chicago = Dewey, Angell, Carr; Columbia = Cattell, Woodworth, Thorndike.

Finally, note that functionalism did not "die" the way structuralism did. It had no single dramatic refutation; instead it dissolved into the mainstream of American psychology, becoming so widely accepted that it ceased to be a distinct school. In the process it gave birth to behaviorism (through Thorndike's animal learning and the focus on observable function) and seeded the entire enterprise of applied and clinical psychology. Its very success was its disappearance.

Memory hook

"Function = For." Functionalism asks what the mind is For, born For-Darwin (adaptation), and is remembered For opening psychology to applied fields. People hook: "James Dreamed, Dewey Arced, Angell Announced" (James inspired, Dewey wrote the Reflex Arc, Angell announced the program).

4. Gestalt Psychology

In the opening years of the twentieth century, while Wundt's structuralists were busy dissecting consciousness into sensory elements and the behaviorists were preparing to throw consciousness out altogether, a third movement arose in Germany that rejected both. This was Gestalt psychology. The German word Gestalt has no exact English equivalent: it means roughly "form," "shape," "configuration," or "organized whole." The Gestaltists argued that the mind does not register a mosaic of separate sensations that are later glued together; rather, it grasps organized wholes directly and immediately. Their rallying cry was that the whole is different from the sum of its parts.

Definition

Gestalt: an organized whole or configuration whose properties cannot be derived from, or predicted by, the properties of its isolated parts. The melody survives transposition to a new key even though every individual note has changed: the form, not the elements, is what we perceive.

A note of precision that examiners love: the popular phrase "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts" is a loose rendering. The more faithful translation of Wertheimer's claim is that the whole is different from the sum of its parts. The organized whole possesses emergent properties, qualities that simply do not exist at the level of the components and therefore cannot be predicted by adding them up.

The founding demonstration: the phi phenomenon (1912)

The movement is conventionally dated to 1912, when Max Wertheimer published his study of apparent motion, the effect he labelled the phi phenomenon. The set-up is simple: two stationary lights are flashed in rapid succession, first one and then, a fraction of a second later, the other. What the observer sees is not two separate flashes but a single light moving from the first position to the second (this is the same principle that makes a strip of still film frames look like continuous motion). The crucial point is that the motion exists only in the perceived whole. There is no movement in either light considered alone; movement is a property of the configuration. Elementism, which insists that experience is built up from independent sensory atoms, cannot account for this. Wertheimer's collaborators and observers in these experiments were Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, and the three became the founders of the school.

High-yield

Phi phenomenon, Max Wertheimer, 1912 is the founding study of Gestalt psychology. Apparent motion proves that the organized whole has properties (here, motion) not present in its parts, refuting structuralist elementism.

The laws of perceptual organization

The Gestaltists' most enduring legacy is their catalogue of the principles by which the perceptual system organizes a raw sensory field into coherent objects and groups. Presiding over all of them is the law of Prägnanz.

Definition

Prägnanz (the "law of good figure," "law of simplicity," or minimum principle): of all the ways a stimulus could be organized, we perceive the one that is the simplest, most stable, most regular, and most economical. It is the master principle from which the other laws follow.

Insight learning (Köhler)

Köhler, marooned on Tenerife during the First World War, studied chimpanzees, the most famous of whom was named Sultan. Placed in a cage with a banana hung out of reach and some boxes and sticks lying about, Sultan would, after a period of apparent contemplation, suddenly stack the boxes or fit two sticks together to rake in the fruit. Köhler called this insight: a sudden reorganization of the perceptual field in which the relations among the elements abruptly fall into place, the famous "aha!" moment. Crucially, once achieved, the solution was retained and could be transferred to new but structurally similar problems.

Trap

Köhler's insight learning vs. Thorndike's trial-and-error. Thorndike's cats in the puzzle box learned gradually and blindly, by random fumbling that was slowly stamped in by reward (a smooth learning curve). Köhler's apes solved problems suddenly and intelligently, by grasping relationships (an abrupt, all-or-none jump). Insight = perceptual reorganization, not incremental association. Exam items love to swap these two.

Isomorphism

To explain why we perceive organized wholes, Köhler proposed the doctrine of psychophysical isomorphism: conscious perceptual experience and the underlying processes in the brain share the same structural form. The organization we experience is not imposed on a chaotic brain; it mirrors a corresponding organization in the cortical "field." Perceived form and neural form are, in this sense, isomorphic (literally "same shape").

Kurt Lewin: field theory, the bridge to social psychology

Kurt Lewin extended the Gestalt insight, that elements are governed by the whole field they belong to, from perception to motivation, personality, and social behavior. He is the bridge from Gestalt perception to modern social and organizational psychology. His central construct was the life space: the totality of all the facts (needs, goals, barriers, possibilities) that influence a person at a given moment. From this came his celebrated formula:

B = f(P, E) — behavior is a function of the person and the environment, taken together as an interacting field. Memorize the formula exactly.

  • Tension systems: an unmet goal creates a state of psychological tension that presses toward resolution.
  • The Zeigarnik effect: Lewin's student Bluma Zeigarnik showed that uncompleted tasks are remembered better than completed ones, because the unresolved tension system keeps them active in memory.
  • Conflict types: approach-approach (two desirable goals), avoidance-avoidance (two undesirable options, "between a rock and a hard place"), and approach-avoidance (one goal with both attractive and repellent features).
  • Group dynamics and action research: Lewin founded the systematic study of groups and championed research conducted in real-world settings to produce social change, summed up in his maxim, "There is nothing so practical as a good theory."

Legacy

Gestalt psychology founded the scientific study of perceptual organization and its laws are still taught essentially intact. Its emphasis on holistic mental structure fed directly into the rise of cognitive psychology and into research on problem-solving and insight. Through Lewin it seeded much of modern social and organizational psychology.

Trap

Do not confuse the Gestalt psychology of Wertheimer, Köhler, and Koffka (a school of perception and cognition) with Fritz Perls' Gestalt therapy (a humanistic-existential psychotherapy of the here-and-now, the empty chair, awareness). They share only the word "gestalt" and a loose holistic spirit. Perls was not one of the founders, and Gestalt therapy is not an application of the laws of perceptual organization.

5. Behaviorism

Behaviorism dominated American psychology from roughly 1913 to the 1960s and remains, even now, the single most heavily tested school on Indian clinical entrances. It grew out of a frustration with structuralism and its method of introspection, which produced unreliable, unverifiable reports of private experience. The behaviorists' radical answer was to throw out the mind altogether as a subject matter and rebuild psychology as an objective natural science of behavior, governed by laws of learning that could be observed in a laboratory and applied to predict and control what organisms do.

Definition

Behaviorism is the school holding that psychology should study only observable, measurable behavior (stimulus-response relations), not the mind or consciousness. Its goal is to predict and control behavior, and its core mechanism is learning through conditioning.

5.1 The behaviorist manifesto: John B. Watson

John B. Watson (1878-1958) is the founder of behaviorism as a formal school. In 1913 he published "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" in Psychological Review, a paper so programmatic it is simply called the "Behaviorist Manifesto." Watson declared that psychology is "a purely objective experimental branch of natural science," whose theoretical goal is the prediction and control of behavior. He rejected consciousness, mental states, and introspection as outside the bounds of science, and demanded that psychology deal only in observable stimuli and responses.

Watson's stance is best labelled methodological behaviorism: he did not deny that inner events exist, but insisted they are scientifically useless because they cannot be publicly observed. He was a fierce environmentalist, denying that heredity shapes personality and ability. His most quoted line captures this radical environmentalism: "Give me a dozen healthy infants, well-formed, and my own specified world to bring them up in and I'll guarantee to take any one at random and train him to become any type of specialist I might select... regardless of his talents, penchants, tendencies, abilities, vocations, and race of his ancestors."

In 1920, with his student and later wife Rosalie Rayner, Watson ran the famous "Little Albert" experiment. A roughly 11-month-old infant ("Albert B.") was shown a white rat (initially a neutral stimulus he did not fear). Each time he reached for it, the experimenters struck a steel bar behind his head, producing a loud, frightening noise (UCS). After repeated pairings, Albert came to fear the white rat alone (a conditioned emotional response), and the fear generalized to other furry white objects, a rabbit, a dog, cotton wool, even a Santa Claus mask. The study demonstrated that emotions like fear can be classically conditioned in humans, anchoring Watson's environmentalism. (It is also a standard ethics case study: there was no evidence Albert's fear was ever extinguished.) Watson left academia in 1920 after his affair with Rayner became a scandal and went on to a successful career in advertising, applying conditioning principles to consumer behavior.

High-yield

Three dates examiners love: Watson's 1913 manifesto ("Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It"), Pavlov's classical conditioning (work from the late 1890s-1900s), and Little Albert 1920 (Watson & Rayner). Watson = founder of behaviorism; goal = prediction and control.

5.2 Classical conditioning: Ivan Pavlov

Ivan Pavlov (1849-1936) was a Russian physiologist who won the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on digestion, not for psychology. While studying salivation in dogs, he noticed they began to salivate before food touched their mouths, at the sight of the feeder or the sound of footsteps. He called these anticipatory responses "psychic secretions" and turned to studying them systematically, founding classical (Pavlovian/respondent) conditioning.

In classical conditioning a neutral stimulus (a bell, originally producing no salivation) is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus (food) that automatically triggers an unconditioned response (salivation). After enough pairings, the bell alone elicits salivation, now a conditioned response to a conditioned stimulus.

Several lawful phenomena follow:

  • Acquisition — the building of the CS-CR association across pairings. Learning is strongest when the CS slightly precedes the UCS (forward/delayed conditioning); contiguity (close timing of CS and UCS) is essential, and short CS-UCS intervals work best.
  • Extinction — presenting the CS repeatedly without the UCS weakens and eventually abolishes the CR.
  • Spontaneous recovery — after extinction and a rest interval, the CR reappears (weakly) when the CS is presented, showing extinction suppresses rather than erases learning.
  • Generalization — stimuli similar to the CS (a higher-pitched bell) also evoke the CR.
  • Discrimination — the organism learns to respond only to the specific CS and not to similar stimuli (the opposite of generalization).
  • Higher-order (second-order) conditioning — a well-established CS can itself act like a UCS to condition a new neutral stimulus (e.g., pairing a light with the already-conditioned bell so the light alone evokes salivation).

Watson explicitly built on Pavlov: Little Albert is classical conditioning of a fear response in a human, with the loud noise as UCS and the rat as CS.

5.3 Edwin Guthrie: contiguity and one-trial learning

Edwin Guthrie (1886-1959) offered the most economical learning theory: the law of contiguity. A stimulus pattern that accompanies a response will, on its recurrence, tend to be followed by that response, no reinforcement required. Crucially, Guthrie argued the full-strength S-R bond is formed in a single pairing (one-trial learning); apparent gradual learning is just the building up of many independent stimulus-response connections. His practical legacy is the idea that breaking a habit means attaching the old cue to a new, incompatible response.

5.4 Edward Thorndike: connectionism and the Law of Effect

Edward L. Thorndike (1874-1949) studied how animals learn by placing hungry cats in a puzzle box from which they had to escape (by pressing a lever or pulling a loop) to reach food. Cats did not "reason" their way out; they thrashed about until they stumbled on the correct response, and over trials the time to escape steadily fell. He called this trial-and-error learning and his theory connectionism, because learning is the strengthening of bonds (connections) between stimuli and responses.

Thorndike's laws:

  • Law of Effect — responses followed by a satisfying state of affairs are "stamped in" (strengthened); responses followed by discomfort are "stamped out." This is the cornerstone law.
  • Law of Exercise — connections are strengthened by use (repetition) and weakened by disuse. (He later softened this, recognising practice without feedback does little.)
  • Law of Readiness — learning depends on the organism's preparedness to act; acting when ready is satisfying, being blocked is annoying.
High-yield

Thorndike's Law of Effect is the direct conceptual ancestor of Skinner's operant conditioning. "Satisfying consequences strengthen behavior" (Thorndike) becomes "reinforcement increases behavior" (Skinner). Note that Thorndike is sometimes classified under functionalism rather than behaviorism, because of his focus on the adaptive function of learning.

5.5 B. F. Skinner: radical behaviorism and operant conditioning

B. F. Skinner (1904-1990) is the most influential behaviorist. Unlike Watson, his radical behaviorism did not deny private events (thoughts, feelings): it accepts they exist but treats them as behavior to be explained, never as inner causes of action. Causes always lie in the environment and the organism's reinforcement history. Skinner distinguished respondent behavior (reflexive, Pavlovian, elicited by stimuli) from operant behavior, which is emitted and "operates on" the environment, controlled by its consequences.

He invented the operant chamber ("Skinner box"), in which a rat presses a lever or a pigeon pecks a key to obtain food. The unit of analysis is the three-term contingency or A-B-C: an Antecedent (discriminative stimulus that signals availability of reinforcement), the Behavior (response), and the Consequence (reinforcer or punisher) that changes the future probability of that behavior.

Definition

Reinforcement = any consequence that increases the future probability of the behavior it follows. Punishment = any consequence that decreases it. "Positive" and "negative" do not mean good/bad: positive = add a stimulus, negative = remove a stimulus.

Trap

Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment. This is the most-missed item on these exams. Negative reinforcement removes an aversive stimulus and therefore increases behavior (escape/avoidance). Punishment decreases behavior. If the behavior goes up, it is reinforcement, full stop, regardless of "negative."

Memory hook

Two questions decode the 2x2 every time: (1) Did the behavior go UP or DOWN? Up = Reinforcement, Down = Punishment. (2) Was something ADDED or TAKEN AWAY? Added = Positive, Removed = Negative. Solve them in that order and you can never confuse the cells.

More Skinnerian machinery:

  • Primary vs secondary reinforcers — primary reinforcers satisfy biological needs innately (food, water, warmth); secondary (conditioned) reinforcers acquire their power by association (money, tokens, grades, praise).
  • Shaping (successive approximation) — reinforcing closer and closer approximations to a target behavior to build complex responses that would never occur spontaneously.

Skinner's principles powered behavior modification: the token economy (institutional patients earn tokens, secondary reinforcers, for target behaviors, later exchanged for privileges), programmed instruction, and a wide range of clinical and educational interventions. In 1957 he published Verbal Behavior, arguing language too is learned operant behavior. In 1959 Noam Chomsky's scathing review demolished it, arguing the speed and generativity of children's language acquisition could not be explained by reinforcement and required innate cognitive structures. That critique is widely cited as a turning point that helped launch the cognitive revolution.

5.6 Neo-behaviorism: the S-O-R turn

The neo-behaviorists kept the objective method but inserted the Organism (O) between Stimulus and Response, allowing unobservable intervening variables as theoretical constructs (S-O-R rather than bare S-R).

  • Clark Hull (1884-1952) built a formal hypothetico-deductive system of learning expressed in equations. His drive-reduction theory held that learning occurs when a response reduces a biological drive (hunger, thirst), restoring homeostasis. His central formula, sEr (reaction potential) = sHr (habit strength) x D (drive) and further terms, treats habit strength and drive as intervening variables linking stimulus to response.
  • Edward Tolman (1886-1959) championed purposive (or cognitive) behaviorism. His rats in mazes showed latent learning: rats that explored a maze without reward still learned its layout, showing a sudden drop in errors once reward was introduced, proving learning had occurred without reinforcement. He argued animals build internal cognitive maps of their environment. Tolman effectively cracked open the "black box," reintroducing internal representation and purpose, and prefigured cognitive psychology.
  • Kenneth Spence (1907-1967), Hull's foremost collaborator, refined the Hull-Spence theory, especially work on the role of incentive motivation (K) and discrimination learning.

5.7 Social learning: Albert Bandura, the bridge to cognitive

Albert Bandura (1925-2021) showed that much learning occurs without direct reinforcement, through observation and modeling. In the Bobo doll experiment (1961), children who watched an adult aggressively beat an inflatable Bobo doll later imitated that aggression, while those who saw a non-aggressive model did not. Children also learned vicariously: when a model was rewarded for aggression, imitation rose; when punished, it fell (vicarious reinforcement/punishment).

Bandura identified four processes required for observational learning: Attention (notice the model), Retention (remember the behavior), Reproduction (physically reproduce it), and Motivation (a reason, often vicarious reinforcement, to perform it). He proposed reciprocal determinism, the continuous mutual influence of person (cognition), behavior, and environment, and later developed the influential concept of self-efficacy, one's belief in one's capability to execute the actions needed to attain goals. Because Bandura insists on internal cognitive mediators (expectations, beliefs), his social-cognitive theory is the explicit bridge from behaviorism to cognitive psychology.

5.8 The clinical fruit: Joseph Wolpe

Joseph Wolpe (1915-1997) translated classical-conditioning principles into therapy. His systematic desensitization treats phobias and anxiety by pairing a graded hierarchy of feared situations with deep relaxation, exploiting reciprocal inhibition: because relaxation and anxiety are physiologically incompatible, a relaxation response that occurs in the presence of the feared stimulus inhibits and weakens the anxiety response. This counter-conditioning is the direct clinical descendant of Pavlov and Watson, and a foundation stone of behavior therapy.

Trap

Classical vs operant, the #1 tested confusion. Classical conditioning is involuntary/respondent: an antecedent stimulus (CS) elicits an automatic response, and the organism is passive (Pavlov's dog, Little Albert). Operant conditioning is voluntary/emitted: the behavior operates on the environment and is controlled by its consequences (Skinner's lever-press). Rule of thumb: if the key event comes before the response and triggers it, it's classical; if it comes after and changes its future probability, it's operant.

6. Psychoanalysis & the Psychodynamic School

Psychoanalysis is the first force in psychology, the school that put the unconscious mind at the centre of mental life. Born in late nineteenth-century Vienna, it began not in a laboratory but in a clinic, with patients whose suffering had no visible bodily cause. Where Wundt and the structuralists studied the contents of consciousness, Freud insisted that the bulk of the mind lies beneath consciousness, and that this hidden territory drives our thoughts, symptoms, dreams, and slips of the tongue. From this single idea grew a sprawling tradition: Freud's own psychoanalysis, the breakaway "neo-Freudians," ego psychology, object relations, and self psychology. For Indian clinical entrance exams this section is extremely high-yield: examiners love to test who-said-what and to bait you with confusable theorists.

6.1 Sigmund Freud — the founder

Freud (1856-1939), a Viennese neurologist, did not set out to build a school of psychology. His path ran through hysteria, a disorder in which patients showed paralyses, blindness, or convulsions with no organic basis. His older colleague Josef Breuer treated a famous patient, Anna O. (real name Bertha Pappenheim), who found that her symptoms eased when she talked through their origins under hypnosis. She called it the "talking cure" (and, wryly, "chimney-sweeping"). The emotional discharge that accompanied this verbalisation Breuer and Freud named catharsis. Freud gradually abandoned hypnosis in favour of free association (saying whatever comes to mind, however trivial or shameful), which became the basic technique of psychoanalysis.

Definition

The unconscious is the vast region of the mind containing wishes, memories, and impulses that are kept out of awareness yet continue to influence behaviour. For Freud it is dynamic: kept hidden by active forces (repression), not merely forgotten.

The topographic model describes the mind by level of awareness. The conscious is what we are aware of right now; the preconscious holds material easily brought to mind (your phone number); the unconscious holds repressed material that cannot reach awareness directly. Freud later found this model too limited and introduced the structural model, which divides the mind by function into three agencies:

  • Id — present from birth, entirely unconscious, the reservoir of instinctual drives. It runs on the pleasure principle (demand immediate gratification) and uses primary process thinking (illogical, image-based, wish-fulfilling).
  • Ego — the executive, "I." It develops to mediate between id, reality, and superego. It runs on the reality principle (delay gratification until a safe, appropriate object is found) and uses secondary process thinking (logical, planful).
  • Superego — the moral agency, internalised around age 5 from parents and culture. It has two parts: the conscience (punishes with guilt for what is "bad") and the ego-ideal (rewards with pride for living up to standards).

The fuel for all this is libido, the psychic energy of the life instinct (Eros). Freud later paired it with a death instinct (Thanatos). Mental life is a constant dynamic struggle among the three agencies for control of this finite energy, which is why the school is called "psychodynamic."

High-yield

Memorise the mapping: Id, pleasure principle, primary process; Ego, reality principle, secondary process; Superego, morality (conscience + ego-ideal). And keep the two models straight: the topographic model classifies by awareness (conscious/preconscious/unconscious); the structural model classifies by function (id/ego/superego). The ego and superego operate at all three topographic levels.

6.4 Dreams, slips, transference, and resistance

Freud called dreams the "royal road to the unconscious." Every dream is a disguised wish fulfilment. The manifest content is the dream as remembered; the latent content is the hidden unconscious wish. The transformation of latent into manifest content is the dream work, accomplished through condensation (fusing several ideas into one image), displacement (shifting emotional weight onto a trivial detail), symbolisation, and secondary revision (smoothing the dream into a story). Everyday parapraxes ("Freudian slips") — slips of the tongue, forgetting names, mislaying objects — likewise reveal unconscious wishes.

In therapy two phenomena are central. Transference is the patient's unconscious redirection onto the analyst of feelings originally felt toward significant figures (usually parents); working it through is the engine of cure. Resistance is everything the patient does to block the progress of analysis (falling silent, arriving late, "forgetting" the appointment), and it signals that the work is touching something defended.

6.6 The neo-Freudians and dissenters

Freud's circle fractured. Several followers rejected his emphasis on infantile sexuality and built their own systems, while others extended his work into new territory.

Carl Jung — Analytical Psychology. Jung's break was the deepest. He rejected Freud's view of libido as purely sexual, redefining it as a general life energy. He divided the unconscious into the personal unconscious (an individual's repressed material) and the collective unconscious, a deeper layer inherited by all humans and shared across cultures. Its contents are archetypes, universal primordial images: the persona (the social mask), the shadow (the dark, disowned self), the anima/animus (the inner feminine in men / masculine in women), and the self (the archetype of wholeness and unity). Jung coined introversion/extraversion, and described the lifelong process of becoming whole as individuation.

Alfred Adler — Individual Psychology. Adler held that the master motive is not sex but the striving for superiority (mastery, completion), arising from a universal childhood sense of helplessness. When this sense becomes pathological it forms an inferiority complex; we cope through compensation. Each person develops a unique style of life to pursue their goals, guided by fictional finalism (imagined future ideals that motivate present action). Mental health is marked by social interest (German Gemeinschaftsgefühl, community feeling). Adler also stressed birth order as a shaper of personality.

Karen Horney. Horney rooted neurosis in social rather than biological causes. The child who feels unsafe develops basic anxiety: a feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world. To cope, people develop ten neurotic needs, which cluster into three interpersonal trends: moving toward people (compliance), moving against people (aggression/hostility), and moving away from people (detachment). She is famous for rejecting penis envy as a cultural artefact of male-dominated society, countering it with the idea of "womb envy" (men's envy of women's capacity to bear life).

Erik Erikson — Ego Psychology. Erikson extended Freud across the whole lifespan, replacing psychosexual stages with eight psychosocial stages, each a crisis between two outcomes, unfolding by the epigenetic principle (a built-in ground plan). He coined the term identity crisis.

  1. Trust vs Mistrust (infancy)
  2. Autonomy vs Shame & Doubt (toddler)
  3. Initiative vs Guilt (preschool)
  4. Industry vs Inferiority (school age)
  5. Identity vs Role Confusion (adolescence)
  6. Intimacy vs Isolation (young adult)
  7. Generativity vs Stagnation (middle adult)
  8. Integrity vs Despair (old age)

Harry Stack Sullivan — Interpersonal Theory. Sullivan held that personality exists only in the web of interpersonal relations. To manage anxiety the child builds a self-system, organised into three personifications learned from how others (especially the mother) treat the child: "good me" (associated with approval and security), "bad me" (associated with anxiety and disapproval), and "not me" (split-off, dread-laden experiences too threatening to own).

Object Relations theorists shifted the focus from drives to relationships with "objects" (people) internalised from infancy. Melanie Klein described the infant moving between the paranoid-schizoid position (relating to part-objects like the "good breast/bad breast," using splitting) and the depressive position (recognising the mother as a whole object and feeling guilt/concern); she pioneered play therapy with children. Donald Winnicott gave us the "good-enough mother," the transitional object (the child's blanket/teddy bridging self and world), the holding environment, and the true self vs false self. Margaret Mahler mapped separation-individuation, the toddler's psychological birth out of symbiosis with the mother. W.R.D. Fairbairn argued the libido is fundamentally object-seeking, not pleasure-seeking.

Ego Psychology grew from Anna Freud (defenses) and Heinz Hartmann, who proposed a conflict-free ego sphere: ego functions such as perception, memory, and motor control that develop autonomously and serve adaptation to the environment, not just defence against the id.

Self Psychology. Heinz Kohut reframed narcissism as a normal developmental line. The child needs selfobjects (others experienced as part of the self) who provide mirroring (admiring responsiveness) and serve as idealised figures. Empathy is the central tool of treatment; failures of empathic parenting, not drive conflict, produce the fragile self of narcissistic disorders.

Trap

Keep the dissenters' signature concepts separate. Jung = collective unconscious + archetypes (a shared, inherited layer); do not confuse this with Freud's personal/dynamic unconscious (one individual's repressed material). Adler = inferiority/superiority, compensation, social interest (NOT Jung, a frequent swap). Horney = basic anxiety (social, not the id-driven neurotic anxiety of Freud). The "Electra complex" was named by Jung, not Freud.

Trap

The single most-confused pair on entrance exams: Freud's PSYCHOSEXUAL stages (oral/anal/phallic/latency/genital, driven by libido, complete by age ~6) versus Erikson's PSYCHOSOCIAL stages (8 stages, lifelong, driven by social crises). "Sexual = Freud, Social = Erikson." Trust vs Mistrust, identity crisis, integrity vs despair are all Erikson.

6.7 Evaluation

Psychoanalysis has been heavily criticised. Karl Popper attacked it as unfalsifiable and therefore unscientific: its concepts can "explain" any outcome and its opposite, so no observation could ever refute it. Critics point to Freud's over-emphasis on sex and aggression, his reliance on a small, biased sample (mostly affluent neurotic Viennese women), his retrospective and unverifiable data, and his now-discredited views on female psychology. Modern research questions the literal mechanism of repressed memories.

Yet the legacy is enormous and enduring. Psychoanalysis gave psychology the unconscious, the defense mechanisms (still used across all schools), the recognition that early childhood shapes adult personality, and the very idea of the talking cure, psychotherapy itself. Concepts like transference, the ego, and identity remain woven into clinical practice and everyday language. Whatever its scientific status, no movement has influenced twentieth-century thought about the mind more deeply.

High-yield

One-line takeaways: Freud = drives + unconscious + psychosexual. Jung = collective unconscious + archetypes. Adler = inferiority/superiority + social interest. Horney = basic anxiety + womb envy. Erikson = 8 psychosocial stages + identity. Sullivan = interpersonal + good/bad/not-me. Klein/Winnicott/Mahler = object relations. Hartmann = conflict-free ego. Kohut = self psychology + narcissism/selfobjects.

7. Humanistic & Existential Psychology

By the middle of the twentieth century, two great schools dominated psychology, and the humanists found both wanting. Psychoanalysis, they argued, was deterministic and pessimistic: it explained the human being through unconscious drives and childhood traumas, and it built its theory on the study of the neurotic and the sick. Behaviorism was mechanistic and reductionistic: it dissolved the rich inner life of a person into chains of stimulus and response, treating people as little more than complicated rats or pigeons. Against both, a new movement arose in the 1950s and 1960s that called itself the "Third Force."

The Third Force rested on a cluster of commitments: free will (people choose, they are not merely pushed); holism (the person must be understood as an integrated whole, not decomposed into parts); the primacy of subjective, phenomenological experience (what matters is how the world appears to the experiencing person); an inherent drive toward growth and fulfillment; a focus on the present rather than the buried past; and a deep respect for human dignity, uniqueness, and the study of healthy, whole people rather than the pathological. Its philosophical roots lay in existential and phenomenological philosophy (Kierkegaard, Husserl, Heidegger, Sartre).

Abraham Maslow and the Hierarchy of Needs

Abraham Maslow (1908-1970), often called the spiritual father of humanistic psychology, proposed that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy: lower needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher ones become motivating.

  1. Physiological needs (food, water, sleep, air)
  2. Safety needs (security, stability, freedom from fear)
  3. Love and belonging needs (affection, acceptance, relationships)
  4. Esteem needs (respect, status, competence, recognition)
  5. Self-actualization (realizing one's full potential)

This is the classic five-level pyramid. Later, Maslow expanded the model, inserting cognitive needs (to know and understand) and aesthetic needs (order, beauty) below self-actualization, and crowning it with self-transcendence (serving something beyond the self) at the very top. Maslow distinguished deficiency (D) needs, the lower four, which are felt as a lack and which subside once met, from being (B) needs or growth needs, which are never fully satisfied but expand as they are pursued.

Crucially, Maslow studied healthy exemplars (figures like Lincoln, Einstein, and Eleanor Roosevelt) rather than patients. Self-actualizers, he found, share characteristics: efficient perception of reality, acceptance of self and others, spontaneity, problem-centering, autonomy, deep interpersonal relations, a fresh appreciation of life, and frequent peak experiences, moments of intense joy, wonder, and self-transcendent awe.

Definition

Self-actualization: the innate human tendency to fulfill one's unique potential, to "become everything one is capable of becoming." It sits at the apex of Maslow's hierarchy.

Carl Rogers and Person-Centered Therapy

Carl Rogers (1902-1987) developed person-centered (client-centered) therapy, the most influential humanistic clinical approach. At its heart is the self-concept: the organized picture one holds of oneself. Rogers distinguished the real self (who one actually is) from the ideal self (who one feels one ought to be). When these align there is congruence; a large gap produces incongruence, which Rogers saw as the root of psychological distress.

Distress, for Rogers, arises from conditions of worth: the child learns that love and approval are conditional ("I am valued only if I behave this way"), and so begins to distort or deny experiences that conflict with that imposed self-image. The antidote is unconditional positive regard. Every person, Rogers held, possesses an actualizing tendency and an organismic valuing process, an inner compass that, when trusted, guides growth toward becoming a "fully functioning person": open to experience, living in the present, and trusting one's own judgment.

Definition

Unconditional positive regard: accepting and valuing a person completely, without conditions or judgment, regardless of their behavior or feelings.

Rogers' therapy is non-directive: the therapist does not interpret or instruct but creates a climate of growth through three core conditions, which he famously called the "necessary and sufficient" conditions for therapeutic change:

  1. Genuineness / congruence (the therapist is real and transparent)
  2. Unconditional positive regard (warm, non-judgmental acceptance)
  3. Empathic understanding (accurately sensing the client's inner world)

Existential Psychology

Closely allied to humanism but darker in tone is existential psychology. Rollo May (1909-1994) brought European existentialism to America, writing on anxiety as a normal part of confronting existence and on the daimonic (any natural function, such as anger or sex, that can take over the whole person); his works include Love and Will. Viktor Frankl (1905-1997), a survivor of Nazi concentration camps, founded logotherapy, built on the "will to meaning"; his classic Man's Search for Meaning argues that the absence of meaning produces an existential vacuum of boredom and despair. Ludwig Binswanger developed Daseinsanalysis, and R. D. Laing offered an existential reading of psychosis.

Irvin Yalom crystallized the four existential "givens" that therapy must confront: death, freedom (and its burden of responsibility), isolation, and meaninglessness. Here lies the key contrast: humanistic psychology is optimistic, emphasizing growth and self-actualization, whereas existential psychology, though overlapping, is more sober, asking the person to face the anxieties inherent in existence itself.

Evaluation

Critics charge that humanistic concepts (self-actualization, peak experience) are vague and hard to test scientifically, and that its view of human nature is overly optimistic, neglecting human cruelty. Yet its legacy is enormous: it transformed counseling and psychotherapy, established the centrality of the therapeutic alliance, and seeded the modern positive psychology movement (Martin Seligman is its clearest descendant).

High-yield

Maslow's hierarchy (bottom to top): Physiological, Safety, Love/belonging, Esteem, Self-actualization (with self-transcendence later added at the apex). Rogers' three core conditions: congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard, empathic understanding. Humanism = the "Third Force."

Trap

Do not confuse Maslow's self-actualization (apex of the hierarchy, becoming one's full potential) with Jung's self-realization / individuation (integration of conscious and unconscious into the Self). Both peak the personality, but they belong to different theorists and frameworks. Also remember: humanistic = the Third Force; existential = confronting the givens of existence.

For the hierarchy, climb the ladder: "Please Stop Liking Each Slide" = Physiological, Safety, Love, Esteem, Self-actualization.

8. Cognitive Psychology

If behaviorism had banished the mind from psychology, the cognitive revolution of the 1950s and 1960s brought it back. Behaviorists had refused to study internal mental events on the grounds that they could not be directly observed. Cognitivists countered that the mind could be studied scientifically and indirectly, by inferring internal processes from carefully measured behavior, just as physicists infer unseen particles from their effects.

Drivers of the Cognitive Revolution

Several developments converged to reinstate the mind as a legitimate object of study:

  • Chomsky's 1959 critique of Skinner's Verbal Behavior: Noam Chomsky argued that language is generative (we produce sentences never heard before), that children acquire it too fast and too well to be explained by reinforcement, and that humans possess an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD) and a transformational grammar. This devastating review is often taken as the symbolic launch of the revolution.
  • The computer / information-processing metaphor: the mind came to be seen as an information processor that takes input, encodes, stores, retrieves, and outputs, mirroring a computer.
  • Shannon's information theory, which gave psychology a formal vocabulary for measuring information.
  • The 1956 "annus mirabilis": George Miller's paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two"; Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist (an early AI program); and the Dartmouth conference that christened "artificial intelligence."
  • Earlier groundwork by Frederic Bartlett, whose 1932 work on schemas showed that memory is reconstructive, not photographic.
Definition

Information-processing model: the view that the mind operates like a computer, handling information through a sequence of stages: input, encoding, storage, retrieval, and output.

Ulric Neisser and the Naming of the Field

Ulric Neisser's 1967 textbook Cognitive Psychology named and consolidated the discipline, earning him the title "father of cognitive psychology."

The Information-Processing Model of Memory

The dominant framework was a set of stage models, debating whether processing is serial (one step at a time) or parallel. The landmark Atkinson-Shiffrin multi-store model (1968) proposed three stores:

Sensory registerVery largeFraction of a second

Refinements followed. Baddeley and Hitch (1974) replaced the passive short-term store with an active working memory comprising a central executive directing two subsystems, the phonological loop (verbal/acoustic information) and the visuospatial sketchpad (visual and spatial information), with an episodic buffer added later (2000) to integrate them. Craik and Lockhart (1972) offered an alternative levels of processing framework: memory depends not on which store but on how deeply material is encoded (shallow surface processing versus deep semantic processing).

In attention, Donald Broadbent proposed the influential filter (bottleneck) model, in which a filter selects one channel of incoming information for further processing.

Key Figures and Allied Lines

George Miller quantified short-term capacity at seven plus or minus two "chunks." Herbert Simon and Allen Newell pioneered the study of problem-solving, building the General Problem Solver (GPS) and introducing bounded rationality (the idea that human reasoning is rational but limited by information and capacity). Jerome Bruner, also a founder of the field, advanced discovery learning and three modes of representation: enactive (action-based), iconic (image-based), and symbolic (language-based). Jean Piaget is often placed here as the great cognitive-developmental theorist.

Later, Kahneman and Tversky showed that judgment relies on heuristics (mental shortcuts) such as availability, representativeness, and anchoring, extending Simon's bounded rationality into the study of cognitive bias. Cognitive psychology also yielded clinical fruit: Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, built on the cognitive triad (negative views of self, world, and future) and systematic cognitive distortions, and Albert Ellis's Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) with its ABC model (Activating event, Beliefs, Consequences).

Definition

Schema: a mental framework or organized knowledge structure that guides how we perceive, interpret, and remember information.

Connectionism and the Cognitive Science Turn

From the 1980s, connectionism / parallel distributed processing (PDP) modeled cognition through networks of simple interconnected units, inspired by the brain rather than the serial computer. The field broadened into cognitive science (uniting psychology, linguistics, computer science, and philosophy) and then into cognitive neuroscience, which uses brain imaging to ground mental processes in neural activity.

Legacy

Cognitive psychology is the dominant paradigm in psychology today and the theoretical foundation of CBT, the most empirically supported family of psychotherapies.

High-yield

Cognitive revolution drivers: Chomsky's 1959 critique of Skinner, the computer/information-processing metaphor, Shannon's information theory, and the 1956 events (Miller, Newell & Simon, Dartmouth AI). Neisser, 1967 named the field. Miller: 7 ± 2. Atkinson-Shiffrin: three stores, sensory register, short-term, long-term.

Trap

Distinguish cognitive psychology (the experimental science of mental processes, founded by Neisser, Miller, Bruner, etc.) from cognitive therapy (the clinical treatment founded by Aaron Beck). And remember it was Chomsky's critique of Skinner, not Skinner himself, that helped launch the revolution.

Memory hook

Three stores in a row, "Sensory, Short, Long", like a relay: information sprints through the sensory register, pauses in short-term (hold only 7 ± 2), then settles into long-term storage.

9. Indian Psychology & Eastern Systems

9.1 Orientation: What "Indian Psychology" Means

Western academic psychology grew out of physiology and philosophy in 19th-century Europe and defined itself as the empirical study of behaviour and mental processes, with health understood largely as the absence of pathology. Indian psychology is a different enterprise altogether. It is indigenous, rooted in philosophy and spiritual practice (the Vedas, Upanishads, and the great darshanas), and its goal is not the removal of symptoms but self-realization and liberation (moksha) and the deliberate transformation of consciousness. Where Western psychology asks "how does the mind work?", Indian psychology asks "who is the knower behind the mind, and how is one freed?" The "self" here is not the ego or personality but a deeper witnessing consciousness.

Indian thought is traditionally divided into nine systems (darshanas, literally "ways of seeing"). The six orthodox systems are called āstika because they accept the authority of the Vedas; the heterodox (nāstika) systems reject Vedic authority. The six orthodox darshanas are conventionally studied in three allied pairs.

The three heterodox (nāstika) systems are Buddhism, Jainism, and Charvaka (a materialist, empiricist school that denied the soul and afterlife). For psychology, the most heavily examined systems are Samkhya, Yoga, Vedanta, and Buddhism.

9.2 Samkhya: Purusha, Prakriti, and the Three Gunas

Samkhya, attributed to the sage Kapila, is the oldest of the systems and supplies the conceptual backbone for much of Indian psychology. It is a dualism of two ultimate, eternal realities. Purusha is pure consciousness, the passive witness (sakshi); it is many (each being has its own Purusha), changeless, and does nothing. Prakriti is primordial matter or nature, the active, unconscious source of everything mental and physical. Bondage arises when Purusha mistakenly identifies with the activities of Prakriti; liberation (kaivalya, "aloneness") comes from discriminating the two.

Prakriti is composed of three gunas (strands or qualities) held in equilibrium until evolution begins. From their disturbance unfolds a fixed sequence of evolutes: first mahat / buddhi (cosmic intellect, the faculty of discrimination), then ahamkara (the ego, the "I-maker"), then manas (mind, the coordinating sense), and from these the five sense-organs, five action-organs, five subtle elements (tanmatras), and five gross elements. Note that buddhi, ahamkara, and manas together form the inner instrument (antahkarana); they are all material, not consciousness itself.

Definition

Guna = a constituent quality or "strand" of Prakriti (nature). The three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas) are present in everything; what differs is their proportion. A person's temperament, mood, food, and even thoughts are classified by which guna dominates.

The three gunas are the single most-tested idea in Indian psychology, so learn them cold.

The three gunas: Sattva = purity/light/balance, Rajas = activity/passion/restlessness, Tamas = inertia/darkness/dullness. All of nature is a mixture of the three; personality is governed by which dominates. This item appears on virtually every Indian entrance.

Memory hook

Gunas in descending "energy quality": S-R-T = Saint, Restless, Tired. The Saint is sattvic (calm/pure), the Restless one is rajasic (driven), the Tired one is tamasic (dull/inert).

9.3 Yoga: Patanjali and the Eight Limbs

The Yoga system, set out in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, shares Samkhya's metaphysics but adds Ishvara (a special Purusha, a focus for devotion) and supplies the practical method for stilling the mind. Its central object is the chitta (mind-stuff), whose constant fluctuations are the chitta vrittis (mental modifications). The defining aphorism is "Yogah chitta vritti nirodhah": yoga is the cessation (restraint) of the modifications of the mind. When the vrittis are stilled, the witness (Purusha) rests in its own nature.

Definition

Chitta vritti = a modification or wave in the mind-stuff (thoughts, perceptions, memories, emotions). Moksha / kaivalya = liberation, the final goal: the witnessing self freed from identification with mind and matter, ending the cycle of suffering and rebirth.

The path is Ashtanga Yoga, the eight limbs:

  1. Yama — ethical restraints (non-violence, truth, non-stealing, continence, non-greed)
  2. Niyama — personal observances (purity, contentment, discipline, study, surrender)
  3. Asana — steady, comfortable posture
  4. Pranayama — regulation of breath / vital energy
  5. Pratyahara — withdrawal of the senses from their objects
  6. Dharana — concentration (fixing attention on one point)
  7. Dhyana — meditation (sustained, uninterrupted flow of attention)
  8. Samadhi — absorption / union, the goal
High-yield

"Yogah chitta vritti nirodhah" (yoga = stilling the mind's modifications) and the eight limbs in order: Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi. Examiners love asking for the sequence or for which limb is "sense-withdrawal" (Pratyahara) versus "concentration" (Dharana).

Memory hook

Eight limbs: "Young Novices Are Pretty Punctual During Daily Sadhana." Y-N-A-P-P-D-D-S. The last three (Dharana-Dhyana-Samadhi) practised together are called samyama.

Patanjali names five kleshas (afflictions) that bind the mind: avidya (ignorance, the root of the rest), asmita (egoism / I-am-ness), raga (attachment to pleasure), dvesha (aversion to pain), and abhinivesha (clinging to life, fear of death). Yoga also describes four states of consciousness: jagrat (waking), swapna (dream), sushupti (deep dreamless sleep), and turiya ("the fourth", pure witnessing consciousness underlying the other three). Samadhi itself has grades, broadly samprajnata (with an object / seed) maturing into asamprajnata (objectless / seedless, nirbija) absorption.

9.4 Vedanta: Brahman, Atman, and the Five Sheaths

Vedanta ("the end of the Vedas", based on the Upanishads) culminates in the Advaita (non-dual) teaching of Adi Shankara. Its claim is radical: there is only one reality, Brahman, and the individual self, Atman, is identical with it. The appearance of a separate world and separate selves is maya (illusion / superimposition). Liberation is not attaining something new but realizing what was always true, summed up in the great saying (mahavakya) "Tat Tvam Asi", "Thou art That."

Vedanta analyses the person as five concentric koshas (sheaths) covering the Atman, from grossest to subtlest:

Trap

Do not confuse Samkhya with Advaita Vedanta. Samkhya is dualist (two realities: Purusha + Prakriti) and is atheistic / non-theistic (no creator God needed). Advaita Vedanta is non-dualist (only Brahman is real; the world is maya). And while Samkhya is non-theistic, Yoga takes the same metaphysics but adds Ishvara, so Yoga is theistic. "Dualism = Samkhya, non-dualism = Advaita" is the line examiners test.

9.5 Buddhist Psychology

Buddhism is a nāstika system but one of the most psychologically sophisticated. Its core is the Four Noble Truths:

  1. Dukkha — life involves suffering / unsatisfactoriness
  2. Samudaya — the cause of suffering is craving / attachment (tanha)
  3. Nirodha — suffering can cease (nirvana)
  4. Magga — the way to cessation is the Eightfold Path

The Noble Eightfold Path covers right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration (grouped as wisdom, ethical conduct, and mental discipline). Three "marks of existence" frame Buddhist psychology: anicca (impermanence, nothing is fixed), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), and anatta (no-self, there is no permanent, unchanging soul, directly opposing the Hindu Atman). The person is only a bundle of five skandhas (aggregates): form (rupa), sensation (vedana), perception (samjna), mental formations (samskara), and consciousness (vijnana). Experience arises through dependent origination (pratitya-samutpada): every state conditions the next in a chain, so there is no independent self at the centre.

Trap

Anatta (Buddhism) vs Atman (Vedanta). Vedanta affirms an eternal self (Atman = Brahman); Buddhism denies any permanent self (anatta / anatman). This direct opposition is a favourite MCQ.

Buddhist practice of mindfulness (sati) and insight meditation (vipassana) has had an enormous modern clinical impact: it is the root of MBSR (Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction), MBCT (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for relapse prevention in depression), and the mindfulness/acceptance components of DBT and ACT. This is the clearest bridge between Eastern systems and contemporary evidence-based therapy.

9.6 Clinical Relevance and the Modern Indian Psychology Movement

Several Indian constructs map directly onto clinical and personality psychology:

  • Triguna model in assessment. The sattva-rajas-tamas scheme is used as an indigenous personality framework; Indian inventories such as the Vedic Personality Inventory and various Triguna scales measure a person's guna profile, an alternative to Western trait models.
  • The self. Indian thought distinguishes the ego/personality from a deeper witnessing self, and tends toward a collectivistic, relational, embedded self, contrasting with the bounded, individualistic Western self.
  • The Purusharthas, the four legitimate aims of life: dharma (duty / righteousness), artha (wealth / livelihood), kama (pleasure / desire), and moksha (liberation). A balanced, integrated life pursues all four, with moksha as the highest.
  • The Bhagavad Gita's psychology. The Gita offers three complementary paths (yogas): Karma yoga (the path of action), Bhakti yoga (the path of devotion), and Jnana yoga (the path of knowledge). Its central teaching, nishkama karma (desireless / non-attached action, acting fully but renouncing attachment to the fruits), is widely cited for stress regulation and resilience.

The modern Indian psychology movement seeks to recover these ideas as a scientific psychology. An early landmark is Jadunath Sinha's multi-volume Indian Psychology (cognition, emotion, will), which systematically extracted psychological content from the classical texts. Contemporary scholars (for example those associated with the Pondicherry / Sri Aurobindo tradition and the broader "indigenous psychology" movement) continue to develop Indian frameworks of consciousness, well-being, and self-realization as a complement to, not merely a translation of, Western psychology.

High-yield

Lock in the founder-text-system links: Samkhya = Kapila (Purusha/Prakriti, gunas); Yoga = Patanjali (Yoga Sutras, eight limbs, kleshas); Advaita Vedanta = Shankara (Brahman/Atman, maya, koshas); Buddhism = Four Noble Truths (anatta, anicca, skandhas). Plus the four Purusharthas (dharma-artha-kama-moksha) and the Gita's nishkama karma.

10.3 The "forces" of psychology

A common exam shorthand calls the major clinical traditions "forces": the first force is psychoanalysis, the second force is behaviorism, the third force is humanistic/existential psychology, and the fourth force is usually given as transpersonal (or, in some texts, multicultural) psychology. The cognitive revolution is sometimes folded in as part of a "cognitive-behavioural" mainstream rather than counted as a separate force.
