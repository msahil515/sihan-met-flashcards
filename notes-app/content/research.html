Research design & methodology

Only an experiment (manipulate the IV and randomly assign participants) can establish causation, because randomisation balances out unknown confounds. Everything else can describe and relate, but not prove cause.

Experimental hierarchy — by degree of control

DesignManipulate IV?Random assignment?Control group?Internal validity
True experimental (RCT)YesYesYesHigh — gold standard for causation
Quasi-experimentalYesNo (intact/pre-existing groups: classes, wards)Usually yesModerate — confounds remain
Pre-experimentalLooselyNoOften noneLow — e.g. one-group pretest-posttest; many threats
Non-experimental (correlational)No——No causal claim

Between- vs within-subjects (and factorial)

  • Between-subjects (independent groups): different people in each condition. No carry-over, but between-person variability is noise and groups may differ to start.
  • Within-subjects (repeated measures): the same people in every condition. Each is their own control, so it is powerful and economical, but risks order/carry-over effects (practice, fatigue) — fixed by counterbalancing.
  • Factorial: two or more IVs crossed, so you can test their interaction (a 2×2 has 4 cells).

Specific designs to know

  • Pretest-posttest control group: the classic two-group RCT.
  • Posttest-only control group: avoids pretest sensitisation entirely.
  • Solomon four-group: combines both. Two groups get a pretest, two do not; half of each get treatment. It detects and separates pretest sensitisation (the interaction of pretest and treatment) from the treatment effect itself. Resource-heavy but the most rigorous design for ruling out testing effects.
  • Crossover design: every subject receives both conditions in different order.
  • Single-case designs (ABA, ABAB, multiple baseline): used in behaviour analysis.

Non-experimental & epidemiological designs

  • Correlational: measure variables as they are and relate them; no manipulation, so no causal claim.
  • Cross-sectional: one snapshot in time; reports prevalence. Cheap, but cannot show temporality or change.
  • Longitudinal: the same people followed over time; can show change and causal order, but slow, costly, and bleeds participants through attrition.
  • Cohort study: follow exposed and unexposed groups forward in time; reports relative risk.
  • Case-control: start with cases and controls, look back at exposure; reports odds ratio.
  • Case study: deep dive into one person/unit (rich, hypothesis-generating, not generalisable).
  • Observational: watch behaviour in situ — naturalistic vs structured; participant vs non-participant; covert vs overt.

Controlling confounds

  • Randomisation — random assignment spreads unknown confounds evenly across groups (the most powerful control).
  • Counterbalancing — vary the order of conditions across subjects to cancel order effects in within-subjects designs.
  • Matching — pair subjects on a key variable (e.g. IQ) before assigning, removing that variable as a confound.
  • Single-blind (participant unaware of condition) and double-blind (participant and experimenter/assessor unaware) — control expectancy and experimenter effects.
Trap

Random sampling vs random assignment. Random sampling = who gets into the study (drives external validity/generalisability). Random assignment = which condition they go to (drives internal validity/causation). A study can have one without the other.

Trap

Counterbalancing (fixes order effects in within-subjects designs) vs matching (controls a specific confound variable in between-subjects designs).

Variables, confounds, mediation & moderation

  • Independent variable (IV): what the experimenter manipulates or groups by — the presumed cause.
  • Dependent variable (DV): the measured outcome — the presumed effect.
  • Control variable: a variable deliberately held constant.
  • Confound: a variable tied to both the IV and the DV, creating a spurious link and offering a rival explanation. Controlled by randomisation, matching, or statistical adjustment.
  • Extraneous variable: any variable other than the IV that could affect the DV; it becomes a confound only if it also correlates with the IV.
  • Operationalisation: defining a fuzzy construct in concrete, measurable terms (e.g. "anxiety" = score on the GAD-7). Bad operationalisation sinks construct validity.

Mediator vs moderator — the high-frequency trap

Both are "third variables," but they do opposite jobs:

  • Mediator explains HOW / WHY the IV affects the DV — the mechanism in the causal chain: IV → Mediator → DV. Stress → cortisol → poorer immunity: cortisol mediates. If you control the mediator, the IV–DV link weakens or vanishes. Tested with Baron-Kenny steps or Hayes PROCESS.
  • Moderator changes WHEN / FOR WHOM / HOW STRONGLY the IV affects the DV — it alters the strength or direction of the relationship but is not on the causal path. Therapy works better for women than men: sex moderates therapy → outcome. Statistically a moderator shows up as an interaction term (X × M) in regression.
Trap

Mediator = mechanism (how, sits between IV and DV). Moderator = condition (for whom / when, an interaction). Hook: mediator = mechanism; moderator = depends.

Validity & threats in a study

This is distinct from test validity (covered under measurement). Here we judge the study's conclusions.

  • Internal validity: did the IV really cause the change in the DV, with no confound responsible? Maximised by experimental control and randomisation.
  • External validity: do the findings generalise to other people, settings, and times? Includes population validity (sample → population) and ecological validity (lab → real life).
  • Construct validity (of the study): does the operationalisation actually match the intended construct?
  • Statistical conclusion validity: is the inference about the relationship sound? Threatened by low power, violated assumptions, and p-hacking.

There is usually a trade-off: tight lab control boosts internal validity, but the artificial setting can hurt external/ecological validity.

Threats to internal validity (Campbell & Stanley)

  • History: an outside event happens during the study and affects the DV.
  • Maturation: participants naturally change over time (grow, tire, heal) regardless of the IV.
  • Testing: taking the pretest itself changes the posttest (practice, sensitisation) — controlled by the Solomon four-group design.
  • Instrumentation: the measuring tool or observer drifts over time.
  • Regression to the mean: people selected for extreme scores drift back toward average on retest, faking an "effect" in pre/post designs without a control.
  • Selection bias: groups differ before the study (the core flaw of non-randomised designs).
  • Attrition (mortality): participants drop out non-randomly, leaving an unrepresentative remainder.
  • Diffusion of treatment: the control group learns from / is contaminated by the experimental group.
  • Hawthorne effect: simply being observed changes behaviour.
  • Demand characteristics: subjects guess the hypothesis and play along.
Trap

Maturation (internal, natural change within people) vs history (an external event). And regression to the mean is mistaken for a treatment effect whenever extreme groups are retested without a control group.

Sampling

A sample stands in for a population. Whether you can generalise depends entirely on how it was drawn.

Probability sampling — everyone has a known, non-zero chance

Only probability methods let you calculate sampling error and generalise with confidence.

MethodHowNote
Simple randomEvery individual has an equal chance (lottery / random-number table)The benchmark; requires a sampling frame
SystematicPick every k-th person from a list (k = N/n) after a random startEasy, but dangerous if the list has a hidden periodicity matching k
StratifiedDivide the population into strata (age bands, sex), then sample randomly within eachGuarantees representation of each stratum and usually reduces sampling error. Proportionate or disproportionate
ClusterDivide into naturally occurring clusters (schools, villages), randomly select whole clusters, study everyone (or many) insideCheaper over wide areas but higher sampling error. Two-stage = sample within selected clusters
MultistageNest the above (random districts → random schools within → random pupils)National surveys

Non-probability sampling — selection chance unknown

  • Convenience: whoever is to hand (the classic undergraduate subject pool). Most prone to bias.
  • Purposive (judgmental): the researcher hand-picks information-rich cases on set criteria.
  • Quota: fill fixed counts per category (50 men, 50 women) but non-randomly within — the non-probability cousin of stratified.
  • Snowball: participants recruit further participants; for hidden/stigmatised populations (e.g. drug users).
Trap

Stratified vs cluster. Stratified samples randomly within every subgroup (you want all strata in) and lowers error; cluster randomly picks some whole subgroups and skips the rest (you sample groups, for cost) and raises error. Both are probability methods.

Trap

Sampling error vs sampling bias. Error is the random, unavoidable difference between a sample statistic and the true value from chance alone; it shrinks as n grows and centres on the truth. Bias is a systematic skew from a flawed method (e.g. convenience sampling); it does not shrink with n — a bigger biased sample is just more precisely wrong.

Levels of measurement

Before any statistic, ask: what kind of numbers am I holding? Stevens (1946) showed that a number can carry four different amounts of information, and the amount you have dictates which statistics are even meaningful. Running a mean on jersey numbers is nonsense not because the arithmetic fails but because the numbers were never on a scale where "average" means anything. This is the single most important idea in the section: the scale chooses the test.

The four levels, from least to most information (NOIR)

Nominal — labels only

Numbers are just names for categories with no order: 1 = male, 2 = female, or blood groups, religion, diagnosis. You can count how many fall in each box and find the mode, but "2 > 1" is meaningless. Permissible: frequencies, proportions, mode, and chi-square on the counts.

Ordinal — rank order, unequal gaps

Numbers now carry order but the distance between ranks is not equal or known. Finishing 1st, 2nd, 3rd tells you who beat whom, not by how much. Likert agreement, pain 0–10, socio-economic class, IQ classification bands. Because gaps are unequal you should not add or average them, so the centre is the median and spread is described with percentiles. Permissible: median, percentile, Spearman's rho, Mann-Whitney U, and rank-based (non-parametric) tests generally.

Interval — equal gaps, no true zero

Equal distances mean a degree is a degree everywhere on the scale, so you can finally add, subtract, and take a mean. But zero is arbitrary (0°C is not "no temperature"), so ratios are meaningless: 20°C is not "twice as hot" as 10°C. IQ scores and calendar years sit here. Permissible: mean, SD, t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson's r.

Ratio — equal gaps and an absolute zero

Zero means none of the thing (0 kg = no weight, 0 ms = no time). Now ratios are valid: 40 kg really is twice 20 kg. Height, weight, reaction time, income, age. All arithmetic plus the geometric mean and coefficient of variation. For test-choice purposes interval and ratio behave identically, which is why people lump them as "scale" or "continuous" data.

Mnemonic

NOIR — Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio, in increasing order of information.

Trap

A scale "having numbers" does not make it interval. A 1–5 Likert item is strictly ordinal (the gap from "disagree" to "neutral" need not equal "neutral" to "agree"); only a summed multi-item Likert scale is commonly treated as interval. MCQs love "temperature in Celsius is ratio" — false, no true zero; Kelvin is ratio. And "ordinal data can use the mean" is false — use the median.

Reliability & validity in measurement

A. K. Singh's home turf. A test score is only as good as its reliability (consistency) and validity (does it measure the intended construct).

Reliability — consistency of measurement

Reliability asks: if I measured again, would I get the same score? It is a coefficient from 0 to 1 (≥ .70 acceptable for research, ≥ .80–.90 for clinical decisions). Conceptually a score = true score + error; reliability = the proportion of true-score variance in observed scores.

  • Test-retest: same test, same people, two occasions; correlate. Indexes stability over time. Threat: practice/memory effects, real change.
  • Parallel-form (alternate-form): two equivalent versions; correlate. Controls memory effects but you must prove the forms are truly equivalent.
  • Split-half: split the items in two (e.g. odd vs even), correlate the halves. Because you have halved the test, correct upward with the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula (longer tests are more reliable).
  • Internal consistency — Cronbach's alpha (α): the average of all possible split-halves; the standard index of whether the items hang together / measure one thing. Rises with more items and higher inter-item correlations. Acceptable ≈ .70–.95. For dichotomous (right/wrong) items, use KR-20 (Kuder-Richardson).
  • Inter-rater reliability: agreement between observers. Cohen's kappa (κ) for categorical ratings corrects for chance agreement (raw % agreement overstates it); the intraclass correlation (ICC) is used for continuous ratings.
High-yield

NIMHANS depth on alpha: it is not a measure of unidimensionality, and a high alpha can simply mean you have many items. Above ~.95 suggests redundancy (items are near-duplicates), not excellence. Alpha assumes tau-equivalence; modern psychometrics often prefers McDonald's omega. Always report alpha alongside the construct it indexes.

Validity — are you measuring the right thing?

  • Face validity: does it look like it measures the construct, to a layperson? Weakest, most superficial — informally a subset of content validity.
  • Content validity: do the items cover the full domain of the construct (a maths test sampling all topics)? Judged by experts / a blueprint.
  • Criterion validity: does the test correlate with an external criterion? Two flavours by timing — concurrent (criterion measured now, e.g. a new depression scale vs an established one today) and predictive (criterion measured later, e.g. an entrance exam predicting future grades).
  • Construct validity: does it truly tap the theoretical construct? Built from convergent evidence (correlates with measures it should) and discriminant/divergent evidence (does not correlate with unrelated measures). Includes factorial validity.
High-yield

Reliability sets a ceiling on validity. A test cannot correlate with anything (be valid) more than it correlates with itself (is reliable). The validity ceiling is max validity = √reliability (assuming a perfectly reliable criterion): rxx = 0.81 → max validity = 0.90. So unreliability caps validity, but the reverse fails — a test can be perfectly reliable yet not valid: a bathroom scale that always reads 5 kg heavy is consistent (reliable) but wrong (invalid). Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

Trap

Reliable ≠ valid. Reliability = consistency; validity = accuracy/measuring the intended thing. A test can be reliable but not valid; it cannot be valid without being reliable. Also concurrent (criterion measured now) vs predictive (criterion in the future) — both are criterion validity.

Scaling (attitude measurement)

Scaling turns attitudes into numbers. Know the logic that distinguishes the four classic methods:

  • Likert (summated rating): respondents rate agreement on a symmetric scale (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree, usually 5 or 7 points); item scores are summed. Easiest to build; individual items are ordinal.
  • Thurstone (equal-appearing intervals): judges first scale many statements for favourability; respondents simply endorse the ones they agree with, and their score is the average scale value of those statements. Aims for interval-level measurement; labour-intensive to construct.
  • Guttman (cumulative / scalogram): items form a hierarchy so that agreeing with a strong item implies agreement with all weaker ones. Unidimensional and reproducible.
  • Semantic differential (Osgood): rate a concept on a series of bipolar adjective pairs (good–bad, weak–strong, fast–slow) on a 7-point line; classically loads onto three dimensions — evaluation, potency, activity (E-P-A).
Trap

Thurstone (judges pre-scale statements; you endorse/reject) vs Likert (you rate degree of agreement, items summed) vs Guttman (cumulative hierarchy) vs semantic differential (bipolar adjective pairs). Osgood = semantic differential = evaluation/potency/activity.

Item analysis & test construction

A. K. Singh's strength: how individual items are evaluated when building a test. After piloting, you examine each item on three indices and keep, revise, or drop it.

  • Item difficulty (p): the proportion of examinees who got the item right. p = 1.0 means everyone passed (too easy); p = 0 means nobody did (too hard). Counter-intuitively, a higher p = an easier item. For maximum discrimination, p ≈ 0.50 is ideal (adjusted upward for guessing on MCQs); keep a spread of difficulties.
  • Discrimination index (D): does the item separate high scorers from low scorers? Split into top and bottom groups (often 27%); D = (proportion correct in upper group) − (proportion correct in lower group). D ranges −1 to +1; ≥ 0.40 excellent, 0.20–0.39 acceptable, < 0.20 poor. A negative D (low scorers beat high scorers) flags a flawed or mis-keyed item — drop it.
  • Item-total correlation: the correlation between the item score and the total test score (for dichotomous items, a point-biserial). High = the item pulls in the same direction as the test as a whole (good internal consistency); near zero or negative = the item does not belong.
Trap

Item difficulty is a proportion correct, so a high p = an easy item, not a hard one. And discrimination, not difficulty, is what tells you whether an item separates strong from weak examinees.

High-yield

NIMHANS depth — IRT basics. Classical test theory (p, D) is sample-dependent: the same item looks "harder" in a weaker group. Item Response Theory (IRT) models the probability of a correct response as a function of latent ability (θ) via an item characteristic curve (ICC), with item parameters a = discrimination (slope), b = difficulty (location along θ), c = guessing (lower asymptote) — the 1PL/Rasch, 2PL and 3PL models. IRT parameters are (ideally) sample-independent, which is why modern computer-adaptive tests (CAT) are built on it.

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise the sample in front of you; they make no claim about a wider population (that is inference). You describe three things about any batch of numbers: where the centre sits (central tendency), how spread out it is (dispersion), and what shape it has (skewness, kurtosis).

Central tendency — where the middle is

Mean

The arithmetic average: add all scores, divide by n. In symbols x̄ = Σx / n. It uses every value, which is its strength and its weakness — every value includes the outliers. One billionaire walking into a room sends the mean income soaring while the median barely moves. Use the mean for roughly symmetric / normal interval-ratio data.

Median

The middle value when scores are ordered (the 50th percentile). With an even n it is the average of the two middle scores. It ignores the magnitude of extreme scores, so it is robust to outliers and skew. Use it for skewed data (income, reaction times, length of stay) and for ordinal data.

Mode

The most frequent value. It is the only central-tendency measure usable on nominal data, and a distribution can be bimodal or multimodal (two humps often hints two subgroups are mixed together).

Mnemonic

Why skew moves them apart: in a symmetric distribution mean = median = mode. In positive (right) skew (long tail of high values, e.g. income) the tail drags the mean up the most, the median less, the mode least, so mode < median < mean. In negative (left) skew it reverses: mean < median < mode. Hook: the mean follows the tail.

Trap

The order under skew. Positive skew → mean is the largest (mean > median > mode). "Which average is most affected by an outlier?" → the mean. "Which is best for skewed/income data?" → the median.

Dispersion — how spread out

Range and IQR

Range = max − min. One number, crude, fully at the mercy of the two most extreme scores. The Interquartile Range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1 spans the middle 50% of the data, throwing away the outer quarters, so it is robust; it is the box in a box-plot.

Variance and SD — the real workhorses

To capture spread we measure how far each score sits from the mean. Deviations (x − x̄) sum to zero, so we square them first. The average squared deviation is the variance. Taking the square root returns to the original units — the standard deviation (SD).

  • Population variance: σ² = Σ(x − μ)² / N
  • Sample variance: s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)
  • SD = √variance. Population σ = √σ²; sample s = √s². SD is in the same units as the data.

What SD actually means: the typical (root-mean-square) distance of a score from the mean. SD = 0 means every score is identical; a larger SD means scores fan out further. In a normal distribution about two-thirds of scores fall within ±1 SD of the mean. It is the default measure of spread because it feeds straight into z-scores, SE, and every parametric test.

High-yield

Why divide by n − 1 (Bessel's correction)? A sample's own mean sits in the middle of the sample, so deviations from it are a touch too small and would under-estimate the population spread. Dividing by n − 1 (the degrees of freedom) inflates the estimate just enough to make it unbiased. Population formulas use N because μ is fixed and known. The full intuition is in the degrees-of-freedom chapter.

Coefficient of variation

CV = (SD / mean) × 100%. A unitless relative spread, so you can compare variability across different scales (is weight more variable than height?). Only meaningful on ratio data with a true zero.

Shape

Skewness

Asymmetry. Positive skew = long tail to the right (high values), negative skew = long tail to the left, zero skew = symmetric. Skew is what breaks the normality assumption and pushes you toward the median or a non-parametric test.

Kurtosis

Tailedness / peakedness relative to the normal curve. Leptokurtic = sharp peak, fat tails (more extreme scores than normal). Platykurtic = flat, thin tails. Mesokurtic = like the normal curve.

Mnemonic

platy = plateau = flat; lepto = leaping peak.

Distributions, z-scores & the Central Limit Theorem

The normal (Gaussian) curve

A symmetric bell, fully defined by just two numbers: its mean (μ) and SD (σ). Mean = median = mode all sit at the peak. It matters because (a) many natural and psychological variables are roughly normal, and (b) the sampling distribution of the mean becomes normal even when the raw data is not (the CLT, below), which is what lets parametric tests work.

High-yield

The 68–95–99.7 (empirical) rule. In any normal distribution: ≈ 68% of scores fall within ±1 SD of the mean, ≈ 95% within ±2 SD (more precisely ±1.96 SD), ≈ 99.7% within ±3 SD. So a score 2 SD above the mean beats about 97.5% of the distribution.

z-scores — putting everything on one ruler

A z-score says how many SDs a value sits from its mean: z = (x − μ) / σ. It standardises any value onto the standard normal distribution (μ = 0, σ = 1), so scores from different scales become comparable and you can read off percentiles from a z-table. A z of +1.5 means "1.5 SDs above average."

Standard scores built on z

Test-makers rescale z so there are no negatives or decimals, but the information is identical:

  • T-score: mean = 50, SD = 10. Formula T = 50 + 10z. The MMPI uses T-scores; T = 70 → +2 SD → 97.5th percentile (the classic clinical cutoff).
  • Stanine ("standard nine"): range 1–9, mean = 5, SD ≈ 2.
  • Sten ("standard ten"): range 1–10, mean = 5.5, SD = 2. The 16PF uses stens.
  • Wechsler / deviation IQ: mean = 100, SD = 15.
  • Percentile rank: the % of people scoring at or below you — a rank, not a z-score, and not equal-interval (percentiles bunch up near the middle of a normal curve).
Trap

T-score (mean 50, SD 10) vs Wechsler IQ (mean 100, SD 15) vs sten (mean 5.5, SD 2) vs stanine (mean 5, SD 2). Also: a percentile is a rank, and the gaps between percentiles are not equal — moving from the 50th to 55th percentile is a tiny score change, but 90th to 95th is a big one.

Sampling distribution & the standard error

Here is the conceptual leap that powers inference. Imagine drawing many samples of size n and writing down each sample's mean. Those means form their own distribution: the sampling distribution of the mean. Its spread is the standard error of the mean (SEM):

SE = SD / √n

SE is the typical distance between a sample mean and the true population mean — how wrong your sample mean is likely to be as an estimate. Because of the √n, SE shrinks as the sample grows, but with diminishing returns (to halve the SE you must quadruple n).

Trap

SD vs SE. SD describes the spread of individual scores; SE (= SD/√n) describes the spread of sample means and is always smaller. Reporting SE instead of SD makes data look tidier than it is — be sceptical.

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The CLT states that as sample size n grows, the sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal distribution regardless of the shape of the underlying population, with mean = μ and SD = σ/√n. This is the foundation of inferential statistics: even on skewed raw data you can use normal-theory (parametric) tests on the mean, provided n is reasonable. Common rule of thumb: n ≥ 30.

Other distributions to recognise

  • t distribution: like the normal but with fatter tails to account for estimating σ from a small sample; shaped by degrees of freedom; → normal as df → ∞.
  • Chi-square (χ²): right-skewed, only positive, for categorical/count data.
  • F distribution: a ratio of two variances; right-skewed; used in ANOVA and regression.
  • Binomial: discrete, counts of successes in n independent trials.
  • Poisson: counts of rare events per fixed interval of time or space.

The logic of inference

Inference is reasoning from a sample to a population. We can never be certain, so we frame the question as a bet against a default assumption and quantify how surprised we would be by our data if that default were true.

Null vs alternative hypotheses

  • Null hypothesis (H₀): the boring default — no effect, no difference, no relationship, status quo (e.g. μ₁ = μ₂, or r = 0). This is what we actually test.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H₁): there is an effect/difference/relationship. Usually what the researcher believes. Two-tailed (any direction) vs one-tailed (a specific direction).

We never "accept" H₀; we either reject it or fail to reject it. Failing to reject is "not enough evidence," not "proof of no effect." Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence.

Two ways to be wrong

H₀ is true (no real effect)H₀ is false (real effect)
Reject H₀Type I error (α) — false positiveCorrect — power (1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀Correct (1 − α)Type II error (β) — false negative
  • Type I (α): claiming an effect that is not real. You set this in advance, conventionally α = 0.05.
  • Type II (β): missing an effect that is real. Conventionally kept ≤ 0.20.
  • Power = 1 − β: the probability of detecting a real effect when it exists. Convention: aim for 0.80.

Power increases when: sample size n ↑, true effect size ↑, variability (SD) ↓, α made more lenient ↑, and (slightly) using a one-tailed test. There is a tension: lowering α (fewer false positives) raises β (more misses) unless you also raise n. An a priori power analysis sets the sample size before data collection.

Trap

Type I vs Type II. α = false positive = convicting an innocent (rejecting a true H₀, wrongly rejecting a TRUE null). β = false negative = letting the guilty go (missing a real effect, wrongly retaining a false null). Hook: "Sigh, I missed it" = Type II.

The p-value — what it is and is not

What it IS: the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as what you observed, assuming H₀ is true. Small p = "my data would be surprising if there were really no effect," casting doubt on H₀. Decision rule: if p < α → reject H₀ (e.g. p = 0.03 < α = 0.05 → reject).

Trap

The classic p-value trap. p is NOT the probability that H₀ is true, NOT the probability your result happened "by chance," and NOT the probability you have made an error. It is conditional on H₀. Also p < 0.05 is a convention, not a law of nature, and statistical significance ≠ clinical/practical importance (a trivial effect turns significant with a huge n). Always report effect size alongside p.

One-tailed vs two-tailed

A two-tailed test asks "is there a difference in either direction?" and splits α into both tails (0.025 each) — the safe default. A one-tailed test asks about a difference in one pre-specified direction, putting all 0.05 in one tail; it has more power but only if you committed to the direction in advance for theoretical reasons. Choosing one-tailed after seeing the data to scrape under 0.05 is p-hacking.

Confidence intervals

A 95% CI is a range of plausible values for the true parameter. Interpretation: if we repeated the study endlessly, 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true value. Practical links: if a 95% CI for a difference excludes 0, then p < 0.05; if a 95% CI for a ratio (OR/RR) excludes 1, then p < 0.05. A wider CI means a less precise estimate (small n or high variability). CIs are often more informative than a bare p because they show effect size and precision together.

Effect size

The p-value tells you whether there is an effect; the effect size tells you how big it is, independent of n. This is why two studies can both be "significant" yet matter very differently.

NIMHANS pushes harder — effect size & power analysis. Clinical psychology expects effect sizes, not just p, and an a priori power analysis (e.g. G*Power) before collecting data: fix α (.05), desired power (.80) and the smallest effect worth detecting, then solve for n. After a null result, examine the CI / effect size before concluding "no effect" — you may simply have been underpowered. Cohen's benchmarks are conventions, not laws; in clinical work a "small" effect on suicidality can be hugely important.

Parametric tests

Parametric tests make assumptions about the population's distribution (chiefly normality) and operate on its parameters (means, variances). When the assumptions hold they are more powerful than their non-parametric cousins — they squeeze more information from the same data.

The four parametric assumptions

  1. Normality of the DV / of the residuals (the CLT rescues this at large n).
  2. Homogeneity of variance — groups have similar spread (checked with Levene's test; Welch's correction if violated).
  3. Independence of observations (except where the design pairs them, as in paired/repeated designs).
  4. Interval or ratio measurement of the DV.

z-test

Compares a sample mean to a known population mean when the population SD (σ) is known (rare) or n is very large. Test statistic z = (x̄ − μ) / (σ/√n). In practice we usually do not know σ, which is exactly why the t-test exists.

t-tests — comparing one or two means

A t-test asks whether a mean difference is big relative to the noise. The general shape is t = (difference in means) / (standard error of that difference). A big t means the signal dwarfs the noise. We use t rather than z because we estimate the SD from the sample, adding uncertainty (hence the fatter-tailed t distribution, indexed by df).

  • One-sample t: sample mean vs a known/hypothesised value. df = n − 1.
  • Independent-samples t: two separate groups' means (treatment vs control). df = n₁ + n₂ − 2. Assumes equal variances (use Welch's if not).
  • Paired (related) t: two scores from the same or matched subjects (pre vs post, twins). It works on the differences within each pair; df = (number of pairs) − 1. By using each subject as their own control it removes between-person variability, making it more powerful when pairing is sensible.
Trap

Independent vs paired t. Two different groups → independent. Same people measured twice (or matched pairs) → paired. Using an independent t on paired data throws away the pairing and loses power.

ANOVA — comparing three or more means

Why not just run many t-tests? Because each test carries a 5% false-positive risk and they pile up (inflated family-wise Type I error). ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) tests all means at once with a single test.

It works by partitioning variance. Total variability splits into between-groups variance (differences among the group means — the signal, driven by the IV) and within-groups variance (scatter of individuals inside each group — the noise, "error"). The test statistic is the F-ratio:

F = between-groups variance / within-groups variance = MSbetween / MSwithin

If H₀ is true (all means equal), both estimate the same error variance and F ≈ 1. A real effect inflates the numerator, pushing F > 1. A significant F says "at least one mean differs" — it does not say which, so you follow with post-hoc tests.

  • One-way ANOVA: one factor with ≥ 3 levels.
  • Factorial (e.g. two-way) ANOVA: two or more factors at once (drug × dose). Tests each factor's main effect plus their interaction.
  • Repeated-measures ANOVA: the within-subjects version (same people across ≥ 3 conditions/times). Adds the sphericity assumption (equal variances of the differences; corrected with Greenhouse-Geisser).
  • ANCOVA: ANOVA with a continuous covariate statistically removed first (e.g. comparing post-test scores while adjusting for pre-test), which reduces error variance and controls a confound.
  • MANOVA: ANOVA with multiple DVs tested together.
High-yield

Main effect vs interaction. A main effect is the effect of one factor averaged over the other ("the drug lowered anxiety overall"). An interaction means one factor's effect depends on the level of the other ("the drug helped young patients but not the old") — on a graph, non-parallel lines. Interactions are usually the interesting finding and override simple talk of main effects.

Post-hoc tests

If the ANOVA F is significant, the omnibus result tells you at least one mean differs but not which. Post-hoc pairwise tests localise the differences while controlling the family-wise error rate.

Pearson r & linear regression

Pearson's r measures the strength and direction of a linear association between two continuous variables, from −1 (perfect negative) through 0 (none) to +1 (perfect positive). r² (coefficient of determination) = the proportion of variance in one variable shared with (explained by) the other; r = .50 → r² = .25 = 25% of variance shared.

Linear regression fits a line to predict Y from X, Ŷ = a + bX, where b (slope) is the predicted change in Y per unit X and a is the intercept; the least-squares fit minimises the sum of squared residuals. Multiple regression uses several predictors (Ŷ = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + …); beware multicollinearity (check VIF). Logistic regression handles a binary outcome, modelling the log-odds and yielding odds ratios.

Chi-square (χ²) — for categorical data

Chi-square compares the frequencies you observed with the frequencies expected under H₀: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E. A big χ² means observed counts depart a lot from expectation.

  • Goodness-of-fit: does one categorical variable's distribution match an expected one (e.g. are births evenly spread across months)?
  • Test of independence: are two categorical variables associated (e.g. smoking × diagnosis in a contingency table)?

Assumptions: independent observations and expected count ≥ 5 in (almost) every cell. If a 2×2 table has any expected cell < 5, use Fisher's exact test. For paired categorical data (same people, before/after yes-no) use McNemar's test; for ≥ 3 related categorical measures, Cochran's Q.

Trap

Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired/related) vs Mann-Whitney U (independent groups) — same pairing logic as paired-vs-independent t. Also Kruskal-Wallis (independent, ≥ 3) vs Friedman (related, ≥ 3).

Correlation, regression & special coefficients

Correlation is not causation

A correlation only says two variables move together. It cannot tell you that X caused Y because of two perennial alternatives: reverse causation (Y → X) and the third-variable (confound) problem (Z drives both). Ice-cream sales correlate with drownings because summer heat drives both. Only a controlled experiment with randomisation licenses a causal claim.

Partial correlation

Partial correlation measures the association between X and Y after statistically removing the influence of a third variable Z from both. If the X–Y correlation collapses once you partial out Z, Z was likely the confound doing the work.

Regression vs correlation

They share machinery but answer different questions. Correlation is symmetric and unitless — it quantifies how tightly two variables relate (r), with no direction of prediction. Regression is directional — it builds an equation to predict Y from X (Ŷ = a + bX) and its slope b is in real units. r tells you the strength; regression lets you forecast and gives the line.

Trap

Correlation vs regression. r = strength of association, no units, symmetric (r of X with Y = r of Y with X). Regression = prediction equation with a slope in units, and X→Y is not the same line as Y→X. "r = 0" only rules out a linear relationship — a perfect U-shape has r ≈ 0. And note: r = +0.90 is a strong positive linear association, but r² = 0.81 means 81% of variance explained, NOT 90%.

Degrees of freedom — from scratch

The one statistics idea everyone memorises as "n − 1" and almost nobody actually gets. Built from zero with one idea you carry the whole way, so by the end you can look at any test and say why its df is what it is, instead of recalling a formula.

Definition

Degrees of freedom = how many numbers are still free to be anything, once you have already fixed some fact about them. Every fact you fix (a total, a mean, an estimate) locks one number down, so that number is no longer free. Count the values, subtract the facts you have pinned. That is it — everything below is just this sentence applied.

The phrase sounds technical but the idea is everyday. "Freedom" here means freedom to vary — to take any value. A degree of freedom is one independent number you could still change without breaking a rule you have agreed to. The moment a rule forces a number to be one specific value, it has lost its freedom and stops counting. The name comes from physics: a particle moving in 3D space has 3 degrees of freedom (x, y, z); pin it to a tabletop and it has 2; pin it to a wire and it has 1. Each constraint removes one direction it is free to move. Statistics borrows the word exactly.

The simplest possible example

Forget statistics. I tell you: "I'm thinking of three numbers, and they add up to 10." How many can you freely guess? You can pick the first — say 4. Still free. The second — say 3. Still free. Now the third is not a guess any more: it must be 3, because the three must sum to 10. The total forced the last number.

Three numbers, one rule (the total), so 2 are free and 1 is forced. Degrees of freedom = 3 − 1 = 2. The "−1" is the one fact you fixed. If I had said "n numbers with a fixed total," then n − 1 are free and the last is determined. df = n − 1, not because of a magic rule, but because fixing the total uses up exactly one number's freedom. The last value is the "dependent" one — it has to mop up whatever is needed to satisfy the rule.

A degree of freedom is spent by every quantity you estimate

In real statistics the "rule you fixed" is almost always this: you calculated a statistic from the data and then reused it. The most common one is the sample mean. To work out how spread out a sample is, you first need its mean — but the mean is computed from the same data. Once you know the mean of n scores, they are no longer all free; they must be consistent with that mean. Five exam scores with a mean of 70 must sum to 350. You are free to set the first four (60, 80, 75, 65 = 280); the fifth must be 70. Four free, one forced. df = 5 − 1 = 4.

Definition

General rule: df = (number of observations) − (number of parameters you estimated from those observations). Each parameter estimated from the data costs one degree of freedom, because using it imposes one constraint. Estimate one quantity (the mean) → lose 1 df. Estimate two (a slope and an intercept) → lose 2. This single principle predicts the df for every test.

Trap

df is not "the sample size." It is the sample size minus the estimates you have made. "With n = 20, the t-test df is 20" is wrong: a one-sample t uses the mean, so df = 19.

Why sample variance divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction)

Now you can reason it out instead of reciting it. For a whole population you divide by N, because you know the true mean and every score really is free: σ² = Σ(x − μ)² / N. But with a sample you do not know μ. You substitute the sample mean x̄, computed from the very same data, and x̄ sits, by construction, right in the middle of your sample — it hugs your data more tightly than the unknown true mean would. So the deviations from x̄ are, on average, a little too small. Divide by n and you systematically underestimate the real spread. Dividing by n − 1 instead makes the estimate a touch larger, exactly correcting that bias: s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1).

Definition

You divide by n − 1 not as a fudge but because only n − 1 of the deviations are free. Once x̄ is fixed, the deviations (x − x̄) must sum to zero, so the last is forced by the other n − 1. There are only n − 1 independent pieces of spread information, so you average over n − 1.

High-yield

NIMHANS depth — this is Bessel's correction. The estimator s² with n − 1 is unbiased for σ² (its long-run average equals the true variance); dividing by n gives a biased (too small) estimator. Subtlety they probe: s (the SD, the square root) is still slightly biased even after the correction, but s² is unbiased. The correction targets the variance.

Degrees of freedom in every test you will meet

Just apply "values minus estimates" to each test — it is the same idea every time.

Why df changes the shape of the curve

df is not just bookkeeping for picking a critical value — it literally controls the shape of the t and χ² distributions.

The t-distribution

With small df your estimate of the spread, s, is shaky — built from very few independent deviations, so it can land too low and make results look more impressive than they are. To protect against that, the t-distribution has fatter tails: it demands a bigger t-value before it believes an effect. As df grows, s becomes a trustworthy estimate of σ, the extra caution is no longer needed, and the t-distribution gets thinner and taller until, at large df, it is essentially the normal (z) distribution. That is why a t-table's values shrink down each column toward the z-values as df rises.

The χ² distribution

χ² is a sum of squared standard-normal pieces, and df is how many independent squared pieces are in the sum. More df = more things added = the distribution shifts right and spreads out. Facts they test: the mean of a χ² distribution equals its df, and its variance equals 2 × df.

High-yield

NIMHANS depth — the F-distribution (used in ANOVA) is a ratio of two χ²-type quantities, each divided by its own df, which is exactly why F needs two df values, numerator and denominator. Same logic, stacked.

Trap

The exact df flips MCQs use: (1) "df equals the sample size" — no, df = sample size minus parameters estimated. (2) Population variance uses N; sample variance uses n − 1. (3) Independent t is n₁ + n₂ − 2, not −1; paired t is n − 1 on the differences. (4) Chi-square independence is (r − 1)(c − 1), a product, not a sum — a 3×4 table is 2 × 3 = 6, not 5. (5) Pearson r and simple regression are n − 2, not n − 1. (6) In ANOVA, between + within df must sum to N − 1.

Qualitative & mixed methods

Qualitative research studies meaning and experience in words, not numbers. It is inductive (builds theory from data), naturalistic, and treats the researcher as the main instrument. Use it when the question is "how / what is it like / why" rather than "how much"; when the topic is new, sensitive, or poorly understood; or when you want lived experience and context.

Research ethics

Born from abuses (Nazi experiments → Nuremberg Code 1947; Tuskegee → the Belmont Report 1979; also Milgram and the Stanford Prison study; the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 onwards). Collected MCQ traps

Trap

Parametric vs non-parametric. Parametric (t, ANOVA, Pearson) needs normality + interval/ratio data and is more powerful when met; non-parametric (Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman, Spearman, χ²) is rank/count-based for ordinal, small, or non-normal data. Do not pick non-parametric "to be safe" if assumptions hold — you lose power.

Trap

Type I vs Type II. Type I (α) = false positive = reject a true H₀ (set, usually .05). Type II (β) = false negative = miss a real effect. Power = 1 − β. "Sigh, I missed it" = II.

Trap

Reliability vs validity. Reliability = consistency; validity = measures the right construct. Reliable-but-invalid is possible (a scale always 5 kg off); valid-but-unreliable is not (reliability caps validity, max validity = √reliability).

Trap

Mediator vs moderator. Mediator = mechanism, the how, sits between IV and DV (IV→M→DV). Moderator = condition, the for whom/when, shows up as an interaction. Me-diator = me-chanism.

Trap

Sampling error vs bias. Error = random, shrinks with n, centres on the truth. Bias = systematic, immune to n — a bigger biased sample is just more precisely wrong.

Trap

Correlation vs regression. r = strength of linear association, symmetric, unitless. Regression = directional prediction equation (Ŷ = a + bX) with a slope in units. r = 0 only rules out a linear relation (a U-curve has r ≈ 0). Correlation ≠ causation. r = +0.90 → r² = 0.81 = 81% of variance explained, not 90%.

Trap

Mean vs median under skew. The mean chases the tail. Positive skew → mean > median > mode; use the median for skewed data. The mean is the most outlier-sensitive average.

Trap

p-value. P(data this extreme | H₀ true), not P(H₀ true) and not "probability it was chance." Significance ≠ importance.

Trap

SD vs SE. SD = spread of individual scores; SE = SD/√n = spread of sample means, always smaller.

Trap

Random sampling vs random assignment. Sampling = who gets in (external validity); assignment = which condition (internal validity).

Trap

Stratified vs cluster sampling. Stratified = random within every subgroup (lowers error); cluster = pick whole subgroups at random (raises error, saves cost). Both are probability methods.

Trap

ANOVA aftermath. A significant F says "at least one mean differs," not which — run post-hoc (Tukey/Bonferroni/Scheffé) for the pairs.

Trap

Solomon four-group controls and measures pretest sensitisation. CONSORT = RCT reporting; PRISMA = systematic reviews/meta-analyses; STARD = diagnostic; COREQ = qualitative.

Worked intuition

What a t actually compares

t is a signal-to-noise ratio: the difference between means (signal) divided by the standard error of that difference (noise). If two group means differ by 6 points and the SE of the difference is 2, t = 3 — the gap is three times the typical sampling wobble, so it is hard to dismiss as chance. If the same 6-point gap came with an SE of 6, t = 1 — the gap is no bigger than the noise, so it is unconvincing. Bigger samples shrink the SE (SE = SD/√n), which is why the same mean difference becomes significant as n grows.

How ANOVA partitions variance

Picture three groups. Total variability in everyone's scores has two sources: scores differ because the group means differ (between-groups, the effect of the IV) and because individuals differ within their own group (within-groups, random error). ANOVA estimates a variance from each source. If the IV does nothing, both estimate the same error variance and their ratio F ≈ 1. If the IV matters, the between-groups estimate balloons and F climbs above 1.

Reading F and p

You compare your F to the F-distribution at the right df (between, within). A large F lands far in the right tail, giving a small p. p < .05 means: "if all the group means were truly equal, an F this large would happen less than 5% of the time" — surprising enough to reject H₀. But F only flags that something differs; the post-hoc tests and the effect size (η²) tell you which groups and how much.

What r² means

Square the correlation and you get the proportion of variance shared. r = .60 → r² = .36, so 36% of the variation in Y is accounted for by its linear relationship with X (and 64% is left to other factors). This is why a "moderate" r of .30 looks less impressive once squared (r² = .09 — only 9% of variance), a useful reality check against over-reading correlations.

Fast glossary

  • Alpha (α): the Type I error rate you set (usually .05); also Cronbach's α for internal consistency.
  • ANCOVA: ANOVA adjusting for a continuous covariate.
  • ANOVA: compares ≥ 3 means via F = between/within variance.
  • Attrition: non-random dropout, a threat to internal validity.
  • Beta (β): Type II error rate; power = 1 − β.
  • Bias (sampling): systematic error from method; not fixed by larger n.
  • Central Limit Theorem: sampling distribution of the mean → normal as n grows.
  • Chi-square (χ²): Σ(O − E)²/E; test on category counts.
  • Cohen's d: standardised mean-difference effect size (.2/.5/.8).
  • Confidence interval: plausible range for a parameter; 95% CI excluding 0 ↔ p < .05.
  • Confound: third variable tied to both IV and DV.
  • Construct validity: does the test tap the intended construct (convergent + discriminant).
  • Counterbalancing: varying condition order to cancel order effects.
  • Degrees of freedom: values free to vary; shapes t/χ²/F.
  • Discrimination index (D): upper − lower group proportion correct on an item.
  • Effect size: magnitude of an effect, independent of n.
  • Eta squared (η²): proportion of variance explained in ANOVA.
  • External validity: generalisability of findings.
  • F-ratio: between-groups variance / within-groups variance.
  • Internal validity: did the IV really cause the DV.
  • IQR: Q3 − Q1, robust spread.
  • Item difficulty (p): proportion answering an item correctly (high p = easy).
  • Kappa (κ): chance-corrected inter-rater agreement.
  • Kurtosis: peakedness/tailedness (lepto/meso/platy).
  • Likert: summated agreement-rating scale.
  • Mean: arithmetic average; outlier-sensitive.
  • Median: middle value; robust to skew.
  • Mediator: mechanism between IV and DV.
  • Mode: most frequent value; only centre for nominal.
  • Moderator: variable that changes the IV–DV relationship (interaction).
  • Nominal/Ordinal/Interval/Ratio: the four measurement levels (NOIR).
  • Null hypothesis (H₀): no effect/difference — what is tested.
  • p-value: P(data this extreme | H₀ true).
  • Power: 1 − β; chance of detecting a real effect.
  • Reliability: consistency of measurement.
  • r²: proportion of variance shared by two variables.
  • Standard deviation: typical distance from the mean (√variance).
  • Standard error: SD/√n; spread of sample means.
  • Skewness: asymmetry of a distribution.
  • Spearman-Brown: corrects split-half reliability for test length.
  • Type I / Type II error: false positive / false negative.
  • Validity: measuring the intended construct (test) or correctness of conclusions (study).
  • Variance: mean squared deviation from the mean.
  • z-score: (x − μ)/σ; SDs from the mean.
