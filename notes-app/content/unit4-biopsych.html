1. Neurons: structure, types & the neural impulse

The neuron is the information-processing and information-transmitting element of the nervous system. Everything else in this unit is about how neurons are built, how they fire, how they talk to each other, and how billions of them are wired into the brain. Master the neuron first and the rest of the unit becomes mechanism rather than memorisation.

Definition

Action potential — a brief electrical event that starts at the axon end nearest the cell body (the axon initial segment) and travels toward the terminal buttons. It is the basic message the axon carries.

Axoplasmic transport

Terminal buttons need molecules that can only be made in the soma, so substances are moved along the axon along microtubule "tracks". This is an active process, separate from the action potential.

  • Anterograde transport (soma → terminal): accomplished by the motor protein kinesin, which "walks" along microtubules carrying cargo. Remarkably fast, up to 500 mm/day.
  • Retrograde transport (terminal → soma): accomplished by the protein dynein; about half as fast as anterograde transport.

Types (classes) of neurons

  • Multipolar neuron — more than two processes extending from the cell body (the most common type).
  • Unipolar neuron — one process extending from the cell body (e.g. sensory dorsal-root neurons).
  • Bipolar neuron — two processes (e.g. in the retina).
  • Interneurons — neurons with a short axon or no axon at all; they integrate neural activity within a single brain structure.

Trap

Cell-body clusters are nuclei in the CNS but ganglia in the PNS; axon bundles are tracts in the CNS but nerves in the PNS. The paper loves to swap these.

Glial cells

There are roughly equal numbers of neurons and glia in the human brain. Four kinds matter:

  • Oligodendrocytes — paddle-shaped processes that wrap myelin around axons in the CNS. A single oligodendrocyte produces up to 50 segments of myelin. Myelin is 80% lipid, 20% protein and speeds axonal conduction.
  • Schwann cells — perform myelination in the PNS; one Schwann cell wraps one axon entirely. After PNS injury they digest dead axons and form cylinders that guide axonal regrowth, which is why PNS axons regenerate and CNS axons largely do not (the difference is in the supporting cells, not the axons).
  • Microglia — the brain's immune cells; multiply after injury, engulf debris and dead cells, and trigger inflammation.
  • Astrocytes — the largest glia, star-shaped; provide physical support, clean up debris, supply chemicals neurons need, gate substances between blood and CNS neurons, and contract/relax blood vessels to match local blood-flow demand.

The resting membrane potential

Recorded with intracellular microelectrodes, the inside of a resting neuron sits at about −70 mV relative to the outside. The neuron is then said to be polarized.

Four ions matter: organic anions (A−), chloride (Cl−), sodium (Na+) and potassium (K+). At rest there is more Na+ outside the cell and more K+ inside; organic anions are trapped inside (too big to cross the membrane). Na+ feels strong pressure to enter from both the concentration gradient and electrostatic (electrical) pressure, but Na+ channels are mostly closed at rest. Balance is held by:

  • The sodium–potassium pump — actively pushes 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in.
  • More open K+ channels than Na+ channels, but few K+ actually leave because the negative interior holds them in.
Mnemonic

Resting potential = "bow and arrow." The membrane has done its work in advance (taut bowstring) so the neuron can fire strongly and rapidly the instant it is stimulated.

The action potential, step by step

  • Depolarization to threshold: a stimulus reduces polarization toward zero. If it crosses the threshold of excitation (about −65 mV), voltage-dependent Na+ channels open wide.
  • Rising phase: Na+ rushes in explosively (driven by diffusion + electrostatic pressure). Membrane potential shoots from −70 mV to about +40 mV, overshooting zero to a reversed polarity.
  • Peak (~1 ms): Na+ channels become refractory (blocked) and cannot reopen until the resting potential is restored. The slower voltage-dependent K+ channels (which need more depolarization to open) are now open.
  • Falling phase: K+ flows out (driven by diffusion + electrostatic pressure since inside is now positive), returning the membrane toward −70 mV. It briefly overshoots (hyperpolarizes) until K+ channels close.
  • Restoration: the Na+/K+ pump removes the Na+ that leaked in and retrieves the K+ that leaked out.
High-yield

All-or-none law: a neuron either fires fully or not at all. The amplitude and velocity of the action potential are independent of stimulus intensity. To encode stronger stimuli the axon changes the rate/timing of firing (rate code), not the size of each spike.

The refractory period

  • Absolute refractory period — the membrane cannot produce another action potential, regardless of stimulation (Na+ channels closed).
  • Relative refractory period — a stronger-than-usual stimulus is needed (K+ is still flowing out faster than usual).

The refractory period is why action potentials normally travel one way. Conduction in the natural direction (soma → terminal) is orthodromic; an experimentally forced backward wave is antidromic.

Conduction & the myelin sheath

In the thinnest unmyelinated axons conduction is <1 m/s; increasing diameter raises it to about 10 m/s. Myelin (fats 80% / proteins 20%, found only on vertebrate axons) raises speed up to 100 m/s. The sheath is interrupted at nodes of Ranvier (~1 µm wide). Between nodes the signal travels passively and decrementally; at each node it regenerates. This jumping from node to node is saltatory conduction — faster and more energy-efficient than continuous conduction.

Postsynaptic potentials & integration

Neurotransmitters typically do one of two things to the receptive membrane:

  • EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential) — depolarizes (e.g. −70 → −67 mV), making firing more likely.
  • IPSP (inhibitory postsynaptic potential) — hyperpolarizes (e.g. −70 → −72 mV), making firing less likely.

EPSPs and IPSPs are graded (size matches stimulus strength), generated at the dendrites, travel passively, and are decremental (shrink with distance). They are summed (integration) two ways: spatial summation (many synapses at once) and temporal summation (one synapse firing repeatedly). If the sum reaches threshold at the axon initial segment, an action potential fires.

The synapse & synaptic transmission

A synapse is the junction where messages pass from one neuron to another. Classified by location: axodendritic, axosomatic, dendrodendritic, axoaxonic. By distance to target: directed (release site near the receptor) vs nondirected (released from varicosities at a distance).

  • Small neurotransmitters are synthesized in the terminal-button cytoplasm and packaged into vesicles by the button's Golgi complex; released in a pulse.
  • Large neurotransmitters (neuropeptides) are assembled in the soma on ribosomes, packaged by the soma's Golgi complex, and transported down microtubules (~40 cm/day); released gradually and continuously.
  • Coexistence: a neuron can contain two neurotransmitters.
  • Release = exocytosis. Three vesicle pools: release-ready (docked, <1%), recycling (10–15%), and reserve (85–90%). Low firing uses release-ready; faster firing recruits the recycling then reserve pools.

Receptors

A receptor is a protein with binding sites for particular neurotransmitters; a neurotransmitter that binds is its ligand. Two main types:

Autoreceptors respond to the neuron's own transmitter, usually on the terminal button, and are metabotropic. They do not change membrane potential; instead they regulate synthesis and release of the transmitter via G proteins.

Terminating the signal

  • Reuptake — transporters draw the transmitter back into the button for recycling.
  • Enzymatic degradation — e.g. acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in the postsynaptic membrane breaks ACh into choline and acetate.

Other forms of communication: electrical synapses (gap junctions, where membranes nearly touch and ions diffuse directly between cells, mostly dendrodendritic) and neuromodulators (mostly peptides, secreted in larger amounts, diffusing widely to modulate many neurons).

2. Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters fall into large-molecule (neuropeptides) and small-molecule classes. The small molecules are the workhorses of fast, directed synaptic transmission.

The families

  • Neuropeptides (large): pituitary peptides, hypothalamic peptides, brain-gut peptides, opioid peptides, and miscellaneous peptides.
  • Amino acids (small): glutamate, aspartate, glycine, GABA.
  • Monoamines (small): catecholamines (dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine) and indolamines (serotonin).
  • Acetylcholine — in a class of its own.
  • Unconventional: soluble gases (nitric oxide, carbon monoxide) and endocannabinoids (e.g. anandamide).

Amino acid neurotransmitters

These mediate the vast majority of fast, directed synapses in the CNS. Glutamate is the most prevalent excitatory transmitter in the mammalian CNS. GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid), synthesized by a simple modification of glutamate, is the most prevalent inhibitory transmitter (though excitatory at a few synapses).

High-yield

Glutamate = main excitatory; GABA = main inhibitory. GABA is literally made from glutamate.

Monoamine neurotransmitters

Each is synthesized from a single amino acid, is slightly larger than the amino acid transmitters, and has more diffuse effects. The cell bodies sit in small clusters mostly in the brain stem, with highly branched axons studded with varicosities that release transmitter diffusely.

  • Catecholamines — dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine; all synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine.
  • Indolamines — serotonin; synthesized from the amino acid tryptophan.

Acetylcholine (ACh)

Created by adding an acetyl group to a choline molecule. It is the transmitter at neuromuscular junctions, at many autonomic synapses, and at several CNS synapses. Neurons that release ACh are cholinergic. ACh is unusual in being deactivated mainly by enzymatic degradation (by AChE) rather than reuptake.

Unconventional neurotransmitters

  • Soluble gases (nitric oxide, carbon monoxide): produced in the cytoplasm and immediately diffuse through membranes (they are lipid-soluble) into nearby cells, where they trigger a second messenger and are quickly deactivated. Involved in retrograde transmission.
  • Endocannabinoids: similar to THC (the psychoactive compound of marijuana); the commonest is anandamide (from Sanskrit ananda, "eternal bliss"). Synthesized on demand from membrane fats, released from dendrites and soma, and act mostly on presynaptic neurons to inhibit subsequent transmission.

3. Structure & functions of the nervous system

The nervous system divides into the central nervous system (CNS) — brain and spinal cord — and the peripheral nervous system (PNS) — the nerves and ganglia outside it. Gray matter is mostly cell bodies and unmyelinated interneurons; white matter is mostly myelinated axons.

The spinal cord

A long conical structure whose principal jobs are to distribute motor fibres to effector organs (glands and muscles) and to collect somatosensory information for the brain. It also has its own reflexive control circuits (a degree of autonomy from the brain).

  • Protected by the vertebral column (24 vertebrae across cervical, thoracic, lumbar regions plus the fused sacral and coccygeal portions). The cord passes through the spinal foramen of each vertebra.
  • Early in development cord and column are the same length; the column then grows faster, displacing the spinal roots downward. The most caudal roots travel farthest and form the cauda equina.
  • 31 paired dorsal and ventral roots fuse to become spinal nerves. Dorsal root axons are sensory (afferent) unipolar neurons with cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglia. Ventral root neurons are motor (efferent) multipolar neurons with cell bodies in the ventral horns.
  • Cross-section: an inner H-shaped core of gray matter surrounded by white matter.

The meninges

Three protective membranes cover the CNS: the tough outer dura mater, the web-like arachnoid mater, and the delicate pia mater hugging the cortex. The subarachnoid space (between arachnoid and pia) is filled with cerebrospinal fluid.

High-yield

Afferent = inward, toward the CNS (sensory). Efferent = outward, away from the CNS to muscles/glands (motor). Dorsal root = sensory; ventral root = motor.

4. Divisions of the nervous system (CNS, PNS & the autonomic nervous system)

The peripheral nervous system

  • Cranial nerves: 12 pairs attached to the ventral surface of the brain, mostly serving sensory and motor functions of the head and neck. The exception is the vagus nerve (X) ("wandering"), which regulates organs throughout the thoracic and abdominal cavities. Olfactory information enters via the olfactory bulbs, not a typical cranial nerve route.
  • Spinal nerves: begin at the junction of dorsal and ventral roots, then travel out to muscles and sensory receptors.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS)

"Autonomic" = self-governing. It regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands — the body's "vegetative processes." It has two divisions, sympathetic and parasympathetic.

Sympathetic division (thoracolumbar)

  • Involved in expenditure of energy (fight-or-flight). Cell bodies of its motor neurons lie in the gray matter of the thoracic and lumbar cord — hence thoracolumbar.
  • Preganglionic axons exit via ventral roots and synapse on postganglionic neurons in the sympathetic ganglion chain; postganglionic neurons project to target organs (intestines, stomach, kidneys, sweat glands).
  • Controls the adrenal medulla (which resembles a sympathetic ganglion), driving release of epinephrine and norepinephrine.
  • Neurotransmitters: preganglionic terminals release acetylcholine; postganglionic terminals on target organs release norepinephrine.

Parasympathetic division (craniosacral)

  • Supports conservation/storage of energy — salivation, gastric and intestinal motility, secretion of digestive juices, increased GI blood flow.
  • Preganglionic cell bodies sit in the nuclei of some cranial nerves (especially the vagus) and the sacral cord — hence craniosacral. Ganglia lie near the target organs, so postganglionic fibres are short.
  • Neurotransmitter: both pre- and postganglionic terminals release acetylcholine.

5. Divisions of the human brain & their functions

The brain develops from the neural tube: the rostral end becomes the forebrain, with the midbrain and hindbrain behind it. In adult mammals the forebrain neocortex surrounds the mid- and hindbrain.

Forebrain — telencephalon: the cerebral cortex

The cerebral cortex surrounds the hemispheres like bark on a tree and is heavily convoluted, with sulci (small grooves), fissures (large grooves) and gyri (bulges). These convolutions triple the cortical surface area (~2360 cm²). Because cell bodies dominate the surface it looks grayish — gray matter; the myelinated axons beneath give white matter.

Four lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital. Three primary sensory areas receive input: the visual cortex, auditory cortex and somatosensory cortex. The association cortex (the bulk of the cortex) does everything between sensation and action — perceiving, learning, remembering, planning, acting. The rostral region handles movement/planning; the caudal region handles perceiving and learning. Sensory association cortex near a primary area handles one modality; far from it, it integrates several.

Forebrain — telencephalon: limbic system & basal ganglia

  • Neocortex = the cortex covering most of the hemispheres ("new" cortex, recent in evolution). The limbic cortex lies around the medial edge ("limbus" = border).
  • Limbic system: Papez (1937) proposed an interconnected circuit for motivation and emotion; MacLean (1949) expanded it and coined "limbic system," noting its evolution coincided with emotional responses. Key members: the amygdala (emotion) and the hippocampus (learning and memory).
  • Basal ganglia: a collection of nuclei beneath the cortex (under the anterior lateral ventricles), involved in the control of movement. Parkinson's disease results from degeneration of midbrain neurons that project to the caudate nucleus and putamen; symptoms include weakness, tremor, limb rigidity, poor balance and difficulty initiating movement.

Forebrain — diencephalon

Sits between the telencephalon and midbrain; made of the thalamus and hypothalamus.

  • Thalamus: two lobes joined by the massa intermedia. Most input to the cortex is relayed through it. Key relay nuclei: the lateral geniculate nucleus (from the eye), the medial geniculate nucleus (from the inner ear), and the ventrolateral nucleus (from the cerebellum to primary motor cortex).
  • Hypothalamus: lies under the thalamus at the base of the brain. Controls the autonomic and endocrine systems and organizes survival behaviours. The pituitary gland attaches via the pituitary stalk; a portal blood system connects the hypothalamus to the anterior pituitary. Neurosecretory cells release hypothalamic hormones that drive the anterior pituitary, and the hypothalamus produces the posterior-pituitary hormones oxytocin and vasopressin (made by hypothalamic neurons whose axons end in the posterior pituitary).

Midbrain (mesencephalon)

Surrounds the cerebral aqueduct; two parts:

  • Tectum (dorsal): the superior colliculi (visual-motor; orient the body toward/away from visual stimuli) and inferior colliculi (auditory).
  • Tegmentum (beneath the tectum): contains the rostral reticular formation, eye-movement nuclei, the periaqueductal gray (mediates the analgesic effects of opioids), the red nucleus and substantia nigra (both sensorimotor), and the ventral tegmental area. The substantia nigra projects to the caudate and putamen.

Hindbrain

Surrounds the fourth ventricle; two divisions:

  • Metencephalon — the cerebellum and pons.
    • Cerebellum: receives visual, auditory, vestibular and somatosensory information plus data on individual muscle movements; integrates these and smooths/coordinates motor output. Attached to the pons by the superior, middle and inferior cerebellar peduncles. Damage impairs standing, walking and coordinated movement.
    • Pons ("bridge"): contains part of the reticular formation (important in sleep and arousal) and a large nucleus relaying cortex-to-cerebellum information.
  • Myelencephalon — the medulla oblongata, containing reticular-formation nuclei that control vital functions: the cardiovascular system, respiration and skeletal muscle tone.

6. The motor (sensorimotor) system

Three organising principles

  • Hierarchical organisation: association cortex → secondary motor cortex → primary motor cortex → brain stem/spinal circuits.
  • Motor output is guided by sensory input. The only movements normally not guided by sensory feedback are ballistic movements — brief, all-or-none, high-speed (e.g. swatting a fly).
  • Learning changes the nature and locus of control — practiced sequences become automatic and shift to lower levels.

Sensorimotor association cortex

  • Posterior parietal association cortex: integrates information about the location of body parts and external objects, directs behaviour with spatial information, and directs attention. It receives from the visual, auditory and somatosensory systems and outputs to dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, secondary motor areas and the frontal eye field. Damage produces deficits in spatial perception/memory, reaching and grasping, eye-movement control and attention — the most striking being apraxia and contralateral neglect.
  • Dorsolateral prefrontal association cortex: receives from posterior parietal cortex and projects to secondary and primary motor cortex and the frontal eye field. The decision to initiate voluntary movement may be made here.

Secondary motor cortex

Receives mostly from association cortex and outputs to primary motor cortex. Areas: the supplementary motor area, the premotor cortex, and the later-identified cingulate motor areas. Mirror neurons (in the ventral premotor area) fire both when performing a goal-directed hand movement and when observing the same movement in another.

Primary motor cortex

Located in the precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe; the major point of convergence and departure of cortical sensorimotor signals. Modern evidence suggests neurons encode the target/goal of a movement rather than just its direction. Large lesions may disrupt independent movement of a body part, produce astereognosia, and reduce speed, accuracy and force — but do not eliminate voluntary movement, thanks to parallel descending pathways.

Cerebellum & basal ganglia in motor control

  • Cerebellum: compares three inputs — descending motor signals, brain-stem motor nuclei, and somatosensory/vestibular feedback — and corrects movements that drift off course. Important in motor learning, especially timing-critical sequences. Diffuse damage costs control of direction, force, velocity and amplitude, causes tremor on attempted movement, and disturbs balance, gait, speech and eye movement.
  • Basal ganglia: a heterogeneous collection of nuclei in cortical loops (cortex → basal ganglia → thalamus → cortex), modulating movement. Important in habit learning — gradual, trial-by-trial motor learning.

Descending motor pathways

Two descend in the dorsolateral region of the cord and two in the ventromedial region. Betz cells are the extremely large pyramidal neurons of primary motor cortex.

  • Dorsolateral tracts: terminate in the contralateral half of one cord segment, often directly on motor neurons; activate distal muscles (e.g. fingers).
  • Ventromedial tracts: more diffuse, innervate interneurons on both sides across several segments; activate proximal trunk and limb muscles (e.g. shoulders).

Skeletal muscles & the neuromuscular junction

  • Flexors move a limb toward the body; extensors ("antigravity muscles") move it away.
  • Extrafusal fibres — served by alpha motor neurons; provide the muscle's motive force. An alpha motor neuron + its axon + its extrafusal fibres = a motor unit.
  • Intrafusal fibres (muscle spindles) — specialised stretch-sensitive sensory organs served by sensory and gamma motor neuron axons; their contraction tunes the sensitivity of the spindle's afferent to stretch. They are muscle-length detectors.
  • Golgi tendon organs — stretch receptors in tendons that encode how hard the muscle is pulling (tension, not length).

A muscle fibre is a bundle of myofibrils of overlapping actin and myosin; the overlap produces the striations of striated muscle. At the neuromuscular junction, ACh from the terminal button depolarizes the motor endplate, producing an endplate potential (much larger than a neuronal EPSP) that always fires the fibre. Depolarization opens voltage-dependent Ca²⁺ channels; calcium acts as a cofactor letting myofibrils extract energy from ATP, triggering a twitch. Contraction strength depends on the average firing rate of the motor units.

7. Sensory systems — vision

Sensory receptors are specialised neurons that detect physical events and, through sensory transduction, convert them into receptor potentials (changes in membrane potential). Most sensory receptors lack axons; their receptor potential modulates neurotransmitter release onto the dendrites of the next neuron.

Anatomy of the eye

The eye sits in the bony orbit, moved by six extraocular muscles. Light enters through the transparent cornea, with the opaque white sclera forming the outer coat elsewhere. The amount of light is set by the pupil (an opening in the pigmented iris). The lens (onion-like transparent layers) changes shape via the ciliary muscles to focus near or far objects — accommodation. Light passes through the vitreous humor to the retina, which is part of the brain (CNS), as is the optic nerve.

The retina & photoreceptors

Three cellular layers: photoreceptive, bipolar-cell, and ganglion-cell. Light must pass through the (transparent) overlying layers to reach the photoreceptors at the back.

  • ~120 million rods and ~6 million cones.
  • Cones: daytime vision, fine detail (highest acuity), and colour. The fovea (central retina, most acute vision) contains only cones.
  • Rods: do not detect colour and give poor acuity, but are far more sensitive to light. In very dim light we use rod vision, so we are effectively colour-blind and lack foveal vision.
  • The optic disk (where ganglion-cell axons leave via the optic nerve) is the blind spot — no receptors there.
  • Signal flow: photoreceptors → bipolar cells → ganglion cells (whose axons form the optic nerve). Horizontal and amacrine cells combine messages laterally.

Transduction

Transduction begins with a photopigment embedded in the lamellae of the outer segment. The rod photopigment rhodopsin = an opsin protein (rod opsin) + retinal. When light hits rhodopsin it splits into rod opsin and retinal, triggering a cascade that hyperpolarizes the photoreceptor. Photoreceptors are therefore "darkness detectors": they depolarize in darkness (releasing more glutamate) and hyperpolarize in light. Bipolar cells respond to glutamate two ways: ON-center cells are hyperpolarized by glutamate, OFF-center cells are depolarized. Photoreceptors and bipolar cells do not fire action potentials; they signal by graded changes in transmitter release.

From retina to cortex

  • Ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve, which meets at the optic chiasm. Axons from the nasal (inner) halves of each retina cross; axons from the temporal (outer) halves stay on the same side.
  • They reach the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus, which has six layers: layers 1, 4, 6 from the contralateral eye; layers 2, 3, 5 from the ipsilateral eye. The inner two layers (large cells) are magnocellular; the outer four (small cells) are parvocellular.
  • LGN axons travel via optic radiations to primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe, also called striate cortex for its dark-staining striation. V1 maps the contralateral visual field, devoting ~25% of itself to the fovea, and is organised into layers, cytochrome-oxidase (CO) blobs and modules. CO blobs (layers 2/3, faintly 5/6) handle colour; interblob regions handle orientation, movement and binocular disparity. Magnocellular input arrives at sublayer 4Cα, parvocellular at 4Cβ, koniocellular at sublayers 2/3.
  • Beyond V1 lies the extrastriate cortex (V2–V5), arranged hierarchically. Visual processing then splits into two streams: the ventral stream (V2 pale/thin stripes → V4 → inferior temporal cortex; the "what" pathway) and the dorsal stream (V2 thick stripes → posterior parietal cortex; the "where"/movement pathway). V5/MT (medial temporal) specialises in movement and can receive magnocellular input directly from V1.
High-yield

LGN layers: 1-4-6 contralateral, 2-3-5 ipsilateral; inner two = magnocellular, outer four = parvocellular. Ventral stream = "what" (temporal), dorsal stream = "where" (parietal).

8. Auditory & somatosensory systems

The auditory system

The three physical dimensions of sound map onto perception: amplitude → loudness, frequency → pitch, complexity → timbre.

Path of sound: outer ear → auditory canal → vibrates the tympanic membrane (eardrum) → the three middle-ear ossicles (malleus/hammer, incus/anvil, stapes/stirrup) → the oval window → fluid in the snail-shaped cochlea → the organ of Corti (the auditory receptor organ). The auditory system is organised tonotopically (by frequency).

Ear to auditory cortex

Auditory-nerve axons synapse in the ipsilateral cochlear nuclei → superior olives on both sides → (via the lateral lemniscus) the inferior colliculi → medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus → primary auditory cortex. Unlike the single major visual pathway, audition uses a network of pathways.

Sound localisation

  • Medial superior olives — respond to slight differences in the time of arrival of signals at the two ears.
  • Lateral superior olives — respond to slight differences in amplitude between the ears.
  • Both project to the superior and inferior colliculi. The deep layers of the superior colliculus (auditory input) form a map of auditory space; the superficial layers (visual input) are organised retinotopically.

Auditory cortex & coding

Primary auditory cortex = three adjacent areas (the core), ringed by the belt (secondary) and then parabelt areas. It is organised in functional columns and tonotopically. Two streams: the anterior pathway → prefrontal cortex identifies sounds (what); the posterior pathway → posterior parietal cortex locates sounds (where). Pitch coding uses both place coding (different frequencies flex different parts of the basilar membrane) and rate coding (pitch proportional to firing frequency). Bilateral lesions cause temporary deafness then permanent loss of sound localisation and frequency discrimination; unilateral lesions disrupt localisation only in contralateral space.

The somatosensory system

Body sensations are somatosensations, served by three interacting systems:

  • Exteroceptive — external stimuli on the skin, with divisions for mechanical (touch), thermal and nociceptive (pain) stimuli.
  • Proprioceptive — body position, from receptors in muscles, joints and organs of balance.
  • Interoceptive — internal conditions (e.g. temperature, blood pressure).

Mechanoreceptors (encapsulated receptors and free nerve endings) detect touch and pressure. Thermoreceptors are free nerve endings of two types: cold sensors just beneath the epidermis, warmth sensors deeper; sensation is relative except at extremes. Pain (nociception) uses networks of free nerve endings; at least three nociceptor types: high-threshold mechanoreceptors (intense pressure), receptors for extreme heat/acids/capsaicin, and TRPA1 receptors (pungent irritants — mustard oil, horseradish, tear gas). The brain can reduce pain via endogenous opioids.

Somatosensory pathways & cortex

  • Axons enter the CNS via spinal nerves; the body area served by one segment's dorsal roots is a dermatome. Face/head input enters via the trigeminal nerve (V).
  • Two major ascending pathways: the dorsal-column medial-lemniscus system (touch and proprioception) and the anterolateral system (pain and temperature).
  • Penfield (1937) mapped primary somatosensory cortex (SI, in the postcentral gyrus) and found it is somatotopic — organised as a map of the body surface, the somatosensory homunculus. SII lies just ventral to SI, receives most input from SI and (unlike SI) substantial bilateral input. SI/SII are organised in columns; their output goes to posterior parietal association cortex. A dorsal stream → posterior parietal cortex (multisensory integration, attention); a ventral stream → SII (object-shape perception).

Somatosensory deficits

Damage to SI is often mild (parallel pathways). Unilateral SI lesions cause minor contralateral deficits — reduced light-touch detection and reduced stereognosis (recognising objects by touch). Agnosias:

  • Astereognosia — inability to recognise objects by touch.
  • Asomatognosia — failure to recognise parts of one's own body (usually left side; damage to right temporal/posterior parietal lobe), often with anosognosia (failure to recognise one's own symptoms) and a component of contralateral neglect.

9. Chemical & vestibular senses

Olfaction (smell) and gustation (taste) are the chemical senses — they monitor the chemical content of the environment. The vestibular sense (in the inner ear) monitors balance and head position and feeds the cerebellum and sound-localisation circuits.

Olfaction

Olfactory receptor cells sit in the olfactory mucosa in the upper nose. Their dendrites lie in the nasal passages; their axons pass through the cribriform plate into the olfactory bulbs, where they synapse on neurons in clusters called olfactory glomeruli (these project via the olfactory tracts). There is mirror symmetry between the two bulbs, and the layout of glomeruli is consistent across members of a species — a poorly understood chemotopic map.

Each bulb projects to the medial temporal lobe — the amygdala and the piriform cortex (the primary olfactory cortex). Two pathways leave the amygdala-piriform area: one to the limbic system (the emotional response to odours), one via the medial dorsal thalamus to the orbitofrontal cortex (conscious perception of odours).

High-yield

Olfaction is the only sensory system whose major pathway reaches the cortex without first passing through the thalamus.

Gustation

Taste receptor cells occur in clusters of 50–100 called taste buds, often on raised papillae (only the larger papillae carry buds). One presynaptic cell per bud synapses onto the carrying neuron; the other cells communicate via gap junctions. There appears to be one receptor protein per taste receptor cell, and taste receptor proteins also occur in the throat, oesophagus and lungs.

Pathway: gustatory afferents leave via the facial (VII), glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagus (X) nerves (front of tongue, back of tongue, back of oral cavity respectively) → solitary nucleus of the medulla → ventral posterior nucleus of the thalamus → primary gustatory cortex in the insula → secondary gustatory cortex in the orbitofrontal cortex. Projections are mainly ipsilateral.

Damage to the chemical senses

  • Anosmia — inability to smell. Commonest neurological cause: a blow to the head displacing the brain and shearing the olfactory nerves at the cribriform plate.
  • Ageusia — inability to taste; rare because taste travels via three separate nerves. Partial ageusia of the anterior two-thirds of the tongue on one side can follow middle-ear damage (the facial nerve's gustatory branch passes through the middle ear).

The vestibular sense

Housed in the inner ear alongside the cochlea, the vestibular apparatus detects head position and movement and contributes to balance. Vestibular information is one of the inputs the cerebellum integrates to coordinate movement, and the vestibular and somatosensory systems feed motor feedback. The auditory nerve (VIII) carries both a vestibular branch and an acoustic branch.

10. Hunger & thirst

Regulatory mechanisms

A regulatory mechanism has four features: a system variable (the quantity regulated), a set point (its optimal value), a detector that monitors it, and a correctional mechanism that restores it. The system runs on negative feedback. Because there is a delay between eating/drinking and replenishment, behaviour is also controlled by satiety mechanisms — brain mechanisms that monitor the correctional behaviour (e.g. drinking) rather than the system variable itself.

Thirst

Body fluid is intracellular and extracellular (the extracellular includes intravascular blood plasma and interstitial fluid). Two kinds of thirst:

  • Osmometric thirst — when the solute concentration of interstitial fluid rises, drawing water out of cells by osmosis. Osmoreceptors (neurons whose firing depends on their hydration) detect cell shrinkage. They lie in the lamina terminalis, in two circumventricular organs — the OVLT (organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis) and the SFO (subfornical organ), both outside the blood–brain barrier so blood-borne substances reach them. Most osmoreceptors are in the OVLT, connected to the anterior cingulate cortex (whose activity tracks felt thirst).
  • Volumetric thirst — when blood plasma volume falls (hypovolemia from blood loss, vomiting, diarrhoea). It produces a salt appetite. The kidneys detect reduced blood flow and trigger the hormone angiotensin, which initiates drinking and salt appetite, conserves water and salt, and raises blood pressure. Atrial baroreceptors (stretch-sensitive cells in the heart's atria) also detect low blood volume.

Integration: the SFO is where blood angiotensin acts; SFO neurons project to the median preoptic nucleus, which integrates OVLT and baroreceptor (via the solitary tract) information and controls drinking. Damage here can cause adipsia (lack of drinking).

Hunger: the metabolic background

Two nutrient reservoirs:

  • Short-term reservoir — glycogen stored in liver and muscle cells.
  • Long-term reservoir — adipose tissue (fat), storing triglycerides (glycerol + three fatty acids).

Signals to start a meal

  • Ghrelin — a peptide hormone released by the GI tract (mainly the stomach) during the fasting phase; binds hypothalamic receptors to stimulate eating. It also activates dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (reward).
  • Hypoglycemia (low blood glucose) is a potent hunger stimulus; liver receptors trigger glucoprivic hunger, relayed to the brain via the vagus nerve. The brain monitors glucose inside the blood–brain barrier; the liver monitors glucose and fatty acids outside it.

Signals to stop a meal (satiety)

  • Sensory and gastric: tasting/swallowing plus stomach distension; falling ghrelin after a meal is a gastric satiety factor.
  • Intestinal: the duodenum secretes cholecystokinin (CCK) in response to fats — it contracts the gallbladder, constricts the pylorus and inhibits gastric contractions. Peptide YY₃₋₃₆ (PYY) is released by the small intestine after a meal in proportion to calories consumed.
  • Liver: nutrients arriving from the intestines signal satiety; insulin also tells the hypothalamus to stop eating.
  • Adipose: leptin is secreted by well-nourished fat cells and acts as an anti-obesity hormone with long-term effects on metabolism and eating.

Brain mechanisms of eating

  • Brain stem: the dorsal medulla (area postrema + nucleus of the solitary tract) receives taste from the tongue and visceral signals from stomach, duodenum and liver, relaying to the forebrain.
  • Lateral hypothalamus triggers eating via orexigenic signals: melanin-concentrating hormone (MCH), orexin, and Neuropeptide Y (NPY) (NPY injections cause ravenous eating; NPY neurons sit in the arcuate nucleus and co-release AGRP, a long-lasting orexigen). Endocannabinoids (and THC) are also orexigenic.
  • Ventromedial hypothalamus suppresses eating. Leptin binds receptors on the NPY/AGRP neurons; the arcuate nucleus also has anorexigenic neurons secreting CART and α-MSH (α-MSH binds MC4 receptors to inhibit eating).
High-yield

Lateral hypothalamus = "go eat" (orexigenic: NPY, AGRP, orexin). Ventromedial hypothalamus = "stop eating" (anorexigenic: leptin, CART, α-MSH).

11. Emotions

The amygdala, hypothalamus and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) are central to processing emotion.

Three components of an emotional response (fear)

  • Behavioural — muscular movements appropriate to the situation.
  • Autonomic — facilitate the behaviour and mobilise energy quickly.
  • Hormonal — reinforce autonomic responses; the adrenal medulla secretes epinephrine and norepinephrine, and the adrenal cortex secretes steroid hormones that make glucose available to muscles.

These components are controlled by separate neural systems, but their integration is controlled by the amygdala.

Amygdala & vmPFC

The amygdala organises behavioural, autonomic and hormonal responses to situations producing fear, anger or disgust, and is involved in the effects of odours/pheromones on sexual and maternal behaviour. It receives inputs from the olfactory system, temporal-lobe association cortex, frontal cortex and the rest of the limbic system; it outputs to frontal cortex, hypothalamus, hippocampal formation and brain-stem nuclei controlling autonomic and species-typical behaviours. Extinction of conditioned emotional responses involves inhibitory control of the amygdala by the vmPFC.

Aggression

Aggression can be an actual attack or just threat behaviours (warning postures); threatened animals show defensive or submissive behaviours. These are modulated by the hypothalamus and amygdala; the periaqueductal gray is involved in defence and predation.

  • Serotonin inhibits risk-taking and aggression. Low CSF 5-HIAA (a serotonin metabolite) correlates with more aggression in monkeys; destroying forebrain serotonergic axons enhances aggression, and drugs that boost serotonin reduce it.
  • Hormonal control: androgens mainly affect offensive attack (not defensive, which females also show), with organisational and activational effects mediated by the medial preoptic area. In humans androgens may primarily increase motivation for dominance, with aggression secondary.

vmPFC, impulse control & moral judgement

The vmPFC governs emotional reactions and impulse control; lesions cause impulsive behaviour and outbursts. The amygdala (anger/violence) matures before the prefrontal cortex (which suppresses violence). Decreased prefrontal and increased subcortical activity accompany impulsive violence; antisocial personality disorder shows reduced prefrontal gray-matter volume. The vmPFC is activated in moral judgements involving conflict between utilitarian and personal moral choices; people with vmPFC damage make more utilitarian judgements.

Communication & recognition of emotion

  • Darwin proposed emotional expressions are innate; Ekman's cross-cultural work (isolated New Guinea tribe) and the similar expressions of blind and sighted children support this.
  • Recognising others' expressions relies more on the right hemisphere; recognising particular faces uses different circuits than recognising expressions. The amygdala is needed to recognise fearful faces and receives visual input from a subcortical route (superior colliculus + pulvinar) and a cortical route — hence affective blindsight (recognising emotional expressions without conscious sight after visual-cortex damage). The fusiform face area (fed by the parvocellular system) recognises faces; basal ganglia/insular damage disrupts recognition of disgust; right somatosensory damage impairs facial-emotion recognition (impaired imitation).
  • Laterality & laughter: the anterior cingulate gyrus handles the motor aspects of laughter; humour appreciation involves the right vmPFC. Emotional vs volitional facial paresis are complementary syndromes (one can smile to an emotion but not on command, or vice versa). The left half of the face tends to be more expressive.

12. Hormonal regulation of behaviour

Definition

Hormones (Greek horman, "to excite") are chemicals secreted by one group of cells and carried in the bloodstream to act on specific target tissues. Many are made by endocrine glands (Greek endon "within" + krinein "to secrete").

The first endocrine experiment was carried out in 1849 by Arnold Adolph Berthold (likely replicating John Hunter's earlier work).

Types of chemical communication

  • Endocrine — hormone released into the bloodstream to affect distant targets.
  • Synaptic (neurocrine) — transmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft; highly localised.
  • Autocrine — released chemical acts on the releasing cell itself (e.g. autoreceptors).
  • Paracrine — diffuses to nearby target cells; strongest effect on the nearest.
  • Pheromone — released into the environment to affect other individuals of the same species.
  • Allomone — affects individuals of another species.

Organising principles of hormones

  • Hormones act gradually (hours to weeks) and change the intensity or probability of behaviours rather than switching them on/off.
  • One hormone has multiple effects; one behaviour can be affected by many hormones; hormones interact.
  • Secreted in small amounts, often in pulses (pulsatile secretion); levels vary rhythmically over the day under circadian clocks.
  • A hormone affects only cells with receptor proteins that recognise it. Its chemical structure is similar across vertebrates, though its function can vary by species.

Neural vs hormonal communication

Both are secretory; both often act via second messengers on receptor binding. The hypothalamic neurosecretory (neuroendocrine) cells are neurons in every way except that their terminals release hormones into the bloodstream rather than transmitter into a synapse — blurring the line between neurons and endocrine cells. Some peptide hormones occur even in single-celled organisms, suggesting a shared evolutionary origin.

Hormones influence cell growth and activity (e.g. without thyroid hormones, fewer brain cells form and mental development is stunted). Secretion is regulated by negative feedback: the hormone's output feeds back to inhibit further release.

Specific hormone systems

  • Adrenal cortex: mineralocorticoids (chiefly aldosterone, which makes kidneys retain sodium and conserve water) and sex steroids (notably androstenedione, contributing to adult body hair; excess in females causes a more masculine appearance).
  • Thyroid gland (below the larynx): thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) = the thyroid hormones; calcitonin promotes calcium deposition in bone. Disorders: goiter (swelling from iodine deficiency) and cretinism / congenital hypothyroidism (reduced stature and intellectual disability from early thyroid deficiency).
  • Gonads: the hypothalamus releases GnRH, driving the anterior pituitary to release the gonadotropins FSH and LH, which drive steroid production in testes and ovaries. Kisspeptin stimulates GnRH neurons and helps govern the onset of puberty; gonadotropin-inhibiting hormone opposes it.
  • Social behaviour: oxytocin is released during orgasm in both sexes and (with vasopressin) modulates social bonding/contact in rodents.
  • Endocrine pathology: parathyroid deficiency → calcium deposition in the basal ganglia and schizophrenia-like symptoms; chronic glucocorticoid excess → Cushing's syndrome (fatigue, depression, hirsutism, autonomic changes).

13. The glandular (endocrine) system

Two types of glands:

  • Exocrine glands (e.g. sweat glands) release chemicals into ducts that carry them to body surfaces.
  • Endocrine (ductless) glands release hormones directly into the circulatory system.

Three classes of vertebrate hormone (gland view)

  • Amino acid derivatives — e.g. epinephrine from the adrenal medulla, synthesized from tyrosine.
  • Peptides and proteins — chains of amino acids (peptide = short, protein = long).
  • Steroids — synthesized from cholesterol; all the sex hormones are steroids.

Sex steroid hormones

Two main gonadal classes: androgens (commonest = testosterone) and estrogens (commonest = estradiol). A third class, progestins (commonest = progesterone), prepares the uterus and breasts for pregnancy in females; its male function is unclear (possibly sperm metabolism).

The pituitary — the "master gland"

Called the master gland because most of its hormones are tropic hormones that control the release of hormones from other glands (e.g. gonadotropin drives the gonads). It is really two glands fused in development:

  • Anterior pituitary — releases the tropic hormones. Controlled by the hypothalamus through the hypothalamo-pituitary portal system (a vascular network).
  • Posterior pituitary — releases vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) and oxytocin. These peptide hormones are made in the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus, transported down axons to the posterior pituitary, and released when action potentials arrive (the cells are neurosecretory cells). Oxytocin drives uterine contraction in labour and milk ejection during suckling; vasopressin promotes water reabsorption by the kidneys; both influence stress-coping and social responses.

How hormone release is regulated

  • Neural signals — all endocrine glands except the anterior pituitary are directly regulated by the nervous system. Pituitary and pineal glands are regulated by cerebral neurons; glands outside the CNS are innervated by the ANS (often both sympathetic and parasympathetic with opposite effects).
  • Hormonal signals — the anterior pituitary's tropic hormones drive their target glands; circulating hormones feed back to the pituitary, hypothalamus and brain (negative feedback maintaining stable blood levels).
  • Non-hormonal chemicals — glucose, calcium and sodium levels influence release (e.g. rising blood glucose increases insulin, which lowers glucose).
  • Pulsatile release — hormones are released in pulses, giving large minute-to-minute fluctuations in circulating levels.

14. Sleep & biological clocks

Measuring & staging sleep

Three standard measures define sleep stages: the EEG (electroencephalogram), the EOG (electrooculogram, eye movements) and the neck EMG (electromyogram, muscle tone). Slow-wave sleep shows large slow EEG waves; REM sleep shows fast desynchronised activity, rapid eye movements and near-total muscle relaxation.

During REM, cerebral blood flow is high in the extrastriate (visual association) cortex but low in the striate cortex and prefrontal cortex; dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is implicated in remembering dreams.

Dreaming

External stimuli can be incorporated into dreams. Most people report dreams when woken from REM, less so from slow-wave sleep. Sleeptalking can occur in any stage (often at the transition to wakefulness); sleepwalking occurs in slow-wave sleep and never in REM (when muscles are relaxed). The activation-synthesis hypothesis holds that REM supplies the cortex with largely random information and the dream is the cortex's attempt to make sense of it.

Theories & effects of deprivation

  • Recuperation theories: being awake disrupts homeostasis and sleep restores it.
  • Adaptation theories: sleep results from an internal 24-hour timing mechanism — we are programmed to sleep at night.
  • Slow-wave deprivation harms cognition more than physical activity. REM sleep facilitates non-declarative learning; slow-wave sleep facilitates declarative learning. After REM deprivation there is a rebound (extra REM on recovery nights). Fatal familial insomnia (inherited) damages the thalamus, causing attention/memory deficits, autonomic and endocrine loss of control, raised body temperature and insomnia.

Sleep-promoting substance: adenosine

Prolonged wakefulness lowers brain glycogen, raising extracellular adenosine (a nucleoside neuromodulator with an inhibitory effect). Accumulating adenosine acts as a sleep-promoting substance — inhibiting wakefulness-promoting regions and producing the cognitive/emotional effects of sleep deprivation.

Neural control of arousal

At least five transmitter systems regulate arousal:

  • Acetylcholine — pontine and basal-forebrain groups produce cortical activation/desynchrony; a medial-septum group controls the hippocampus. ACh release is highest during wakefulness and REM.
  • Norepinephrine — from the locus coeruleus (dorsal pons), projecting widely; catecholamine agonists like amphetamine cause arousal and sleeplessness.
  • Serotonin — from the raphe nuclei (reticular formation); stimulation causes locomotion and cortical arousal.
  • Histamine — from the tuberomammillary nucleus (TMN) of the hypothalamus; directly increases cortical activation (which is why antihistamines cause drowsiness).
  • Orexin (hypocretin) — from the lateral hypothalamus, projecting to nearly every arousal region. Its loss causes narcolepsy.

Flip-flop circuits

  • Sleep/wake flip-flop: slow-wave sleep begins when neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic area (vlPOA) become active and inhibit the wakefulness systems; those systems inhibit the vlPOA in return, creating a bistable switch. Adenosine tips it toward sleep; orexin holds it in the "waking" state.
  • REM flip-flop: the sublaterodorsal nucleus (SLD) in the dorsal pons is the REM-ON region; the ventrolateral periaqueductal gray (vlPAG) is the REM-OFF region. The REM flip-flop can only switch to REM when the sleep/wake flip-flop is in the "sleeping" state. SLD neurons excite spinal inhibitory interneurons to produce the muscular paralysis that stops us acting out dreams.

Disorders of sleep

  • Insomnia: primary (difficulty falling/staying asleep) and secondary (due to pain, substances, or another condition). Treated with CBT, relaxation, sleep hygiene, and hypnotic/benzodiazepine/antihistamine drugs. Can be caused by sleep apnea (treated with surgery or a device to keep the airway open).
  • Narcolepsy: sleep (or its components) at inappropriate times. Symptoms: sleep attacks, cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations. Linked to orexin loss / a chromosome-6 orexin-receptor gene mutation, sometimes after autoimmune attack of orexin neurons. Treated with stimulants, antidepressants or modafinil.
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder: a neurodegenerative disease damages REM paralysis mechanisms so the patient acts out dreams; treated with a benzodiazepine.
  • Slow-wave parasomnias: bedwetting (nocturnal enuresis), sleepwalking (somnambulism), night terrors (pavor nocturnus), and sleep-related eating — most common in children, usually outgrown.

Biological clocks

Circadian rhythms (Latin circa "about" + dies "day") cycle about every 24 hours (sleep/wake, activity, body temperature). Zeitgebers ("time-givers") are external cues such as light or clocks that entrain them. The suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) of the hypothalamus drive circadian (not homeostatic) regulation: they receive light via the photopigment melanopsin and relay through the subparaventricular zone and dorsomedial hypothalamus. The "ticking" lies in individual neurons — interlocking feedback loops of protein production/breakdown (~24 h). Mutations cause shifts: a per2 mutation (chromosome 2) → advanced sleep phase syndrome (4-h advance); a per3 mutation (chromosome 1) → delayed sleep phase syndrome (4-h delay).

Seasonal rhythms: at night the SCN signals the pineal gland to secrete melatonin. Prolonged winter melatonin drives the winter phase of annual cycles; melatonin also helps synchronise circadian rhythms (useful for shift work, jet lag and blind people for whom light is no zeitgeber).

15. Genetics & behaviour

Chromosomes & sex-linked traits

Genes lie on chromosomes — threadlike DNA structures in the nucleus; an allele controlling a trait sits at the same location. Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes (XX or XY).

  • Sex-linked traits are influenced by genes on the sex chromosomes; virtually all are on the X chromosome (the Y is small and carries few genes).
  • X-linked dominant traits occur more often in females; X-linked recessive traits occur more often in males (males have only one X, so a single recessive gene is expressed; females need two). Example: colour-blindness.
  • Sex-limited traits are visible only in one sex (e.g. X-linked hereditary prostate cancer).
  • Chromosomal abnormality: the commonest autosomal one is Down syndrome, caused by trisomy 21.

Behavioural genetics research methods

  • Family studies: assume blood relatives share more genes, so the more shared genetics, the more similar the traits. Use a genogram (a pictorial display of family relationships and medical history) to map hereditary patterns.
  • Twin studies: compare similarity/concordance of traits between monozygotic (identical) twins (share 100% of genes) and dizygotic (fraternal) twins (share ~50%). If a trait is genetic, MZ twins show higher concordance. Drawback: MZ twins often share the same environment.
  • Adoption studies: study twins reared apart to separate genes from environment — e.g. the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart (Bouchard et al.), with 59 MZ and 47 DZ pairs reared apart (plus matched pairs reared together), ages 19–68. Result: adult identical twins were substantially more alike on all psychological dimensions than fraternal twins, regardless of rearing environment. Bouchard estimated the heritability of IQ at .70.
Definition

Heritability estimate — not a statement about an individual's development, but a numerical estimate of the proportion of variability in a trait, in a particular study, attributable to genetic variation in that study.

Genes and experience

Genetic differences can promote psychological differences by influencing experience. When a gene leads an individual to seek experiences that amplify the gene's effects, it has a multiplier effect. Turkheimer (2000) concluded that nearly all human behavioural traits are highly heritable (typically .40–.70), that the particular family environment contributes little to behavioural diversity, while other experiences contribute significantly. In a sample of 7-year-old twins, IQ heritability was low among very poor families but high among affluent families.

Genomic methods

  • Linkage studies compare known DNA markers with a trait across family members (e.g. the Huntington's disease gene was found near a known marker on chromosome 4).
  • Genome-wide association studies compare whole or partial genomes across individuals to correlate genetic differences with diseases/traits.
  • Targeted mutations are lab-made mutated genes inserted into mice: knockout genes (defective, no functional protein), knock-in genes (new/replacement protein), and conditional knockouts (gene switched off when the animal is given a drug, so it develops normally first).
  • Antisense oligonucleotides are modified RNA/DNA strands that bind specific mRNA and block protein production (the trapped mRNA is then destroyed). Example: antisense oligonucleotides destroying sex-hormone receptors in the VMH of female rats inhibited female sexual behaviour.

16. Invasive & non-invasive research methods in biopsychology

Neuroanatomy & histology (invasive)

Histological methods (histo = body tissue) study the structure of brain tissue by fixing, slicing and staining it:

  • Fixation: tissue is preserved (against autolytic enzymes and microbes) in a fixative, usually formalin (an aqueous solution of formaldehyde), which cross-links proteins to strengthen the fragile tissue. Perfusion first removes blood and replaces it with saline.
  • Sectioning: the fixed brain is sliced with a microtome (knife + mounting platform + advancing mechanism) or a cryostat (a microtome inside a freezer for very cold cutting).
  • Staining: slices are placed in chemical solutions, then covered with mounting medium and a coverslip. Key stains: the Golgi stain (silver chromate; Camillo Golgi found it stains whole neurons black, showing individual neurons in silhouette for the first time — used to study neuron shape); the Nissl stain (Franz Nissl, 1880s; commonest dye cresyl violet, also methylene blue; binds cell bodies to count neurons).

Tracing methods

  • Anterograde (forward) tracing: chemicals taken up by cell bodies and transported forward to terminal buttons — traces axons projecting away from an area.
  • Retrograde (backward) tracing: chemicals taken up by terminal buttons and transported back to cell bodies — traces axons projecting into an area.

Lesion methods

  • Aspiration lesions — accessible cortical tissue drawn off by suction through a fine glass pipette.
  • Radio-frequency lesions — heat from a high-frequency current passed through a stereotaxically positioned electrode destroys tissue.
  • Knife cuts — a precise cut eliminates conduction in a nerve or tract without extensive damage.
  • Reversible lesions — temporarily eliminate activity by cooling the structure or injecting an anaesthetic (e.g. lidocaine); a non-destructive alternative.
High-yield

General principle: the behavioural effects of unilateral lesions are usually much milder than bilateral lesions (with notable exceptions).

Recording & neurochemical methods

  • Microelectrode (single-unit) recording: very fine-tipped wires record the activity of individual neurons; implanted via stereotaxic surgery. Signals are tiny and must be amplified.
  • Localising receptors: autoradiography (a radioactive ligand binds receptors; a photographic emulsion localises them) vs immunocytochemistry (dye-tagged antibodies to a receptor protein — no radioactivity, and individual labelled cells can be seen under a microscope).
  • Microdialysis: measures neurotransmitter amounts released in a brain region using a probe with a dialysis membrane permeable to some molecules.
  • Electron microscopy: a transmission electron microscope reveals tiny structures (synaptic vesicles, organelles); a scanning electron microscope shows 3-D at lower magnification; a confocal laser scanning microscope images inside thick or living tissue (cells stained with fluorescent dye).

Imaging methods

Neuroimaging is either anatomical (structure/size) or functional (activation during a task). The three main techniques are CT, PET and MRI.
