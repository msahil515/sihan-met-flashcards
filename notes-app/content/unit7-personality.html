Unit 7 — Personality, Emotion & Motivation

The whole of personality from first principles — the psychodynamic founders (Freud, Jung, Adler) and the neo-Freudians, the trait and type measurers (Allport, Cattell's 16PF, Eysenck's PEN, the Big Five and beyond), the humanistic-existential self (Maslow, Rogers, May), and the behavioural / social-cognitive theorists (Bandura, Rotter, Mischel, Seligman, Kelly) — then how personality is assessed, and finally the affective and developmental engine behind it: theories of emotion, stress and the GAS, theories of motivation, attachment and emotional-social development, and the exploratory/sensation-seeking drive. 1. Freud & the psychoanalytic approach

The one idea. Freud saw the psychological world as a series of opposing tensions (for example the tension between selfishness and society). Underlying every tension were inner pressures that strive for relief. The motor of the whole system is instinct: the German term is Trieb — a driving force or impulse. Instincts are the basic elements of personality, the motivating forces that drive behaviour and determine its direction.

Instincts and motivation

  • Life instincts (Eros). Serve the survival of the individual and the species by satisfying needs for food, water, air and sex. They are oriented toward growth and development. The psychic energy of the life instincts is the libido (Latin for desire/lust).
  • Cathexis. The libido can be attached to, or invested in, objects — Freud called this cathexis.
  • Death instincts (Thanatos). In opposition to the life instincts, Freud postulated a destructive/death instinct. One component is the aggressive drive — the wish to die, turned outward against objects other than the self.

Levels of personality (the topographic model)

LevelWhat it holds
ConsciousAll sensations and experiences in immediate awareness.
Preconscious / subconsciousThe storehouse of memories that can be brought to awareness.
UnconsciousThe most important level and the major portion of personality; holds instincts, drives, desires and emotionally charged memories.

The structure of the mind (the structural model)

  • Id — operates on the pleasure principle; strives solely to satisfy desires and reduce inner tension. It can only act through reflex or through wish-fulfilling hallucinatory/fantasy experience, which Freud called primary-process thought.
  • Ego — based on the reality principle; the structure that develops to deal with the real world using perception, recognition, judgement and memory (secondary-process thought).
  • Superego — based on the morality principle; represents society's demands. Through praise/punishment children learn good and bad: punished behaviours form the conscience; praised behaviours form the ego-ideal.

When the ego and especially the superego do not do their job, elements of the id slip out as parapraxes — mistakes that reveal the unconscious. Freudian slips (errors in speech or writing) are more common when a person is tired or distracted, because defences are down and unconscious impulses surface.

Definition

Fixation. An arresting of part of the libido at an earlier psychosexual stage because of excessive frustration or gratification, leaving a characteristic adult personality pattern.

Anxiety and the defence mechanisms

For Freud, anxiety is a feeling of fear and dread without an obvious cause. He proposed three types: reality anxiety (fear of tangible dangers), neurotic anxiety (id–ego conflict), and moral anxiety (id–superego conflict). When anxiety builds, the ego deploys defence mechanisms:

DefenceWhat it does
RepressionUnconscious denial of the existence of something that causes anxiety
DenialDenying an external threat or traumatic event
Reaction formationExpressing an id impulse that is the opposite of the true one
ProjectionAttributing a disturbing impulse to someone else
RegressionRetreating to an earlier, less frustrating period and acting childishly
RationalizationReinterpreting behaviour to make it more acceptable, less threatening
DisplacementShifting id impulses from a threatening/unavailable object to an available substitute
SublimationDiverting instinctual energy into socially acceptable behaviours
Mnemonic

Three anxieties map to the three conflicts: Reality = external world; Neurotic = id vs ego; Moral = id vs superego. "RNM = world, ego, conscience."

2. Neo-Freudians: Jung, Adler, Horney, ego & object relations

Carl Jung — analytical psychology

Jung divided the psyche into three parts:

  • Conscious ego — the conscious aspect of personality; embodies the sense of self. Personal identity (ego) develops around age 4.
  • Personal unconscious — thoughts/feelings not currently conscious, including material that is merely unimportant now and material actively repressed because it is ego-threatening.
  • Collective unconscious — a deeper, transpersonal layer made of powerful emotional symbols, the archetypes — emotional patterns common to all people, formed from the beginnings of human time, that predispose us to react in predictable ways to recurring stimuli.

Common archetypes

  • Anima (the female element in a man) and animus (the male element in a woman).
  • Persona (Latin for "mask") — the socially acceptable front we present to others.
  • Shadow — the dark, unacceptable side: shameful desires and motives we would rather not admit.
  • Self — represents unity, integration and harmony of the total personality; striving toward this wholeness is the ultimate goal of life (involves balancing all parts).
  • Mother — generativity and fertility, evoked by an actual or figurative mother figure.
  • Hero — the strong, good force that battles the enemy to rescue another. Its opposite is the demon (cruelty and evil).

Complexes, libido and energy

  • A complex is a group of emotionally charged feelings, thoughts and ideas organised around a particular theme (e.g. power, status) in the personal unconscious. Its strength is set by its libido ("value"); complexes may be conscious or unconscious, and those out of conscious control can intrude on consciousness. Jung substantiated complexes with his word-association test, measuring response delays, respiration, galvanic skin response and memory on retest.
  • Jung used libido in two ways: a diffuse general life energy, and (like Freud) a narrower psychic energy that fuels the psyche — but for Jung this is not necessarily sexual.
  • Three principles of psychic energy: opposition (conflict between opposing tendencies generates energy), equivalence (energy withdrawn from one activity is transferred elsewhere), and entropy (a tendency toward balance/equilibrium — the ideal is equal distribution over all structures).

Functions, attitudes and individuation

  • Functions: rational = sensing & thinking; irrational = feeling & intuiting.
  • Attitudes: extroversion (libido directed at the external world) and introversion (inward focus). Two attitudes × four functions = eight psychological types, the basis of the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI).
  • Individuation — psychological health from integrating all conscious and unconscious facets. Steps: confront the unconscious → dethrone the persona → accept our dark sides → accept our anima/animus → transcend.

Alfred Adler — individual psychology

  • Jung coined "complex," but the inferiority complex is Adler's. Overwhelming helplessness can produce it; a person may fabricate a compensating superiority complex. Spoiling and neglect by parents can both cause inferiority feelings.
  • A central core of personality is the striving for superiority (perfection striving).
  • Organ inferiority — everyone is born with some physical weakness; at this "weak link" disease takes root and the body tries to compensate elsewhere. Adler also added an aggression drive and the masculine protest (both boys and girls strive to become independent of, and equal to, the powerful adults around them; drives can be expressed directly or reversed).
  • Fictional finalism — people strive toward imagined future goals ("fictional goals").
  • Three fundamental life tasks everyone must address: occupational (career), societal (friendship/social networks; expressed through social interest — the innate potential to cooperate toward personal and societal goals), and love (finding a life partner).

Style of life — a unique character structure through which each of us strives for perfection, created by the creative power of the self. Basic styles: dominant/ruling (extreme types attack others, become sadists/delinquents/sociopaths; less virulent become alcoholics, drug addicts or suicides), getting (dependent, expects to receive), avoiding (sidesteps problems to avoid failure), and the healthy socially useful type.

Karen Horney

  • Rejected Freud's penis envy; argued women's inferiority feelings stem from upbringing and an overemphasis on securing a man's love. Proposed womb envy — men's unconscious envy of women's ability to bear children.
  • Childhood is dominated by the safety need (security and freedom from fear). Security depends on parents; lack of warmth and affection weakens it.
  • Basic anxiety — a child's feeling of being alone, helpless and insecure, arising from problems in the parental relationship. In reaction, the person settles into one of three modes of relating: passive, aggressive or withdrawn styles.
  • Aspects of the self: the Real Self (inner core, our potential for self-realisation, damaged by parental neglect), the Despised Self (perceptions of inferiority based on others' negative evaluations), and the Ideal Self (a "tyranny of the should" — a composite of all our shoulds). Alienation from the Real Self breeds neurosis and an interpersonal coping strategy; these ideas led to modern "possible selves."
  • Neurotic needs / neurotic trends — irrational defences against anxiety that become permanent personality features, grouped into three categories of behaviour and attitude toward oneself and others (the basis of the moving-toward / against / away grouping).

Ego psychology & object relations

  • Anna Freud brought the ego into focus, emphasising the social environment while keeping the ego linked to id and superego.
  • Heinz Hartmann (1894–1970), sometimes called the founder of ego psychology: the ego is neither controlled by the id nor fully autonomous; it can direct self-preserving but short-term-unpleasant behaviour and functions independently to cope with society's demands.
  • Object relations theories — "object relations" = the mental representation of significant others. The self is socially constructed through specific interpersonal interactions, not emerging purely through biological development.
  • Margaret Mahler — studied symbiosis: some children could not form emotional ties (shutting off from the world); symbiotic psychotic children formed ties so strong they could not form a sense of self. Healthy ties with the mother (the normal symbiotic child) are vital.
  • Melanie Klein — studied the infant's reaction to the removal of the mother's breast (the first source of satisfaction); the infant blames the mother, then resolves the conflict by understanding the mother's love is not just her breast. Extended by Stephen Mitchell into the relational perspective.
  • Heinz Kohut — extended Adler and Klein: a key problem for anxious people is fear of losing an important love object (often the parent), which can produce narcissistic personality disorder (feeling powerless and dependent yet projecting bravado and self-aggrandisement).

3. Erik Erikson — lifespan identity & identity crises

Where Freud held personality fixed by age 5–6, Erikson argued that identity formation is a lifelong process. Personality (he focused on identity) develops through eight stages; the outcome of each stage depends partly on the previous one, and successful negotiation of each ego crisis is essential for optimal growth. His first five stages parallel Freud's psychosexual stages.

4. Trait & type approaches

Jung's extroversion–introversion (as a trait) & the MBTI

For Jung, extroversion = an orientation toward things outside oneself; introversion = turning inward to explore one's feelings. The MBTI, based on Jung, measures introversion/extroversion plus three sub-scales: Sensation–Intuition (realism vs imagination), Thinking–Feeling (logical/objective vs personal/subjective), and Judgment–Perception (structured/judgmental vs flexible/perceptive).

Gordon Allport — trait psychology

"Personality is the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine … characteristic behavior and thought" (Allport, 1961). Allport considered personality discrete/discontinuous — almost two personalities, one for childhood (driven by primitive urges/reflexes) and one for adulthood (more psychological). Traits are predispositions to respond similarly to different stimuli; they are real, cause behaviour, can be shown empirically, are interrelated, and vary with the situation.

  • Individual traits (later relabelled personal dispositions) are unique to a person; common traits are shared by many (e.g. members of a culture).
  • Three grades: cardinal traits (the most pervasive, powerful, dominating traits), central traits (the handful of outstanding traits describing a person), and secondary traits (least important, displayed inconspicuously and inconsistently).
  • Functional autonomy — the motives of mature, healthy adults are no longer functionally connected to the prior experiences in which they first appeared. Perseverative functional autonomy governs low-level routine behaviours; propriate functional autonomy governs values, self-image and lifestyle (the proprium = the ego/self). Propriate functioning follows three principles: organising the energy level, mastery and competence, and propriate patterning (striving for consistency and integration). Allport described seven stages of development of the proprium from infancy to adolescence.

Raymond Cattell — 16PF

  • Defined traits as relatively permanent reaction tendencies, the basic structural units of personality. Distinguished common traits (in everyone to some degree) from unique traits (in one or a few).
  • Used source traits (the underlying factors, obtained by factor analysis) to derive 16 basic personality factors, assessed by the Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire (16PF).
  • Three data sources: Q-data (self-report questionnaires), T-data (objective test/observational data from controlled test situations), and L-data (life-record data, e.g. school records).

Hans Eysenck — the PEN model

Using factor analysis Eysenck identified three dimensions: E — Extraversion vs introversion; N — Neuroticism vs emotional stability; P — Psychoticism vs impulse control (superego functioning). Biologically, extraverts have a lower base level of cortical arousal than introverts, so they actively seek excitement and stimulation; introverts shy away because their cortical arousal is already high.

Trap

Eysenck's arousal logic is counter-intuitive: extraverts are under-aroused and seek stimulation; introverts are over-aroused and avoid it. Cattell = 16 factors; Eysenck = 3 (PEN); the Big Five = 5.

McCrae & Costa — the Five-Factor Model (Big Five / OCEAN)

Emerged from extensive factor analyses of personality-describing adjectives and of personality tests/scales:

  • O — Openness (to experience/culture/intellect): imaginative, witty, original, artistic vs shallow, plain, simple.
  • C — Conscientiousness (lack of impulsivity; early research called it "Will"): cautious, dependable, persevering, organised vs careless, disorderly, undependable.
  • E — Extroversion (surgency): energetic, enthusiastic, dominant, sociable, talkative vs shy, retiring, submissive, quiet.
  • A — Agreeableness: friendly, cooperative, trusting, warm vs cold, quarrelsome, unkind.
  • N — Neuroticism (emotional instability): nervous, high-strung, tense, volatile, moody, worrying vs calm, contented.
Mnemonic

OCEAN = Openness, Conscientiousness, Extroversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.

Extensions of the trait model

  • Ashton & Lee — HEXACO: a six-factor model adding Honesty-Humility (H) to Emotionality, eXtraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness and Openness.
  • Paulhus & Williams — the Dark Triad (2002): Narcissism (extreme selfishness, inflated sense of one's abilities, constant need for admiration), Machiavellianism (the need to manipulate others through cunning, deceit and unscrupulous behaviour), and Psychopathy (callous, insensitive, egocentric, antisocial, takes advantage of others using charm and often violence).

Type approaches

The idea of types is that there are discrete classes of people. Types are usually just the first step toward a fuller understanding of traits. The classic application is the assessment of people prone to heart disease — Type A versus Type B (see Stress).

The motivational approach to traits — Henry Murray

Henry Murray proposed needs related to personality, including the need for Achievement (n Ach), Affiliation (n Aff), Power (n Power) and Exhibition (n Exh).

5. Humanistic approach: Maslow & Rogers

Abraham Maslow — the hierarchy of needs

Maslow proposed a hierarchy of five innate needs that activate and direct behaviour, described as instinctoid (having a hereditary component). From strongest/lowest to weakest/highest: physiological → safety → belongingness and love → esteem → self-actualization. Lower needs must be at least partially satisfied before higher needs become influential. Outside the hierarchy sit the cognitive needs: the need to know and the need to understand.

  • Self-actualization — the highest need: the maximum realisation and fulfilment of one's potentials, talents and abilities.
  • Lower needs are deficit (deficiency, D) needs — failing to satisfy them produces a deficit/crisis; higher needs are growth (being, B) needs — their satisfaction improves health, happiness, contentment, fulfilment and longevity, but failing them produces no crisis.
  • Physiological/safety needs arise in infancy; belongingness/esteem in adolescence; self-actualization not until midlife.

The study of self-actualizers

  • Self-actualizers run on metamotivation (B-motivation / Being-motivation) — concerned with fulfilling potential; others run on D-motivation (Deficiency) — striving to make up for something lacking.
  • Metaneeds are states of being (goodness, uniqueness, perfection) rather than specific goal objects; failing to satisfy them produces metapathology.
  • Conditions for self-actualization: freedom from social/self constraints, not distracted by lower needs, security in self-image and relationships (able to love and be loved), and realistic self-knowledge.
  • Barriers: lower needs unmet; inadequate education or improper child-rearing (the ideal is a balance of permissiveness and regulation); and the Jonah complex — fear that maximising our potential will lead to a situation we cannot cope with. Self-actualization requires courage, discipline and self-control.

Carl Rogers — self / actualization theory

  • The goal of life is self-actualization, an innate tendency toward growth, part of a larger actualizing tendency spanning all physiological and psychological needs.
  • The organismic valuing process evaluates experiences by how well they serve the actualizing tendency: growth-promoting experiences are sought, hindering ones avoided.
  • Positive regard — the need for acceptance, love and approval, especially from the mother in infancy. Unconditional positive regard is granted freely; conditional positive regard ties love to behaviour. Internalised, regard becomes positive self-regard.
  • Conditions of worth (akin to the Freudian superego) — seeing ourselves as worthy only under conditions acceptable to our parents; we avoid perceptions opposing them.
  • Incongruence develops between the self-concept and threatening behaviours; we defend against anxiety by denying threatening parts of the experiential field.
  • The fully functioning person (peak of development): awareness of all experiences with no conditions to defend against, the ability to live fully in each moment, trust in oneself, a sense of freedom and personal power, and creativity and spontaneity.

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan)

A contemporary outgrowth of Maslow's self-actualization. People have an innate tendency to express their interests and develop their capabilities, facilitated by focusing on intrinsic motivation (doing an activity for the interest and challenge of the activity itself). SDT specifies three basic needs whose satisfaction yields well-being: competence (mastering difficult tasks), autonomy (basing one's actions on one's own interests, needs and values) and relatedness (a close connection with others).

6. Existential approach: Rollo May

  • Being-in-the-world (Dasein) — existentialists take a phenomenological approach: we exist in a world best understood from our own perspective. Dasein (German, "to exist there") expresses the basic unity of person and environment.
  • Alienation — the illness of our time, in three areas: separation from nature, lack of meaningful interpersonal relations, and alienation from one's authentic self. We experience three simultaneous modes: Umwelt (the environment, biological drives, natural law), Mitwelt (relations with other people), and Eigenwelt (our relationship with our self).
  • Nonbeing — awareness of self as a living, emerging being leads to dread of not being (nonbeing/nothingness). Death is the most obvious avenue, but nonbeing also appears as blind conformity or generalized hostility.
  • Anxiety — arises when one's existence or a value identified with it might be destroyed. Freedom cannot exist without anxiety. Normal anxiety is proportionate to the threat and can be confronted consciously; neurotic anxiety is disproportionate, involves repression and is managed by blocking off awareness.
  • Guilt — arises when we deny our potentialities, fail to perceive others' needs, or ignore our dependence on nature. Both anxiety and guilt are ontological (about the nature of being). Three forms of ontological guilt correspond to the three modes: separation guilt (Umwelt — separation from nature), guilt of Mitwelt (inadequacy with others), guilt of Eigenwelt (denial/failure of one's own potentialities).
  • Intentionality — the structure that gives meaning to experience and lets people make decisions about the future. Care is an active state in which something matters, the opposite of apathy; without care there is no love, and care is also the source of will ("the capacity to organize one's self so that movement toward a goal may take place").
  • Four forms of love (Western tradition): Sex (biological release of sexual tension), Eros (psychological desire for procreation/creation through enduring union, built on care and tenderness — the salvation of sex), Philia (intimate non-sexual friendship that takes time to grow), and Agape (altruistic, disinterested esteem for the other's welfare). Eros is built on philia; philia needs agape.
  • Freedom and destiny — healthy people both assume their freedom and face their destiny. Freedom of doing (existential freedom) = acting on one's choices; freedom of being (essential freedom) = the inner possibility of changing. Destiny = "the design of the universe speaking through the design of each one of us"; within its boundaries we have the power to choose, but it does not permit any change we wish.
  • The power of myth — myths are the stories that unify a society; for May they are archetypal patterns comparable to Jung's collective unconscious.
  • Psychopathology — for May, apathy and emptiness (not anxiety and guilt) are the malaise of modern times; disturbed people deny their destiny, lose their freedom, and erect neurotic symptoms to renounce freedom. He saw psychopathology as a lack of communication.

7. Behavioural & social-cognitive: Bandura, Rotter, Mischel

B. F. Skinner — the behavioural baseline

The strict behavioural view treats personality as a set of learned response tendencies shaped by reinforcement and punishment, with no need to invoke inner traits — the backdrop against which Bandura, Rotter and Mischel added cognition.

Albert Bandura — social/observational learning

  • Observational learning (modelling). Rather than reinforcing ourselves for each action, we learn through vicarious reinforcement — observing others' behaviour and its consequences. Learning can occur through observation/example, not only direct reinforcement.
  • The Bobo doll experiment. Preschool children watched an adult hit and kick an inflatable Bobo doll while shouting "Sock him in the nose!"; left alone with the doll they imitated the aggression. The experimental group was about twice as aggressive as a control group not exposed to the model. Watching a model perform usually-suppressed behaviour is disinhibition (an online form is trolling).
  • Three factors influencing modelling: characteristics of the models (those similar in appearance/sex/age and of high status are more effective; simple behaviours are imitated more readily than complex ones); characteristics of the observers (people low in self-confidence/self-esteem imitate more); and the reward consequences (seeing a model rewarded or punished affects imitation).
  • Four processes of observational learning: attentional → retention → production → incentive/motivational processes.
  • Self-reinforcement — administering rewards/punishments to oneself for meeting one's own standards.
  • Self-efficacy — our feeling of adequacy, efficiency and competence in coping with life. Low self-efficacy → feeling helpless and unable to control events; high → believing one can deal effectively with situations. Four sources of self-efficacy: performance attainment (mastery experiences), vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological/emotional arousal — each suggesting a way to raise efficacy (reachable goals, good models, encouragement, and managing arousal through diet/stress reduction/exercise).

Walter Mischel — the person-situation debate

Mischel challenged the consistency of broad traits across situations (the person–situation debate), arguing behaviour is highly situation-specific. His later Cognitive-Affective Personality System (CAPS) reframes personality as stable if…then behavioural signatures — a person behaves consistently within similar situations even if they look inconsistent across different ones.

Julian Rotter — social learning & locus of control

  • Behaviour depends on both outcome expectancy (how strongly we expect performance to bring a positive result) and reinforcement value (how much we value the expected reinforcement). The set of relevant behaviours available is the behaviour potential.
  • Generalized vs specific expectancies. Specific expectancies attach a reward to a behaviour in a particular situation; generalized expectancies span a group of situations and give the stable, situationally consistent characteristics we call personality. Out-of-character behaviour can arise from specific expectancies.
  • Rotter described six psychological needs developing out of biological needs: recognition-status, dominance, independence, protection-dependency, love and affection, and physical comfort. Reinforcement value is judged relative to other available reinforcers; behaviour potential, outcome expectancy and reinforcement value combine into the psychological situation.
  • Locus of control (LOC). An internal LOC is the generalized expectancy that one's own actions lead to desired outcomes; an external LOC attributes outcomes to chance or powerful others. Later work found three orthogonal dimensions: internality, luck/chance and powerful others. Internals are more achievement-oriented and higher achievers; externals are less independent and more likely to be depressed and stressed.

8. Cognitive approaches: Kelly, Lewin, Seligman, Kohlberg

Roots: Gestalt and Kurt Lewin

  • Gestalt psychology (influential in 1920s Germany): humans seek meaning, organise sensations into meaningful perceptions, and complex stimuli are not reducible to the sum of their parts.
  • Kurt Lewin — came out of the Gestalt tradition but focused on personality and social psychology. His field theory (1935) centres on the life space — all internal and external forces acting on an individual — and on contemporaneous causation (behaviour is caused at the moment of its occurrence by all influences present then).
  • Cognitive styles — field dependence/independence (Witkin, Asch). Field-dependent people are heavily influenced by salient but irrelevant context, are more holistic/intuitive, and are more sensitive to social/interpersonal contexts; field-independent people are more analytical, restructure problems more, and are guided more by internalised aspects.

George Kelly — personal construct theory

  • Each person has a unique system of constructs used to understand and predict behaviour (their own and others').
  • Constructive alternativism — we are not controlled by our constructs; we are free to revise or replace them with alternatives.
  • The theory is presented in scientific form, organised into a fundamental postulate and 11 corollaries. (Kelly is associated with the person-as-scientist metaphor and the Role Construct Repertory / "Rep" Test.)

Martin Seligman — explanatory styles, learned helplessness & learned optimism

  • Explanatory style — cognitive personality variables capturing one's habitual way of interpreting events. The optimistic style perceives even neutral events positively and finds eventual positives in negatives; the pessimistic style focuses on the negative potential. Optimism is associated with better outcomes, especially under challenge.
  • Defensive pessimism (Julie Norem) — in risky situations a person reduces anxiety and improves performance by anticipating a poorer outcome; excessive optimism can be maladaptive when failure chances are high.
  • Learned helplessness (Seligman, 1975) — repeated exposure to unavoidable punishment leads an organism to accept later punishment even when it becomes avoidable. In the classic study, dogs given inescapable shocks later failed to escape when escape was possible.
  • Learned optimism — training people to think differently about themselves and their situations, developing the healthier responses of the optimistic style.

9. Assessment of personality

Freudian assessment techniques

  • Free association — the patient says whatever comes to mind ("daydreaming out loud"). Catharsis = the expression of emotions expected to reduce disturbing symptoms; resistance = a blockage or refusal to disclose painful memories.
  • Dream analysis — dreams symbolically represent repressed desires, fears and conflicts. The manifest content = the actual events of the dream; the latent content = the hidden symbolic meaning.

Across the theories — how each measures personality

  • Jung — the word-association test (response delay, respiration, GSR, retest memory) to detect complexes; the MBTI derived from his types.
  • Cattell — the 16PF, with Q-data, T-data and L-data.
  • Eysenck — questionnaires measuring the PEN dimensions.
  • McCrae & Costa — the Big Five via factor-analysed self-report scales.
  • Zuckerman — the Sensation Seeking Scale (SSS), a 40-item paper-and-pencil questionnaire (see §15).
  • Murray — needs (n Ach etc.) classically assessed with projective methods (the Thematic Apperception Test).
High-yield

Type A vs Type B is the canonical type assessment (heart-disease proneness). 16PF = Cattell, PEN = Eysenck, OCEAN = McCrae & Costa, MBTI = Jung-derived, SSS = Zuckerman, word-association = Jung.

10. Emotion & its physiology; theories of emotion

Emotion is the "feeling" aspect of consciousness, with three elements: a certain physical arousal, a certain behaviour that reveals the feeling outwardly, and an inner awareness of the feeling.

The physiology of emotion

  • Arousal is created by the sympathetic nervous system: heart rate increases, breathing quickens, pupils dilate, the mouth dries.
  • Different emotions show different physiological signatures: sadness, anger and fear raise heart rate more than disgust; disgust raises skin conductance more than happiness; anger shows vascular signs such as higher diastolic blood pressure compared with fear.
  • The amygdala (in the limbic system, on each side of the brain) is associated with emotions such as fear and pleasure in humans and animals. Other subcortical and cortical areas — notably the frontal lobes — also process emotion: positive emotions are associated with the left frontal lobe, negative feelings (sadness, anxiety, depression) with the right frontal lobe.
  • The endocrine response (hormonal secretions) and autonomic arousal can be triggered by a fast "low road" (thalamus → amygdala) and a slower "high road" (thalamus → cortex → amygdala).

Theories of emotion

11. Stress, the GAS & conflict

Hans Selye — the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)

Selye, the founder of stress research, studied the sequence of physiological reactions the body uses to adapt to a stressor — the general adaptation syndrome (GAS), in three stages:

  1. Alarm. The sympathetic nervous system is activated; the adrenal glands release hormones that increase heart rate, blood pressure and blood-sugar supply, producing a burst of energy. Reactions such as fever, nausea and headache are common.
  2. Resistance. As stress continues, the body settles into sympathetic activity, continuing to release stress hormones to fight off or resist the stressor; early alarm symptoms lessen and the person may actually feel better. One stress hormone, noradrenaline (norepinephrine), affects the brain's processing of pain, producing a kind of analgesia (so a person under stress may not feel hitting an arm or shin).
  3. Exhaustion. When the body's resources are gone, exhaustion occurs, which can lead to stress-related diseases (high blood pressure, weakened immune system) or, without outside help, the death of the organism.

Conflict: sources and types

Whenever you are torn between competing, incompatible desires, goals or actions, you are in conflict:

  • Approach–approach — attraction to two or more desirable goals; least stressful.
  • Avoidance–avoidance — a choice between two or more undesirable situations; more stressful.
  • Approach–avoidance — a single goal/event has both positive and negative aspects; can be most stressful.
  • Multiple approach–avoidance (e.g. double approach–avoidance) — common in daily life when decisions affect two or more goals each with pros and cons.

12. Theories of motivation

Motivation is the process by which activities are started, directed and continued so that physical or psychological needs/wants are met.

  • Intrinsic vs extrinsic. Intrinsic = actions rewarding or satisfying in and of themselves; extrinsic = actions performed because they lead to an external outcome.
  • Instinct approach. Instincts are biologically determined, innate patterns of behaviour. The older view is criticised because instincts are mainly descriptions, not explanations; but the idea that some behaviour is hereditary remains important.
  • Drive-reduction (Hull). A need is a requirement for something essential to survival; a need creates psychological tension (a drive) and physical arousal, and satisfying the need reduces the drive/tension (toward homeostasis). Primary drives involve survival needs (hunger, thirst) and maintain homeostasis; secondary (acquired) drives are learned through experience or conditioning (money, social approval).
  • Psychological-needs / McClelland. Some theories highlight learned psychological needs: need for achievement (nAch), need for affiliation (nAff), and need for power (nPow). Dweck suggests need for achievement is linked to a person's view of self (fixed vs changeable mindset) and locus of control.
  • Arousal theory. People have an optimal level of tension/arousal they work to maintain (the Yerkes-Dodson law — an inverted-U where performance peaks at moderate arousal). The need for stimulation usually settles at a moderate level but ranges low to high (sensation seeking).
  • Incentive approach. Based partly on principles of learning: incentives are things that attract or lure people into action, most often through their rewarding properties (motivation pulled from outside, not just pushed by internal drives).
  • Humanistic. Based primarily on Maslow's hierarchy — basic needs must be met before higher levels can be reached (other versions differ in number of levels and focus).
  • Self-determination theory. Similar to Maslow's hierarchy: three universal needs — autonomy, competence and relatedness — must be met for well-being.
High-yield

Match the theorist: drive-reduction = Hull; arousal/optimal-arousal = Yerkes-Dodson (inverted-U); nAch/nAff/nPow = McClelland; hierarchy = Maslow; autonomy/competence/relatedness = Self-Determination (Deci & Ryan). Drives push from inside; incentives pull from outside.

13. Emotional & social development

Emotional expression & self-conscious emotions

  • Self-conscious emotions — embarrassment, shame, guilt, envy and pride — each involves some damage to, or enhancement of, the sense of self.
  • Self-evaluative emotions (shame, guilt, pride) require both self-recognition (recognising oneself in a mirror/photo) and an understanding of rules or standards for evaluating one's conduct.
  • Parents socialize emotions by modelling positive emotions, attending to infants' pleasant feelings, and being less responsive to negative displays.

Emotional self-regulation

  • Begins by the end of the first year and develops slowly: toddlers move from depending on others to regulating emotions on their own; grade-schoolers comply with culturally defined emotional display rules (suppressing unacceptable emotions and outwardly replacing them with the called-for feeling).
  • Social referencing — by 8–10 months, infants use others' emotional expressions to infer the meaning of ambiguous situations.
  • Emotional competence has three components: competent emotional expressivity (frequent positive, infrequent negative displays), emotional knowledge (correctly identifying others' feelings and their causes), and emotional regulation (adjusting one's arousal to an appropriate intensity to achieve goals). Each component is related to children's social competence.

Temperament & development (Thomas & Chess; Rothbart)

Temperament is a person's tendency to respond in predictable ways to environmental events, influenced by genetic and environmental factors; negatively-toned attributes are shaped more by nonshared environmental influences (aspects siblings do not share). Rothbart & Bates' dimensions: fearful distress, irritable distress (frustration/anger), positive affect (sociability), activity level, attention span/persistence, and rhythmicity (regularity of bodily functions). Temperamental attributes (activity level, irritability, sociability, fearfulness) are moderately stable over time, including behavioral inhibition (withdrawal from unfamiliar people/situations).

Thomas & Chess grouped these into profiles:

  • Easy — even-tempered, typically positive mood, open and adaptable; regular, predictable habits.
  • Difficult — active, irritable, irregular in habits.
  • Slow-to-warm-up — inactive, somewhat moody, slow to adapt; responds to novelty in mildly (not intensely) negative ways.

Difficult and slow-to-warm-up children are at greater risk of adjustment problems, depending on the goodness-of-fit between parenting and the child's temperament.

Social development & person perception

Age trends in how children perceive others:

  • Children younger than 7–8 describe others in the same concrete, observable terms they use for the self.
  • Behavioral comparisons phase — grade-schoolers form impressions by comparing/contrasting overt behaviours.
  • Psychological constructs phase — later, they rely on stable traits to describe patterns.
  • Psychological comparisons phase — young adolescents compare/contrast people on abstract psychological dimensions; by 14–16 they know situational influences can make a person act "out of character."

Theories of social-cognitive development — Selman

  • Robert Selman's role-taking theory (1980): the growth of role-taking skills underlies a mature understanding of self and others, tested with the Holly dilemma (an 8-year-old who promised her father not to climb trees, but is the only one who can rescue a kitten stuck in a tree).
  • Social influences — equal-status contacts with friends and peers are crucial: they contribute indirectly by fostering role-taking (children must compromise to keep play going) and directly by providing experiences of what others are like. Piaget assumed equal-status peer contact is an important contributor to social perspective-taking.

14. Attachment

Attachment (John Bowlby, 1969) is the strong affectional tie we feel with the special people in our lives. Infants form reciprocal affectional ties to caregivers during the first year; parents' bonding strengthens as they gear behaviour to the infant's social signals and establish synchronized routines.

The phases of attachment

  • Asocial phase (0–6 weeks).
  • Phase of indiscriminate attachment (6 weeks–7 months).
  • Phase of specific attachments (7–9 months) — the first true attachments; the attachment object becomes a secure base for exploration.
  • Phase of multiple attachments (9–18 months).

Theories of attachment

  • Psychoanalytic and learning theories were discredited because feeding plays less of a role in human attachment than they expected.
  • Cognitive-developmental — attachments depend on cognitive development: infants must recognise that familiar companions have permanence (object permanence), since a stable relationship is hard to form with someone who seems to cease to exist when out of view.
  • Harlow & Zimmerman — infant monkeys separated at birth and raised with a wire surrogate or a cloth surrogate spent time with the wire mother only while feeding but ran to the cloth mother when upset or afraid — showing contact comfort is a more powerful contributor to attachment than feeding/hunger reduction.
  • Ethological theory — Konrad Lorenz: young goslings followed almost any moving object (their mother, a duck, even a human), a behaviour he called imprinting. Imprinting is automatic (no teaching needed), occurs only within a narrowly delimited critical period after hatching, and is irreversible. In humans, infants inherit attributes that maintain contact and elicit caregiving — Lorenz's "kewpie doll" appearance (large forehead, chubby cheeks, soft rounded features) makes infants seem cute/lovable, and smiling/babbling reinforces caregiving.

Attachment-related fears

Stranger anxiety and separation anxiety stem from infants' wariness of strange situations and their inability to explain who strangers are or where absent companions have gone. Both usually decline in the second year as toddlers mature and venture from their secure bases to explore.

Attachment quality — Ainsworth's Strange Situation

The Strange Situation (Mary Ainsworth, 1978) assesses attachment quality in 1- to 2-year-olds. Four classifications:

ClassificationBehaviour
SecureActively explores while alone with mother; may be upset by separation but greets her warmly on return and seeks contact that alleviates distress; outgoing with strangers when mother is present.
Resistant (ambivalent)Stays close, explores little; very distressed at departure; ambivalent on return — angry, resists physical contact; very wary of strangers even when mother is present.
AvoidantLittle distress on separation; turns away from / ignores mother even when she tries to gain attention; often sociable with strangers but may avoid them too.
Disorganized / disoriented (Main & Solomon, 1990)A confusing combination of resistant and avoidant patterns; on reunion may seem dazed, freeze, or approach then abruptly move away — confusion about whether to approach or avoid the caregiver.

Cultural variation. The distribution of classifications varies across cultures and reflects child-rearing differences — for example Japanese mothers emphasize social routines more and exploration less than American mothers, and seek to promote the infant's amae (a state of total dependence on the mother and a presumption of her love and indulgence).

Factors influencing attachment security & later development

  • Caregiving hypothesis — sensitive, responsive caregiving → secure attachments; inconsistent, neglectful, over-intrusive or abusive caregiving → insecure attachments. Environmental stressors (poverty, a stormy marriage) also contribute. Caregiving may determine whether attachment is secure or insecure.
  • Temperament hypothesis — infant characteristics and temperament may influence attachment by shaping caregiver–infant interactions; child temperament may determine the kind of insecurity shown when caregiving is insensitive.
  • Internal working models — cognitive representations of self, others and relationships, constructed from interactions with caregivers; often stable over time and shaping reactions to people and challenges for years. Secure attachment predicts later intellectual curiosity and social competence. Parents' working models correspond closely to their children's. Working models can change: a secure history is no guarantee of positive adjustment, nor insecurity a certain indicator of poor outcomes.

15. Exploratory behaviour, curiosity & sensation seeking

Zuckerman's sensation seeking

Sensation seeking is the need for varied, novel, complex and intense sensations and experiences, and the willingness to take physical, social, legal and financial risks for the sake of such experience. Measured by the Sensation Seeking Scale (SSS), a 40-item paper-and-pencil questionnaire.

Four components

  • Thrill and adventure seeking — desire for physical activities involving speed, danger, novelty and defiance of gravity (parachuting, scuba diving, bungee jumping).
  • Experience seeking — novel experiences through travel, music, art or a nonconformist lifestyle with similar people.
  • Disinhibition — release through uninhibited social activities (risky sex, impulsiveness, aggressiveness, antisocial behaviour).
  • Boredom susceptibility — aversion to repetition, routine work and predictable people; restless discontent when exposed to them.

Good vs bad sensation seeking

  • Good sensation seeking — non-impulsive, socialized; chiefly the thrill-and-adventure-seeking component.
  • Bad sensation seeking — impulsive, unsocialized; high scores on disinhibition, experience seeking and boredom susceptibility, plus high scores on Eysenck's psychoticism scale.

Characteristics of sensation seekers

  • Age — younger people seek adventure, risk and novelty more than older people.
  • Gender — men score higher on thrill-and-adventure seeking, disinhibition and boredom susceptibility (and higher overall sensation seeking with lower impulse control); women score higher on experience seeking.
  • Racial/cultural — Asians scored lower on the SSS than people in Western countries; white subjects scored higher than non-whites.
  • Risk-taking groups — three types of risk takers were identified: antisocial, adventurous, and prosocial risk takers. Antisocial (drug addicts, criminals) and adventurous (mountain climbers, sky divers) scored significantly higher on the SSS than prosocial risk takers (police, firefighters). Skydivers, firefighters, riot-control police, bungee jumpers and race-car drivers all score high.
  • Behavioural differences — high sensation seekers are more likely than low to use and sell illicit drugs (and earlier), drink alcohol, shoplift and commit delinquent behaviour; more had tattoos and body piercings; at work they scored lower on job performance, were less likely to form social relationships at work, and less likely to try to obtain information from co-workers or supervisors.

Personality correlates

  • SSS scores (especially disinhibition) relate to Eysenck's extraversion and to the asocial tendencies of psychoticism.
  • High scores correlate with extraversion as described by Jung and measured by the MBTI.
  • No correlation was found between sensation seeking and neuroticism.
  • High SSS scorers show high autonomy: they openly express emotions, are assertive, nonconforming and confirmed risk takers, acting independently of social conventions and others' needs.

Self-regulation (Bandura, applied to exploration)

When people have high self-efficacy, are confident in their reliance on proxies, and possess solid collective efficacy, they have considerable capacity to regulate their own behaviour. Bandura (1994) held that people use both reactive and proactive strategies.

  • External factors provide a standard for evaluating our behaviour and the means for reinforcement.
  • Internal factors: self-observation (monitoring our own performance, even if attention is not complete or accurate); judgmental processes (regulating behaviour through cognitive mediation, comparing performance against personal standards rather than others' conduct, weighing the value of the activity and the performance attribution of causes); and self-reaction (responding positively or negatively to our own behaviour depending on how it measures up to personal standards).
