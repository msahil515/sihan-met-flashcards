1 · Cognitive restructuring

The actual mechanics — the 5 moves
  1. Catch the automatic thought. The moment mood drops, get the exact thought in the client's own words ("I'm going to fail and everyone will see") — not a paraphrase. Pin the situation, the thought, the emotion.
  2. Rate it. Belief in the thought 0–100%, intensity of the emotion 0–100. This is what makes the change measurable: you re-rate at the end.
  3. Name the cognitive distortion driving it. You're labelling the type of thinking error, which itself loosens it:
all-or-nothing (black/white) catastrophising mind-reading overgeneralisation mental filter (only the negatives) discounting the positive emotional reasoning "should" statements labelling personalisation fortune-telling
  1. Test the evidence. Evidence for vs. evidence against; the double-standard question ("would you hold a friend to this?"); de-catastrophise (worst case / best case / most likely, and "could you cope if it happened?").
  2. Build the balanced alternative — a believable, more accurate thought (not a fake positive affirmation), then re-rate belief and emotion to show the shift. Often set as a written thought record for homework.
It would be important to identify certain automatic thoughts that we may have that doesnt allow us to accept these thoughts.to Levi
For now, the goal is not to stop the voice, but to change your response to it. When it shows up, pause and say — "This is my old critical voice, not reality."to Vaisakh (inner critic)
Say it in the PI:"Cognitive restructuring is my headline CBT tool and I run it in steps. First I catch the exact automatic thought and have the client rate how much they believe it and how strong the emotion is, 0 to 100. Then I name the distortion it's running on — with a self-critical client it was labelling and emotional reasoning. We test it: evidence for, evidence against, the double-standard question. Then we build a balanced replacement and re-rate the belief, which usually drops. With one client stuck in harsh self-talk I had him label the thought 'this is my old critical voice, not reality' so he could respond to it as a thought instead of treating it as fact."

2 · Graded exposure (with a fear hierarchy + SUDS)

The actual mechanics
  1. Build a fear/avoidance hierarchy. List the avoided situations and rate each on SUDS — Subjective Units of Distress, 0 (calm) to 100 (worst panic imaginable). Order them low → high into a ladder.
  2. Start low. Begin on a rung around SUDS 40–50 — challenging but doable. Too easy does nothing; too hard and they flee.
  3. Stay until it drops. Remain in the situation until SUDS falls by roughly half (habituation). Escaping at the peak is the one thing that strengthens the fear, because relief reinforces avoidance.
  4. Repeat the same rung until it reliably triggers low distress, then climb to the next.
  5. Drop safety behaviours (reassurance-seeking, checking, gripping). The new learning — "the feared thing didn't happen, and I coped" — only lands when the safety crutch is gone (inhibitory learning).
entirely stopping … may be difficult and create more anxiety without having an alternate coping system in place… Goal is not to stop it abruptly but to be able to manage anxiety or these triggers healthily.to Akashdeep (graded, not abrupt)
Say it in the PI:"For avoidance and anxiety I use graded exposure. We build a hierarchy of the feared situations and rate each one on SUDS, 0 to 100, then order them into a ladder. We start on a rung around 40–50 — hard but doable — and the key rule is to stay in it until the SUDS drops by about half, because if they escape at the peak the relief just reinforces the fear. We repeat a rung until it's easy, then climb. And I get them to drop the safety behaviours, the checking and reassurance, so the brain actually learns the feared outcome doesn't come. I keep it graded rather than flooding, especially with a harm-reduction case."

3 · Behavioural activation (activity scheduling + mastery/pleasure)

The actual mechanics
  1. Monitor first. Have the client log activity hour by hour for a few days and rate each one on two scales: Mastery (sense of achievement, 0–10) and Pleasure (enjoyment, 0–10).
  2. Read the pattern. Low mood tracks withdrawal and avoidance; the log shows which activities still give any M or any P, and where the dead zones are.
  3. Schedule activities at specific times — deliberately mixing mastery and pleasure tasks, graded easy → hard, and tied to the client's values, not just "keeping busy".
  4. Act from the schedule, not the mood. The principle is action-before-motivation: you do the activity first and the feeling follows, instead of waiting to feel like it.
  5. Review the M/P logs, troubleshoot what blocked a task, and grade up.

I start by having them monitor their activities and rate each one for mastery and pleasure, 0 to 10, so we can see that mood is tracking avoidance. 4 · Stimulus control

The actual mechanics
  1. Find the antecedent cues that reliably set off the behaviour — the time, place, object, mood, or person that precedes it.
  2. Change the environment, not the willpower. Remove or limit the cues for the problem behaviour (put the object out of reach, change the route/time, leave the phone in another room) and add cues for the wanted behaviour.
  3. Narrow the context. Classic example is insomnia — bed used only for sleep so the bed re-cues sleep, not wakeful rumination. Same logic for any habit: shrink the situations where the behaviour can be triggered.
  4. Pair it with an alternative response so the cue, when it does fire, routes to a coping action instead.
entirely stopping … may be difficult … without having an alternate coping system in place… Goal is … to be able to manage anxiety or these triggers healthily.to Akashdeep — alternative system before removal
Say it in the PI:"I lean on stimulus control rather than relying on willpower at the peak of an urge, because willpower fails right when it's needed. We map the cues that trigger the behaviour — the time, place, object, the mood — and then change the environment: get the cue out of reach, change the route or the timing, and pre-load an alternative response so when the trigger does fire it routes somewhere healthier. It's the same principle as telling an insomniac to use the bed only for sleep."

5 · Response prevention (ERP) & urge-surfing

The actual mechanics
  1. Expose to the trigger/urge, then deliberately block the compulsive response (the checking, the reassurance, the behaviour) instead of acting on it.
  2. Urge-surf. Teach the client that an urge is a wave — it rises, peaks, and falls on its own, usually within ~20–30 minutes — if you don't feed it. You ride it out rather than fight it.
  3. Each blocked response weakens the loop. The urge→act→relief cycle only survives because the relief reinforces it; prevent the response and the urge gradually loses its grip.
  4. Build the alternative coping skill first (harm-reduction), then extend the delay — 10 minutes, then longer — rather than demanding zero overnight.
label it as 'anxiety thought' and delay any checking by 10 minutes.health-anxiety client — graded response delay
Say it in the PI:"With compulsive behaviour I use exposure and response prevention. The client faces the trigger and we block the compulsion rather than letting them act on it, and I teach urge-surfing — an urge is a wave that peaks and falls within about twenty, thirty minutes if you don't feed it. Every time the response is prevented, the urge-relief loop weakens. I do it inside a harm-reduction frame though — I won't ask someone to white-knuckle to zero overnight, we build an alternative coping skill first and extend the delay, ten minutes then longer."

6 · Cognitive defusion (ACT)

The actual mechanics
  1. Change the relationship to the thought, not its content. You don't argue whether it's true — you create distance so it stops dictating action.
  2. The defusion moves: add the frame "I'm having the thought that…"; label it ("that's an anxiety thought"); thank the mind for it; leaves-on-a-stream (watch the thought float past); or repeat the hot word until it loses its charge.
  3. Pair with an action delay — once unhooked, the urge the thought was driving can be delayed rather than obeyed.
label it as 'anxiety thought' and delay any checking by 10 minutes.health-anxiety client
this is him trying to trigger me and not a fact about medefusing a thought from its content
Say it in the PI:"When a thought is sticky I use defusion rather than debate, because arguing with a thought keeps you hooked to it. We name it — 'that's an anxiety thought', 'I'm having the thought that…' — so the client steps back and sees it as a mental event rather than a fact, and then delays the urge it was driving. It takes the power out of the thought without me having to prove it wrong, which matters because you usually can't win that argument."

7 · Behavioural chain analysis (chaining)

The actual mechanics
  1. Reconstruct the chain link by link, in order: vulnerability factors (poor sleep, stress, hunger, low mood that lowered the threshold) → prompting event (the trigger) → the links (every thought, body sensation, emotion and action between the trigger and the behaviour) → problem behaviour → consequences (short-term relief, longer-term cost).
  2. Find the weak links. Walk the chain with the client and locate the points where a different thought, a delay, a coping skill or a changed environment could have broken the path — the earlier the better.
  3. Insert a skill at each link and reframe the short-term relief as the reinforcer that maintains the whole chain.

Say it in the PI:"I do a behavioural chain analysis — I walk the whole sequence with the client: the vulnerability factors that lowered the threshold, the prompting event, then every thought, sensation and urge that linked the trigger to the behaviour, and finally the consequence. The point is to find the weak links, the earliest spot where a delay or a different response could have broken the chain, and put a skill there. 8 · Contingency management

The actual mechanics
  1. Behaviour follows its consequences. Identify what currently reinforces the problem behaviour (relief, escape, attention) and reduce it.
  2. Attach reinforcement to the target behaviour instead — a self-reward for a completed step, tracking a streak, social reinforcement (genuine praise, handing back evidence of competence).
  3. Make rewards immediate and achievable, and shape by reinforcing successive approximations — reward the small step now, raise the bar gradually.

Say it in the PI:"I use contingency management — behaviour is shaped by what follows it. So I figure out what's currently reinforcing the problem behaviour, usually the relief or escape, and then attach reinforcement to the behaviour I want instead: an immediate, achievable reward, tracking a streak, or just genuine social reinforcement for the attempt. I deliberately set the first step small enough that it gets reinforced, and I shape it up from there rather than demanding the whole change at once."

9 · Relapse-prevention planning

The actual mechanics (Marlatt model)
  1. Map the high-risk situations — the emotional states, people, places and times when a slip is most likely.
  2. Separate a lapse from a relapse. A lapse is one slip; a relapse is a full return. The danger is the Abstinence Violation Effect — "I've already blown it, so I may as well go all in" — and you pre-empt that thinking directly.
  3. Build a coping plan for each high-risk situation, plus early-warning signs and an if-then plan ("if I notice X, I do Y").
  4. Reframe a lapse as data, not failure — what was the trigger, what link broke, what would change next time.
The relapse is actually evidence that you can change the pattern, and one relapse doesn't erase that progress.to Akashdeep — exactly the abstinence-violation reframe
Say it in the PI:"I build a relapse-prevention plan. We map the high-risk situations — the moods, people, times when a slip is most likely — and we plan a coping response for each, with early-warning signs and an if-then plan. The crucial part is separating a lapse from a relapse: I prep the client for the abstinence-violation effect, that 'I've already slipped so I may as well give up' thought, because that thought is what turns one slip into a full relapse. 11 · Socratic questioning / guided discovery

The actual mechanics
  1. Don't tell, ask. Use questions that lead the client to their own conclusion rather than supplying it — it sticks because they reached it themselves.
  2. The question types: clarifying ("what does that look like exactly?"), evidence ("what makes you think that?"), alternative-perspective ("how else could you read it?"), consequence ("what would it mean if that were true?"), and the summary/synthesis question that ties it together.
  3. Move from label to phenomenology — from "I have ADHD" to what actually happens, when, and for how long.

12 · Motivational interviewing

The actual mechanics
  1. Spirit: collaboration, evocation, autonomy — you draw the client's own reasons for change out, you don't install them.
  2. OARS — Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, Summaries.
  3. Develop discrepancy between the behaviour and the client's own goals/values; roll with resistance rather than confronting it (arguing for change makes them argue against it).

14 · Grounding & arousal regulation

The actual mechanics
  1. Body before mind. A flooded nervous system can't do cognitive work, so you down-regulate arousal first.
  2. 5-4-3-2-1 sensory grounding — name 5 things you see, 4 you touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste — pulls attention out of the spiral and into the present.
  3. Paced breathing with a long exhale — inhale 4, hold 2, exhale 6. The longer exhale engages the parasympathetic (vagal) brake and physically lowers arousal; that's why exhale > inhale, not the other way round.

6 · Interview answers, built from your own work

Each of these is concrete on purpose: a named technique, the actual steps, and a real moment. That's what reads as a clinician. Say them in your own voice.

"What is your therapeutic approach?" "I'd call myself integrative but state-driven. My base is person-centred — I validate and build safety before anything else — and my working spine is cognitive-behavioural: I work with automatic thoughts, run functional analyses of the maintaining loop, and use graded behavioural steps. But I don't apply one school to every client. I read where the person is and switch: if they're flooded I regulate arousal first with grounding and paced breathing; if they're stuck in a thought I do cognitive restructuring or defusion; if they're ambivalent I use motivational interviewing; if they're shut down and avoidant I use behavioural activation. The rule underneath all of it is regulate before you explore, validate before you intervene, and pick the technique off the client's state in the moment, not off a manual."
"Give an example of how you handle a client in distress." "I run a two-gear model. Default gear: validate, one focused question, one small step. But the moment a client floods — 'I don't know', 'nothing helps', or they start looping — I switch into containment. Concretely: I stop exploring and stop problem-solving, and I regulate the body first, because you can't do cognitive work on a flooded nervous system. I'll do 5-4-3-2-1 grounding and paced breathing with a long exhale — inhale four, hold two, exhale six — which engages the vagal brake and physically drops the arousal. I'll say something like 'you've shared a lot, it's okay not to say more, we can just sit with what's here.' Only once they're regulated do we think again."
"Walk me through how you'd treat a specific case — say, a client with health anxiety." "First I'd do a functional analysis and map the loop with them: a body sensation triggers a catastrophic thought, that drives the urge to check or seek reassurance, they check, they get short-term relief, then guilt, and round again. The key move is showing them the relief is the problem — I'd say the relief after checking is exactly what keeps the anxiety alive, it isn't solving it. Then graded exposure with response prevention: we build a hierarchy of the feared situations rated on SUDS, 0 to 100, and we practise sitting with the trigger while blocking the checking — starting with a 10-minute delay and extending it, urge-surfing the wave until it falls. Alongside that, cognitive restructuring on the catastrophic thought — name the distortion, usually catastrophising and emotional reasoning, test the evidence, build a balanced alternative — and defusion when it's too sticky to argue with, just labelling it 'that's an anxiety thought'. I keep the whole thing graded and harm-reduction, not flooding."
"How do you build the therapeutic relationship?" "Consistency and permission, and I'm deliberate about it. I open every session identically — who I am, the time we have, that it's confidential unless there's risk of harm — so the frame is safe and predictable, which is itself a rapport move. Then I let the client lead: 'feel free to start wherever you'd like.' I use reflective listening and strength-spotting, handing their own competence back to them as evidence, and I normalise how hard sharing is. I pace with one open question at a time so it never becomes an interrogation. I'd rather the person feel understood than get through my agenda — the alliance is the thing that makes any technique land."
"How do you handle risk?" "I screen warmly but directly — I don't dance around it. If someone signals it, I ask plainly: 'right now, do you ever get thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life?' If it's there I assess it — frequency, any plan, any intent, any means, and protective factors — then I stabilise and safety-plan concretely: is someone able to be in the room with you, can we remove access to means, who do you contact if it escalates. And I hold my scope cleanly: I'm a psychologist, I don't prescribe, and I refer to psychiatry when it's medically indicated. I've actually done that referral mid-session when a case was outside my lane."
"Tell me about a difficult case / a mistake you learned from." "I had a session with a client in real distress where I over-talked — I chased every thread he raised and over-psychoeducated instead of anchoring one target, and he left without anything concrete to hold. My own note afterwards was that he needed containment, not commentary. What I changed is specific: now I anchor one target per session, and when someone's flooded I contain and regulate first — grounding, permission to not say more — before I do any exploring. I also watch my longest turns, because that's where I tip from collaborative into lecturing. It made me a much more focused clinician, and I can show the before-and-after."
"How do you work with habits or compulsive behaviour?" "Harm-reduction and a behavioural chain analysis, not white-knuckling. I won't ask someone to quit abruptly — that spikes anxiety and shame and usually backfires. I reconstruct the chain with them: the vulnerability factors that lowered the threshold, the prompting event, the thoughts and urges that linked it to the behaviour, the behaviour, and the relief that reinforces it. Then I intervene at the earliest weak link, where there's still a choice — stimulus control to change the cues, response prevention to delay and urge-surf the urge, and an alternative coping skill built first so they're not left with nothing. And I always build a relapse-prevention plan: high-risk situations mapped, and I prep them for the abstinence-violation effect — the 'I've already slipped so I may as well give up' thought — because that thought is what turns one lapse into a full relapse. I treat a slip as data, not failure."
"How do you handle a client who self-diagnoses from the internet?" "I stay neutral on the label and move to lived experience using Socratic questioning. If someone says 'I have ADHD and hypomania', I don't confirm it and I don't argue — I ask what it actually looks like for them, when it happens, how long it lasts, so we explore the phenomenology before the pathology. Clinically I'm also alert that there can be a tendency to over-report or self-diagnose, so I don't take every label at face value — I anchor on observable behaviour. It keeps it clinical and collaborative without becoming a diagnostic tug-of-war."
"How do you use behavioural activation?" "For low mood and avoidance. I start with monitoring — the client logs their activities and rates each one for mastery, the sense of achievement, and pleasure, both 0 to 10 — and that usually shows them their mood is tracking their withdrawal. Then we schedule specific activities at set times, mixing mastery and pleasure tasks, graded from easy, and tied to what they actually value rather than just keeping busy. The core principle I teach them is action before motivation: you act from the schedule, not from how you feel, because the feeling follows the action, not the other way round. I start deliberately tiny — one 15-minute step — so the first win is near-guaranteed and it rebuilds momentum."
