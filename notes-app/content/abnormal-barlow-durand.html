What is "Barlow & Durand" and why a second abnormal page. The Ahuja page reads the field as Indian psychiatry: definitions, criteria, drugs, fast and exam-tight. Barlow & Durand reads it as integrative science: it refuses any single cause and forces every disorder through a multidimensional model, and it teaches from cases. That's the angle this page adds. Same syllabus, deeper "why does it happen" answers (causes), plus the triple vulnerability theory (Barlow's own) for the anxiety block and the equifinality / multifinality developmental ideas — both pure B&D, both testable. Use Ahuja for the cut-offs and drugs, use this for the model questions ("which approach / what causes it / which dimension").
Two ideas hold the whole book together. (1) The multidimensional integrative model. No disorder has one cause; each is the product of biological (genes, brain circuits, neurotransmitters), psychological (learning, cognition, conditioning), emotional, social/cultural and developmental dimensions interacting. (2) Diathesis-stress run forward in time. A vulnerability (diathesis) only becomes a disorder when life delivers the stress; genes and environment are not separate but reciprocal (your genes shape the environments you pick), and experience switches genes on and off (epigenetics). Hold these two and you can reconstruct almost every "what causes X / which model" answer.

Part One · Foundations & the integrative model

Ch 1. What makes behaviour a disorder, and the three traditions

Barlow & Durand do not use a "4 Ds" list. Their working definition of a psychological disorder has three components, and all three usually travel together: (1) psychological dysfunction — a breakdown in cognitive, emotional or behavioural functioning; (2) distress or impairment — it hurts the person or wrecks their day-to-day functioning; (3) an atypical or culturally unexpected response — it deviates from what the culture expects (and is not merely a deviant act society dislikes). They lean on Wakefield's "harmful dysfunction": a disorder is a failure of an internal mechanism to do what it evolved to do (dysfunction) and that failure is harmful to the person by their culture's standards.

B&D's 3 vs Ahuja's 4 Ds. If a question cites "dysfunction + distress/impairment + atypical/culturally unexpected," that's the Barlow & Durand definition (3 components). The "Deviance, Distress, Dysfunction, Danger" 4-D list is the more common Indian-text framing. Danger is NOT one of B&D's three. Both agree dysfunction/impairment is the indispensable core and distress alone is neither necessary nor sufficient (a person in mania feels great).
TraditionCore claim · key namesTreatment it implies
SupernaturalPossession, sin, the moon, witchcraft; Malleus Maleficarum (1487); the "mass hysteria"/St Vitus dancesExorcism, prayer, shunning
BiologicalHippocrates' four humours (imbalance = illness); Galen; Kraepelin (classification, dementia praecox); the discovery that general paresis = late-stage syphilis cemented "mental illness can be physical"Rest, drugs, ECT, psychosurgery
PsychologicalMoral therapy (Pinel at Bicêtre, Tuke's York Retreat — humane treatment); Freud & psychoanalysis (unconscious, defences); Watson/Pavlov/Skinner behaviourism (learning); humanism (Rogers)Talk therapy, conditioning, person-centred

B&D frame the modern clinician as a scientist-practitioner: a consumer and producer of research who treats each case as testable. The whole book is then an argument that the three traditions are not rivals but dimensions of one integrative explanation.

Recap Ch 1. B&D disorder = psychological dysfunction + distress/impairment + atypical/culturally unexpected (3 components, NOT 4 Ds; "harmful dysfunction," Wakefield). Three historical traditions — supernatural, biological (humours → Kraepelin → general paresis), psychological (moral therapy → Freud → behaviourism) — converge in the integrative model. The clinician is a scientist-practitioner.

Ch 2. The integrative (multidimensional) model — the signature of the book

This is the chapter the rest of the book runs on, and the one most worth knowing cold. No disorder has one cause; B&D insist on a multidimensional integrative reading where several dimensions interact over time.

DimensionWhat it contributes · the B&D concepts
GeneticGenes rarely cause a disorder alone; they set vulnerability. Key B&D ideas: diathesis-stress (a gene/vulnerability lies dormant until stress triggers it), the reciprocal gene-environment model (your genetic make-up makes you seek environments that then express the gene), and epigenetics (experience switches gene expression on/off without changing DNA). Polygenic, not one "gene for" anything.
NeuroscienceBrain structure (limbic system, amygdala = fear; hippocampus; prefrontal cortex; basal ganglia; HPA axis) and neurotransmitters as currents, not switches: serotonin (regulation/restraint of behaviour), GABA (the brake — reduces anxiety; benzodiazepines act here), glutamate (the accelerator), norepinephrine (alarm/arousal), dopamine (reward, "switch" for other systems; implicated in schizophrenia & addiction). The HPA axis → cortisol is the body's stress engine.
Behavioural & cognitiveClassical & operant conditioning of maladaptive responses; learned helplessness (Seligman — uncontrollable aversive events → giving up → a model of depression); prepared learning (Seligman — we are evolutionarily primed to fear snakes/heights, not electrical outlets); cognitive appraisal and attributional style. Learning is not just behaviour: even one bad experience plus information transmission plants a fear.
EmotionalEmotions (basic-emotion theory) are action tendencies that, dysregulated, drive disorders; emotion contributes to behaviour (panic = the fight-flight emotion fired with no danger). Suppressing/avoiding emotion maintains anxiety.
Social & culturalSocial relationships buffer or worsen risk (loneliness predicts illness and death); culture shapes the form a disorder takes (e.g. somatic expression of depression, culture-bound syndromes). Gender roles channel which disorders appear.
Developmental / life-spanThe principle of equifinality — many different paths can lead to the same disorder — and its partner multifinality — the same risk factor can lead to different outcomes. Timing matters: the same insult has different effects at different ages.
NIMHANS/MET love these named B&D concepts: the diathesis-stress model, the reciprocal gene-environment model, epigenetics, equifinality (many roads → one disorder) vs multifinality, learned helplessness (Seligman → depression), prepared learning (why phobias cluster on evolutionary threats), and the neurotransmitter "currents" reading (GABA = brake, glutamate = accelerator, serotonin = regulator). The integrative model itself is the answer to any "which approach best explains psychopathology today" item.
Equifinality vs multifinality. Equifinality = different starting points reach the same endpoint (abuse, genes, or loss can all end in depression). Multifinality = one starting point reaches different endpoints (one abused child becomes depressed, another resilient). Same prefix logic as "equi = equal end."
Recap Ch 2. Every disorder = biological × psychological × emotional × social × developmental, interacting. Genetic dimension: diathesis-stress, reciprocal gene-environment, epigenetics. Neuroscience: GABA brake / glutamate accelerator / serotonin regulator / dopamine reward / HPA-cortisol stress. Behavioural-cognitive: learned helplessness, prepared learning. Developmental: equifinality & multifinality.

Ch 3. Clinical assessment & diagnosis

Good assessment is reliable (consistent — same result across raters/time), valid (measures what it claims), and standardised (fixed procedure + norms). B&D's tools: the clinical interview + mental status exam (appearance/behaviour, thought process & content, mood & affect, intellectual functioning, sensorium); behavioural assessment (ABCs — antecedent, behaviour, consequence); psychological tests — projective (Rorschach, TAT — unstructured, weak psychometrics), objective/personality (MMPI), intelligence (WAIS/WISC), and neuropsychological (Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska); plus neuroimaging (CT/MRI structure; fMRI/PET function) and psychophysiology (EEG, heart rate, EDA).

DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022)
  • Mostly categorical (you have it or you don't) with criteria sets + thresholds.
  • Multiaxial system abandoned in DSM-5 (the old Axes I–V are gone).
  • Organised so related disorders sit together (anxiety / OCD / trauma split into 3 chapters).
  • Adds dimensional severity ratings and cross-cutting measures alongside categories.
ICD-11 (WHO, in force 2022)
  • Global standard; mental disorders = Chapter 06. India follows ICD officially.
  • More prototype/description-based — leans on clinician judgement, fewer rigid counts.
  • Built-in dimensionality (clearest in personality disorders).
  • Never used DSM-style axes.
Reliability ≠ validity. A test can be reliable (consistent) yet invalid (consistently wrong). Validity requires reliability but not the reverse. Projective tests (Rorschach/TAT) = high clinical richness, weak reliability/validity; objective tests (MMPI) = empirically keyed, strong psychometrics.
Recap Ch 3. Assessment must be reliable + valid + standardised. Tools: interview + MSE, behavioural ABC, projective vs objective tests, IQ, neuropsych, imaging, psychophysiology. DSM-5-TR = categorical, no axes; ICD-11 = more dimensional, prototype-based, Chapter 06.

Part Two · Anxiety, trauma & OCD (B&D Ch 5)

Ch 4. Anxiety disorders, and Barlow's triple vulnerability

B&D draw a sharp line between three states. Anxiety = a future-oriented mood state, apprehension about possible threat (cognitive/somatic tension). Fear = an immediate alarm reaction to present danger — the fight-flight response firing now. Panic attack = fear occurring when there is nothing to be afraid of — a false alarm. Cued (situational) vs uncued/unexpected panic attacks; only unexpected ones define panic disorder.

The triple vulnerability theory (Barlow) — pure B&D, very testable. Anxiety disorders grow from three vulnerabilities stacking: (1) generalised biological vulnerability (a heritable tendency to be tense/neurotic); (2) generalised psychological vulnerability (a sense, from early experience, that the world is dangerous and uncontrollable); (3) specific psychological vulnerability (learning, from family/experience, what to focus the anxiety on — e.g. "physical symptoms are dangerous" → panic; "social evaluation is dangerous" → social anxiety). General vulnerabilities alone aren't enough; the specific one points the anxiety at its target.

Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Clinical snapshot A woman describes herself as a lifelong worrier: she frets about her kids, money, her health, being late, things that "might" go wrong, can't switch it off, feels keyed-up and tired, sleeps badly, neck always tense. There's no single feared object, just a roving, uncontrollable worry that has been there for years.

Clinical description: excessive, uncontrollable worry about multiple everyday domains, with physical tension. The cognition (worry) is the engine; B&D note GAD patients show autonomic restriction (chronically tense, not spiking) — worry may be an avoidance of worse imagery. Statistics: ~3% (1-yr), insidious onset, often early; ~2:1 female. Causes: generalised biological + psychological vulnerability; worry as cognitive avoidance. Treatment: CBT (especially targeting the worry process), SSRIs/SNRIs, short-term benzodiazepines for acute relief only.

DSM-5-TR — GAD
  • Excessive anxiety/worry, more days than not, for ≥6 months, about several events.
  • Worry is hard to control.
  • ≥3 of 6: restlessness, easily fatigued, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance.
  • Distress/impairment; not substance/medical.
ICD-11 — GAD
  • Marked general apprehension or worry about multiple events, ≥ several months (most days).
  • Plus additional symptoms: muscle tension, restlessness, autonomic overactivity, difficulty concentrating, irritability, sleep disturbance.
  • Less wedded to the exact "≥3 of 6 / 6-month" count — clinician judgement of persistence.
GAD vs normal worry vs OCD. GAD = free-floating worry about real-life domains, ego-syntonic ("I'm a worrier"). OCD = intrusive, unwanted, often senseless thoughts (contamination, harm) that the person resists. The GAD cut-off is the 6-month rule in DSM.
Recap Ch 4. Anxiety (future) vs fear (present alarm = fight-flight) vs panic (false alarm). Triple vulnerability = generalised biological + generalised psychological + specific psychological. GAD = uncontrollable free-floating worry ≥6 months (DSM) + ≥3 symptoms; CBT + SSRIs.

Ch 5. Panic disorder & agoraphobia

Clinical snapshot A young man's heart suddenly races in a supermarket, he can't breathe, his hands tingle, he's sure he's having a heart attack and is going to die. ER tests are normal. Now he scans his body constantly and avoids supermarkets, then buses, then leaving home alone, in case "it" happens again where he can't get help.

Clinical description: recurrent unexpected panic attacks (abrupt surge of fear peaking within minutes — palpitations, sweating, choking, chest pain, derealisation, fear of dying/going crazy) followed by persistent worry about more attacks or a maladaptive change in behaviour. Agoraphobia = fear/avoidance of situations where escape is hard or help unavailable if panic strikes (transport, open/enclosed spaces, crowds, queues, being out alone). Statistics: panic ~2–3%, onset adolescence–mid-30s, ~2:1 female; agoraphobia often follows panic. Causes (B&D's model): an initial false alarm (a panic attack out of the blue) becomes a learned alarm through interoceptive conditioning — the body's own sensations (racing heart) become conditioned cues for panic — layered on anxiety sensitivity (the belief that bodily sensations are dangerous). Treatment: CBT with interoceptive exposure (deliberately bring on the sensations and disconfirm catastrophe) + in-vivo exposure for agoraphobia; SSRIs/SNRIs; benzodiazepines only short-term.

DSM-5-TR — Panic disorder
  • Recurrent unexpected panic attacks.
  • ≥1 month of persistent worry about further attacks or a maladaptive behaviour change.
  • Agoraphobia is a separate, standalone diagnosis (can be coded with or without panic).
  • "Panic attack" can be a specifier on any disorder.
ICD-11 — Panic disorder
  • Recurrent unexpected panic attacks + persistent concern/avoidance.
  • Agoraphobia also a standalone category (marked fear in multiple situations where escape is difficult).
  • Panic attacks can be added as a qualifier to other anxiety/fear disorders.
The attack is not the disorder. A panic attack can occur in many conditions; panic disorder requires the attacks be unexpected + ≥1 month of anticipatory worry/behaviour change. Interoceptive conditioning (fearing your own heartbeat) is the B&D mechanism — a classic MCQ term.
Recap Ch 5. Panic disorder = unexpected attacks + ≥1 month worry; false alarm → learned alarm via interoceptive conditioning + anxiety sensitivity. Agoraphobia is now standalone in both DSM-5 and ICD-11. Treat with interoceptive + in-vivo exposure CBT and SSRIs.

Ch 6. Specific phobia & social anxiety disorder

Clinical snapshot (B&D's classic "Judy" case) A bright teenager faints whenever she sees blood, even a drop, even on TV in biology class — her blood pressure spikes then crashes (the vasovagal drop), she goes pale and passes out. She has reorganised her whole life to avoid the sight of blood or injury.

Specific phobia = marked, persistent, unreasonable fear of a specific object/situation, ≥6 months, with avoidance. B&D's four subtypes: blood-injection-injury (BII), situational (planes, lifts), natural environment (heights, water, storms), animal (+ "other"). BII is unique: a biphasic, diphasic vasovagal response — BP and HR briefly rise then sharply drop, causing fainting — and it runs strongly in families (the only phobia where you faint). Social anxiety disorder (social phobia) = marked fear of scrutiny/negative evaluation in social or performance situations. Causes: direct conditioning, vicarious learning (watching someone else's fear), information transmission, all on a base of prepared learning (Seligman) + the triple vulnerability. Treatment: exposure is first-line (graded exposure, systematic desensitisation, participant modelling); for BII add applied tension (tense muscles to keep BP up and prevent the faint); social anxiety adds cognitive restructuring + SSRIs.

BII phobia is the one that makes you faint. Every other phobia drives BP/HR up (sympathetic) and you want to flee; BII is biphasic — up then a parasympathetic crash → vasovagal fainting. Treatment quirk: applied muscle tension, not relaxation. High-yield distractor.
Social anxiety vs avoidant PD vs agoraphobia. Social anxiety = fear of judgement/embarrassment; agoraphobia = fear that escape/help is unavailable; avoidant personality disorder = a pervasive, lifelong pattern of feeling inadequate & hypersensitive to rejection (the trait-level version of social anxiety).
Recap Ch 6. Specific phobia (4 types: BII, situational, natural-environment, animal), ≥6 months; BII = biphasic vasovagal faint, treated with applied tension. Social anxiety = fear of evaluation. Learned by conditioning/vicarious/information on a prepared-learning base; exposure is first-line.

Ch 7. Trauma- & stressor-related disorders (PTSD)

Clinical snapshot Months after a motorway pile-up, a driver still jolts awake from nightmares of the crash, can't drive past the spot, feels numb and detached from his family, can't remember part of the crash, and snaps at any sudden noise. It hasn't faded; it's running his life.

PTSD follows exposure to actual/threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence (directly, witnessed, or learned-of for close others). DSM-5 moved PTSD out of the anxiety chapter into "Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders." Four symptom clusters: intrusion (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, arousal/reactivity (hypervigilance, exaggerated startle), lasting >1 month. Acute Stress Disorder = the same picture lasting 3 days–1 month right after the trauma. Causes: the trauma is necessary but not sufficient — pre-trauma vulnerability (biological reactivity, prior trauma), peri-trauma severity, and post-trauma factors (lack of social support) decide who develops it; a conditioned-fear + failure-of-extinction model. Treatment: trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR; SSRIs.

DSM-5-TR — Trauma disorders
  • One PTSD diagnosis, 4 clusters (intrusion, avoidance, negative cognition/mood, arousal), >1 month.
  • Acute Stress Disorder for the 3-day–1-month window.
  • A dissociative subtype of PTSD (depersonalisation/derealisation).
  • No Complex PTSD category.
ICD-11 — Trauma disorders
  • Simplified PTSD: re-experiencing in the here-and-now + avoidance + sense of current threat.
  • Adds a separate Complex PTSD (CPTSD): PTSD + disturbances in self-organisation (affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship difficulties) after prolonged/repeated trauma.
  • Adds Prolonged Grief Disorder and Adjustment disorder in this grouping.
The reclassification MET likes: ICD-11 has Complex PTSD, DSM-5-TR does not. CPTSD = PTSD core + the three "self-organisation" disturbances, after chronic trauma (childhood abuse, captivity). Also know: both manuals now have Prolonged Grief Disorder (DSM-5-TR added it in 2022).
Recap Ch 7. PTSD = post-trauma, 4 clusters (intrusion/avoidance/negative mood/arousal), >1 month; ASD = 3 days–1 month. ICD-11 adds Complex PTSD (+ disturbances in self-organisation); DSM doesn't. Trauma necessary, not sufficient — vulnerability + support decide. Prolonged exposure / TF-CBT / EMDR + SSRIs.

Ch 8. Obsessive-Compulsive & Related Disorders

Clinical snapshot A student washes his hands until they bleed because an intrusive image of "germs spreading death to my family" floods him with dread; the washing relieves it for minutes, then it returns. He knows it's irrational, hates it, and it now eats four hours a day.

DSM-5 carved OCD out of anxiety into its own grouping (ICD-11 too). OCD = obsessions (recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts/images/urges that cause anxiety — contamination, doubt, symmetry, taboo/aggressive) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours/mental acts done to neutralise the anxiety — washing, checking, counting, ordering, praying), time-consuming (>1 hr/day). Insight is usually retained (ego-dystonic). Related: Body Dysmorphic Disorder (preoccupation with an imagined/slight defect + mirror-checking), Hoarding disorder (new), Trichotillomania (hair-pulling), Excoriation (skin-picking). Causes: cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop overactivity, serotonin; cognitively, thought-action fusion (treating a thought as morally equal to the act) and inflated responsibility maintain it; the compulsion is negatively reinforced by anxiety relief. Treatment: Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is the treatment of choice (expose to the trigger, block the compulsion, let anxiety habituate) + SSRIs (often high-dose); clomipramine.

OCD vs OCPD. OCD = ego-dystonic intrusive obsessions + compulsions, the person is distressed by them (a clinical disorder). OCPD = ego-syntonic personality style — perfectionism, orderliness, control, rigidity, no true obsessions/compulsions — the person thinks they're right. Distressed by the thoughts → OCD; thinks the rigidity is correct → OCPD.
Obsession vs compulsion vs delusion. Obsession = intrusive thought; compulsion = the act that relieves it; both retain insight. A delusion = a fixed false belief held with conviction (no insight) — that's psychosis, not OCD.
Recap Ch 8. OCD = obsessions (thoughts) + compulsions (acts), ego-dystonic, >1hr/day, own DSM-5/ICD-11 chapter. Relatives: BDD, hoarding, trichotillomania, excoriation. Maintained by negative reinforcement + thought-action fusion. ERP is first-line + SSRIs/clomipramine. Don't confuse with OCPD.

Part Three · Somatic symptom & dissociative disorders (B&D Ch 6)

Ch 9. Somatic symptom & related disorders

Clinical snapshot A man is convinced his stomach aches mean cancer. Test after test is clear, doctor after doctor reassures him, but within days the fear is back; he reads symptom websites for hours, books more scans, and his life narrows to monitoring his body.

The body is the stage; the distress is psychological. DSM-5 collapsed the old "somatoform" list into fewer, clearer categories and dropped the requirement that symptoms be medically unexplained — what matters now is the excessive response to symptoms.

DisorderCore
Somatic Symptom DisorderOne+ distressing somatic symptoms + excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviours about them (≥6 months). Symptom may even be medically real — it's the disproportionate response that's the disorder.
Illness Anxiety DisorderPreoccupation with having/getting a serious illness with few or no somatic symptoms; high health anxiety, checking or avoidance. (The reworked "hypochondriasis.")
Conversion Disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder)Neurological symptoms (paralysis, blindness, non-epileptic seizures, aphonia) incompatible with any medical disease; classic sign la belle indifférence (oddly calm about a dramatic deficit). Freud's original "hysteria."
Factitious DisorderDeliberately faking/inducing symptoms to assume the sick role (no external reward). Imposed on another = factitious disorder imposed on another (old Munchausen by proxy).
Conversion vs factitious vs malingering — the classic 3-way. All three present with symptoms that don't fit disease. Conversion = symptom is unconscious/genuine (not faked), no external gain. Factitious = consciously faked for the internal reward of being a patient (the sick role). Malingering = consciously faked for an external reward (money, drugs, avoiding work/jail) — and malingering is not a mental disorder. Split on: conscious? and external gain?
Recap Ch 9. DSM-5 dropped "medically unexplained" — it's the excessive response that defines somatic symptom disorder. Illness anxiety = fear of disease, few symptoms. Conversion = genuine, unconscious neuro symptom (la belle indifférence). Factitious (sick role, no reward) vs malingering (external reward, not a disorder).

Ch 10. Dissociative disorders

A disruption of the normally integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity and perception — usually trauma-linked.

DisorderCore
Dissociative AmnesiaInability to recall important (usually traumatic) autobiographical information, beyond ordinary forgetting. Dissociative fugue = amnesia + sudden purposeful travel/wandering, now a specifier of amnesia.
Depersonalisation / Derealisation DisorderPersistent detachment from one's self (depersonalisation — "I'm watching myself," unreal body) or surroundings (derealisation — the world looks dreamlike/foggy), with intact reality testing.
Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID)Two+ distinct personality states + recurrent gaps in memory. Strongly linked to severe, early, chronic trauma (the post-traumatic model). Formerly "multiple personality disorder."
DID ≠ schizophrenia. "Split mind" (Bleuler's term for schizophrenia) = split among thought/emotion/behaviour, not split into multiple people. DID = multiple identity states. A common deliberate distractor. Also: depersonalisation keeps reality testing intact (you know it's not real), unlike psychosis.
Recap Ch 10. Dissociation = breakdown of integrated consciousness/memory/identity. Amnesia (± fugue travel), depersonalisation/derealisation (detachment, reality intact), DID (≥2 identity states + amnesia, trauma-driven). DID is not schizophrenia.

Part Four · Mood disorders & suicide (B&D Ch 7)

Ch 11. Mood (depressive & bipolar) disorders

Clinical snapshot For two months a woman has felt flat and hopeless, dropped the things she used to love, sleeps badly and wakes at 4am, can't concentrate at work, feels worthless and "a burden," and has started thinking everyone would be better off without her.

B&D build the chapter from mood episodes (the building blocks) up to the disorders (defined by which episodes occur).

EpisodeDefinition
Major depressive episode≥5/9 over the same 2 weeks (≥1 = depressed mood or anhedonia): mood, anhedonia, weight/appetite, sleep, psychomotor change, fatigue, worthlessness/guilt, poor concentration, suicidality (SIG E CAPS).
Manic episodeAbnormally elevated/expansive/irritable mood + increased energy, ≥1 week, ≥3 symptoms (DIG FAST: distractibility, indiscretion, grandiosity, flight of ideas, activity↑, sleep need↓, talkativeness); marked impairment / may be psychotic.
Hypomanic episodeSame symptoms, ≥4 days, no marked impairment, no psychosis, no hospitalisation.
DisorderDefining requirement
Major Depressive Disorder≥1 major depressive episode, no mania/hypomania ever.
Persistent Depressive Disorder (dysthymia)Depressed mood most days ≥2 years (1 yr in youth); chronic but milder. "Double depression" = MDD on top of PDD.
Bipolar I≥1 full manic episode (depression not required).
Bipolar II≥1 hypomanic + ≥1 major depressive; never a full manic episode.
Cyclothymia≥2 years of fluctuating subthreshold hypomanic + depressive symptoms.

Statistics: MDD lifetime ~16%, ~2:1 female, mean onset late 20s; bipolar ~1–2%, equal sexes, the most heritable mood disorder (MZ concordance ~60–70%). Causes (integrative): heritability ~40% (depression) up to ~80% (bipolar); monoamine (serotonin/NE/dopamine) dysregulation; HPA-axis/cortisol overactivity; psychologically, Beck's negative cognitive triad (negative view of self, world, future) + depressogenic schemas, and Seligman's learned helplessness → hopelessness theory (attributing bad events to internal-stable-global causes). Stressful life events + low social support trigger episodes in the vulnerable. Treatment: depression — SSRIs first-line, SNRIs/TCAs/MAOIs, ECT for severe/psychotic/refractory; psychotherapy CBT & IPT have the best evidence. Bipolar — lithium (mood stabiliser, anti-suicidal, narrow therapeutic index), valproate/lamotrigine, atypical antipsychotics; antidepressant monotherapy avoided (can flip to mania).

DSM-5-TR — Mood
  • Depressive and bipolar disorders are two separate chapters.
  • Bereavement exclusion removed — MDD can be diagnosed soon after a loss.
  • Added DMDD (child irritability) and PMDD as depressive disorders; Prolonged Grief Disorder (2022).
  • Specifiers: melancholic, atypical, peripartum, seasonal, with mixed features, with anxious distress.
ICD-11 — Mood
  • Single "Mood disorders" grouping, then depressive vs bipolar.
  • Depression graded by severity (mild/moderate/severe ± psychotic) and single vs recurrent.
  • Has Bipolar I and II and cyclothymia; no DMDD (irritability handled differently).
  • Prolonged Grief Disorder sits under trauma/stress, not mood.
BP-I vs BP-II vs cyclothymia. One full mania ever = BP-I (forever). Hypomania + depression, no mania = BP-II. Chronic subthreshold ups-and-downs ≥2 yrs = cyclothymia. MDD vs grief: grief comes in waves, centres on the loss, self-esteem preserved; MDD is pervasive with worthlessness/anhedonia (and DSM-5 dropped the bereavement exclusion).
Recap Ch 11. Episodes (MDE ≥5/9 / 2wk; mania ≥1wk; hypomania ≥4 days) build the disorders (MDD, PDD ≥2yr, BP-I one mania, BP-II hypomania+depression, cyclothymia ≥2yr). Monoamine + HPA + Beck's triad + learned helplessness. SSRIs/ECT + CBT/IPT for depression; lithium for bipolar, no antidepressant alone.

Ch 12. Suicide

B&D treat suicide in the mood chapter. Durkheim's sociological types: egoistic (low social integration, isolation), altruistic (over-integration, dying for the group), anomic (sudden disruption of norms — a crash, a job loss), fatalistic (over-regulation, no escape). Risk factors: prior attempt (the strongest predictor), a clear plan/means, hopelessness, male sex (men complete more; women attempt more), older age, substance use, recent loss, family history, access to lethal means. Terms: ideation → plan → attempt → completion; parasuicide/non-suicidal self-injury (self-harm without intent to die). Prevention: means restriction, crisis lines, treating the underlying disorder; lithium specifically lowers suicide in bipolar.

Men vs women. Women attempt suicide more often; men complete (die by) suicide more often, largely because of more lethal means. The single best predictor of a future attempt is a past attempt.
Recap Ch 12. Durkheim: egoistic / altruistic / anomic / fatalistic. Strongest predictor = prior attempt; hopelessness + plan + means raise risk. Women attempt more, men complete more. Restrict means, treat the disorder.

Part Five · Eating & sleep–wake disorders (B&D Ch 8)

Ch 13. Eating disorders

Clinical snapshot A 17-year-old at 78% of expected weight insists she is "fat," skips meals, runs daily, weighs herself five times a day, and has stopped menstruating; her family is frightened but she cannot see a problem.
DisorderCore
Anorexia NervosaRestriction → significantly low weight, intense fear of weight gain, distorted body image / denial of seriousness. Subtypes: restricting vs binge-eating/purging. (DSM-5 dropped the amenorrhoea requirement.) Highest mortality of any mental disorder.
Bulimia NervosaRecurrent binge + compensatory behaviour (vomiting, laxatives, fasting, over-exercise), self-evaluation over-tied to shape/weight; weight usually normal. Signs: Russell's sign (knuckle calluses), parotid swelling, eroded enamel.
Binge-Eating DisorderRecurrent binges with no compensatory behaviour; distress; new full DSM-5 category. Most common eating disorder; linked to obesity.

Statistics: ~90% female, onset adolescence, Western/industrialised & weight-focused subcultures over-represented. Causes (integrative): sociocultural thin-ideal internalisation + dieting + perfectionism + low self-esteem, on a genetic/temperamental base; serotonin involvement. Treatment: CBT is first-line for bulimia and binge-eating; anorexia — weight restoration first, then family-based treatment (Maudsley) for adolescents; SSRIs help bulimia.

DSM-5-TR — Eating
  • Anorexia, bulimia, binge-eating, ARFID, pica, rumination.
  • Anorexia: no amenorrhoea requirement; severity by BMI.
  • Binge-Eating Disorder = a full standalone diagnosis.
ICD-11 — Eating
  • Same core set; adds ARFID and recognises Binge-Eating Disorder.
  • Anorexia defined by significantly low body weight for age/height + dread of weight gain (no amenorrhoea either).
  • Allows an "anorexia with dangerously low weight" severity flag.
Anorexia vs bulimia. The split is weight: anorexia = significantly underweight (the binge/purge subtype of anorexia still exists, but they're underweight); bulimia = normal/near-normal weight with binge-purge cycles. If markedly underweight → anorexia regardless of purging.
Recap Ch 13. Anorexia (low weight, fear of gain, distorted image; no amenorrhoea criterion; highest mortality), bulimia (binge + compensation, normal weight), binge-eating (no compensation, new category). Thin-ideal + perfectionism on a genetic base. CBT first-line for bulimia/BED; FBT (Maudsley) for adolescent anorexia.

Ch 14. Sleep–wake disorders

GroupKey disorders
Dyssomnias (amount/quality/timing)Insomnia disorder (trouble falling/staying asleep); Hypersomnolence; Narcolepsy (sleep attacks + cataplexy + sleep-onset REM, low hypocretin/orexin); breathing-related (sleep apnoea); circadian-rhythm disorders.
Parasomnias (abnormal events)NREM: sleepwalking & sleep terrors (deep slow-wave sleep, no dream recall, hard to wake). REM: nightmare disorder (vivid dreams, easily recalled) & REM sleep behaviour disorder.
Sleep terror vs nightmare. Sleep terror = NREM (slow-wave), early night, child screams/agitated but unwakeable and amnesic for it. Nightmare = REM, later night, person wakes fully and remembers the dream. Narcolepsy's giveaway = cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone with emotion).

Treatment: insomnia — CBT-I (stimulus control, sleep restriction, sleep hygiene) is first-line over hypnotics; narcolepsy — stimulants/modafinil + scheduled naps; apnoea — CPAP.

Recap Ch 14. Dyssomnias (insomnia, narcolepsy+cataplexy, apnoea, circadian) vs parasomnias (NREM sleep terrors/sleepwalking — amnesic; REM nightmares — recalled). CBT-I first-line for insomnia.

Part Six · Sexual / gender, substance & impulse (B&D Ch 10–11)

Ch 15. Sexual dysfunctions, gender dysphoria & paraphilic disorders

B&D split this into three very different things — don't blur them.

GroupCore
Sexual dysfunctionsProblems in the sexual response cycle: desire (low desire), arousal (erectile, female arousal), orgasm (premature/delayed/female orgasmic), pain (genito-pelvic pain/penetration). Persistent + distressing.
Gender dysphoriaDistress from a marked incongruence between experienced and assigned gender. The diagnosis is the distress, not the identity.
Paraphilic disordersIntense atypical sexual interest (e.g. voyeuristic, exhibitionistic, frotteuristic, sexual sadism/masochism, pedophilic, fetishistic) that causes distress/impairment or harms others. A paraphilia is only a disorder when it harms or distresses.
DSM-5-TR
  • Keeps "Gender Dysphoria" as a diagnosis (distress-based) within mental disorders.
  • Paraphilia vs paraphilic disorder distinction (interest vs harmful/distressing).
ICD-11
  • Moved gender out of mental disorders: renamed "Gender incongruence," placed in a new chapter on sexual health — explicitly de-pathologised.
  • Paraphilic = "Paraphilic disorders," focused on risk/non-consent.
The MET/RCI favourite: ICD-11 removed gender incongruence from the mental-disorders chapter (it's now under sexual health), while DSM-5-TR still lists Gender Dysphoria. Also: a paraphilia (the interest) is not itself a disorder — it becomes a paraphilic disorder only with distress, impairment, or harm to others/non-consent.
Recap Ch 15. Three separate things: sexual dysfunctions (response-cycle problems), gender dysphoria/incongruence (distress, NOT the identity — ICD-11 moved it out of mental disorders), paraphilic disorders (atypical interest only a disorder if harmful/distressing).

Ch 16. Substance-related, addictive & impulse-control disorders

DSM-5 merged abuse + dependence into a single Substance Use Disorder on a severity spectrum (mild/moderate/severe by symptom count across 4 groups: impaired control, social impairment, risky use, pharmacological = tolerance + withdrawal). Key terms: tolerance (need more for the same effect), withdrawal (a substance-specific syndrome on stopping), intoxication. Substance classes: depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids — though opioids are often listed apart), stimulants (cocaine, amphetamines, nicotine, caffeine), opioids, hallucinogens, cannabis. Behavioural addiction: DSM-5 added Gambling Disorder (the first non-substance addiction, reflecting a shared reward-circuit/dopamine basis); ICD-11 added Gaming disorder too. Causes: dopamine reward pathway (nucleus accumbens, VTA), positive then negative reinforcement (use to feel good → use to stop feeling bad), genetic vulnerability, social learning, conditioning of cues. Treatment: motivational interviewing, CBT/relapse-prevention, contingency management, 12-step; agonist/antagonist meds (methadone/buprenorphine, naltrexone, disulfiram, nicotine replacement).

Reclassifications to know: DSM-5 collapsed abuse + dependence into one Substance Use Disorder (severity-graded), and added Gambling Disorder as a behavioural addiction; ICD-11 added Gaming disorder. These "behavioural addictions" sitting beside substances is exactly the kind of update MET tests.
Recap Ch 16. One Substance Use Disorder, severity-graded (impaired control / social / risky / pharmacological). Tolerance vs withdrawal vs intoxication. Gambling Disorder (DSM-5) & Gaming disorder (ICD-11) = behavioural addictions on the dopamine reward circuit. MI + CBT + agonist/antagonist meds.

Part Seven · Personality disorders (B&D Ch 12)

Ch 17. Personality disorders

An enduring, pervasive, inflexible pattern of inner experience and behaviour deviating markedly from cultural expectation, stable from adolescence/early adulthood, causing distress/impairment — across cognition, affect, interpersonal functioning, impulse control. DSM-5 keeps 10 categorical types in three clusters.

ClusterThemeDisorders (the one-line tell)
A — "odd / eccentric"Withdrawn, mistrustfulParanoid (pervasive distrust); Schizoid (detached loner, no desire for relationships); Schizotypal (eccentric, magical thinking, odd perceptions — on the schizophrenia spectrum)
B — "dramatic / erratic"Emotional, impulsiveAntisocial (disregard for/violation of others' rights, deceit, no remorse; ≥18, conduct disorder before 15); Borderline (instability of relationships, self-image, affect + impulsivity, fear of abandonment, self-harm); Histrionic (attention-seeking, shallow dramatic emotion); Narcissistic (grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of empathy)
C — "anxious / fearful"Tense, inhibitedAvoidant (social inhibition, feels inadequate, hypersensitive to rejection); Dependent (clinging, can't decide alone, needs to be taken care of); Obsessive-Compulsive PD (perfectionism, orderliness, control — ego-syntonic)
DSM-5-TR — PDs
  • Kept the 10 categorical types in 3 clusters (Section II).
  • An Alternative DSM-5 Model (dimensional: impairment + 5 trait domains) sits in Section III for study.
ICD-11 — PDs
  • Abolished the categorical types entirely. A single "Personality disorder" diagnosis graded by severity (mild/moderate/severe).
  • Then optional trait domain qualifiers: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, anankastia.
  • Plus a borderline pattern qualifier (kept for treatment relevance).
The single biggest PD reclassification: ICD-11 went fully dimensional — it scrapped the named types and grades severity + trait domains, keeping only a "borderline pattern" specifier. DSM-5 kept the 10 categories (dimensional model only in Section III). This DSM-categorical vs ICD-dimensional split is a stock MET/NIMHANS item.
BPD vs NPD. The BPD feature NOT in NPD = pervasive instability of relationships, self-image and affect + frantic efforts to avoid abandonment + self-harm. NPD = stable grandiosity + need for admiration + lack of empathy. Schizoid vs avoidant: schizoid doesn't want relationships; avoidant wants them but fears rejection. Schizotypal = the odd-beliefs/eccentric one, on the schizophrenia spectrum.
Recap Ch 17. Enduring, pervasive, inflexible pattern from early adulthood. Cluster A odd (paranoid/schizoid/schizotypal), B dramatic (antisocial/borderline/histrionic/narcissistic), C anxious (avoidant/dependent/OCPD). DSM keeps 10 categories; ICD-11 is fully dimensional (severity + traits + borderline pattern). BPD = instability; schizoid doesn't want vs avoidant fears.

Part Eight · Schizophrenia spectrum (B&D Ch 13)

Ch 18. Schizophrenia & other psychotic disorders

Clinical snapshot A 21-year-old, withdrawn over the past year, now believes the TV is sending him coded messages, hears two voices commenting on everything he does, speaks in derailed half-sentences, and has stopped washing or going to class.

Clinical description: a disturbance of thought, perception, affect and behaviour. Positive symptoms (excess of normal function): delusions (fixed false beliefs — persecutory, grandiose, referential, of control), hallucinations (usually auditory), disorganised speech (derailment, tangentiality, word salad), grossly disorganised/catatonic behaviour. Negative symptoms (loss): avolition (apathy), alogia (poverty of speech), anhedonia, asociality, flat affect. Disorganised symptoms (speech + behaviour + inappropriate affect). Statistics: lifetime ~1%, equal sexes, earlier onset in men (late teens–20s) than women. Causes (integrative): strongest is genetic (MZ concordance ~48%, ~10% in a first-degree relative); the dopamine hypothesis (mesolimbic D2 overactivity → positive symptoms; all antipsychotics block D2) refined to include glutamate/NMDA; neurodevelopmental markers (enlarged ventricles, reduced grey matter, obstetric complications, winter birth, maternal infection); and the psychosocial expressed emotion (EE) finding — high family criticism/over-involvement predicts relapse. Treatment: antipsychotics — typical/first-gen (haloperidol, chlorpromazine; strong D2 block → extrapyramidal effects, tardive dyskinesia) and atypical/second-gen (risperidone, olanzapine; clozapine for treatment-resistant, needs WBC monitoring for agranulocytosis) + psychosocial (CBT for psychosis, family therapy to lower EE, social-skills training).

DSM-5-TR — Schizophrenia
  • ≥2 of [delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised/catatonic, negative], ≥1 of the first three.
  • Continuous signs ≥6 months (≥1 month active) + functional decline.
  • Subtypes abolished.
ICD-11 — Schizophrenia
  • ≥2 core symptoms (≥1 of delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking, experiences of influence/passivity) for ≥1 month.
  • No 6-month / functional-decline requirement.
  • Subtypes dropped; replaced by symptom specifiers (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive).
Spectrum neighbourTell
Brief psychotic disorder (DSM) / acute & transient (ICD)Psychosis <1 month, full recovery
Schizophreniform (DSM)Full picture 1–6 months
SchizoaffectiveSchizophrenia + mood episode, PLUS ≥2 weeks of psychosis without mood symptoms
Delusional disorder≥1 month of delusion(s), function otherwise intact
Shared psychotic (folie à deux)Delusion transferred to a close, dependent partner
The duration delta: schizophrenia = 6 months in DSM, 1 month in ICD. By duration: brief psychotic <1mo → schizophreniform 1–6mo → schizophrenia ≥6mo. Kraepelin named it dementia praecox; Bleuler renamed it schizophrenia ("splitting of psychic functions" — NOT split personality / DID). Subtypes (paranoid, catatonic, hebephrenic) are abolished in both manuals.
Recap Ch 18. Positive (delusions/hallucinations/disorganised) + negative (avolition/alogia/anhedonia/asociality/flat affect). ~1%, genes strongest, dopamine D2 + neurodevelopment + high EE → relapse. DSM 6mo / ICD 1mo; subtypes gone. Antipsychotics (clozapine if resistant) + family therapy. Bleuler ≠ split personality.

Part Nine · Neurodevelopmental & neurocognitive disorders (B&D Ch 14–15)

Ch 19. Neurodevelopmental disorders

Onset in the developmental period (childhood), with deficits that impair personal/social/academic functioning.

DisorderCore
Intellectual DisabilityDeficits in intellectual (reasoning, IQ ~<70) and adaptive functioning, onset in development. Severity now graded by adaptive functioning, not IQ score alone.
Autism Spectrum DisorderTwo domains: (1) persistent deficits in social communication/interaction; (2) restricted, repetitive behaviours/interests + sensory sensitivities. DSM-5 merged autistic disorder, Asperger's and PDD-NOS into one spectrum with severity levels.
ADHDInattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, ≥6 symptoms, several before age 12, in ≥2 settings. Presentations: inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, combined. Treated with stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines) + behavioural therapy.
Specific Learning DisorderDifficulty in reading (dyslexia), writing or maths (dyscalculia) well below age, despite adequate instruction/IQ.
Tic disordersTourette's = multiple motor + ≥1 vocal tic, >1 year.
ASD reclassification. DSM-5 abolished Asperger's as a separate diagnosis — it's folded into Autism Spectrum Disorder with severity levels. ADHD onset moved from "before 7" (DSM-IV) to "before age 12" (DSM-5). High-yield change items.
Recap Ch 19. ID (intellectual + adaptive, graded by adaptive function), ASD (social-communication + repetitive; Asperger's merged in), ADHD (inattention/hyperactivity, <12, ≥2 settings, stimulants), specific learning disorder, Tourette's.

Ch 20. Neurocognitive disorders

DisorderCore · the delirium vs dementia split
DeliriumAcute, fluctuating disturbance of attention & awareness + cognition, over hours–days, from a medical cause (infection, drugs, metabolic). Reversible, clouded consciousness. A medical emergency.
Major Neurocognitive Disorder (dementia)Gradual, progressive decline in ≥1 cognitive domain (memory, executive, language, etc.) that impairs independence, with clear consciousness. Subtypes: Alzheimer's (commonest — amyloid plaques + tau tangles, insidious memory-first), vascular, frontotemporal, Lewy body, Parkinson's, HIV. Mild NCD = decline without loss of independence.
Delirium vs dementia — the exam's favourite pair. Delirium = acute onset, fluctuating, attention impaired, consciousness clouded, usually reversible. Dementia = gradual, progressive, memory-led, consciousness clear, usually irreversible. (DSM-5 renamed dementia "major neurocognitive disorder.")
Recap Ch 20. Delirium = acute, fluctuating, attention/consciousness, reversible. Major NCD (dementia) = gradual, progressive, memory, clear consciousness; Alzheimer's = plaques + tangles, commonest. Don't swap the two.

Self-test (cover the answers, active recall)

  1. What are Barlow & Durand's three components of a psychological disorder, and which "D" is missing vs the 4-D list? — dysfunction + distress/impairment + atypical/culturally unexpected; Danger is missing.
  2. Name the three vulnerabilities in Barlow's triple vulnerability theory. — generalised biological, generalised psychological, specific psychological.
  3. Equifinality vs multifinality? — many roads to one disorder vs one cause to many outcomes.
  4. Which neurotransmitter is the "brake" and which the "accelerator"? — GABA (brake), glutamate (accelerator).
  5. Panic disorder needs how long of anticipatory worry after the attacks? — ≥1 month.
  6. What is the B&D mechanism that turns a first panic attack into panic disorder? — interoceptive conditioning + anxiety sensitivity (false alarm → learned alarm).
  7. Which phobia makes you faint, and how is it treated? — blood-injection-injury (biphasic vasovagal); applied tension.
  8. Which manual has Complex PTSD? — ICD-11 (not DSM-5-TR).
  9. Conversion vs factitious vs malingering — split them. — unconscious / sick-role (internal) / external reward (and malingering isn't a disorder).
  10. DID vs schizophrenia — what does "split" actually mean in schizophrenia? — split among thought/emotion/behaviour (Bleuler), not multiple people.
  11. Schizophrenia duration: DSM vs ICD? — 6 months vs 1 month.
  12. BP-I vs BP-II? — one full mania (BP-I) vs hypomania + depression and never mania (BP-II).
  13. First-line therapy for OCD? — Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP).
  14. What did ICD-11 do with gender incongruence and personality disorders? — moved gender out of mental disorders; made PDs fully dimensional.
  15. Delirium vs dementia in four words each. — acute/fluctuating/attention/reversible vs gradual/progressive/memory/clear.
  16. Sleep terror vs nightmare — sleep stage and recall? — NREM, amnesic vs REM, recalled.
  17. Anorexia vs bulimia — the deciding feature? — weight (underweight vs normal).
  18. Durkheim's four suicide types? — egoistic, altruistic, anomic, fatalistic.
  19. Which two behavioural addictions did the manuals add (and to which)? — Gambling Disorder (DSM-5), Gaming disorder (ICD-11).
  20. Best single predictor of a future suicide attempt? — a prior attempt.
