1. Approaches to human cognition

Eysenck & Keane open by saying cognitive psychology is not one method but four converging approaches, each with its own logic and limits. The point of knowing all four is that an exam stem will describe a technique and ask which approach it belongs to.

Experimental cognitive psychology

What it claims: you can infer the hidden stages of mental processing in healthy people by measuring behaviour under controlled conditions — chiefly reaction time (RT) and accuracy/error rate. It is the oldest strand and the historical core of the field. If a manipulation slows people down, that stage must require processing.

Key logic: the subtraction/additive-factors idea — compare two tasks differing in one operation, and the RT difference estimates that operation's duration. Limit: behaviour alone is ambiguous about the underlying architecture (many models predict the same RT), and lab tasks may lack ecological validity.

Cognitive neuropsychology + modularity

What it claims: studying brain-damaged patients reveals the normal architecture of cognition, because damage can knock out one ability while sparing another. The bedrock assumption is modularity (Fodor): the mind contains specialised, domain-specific, informationally encapsulated modules that can be independently impaired.

Key logic — the double dissociation: patient A is impaired on task X but fine on Y, while patient B is fine on X but impaired on Y. That pattern is strong evidence the two tasks rely on separate systems (one alone could be explained by task difficulty). Example: H.M. lost the ability to form new long-term memories yet had normal short-term span; other patients (e.g. K.F.) show the reverse — together a double dissociation of STM and LTM. Limit: damage is rarely clean, the brain reorganises, and single cases generalise poorly.

Cognitive neuroscience methods

What it claims: by measuring the living brain we can locate where and time when cognition happens. The methods trade off spatial against temporal resolution, and the exam loves to ask which method gives which.

Computational cognitive science & connectionism (PDP)

What it claims: if a theory is precise enough, you can implement it as a running computer program; a model that actually reproduces human performance (including human errors) is a strong test of the theory. The dominant style is connectionism / parallel distributed processing (PDP): networks of simple, neuron-like units connected by weighted links, where knowledge is distributed across the pattern of weights rather than stored in one location, and learning happens by gradually adjusting weights (e.g. back-propagation).

NIMHANS tests harder — connectionism. Be ready for the contrast with classical "symbolic" models. Symbolic (e.g. Newell & Simon's physical-symbol-system) manipulates discrete rules and symbols, processing serially. Connectionist/PDP uses distributed sub-symbolic representations and parallel processing; its hallmarks are graceful degradation (partial damage = partial loss, not total crash), generalisation, and learning from examples. NIMHANS may ask which framework explains prototype effects or developmental error patterns (e.g. the U-shaped curve in learning the past tense) — that's connectionism.

Bottom-up vs top-down; serial vs parallel

Two cross-cutting distinctions run through the whole book. Bottom-up (data-driven) processing starts from the stimulus and works up. Top-down (concept-driven) processing brings expectations, context and stored knowledge to bear. Information processing (the frame Broadbent founded) treats the mind as stages from input → encoding → storage → retrieval → output. Within that frame, processing can be serial (one stage at a time, in sequence) or parallel (several at once). Almost every debate below is really "how much top-down vs bottom-up?" and "serial or parallel?"

Recap — approaches. Experimental = RT/accuracy in healthy people. Neuropsychology = patients + double dissociation + modularity. Neuroscience = fMRI/PET (where) · EEG-ERP (when) · TMS (necessary). Computational = running models; PDP = distributed, parallel, learns weights, degrades gracefully. Cutting across all: bottom-up vs top-down, serial vs parallel.

2. Visual perception

The central question: how much does perception build on the stimulus alone, and how much does it require inference from stored knowledge? The two classic answers are Gregory's and Gibson's, and they are the single most tested pair in perception.

Gregory — constructivist / indirect theory (top-down)

What it claims: the retinal image is impoverished and ambiguous, so perception is an active construction — the brain forms and tests perceptual hypotheses, the "best guess" about what is out there, using stored knowledge and context. Perception is therefore largely top-down and indirect.

Key evidence: visual illusions are Gregory's strongest card. The hollow-mask illusion (a concave face is seen as convex because we "know" faces are convex) and the Müller-Lyer illusion show perception departing from the stimulus toward what the brain expects. He also pointed to degraded conditions (poor light) where guessing is clearly needed.

Gibson — direct / ecological theory (bottom-up)

What it claims: in the real world the optic array is information-rich, not impoverished. As we move, the optic flow and invariants (texture gradients, horizon ratio) specify layout directly, so no inference or hypothesis is needed — perception is bottom-up and "direct." His signature concept is the affordance: objects directly offer their possibilities for action (a chair affords sitting, a handle affords grasping).

Key evidence: pilots and animals navigate using optic flow; texture gradients give depth without "thinking." Limit: Gibson struggles to explain illusions (why would the array mislead us?) and downplays the role of stored knowledge.

Gregory vs Gibson: Gregory = indirect/constructivist, top-down, perception is a hypothesis (best for illusions and poor viewing conditions). Gibson = direct/ecological, bottom-up, the info is already in the optic array (best for rich, moving real-world viewing). Stem says "inference / hypothesis / illusion" → Gregory. Stem says "affordance / optic array / optic flow / invariant" → Gibson. Modern view: both are partly right — perception is mostly direct but uses top-down processing when input is degraded.

Marr — computational theory of vision

What it claims: vision is information-processing that builds the scene in stages of increasingly rich representation, mostly bottom-up. The three stages:

  1. Primal sketch — 2-D, captures edges, bars, blobs and intensity changes from the raw image.
  2. 2½-D sketch — depth and orientation of surfaces from the viewer's vantage point (still egocentric).
  3. 3-D model — an object-centred representation, independent of viewpoint, suitable for recognition.

Marr also famously argued any information-processing system must be understood at three levels: the computational (what problem is solved and why), the algorithmic (what representations and steps), and the implementational/hardware (how it is physically realised).

Gestalt grouping laws

Who: Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler. What it claims: the perceptual system organises elements into wholes by innate principles, so that "the whole is other than the sum of its parts." The grouping laws: proximity (near things group), similarity (alike things group), closure (we complete gaps), continuity / good continuation (we follow smooth lines), common fate (things moving together group), and the overarching law of Prägnanz (we perceive the simplest, most stable, "best" organisation possible). Limit: the laws are descriptive, not explanatory, and were mostly demonstrated with 2-D figures.

Depth cues and constancies

Binocular cues need two eyes: retinal (binocular) disparity (the slight difference between the two retinal images, the basis of stereopsis) and convergence (the inward turn of the eyes for near objects). Monocular / pictorial cues need only one eye and work in pictures: linear perspective, interposition (overlap), texture gradient, relative size, height in the visual field, aerial perspective, light and shadow, plus the motion-based motion parallax. Perceptual constancies keep the world stable despite a changing retinal image: size, shape, colour and brightness/lightness constancy.

Milner & Goodale — two visual streams

What it claims: the visual cortex splits into two functionally distinct pathways. The ventral stream ("what" / vision-for-perception) runs to the temporal lobe and supports conscious recognition and identification. The dorsal stream ("where / how" / vision-for-action) runs to the parietal lobe and guides visually controlled action online.

Key evidence — patient DF: DF had ventral-stream damage (visual-form agnosia from carbon-monoxide poisoning). She could not report the orientation of a slot, yet she could post a card through it accurately — her action system "knew" what her perception system could not. The mirror-image patient (optic ataxia, dorsal damage) can describe an object but mis-reaches for it. That is a double dissociation of perception and action.

Ventral vs dorsal: Ventral = "What" (temporal lobe, conscious recognition; DF's damaged stream). Dorsal = "Where/How" (parietal lobe, guiding action; optic-ataxia patients). DF can act on what she cannot consciously perceive — remember "post the card she can't describe."
Recap — perception. Gregory (top-down, hypotheses, illusions) vs Gibson (bottom-up, affordances, optic array). Marr builds primal → 2½-D → 3-D, analysed at computational/algorithmic/implementational levels. Gestalt = proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common fate, Prägnanz. Depth = binocular (disparity, convergence) + monocular/pictorial. Milner & Goodale: ventral "what" vs dorsal "where/how", patient DF dissociates them.

3. Object & face recognition

How do we go from a 2-D retinal pattern to "that's a cup" or "that's my brother" despite changes in size, angle and lighting? The chapter runs from crude template matching to Biederman's geons, then to a dedicated face model.

Template theory

What it claims: recognition is matching the input to a stored whole-pattern template; the best match wins. Fatal flaw: it needs a separate template for every size, orientation, font and partial view of every object — combinatorially impossible. Normalisation (rescaling/rotating before matching) only partly rescues it. This failure motivated feature theories.

Feature theory — Pandemonium (Selfridge)

What it claims: recognition proceeds by detecting component features rather than whole templates. Selfridge's Pandemonium model is the classic metaphor: layers of "demons" — image demons register the raw input, feature demons shout when their feature is present, cognitive demons shout louder the more of their features are detected, and a decision demon picks the loudest. Support: we confuse letters that share features (E↔F), and visual search is harder among similar-featured distractors. Limit: ignores context and the spatial relations between features (a face is not just two eyes + a nose in any arrangement).

Biederman — recognition-by-components (geons)

What it claims: objects are recognised by decomposing them into a small alphabet of basic 3-D volumetric primitives called geons (geometric ions — cylinders, cones, blocks, wedges; about 36 of them). Because geons are defined by non-accidental properties (features like straight vs curved edges that stay constant across most viewpoints), recognition is largely view-invariant: as long as the key geons and their arrangement are visible, you recognise the object from many angles. Builds on Marr & Nishihara's object-centred (viewpoint-independent) representation idea. Support: objects become hard to recognise precisely when degradation hides geon junctions; recognition survives degradation that spares them. Limit: recognition is not perfectly view-invariant (some views are reliably faster), and it explains category-level ("a chair") far better than specific-instance ("my chair") recognition.

Bruce & Young — model of face recognition

What it claims: face recognition runs through a sequence of stages with separable routes. Faces first undergo structural encoding (building a description of the face). For familiar faces this feeds Face Recognition Units (FRUs) — one per known face, signalling "this face is familiar." An activated FRU triggers the Person Identity Node (PIN), which stores semantic/biographical info (their job, where you know them from). Only after the PIN can you retrieve the name, via a separate name-generation store.

Key prediction and evidence: because the name comes last and through its own route, you can feel a face is familiar and recall facts about the person yet be unable to retrieve the name — but you never get the reverse (a name without familiarity). Diary studies of everyday recognition errors confirm exactly this asymmetry. Expression analysis and lip-reading run in parallel separate routes, which is why some patients lose identity recognition but still read emotion, or vice versa.

Prosopagnosia & the FFA

What it claims/observes: prosopagnosia is a selective inability to recognise faces despite largely intact object recognition and intact vision — patients may identify people only by voice, gait or a distinctive feature. It is associated with damage to the fusiform face area (FFA) in the ventral temporal cortex, a region that responds preferentially to faces. The double dissociation between prosopagnosia (faces lost, objects spared) and object agnosia (objects lost, faces relatively spared) supports the idea that face processing is at least partly special / domain-specific.

Apperceptive vs associative agnosia (Lissauer)

What it claims: visual agnosia is failure to recognise objects despite adequate vision, and Lissauer split it in two by where the breakdown is. Apperceptive agnosia = the percept itself cannot be formed; the patient cannot copy or match a drawing. Associative agnosia = the percept is built fine (the patient can copy the drawing accurately) but it cannot be linked to stored meaning, so they still cannot name it or say what it is for.

Apperceptive vs associative agnosia: apperceptive can't copy the drawing (perception is broken); associative can copy it faithfully but still can't say what it is (perception intact, meaning lost). The copying test is the dividing line. Lissauer = "perception lost" vs "meaning lost."
Recap — recognition. Template (rigid, fails on variation) → feature/Pandemonium (Selfridge's demons, ignores relations) → Biederman's geons (view-invariant volumetric parts, builds on Marr & Nishihara). Faces: Bruce & Young structural encoding → FRU → PIN → name (name is last, hence familiarity-without-name but never the reverse). Prosopagnosia/FFA = faces lost specifically. Agnosia: apperceptive (can't copy) vs associative (can copy, can't name).

4. Attention

Attention is "selection under limited capacity." The historic debate is where in processing the bottleneck sits — early (before meaning) or late (at response). The tool that built the debate is dichotic listening: different messages to each ear, and the listener "shadows" (repeats aloud) one.

Broadbent — filter (early-selection) theory

What it claims: there is a single limited-capacity channel preceded by a filter that selects one input on the basis of physical features (which ear, pitch) before meaning is analysed. The unattended channel is blocked.

Key evidence: in dichotic split-span studies, people recalled by ear rather than chronological order, fitting channel-by-channel selection. The finding that broke it: listeners often noticed their own name on the unattended ear (Moray's cocktail-party result) and could switch ears to follow meaning — impossible if the filter blocks meaning entirely.

Treisman — attenuation theory

What it claims: the filter does not block the unattended channel, it turns it down (attenuates). Attenuated input is still analysed, but only items with low thresholds — your name, danger words, contextually expected words — get through. This is the cocktail-party account: most background talk is filtered, but salient input breaks through.

Status: early selection but leaky; explains the own-name effect that sinks strict Broadbent while keeping selection early.

Deutsch & Norman — late-selection theory

What it claims: all inputs are fully processed for meaning, and selection happens late — at the response/awareness stage — based on relevance. Deutsch & Deutsch proposed it; Norman refined it (selection by pertinence). Tension: full semantic analysis of everything is metabolically expensive, and most evidence favours an early-but-flexible bottleneck (Treisman), so late selection is generally seen as too strong.

Kahneman — capacity / resource theory

What it claims: instead of a fixed structural bottleneck, attention is a limited pool of mental effort/resources flexibly allocated. Two tasks interfere when their combined demand exceeds the pool; effort rises with arousal and task difficulty (indexed by pupil dilation). This explains dual-task performance: easy tasks combine, hard ones compete. Refinement (Allport, Wickens): there may be multiple resource pools (e.g. separate verbal and spatial), so two tasks interfere more if they draw on the same pool.

Posner spotlight + cueing; Eriksen zoom-lens

What it claims: visual attention is like a movable spotlight that can be directed independently of the eyes (covert attention). Posner's cueing paradigm: a cue points to a location, then a target appears either at the cued (valid) or another (invalid) location. Finding: RTs are faster after valid cues and slower after invalid ones (a "cost" for re-orienting) — direct evidence attention can move covertly to a location and speed processing there. Eriksen's zoom-lens adds that the spotlight's size is adjustable: it can be a narrow high-resolution beam or a wide low-resolution one, trading area for processing power.

Treisman — Feature Integration Theory (FIT)

What it claims: individual features (colour, orientation, size) are registered in parallel, pre-attentively, across the whole field on separate "feature maps." Focused attention is then the "glue" that binds features at a location into a single object. Key evidence: (1) Pop-out vs serial search — a target defined by a single feature (a red bar among green) "pops out" with search time flat regardless of set size (parallel), whereas a target defined by a conjunction of features (a red vertical among red horizontals and green verticals) requires slow item-by-item search rising with set size (serial). (2) Illusory conjunctions — when attention is overloaded or divided, people wrongly recombine features across objects (report a "red X" when shown a red O and a green X), proving features are coded separately before binding.

Inattentional & change blindness

Inattentional blindness (Simons & Chabris): when attention is engaged elsewhere (counting basketball passes), people fail to see a salient unexpected event — the person in the gorilla suit walking through. Change blindness (Rensink): with a brief disruption (a flicker or "mudsplash") between two scenes, people fail to notice large changes to the scene. Both show that without attention there is strikingly little conscious perception, and that we do not hold a rich detailed picture of the world in mind.

Automatic vs controlled processing (Shiffrin & Schneider) + Stroop

What it claims: with practice, controlled processing (slow, serial, effortful, capacity-limited, flexible) becomes automatic (fast, parallel, effortless, capacity-free, hard to suppress). Evidence: Schneider & Shiffrin's consistent-mapping search becomes automatic and set-size-independent with practice, whereas varied-mapping search stays controlled. The Stroop effect is the showcase of unavoidable automaticity: naming the ink colour of a colour word ("RED" printed in blue) is slowed because reading the word is automatic and intrudes on the controlled colour-naming task.

Early vs late selection: Broadbent filters before meaning and blocks the rest (early, all-or-none). Treisman attenuates rather than blocks, so salient meaning leaks through (early but leaky). Deutsch-Norman processes everything for meaning and selects at response (late). The own-name / cocktail-party finding kills strict Broadbent and best fits Treisman.
Spotlight vs zoom-lens vs FIT: Posner's spotlight = attention moves to a location (cueing costs/benefits). Eriksen's zoom-lens = the spotlight changes size (area vs resolution trade-off). Treisman's FIT = attention binds features into objects (pop-out vs serial search, illusory conjunctions). Don't mix the "where" of attention (spotlight) with the "binding" job (FIT).
Recap — attention. Bottleneck debate: Broadbent (early, blocks) → Treisman (early, attenuates, own-name leaks) → Deutsch-Norman (late, by pertinence) → Kahneman (capacity/effort, no fixed bottleneck). Space: Posner spotlight (cueing), Eriksen zoom-lens. Binding: Treisman's FIT (parallel feature maps + attention as glue; pop-out vs serial; illusory conjunctions). Limits of attention: inattentional (gorilla) and change blindness. Practice: controlled → automatic; Stroop = automatic reading intruding.

5. Short-term & working memory

The chapter moves from the structural "three boxes" model to Baddeley & Hitch's active, multi-component working memory, and ends with the levels-of-processing critique.

Atkinson & Shiffrin — multi-store (modal) model

What it claims: memory is three structural stores in series — sensory register → short-term store (STS) → long-term store (LTS) — linked by control processes. Attention moves info from sensory to STM; rehearsal maintains it in STM and transfers it to LTM. STM is limited in capacity and duration; LTM is effectively unlimited and permanent.

Key evidence — the serial-position curve: in free recall, the first items (primacy) and last items (recency) are recalled best. Primacy reflects extra rehearsal into LTM; recency reflects items still in STM. Crucially they dissociate: a delay with a distractor task wipes out recency (STM emptied) but leaves primacy intact; a faster presentation rate hurts primacy (less rehearsal) but not recency. Two variables, two ends → two stores. Patient evidence (H.M.: good STM, no new LTM; K.F.: poor STM, normal LTM) gives the double dissociation. Limit: treats STM as a single passive store and rehearsal as the only route to LTM — both wrong.

Sperling — iconic memory (and echoic)

What it claims: there is a brief, high-capacity sensory store for vision (iconic memory). The experiment: Sperling flashed a 3×4 grid of letters for 50 ms. In whole report people recalled only ~4–5 letters. In partial report, a tone (high/medium/low) cued which row to report after the display vanished; people then reported almost any cued row in full. Since they could report whichever row was cued, all ~9–12 letters were briefly available — capacity is large. As the cue was delayed, performance fell to whole-report levels by ~1 second, showing iconic memory decays in about ½ second. The auditory equivalent, echoic memory, lasts longer (~2–4 s).

Miller — magic number 7 ± 2 & chunking

What it claims: immediate (short-term) memory span is about 7 ± 2 items. The key insight is that the limit is set by the number of chunks, not raw items: by recoding ("HBOFBICIA" → "HBO FBI CIA") you pack more information into the same number of slots, so chunking expands effective capacity. (Later work, e.g. Cowan, argues the true limit is closer to ~4 chunks once rehearsal is controlled.)

Baddeley & Hitch — working memory model

What it claims: STM is not one passive store but an active multi-component system that holds and manipulates information. Components:

  • Central executive — attentional controller; allocates resources, switches and coordinates tasks. Limited capacity, modality-free; the least specified part.
  • Phonological loop — verbal/acoustic store with two parts: the phonological store ("inner ear," holds speech-based info for ~2 s) and the articulatory rehearsal process ("inner voice," refreshes it by subvocal rehearsal).
  • Visuospatial sketchpad — holds and manipulates visual and spatial information ("inner eye").
  • Episodic buffer (added 2000) — limited store that binds information from the loop, sketchpad and LTM into a single multimodal episode, and is the interface to long-term memory.

Evidence for the phonological loop: (1) Word-length effect — memory span is worse for longer words because they take longer to rehearse (the loop holds about 2 s of speech). (2) Phonological-similarity effect — lists of similar-sounding letters (B, P, T, V) are recalled worse than dissimilar ones, showing the store is acoustic/phonological. (3) Articulatory suppression — repeating "the, the, the" ties up the rehearsal process and abolishes both the word-length effect and (for visually presented items) the phonological-similarity effect. Evidence for separate subsystems: people can do a verbal task and a visuospatial task at the same time with little interference, but two verbal (or two spatial) tasks compete — the dual-task logic that demands two specialised stores.

Craik & Lockhart — levels of processing

What it claims: retention depends not on which "store" or how long you rehearse, but on the depth of processing at encoding. Shallow processing of physical features (is it capital letters?) or sound (does it rhyme?) gives poor memory; deep, semantic processing (does it fit this sentence? what does it mean?) gives strong memory. Memory is a by-product of how you processed the item. Evidence: Craik & Tulving's orienting-task studies — deeper (semantic) judgements produced far better later recognition than shallow ones, even though semantic processing felt no "harder." Distinguishes maintenance rehearsal (mere repetition, little benefit) from elaborative rehearsal (linking to meaning, big benefit). Limit: "depth" is hard to define independently of the memory it predicts (circularity).

Multi-store vs working memory: Atkinson-Shiffrin's STM is a single passive store; Baddeley-Hitch splits it into active components doing different jobs. The decisive evidence is dual-task: verbal + visuospatial can run together (different subsystems) but two verbal tasks collide (same subsystem) — impossible for a single STM box.
Iconic (Sperling) vs working memory: iconic memory is a pre-attentive sensory store, very large, lasts ~½ s, decays automatically. STM/WM is post-attentive, ~7 chunks (or ~4), lasts ~15–30 s without rehearsal, and (in WM) manipulates info. Don't call the partial-report store "short-term memory."
Recap — STM/WM. Atkinson-Shiffrin: sensory → STM → LTM, supported by the serial-position curve (recency = STM, primacy = LTM, and they dissociate). Sperling's partial report: iconic memory is large but ~½ s. Miller: 7±2 chunks, expandable by chunking. Baddeley-Hitch WM: central executive + phonological loop (word-length, phonological-similarity, articulatory suppression) + visuospatial sketchpad + episodic buffer. Craik & Lockhart: depth of processing beats rehearsal time; elaborative > maintenance.

6. Long-term memory

LTM is not one thing. The chapter carves it into systems (declarative vs procedural; episodic vs semantic), explains how retrieval depends on cues, and grounds the divisions in amnesia.

Cohen & Squire — declarative vs procedural (non-declarative)

What it claims: the deepest split is between declarative memory (explicit, conscious, "knowing that" — facts and events, depends on the medial temporal lobe/hippocampus) and procedural / non-declarative memory (implicit, "knowing how" — skills, conditioning, priming, depends on other systems like the basal ganglia and cerebellum). Evidence: amnesics like H.M. cannot form new declarative memories yet can learn new motor skills (mirror-drawing) and show priming — improving across days while denying they ever did the task. That dissociation proves the two are separate systems.

Tulving — episodic vs semantic

What it claims: within declarative memory, episodic memory stores personally experienced events tied to a time and place ("my last birthday"), accompanied by mental "time travel" and conscious recollection. Semantic memory stores general facts and knowledge stripped of their learning context ("Paris is the capital of France"). Evidence: in some amnesics episodic memory is devastated while semantic knowledge is relatively preserved (and developmental amnesics can acquire semantic facts despite poor episodic memory) — a dissociation supporting two systems.

Tulving & Thomson — encoding specificity

What it claims: retrieval succeeds to the extent that cues present at recall match the cues encoded at the time of learning — memory is about the match between encoding and retrieval, not the strength of the trace alone. This is the umbrella for: context-dependent memory (Godden & Baddeley's divers recalled word lists best when tested in the same environment — land or underwater — as they learned them) and state-dependent memory (recall is better when your internal state, e.g. mood or sobriety, matches learning). It also explains why recognition can sometimes fail where recall succeeds (recognition failure of recallable words).

Ebbinghaus — the forgetting curve

What it did: Ebbinghaus tested himself on lists of nonsense syllables (to control for prior meaning) and measured the savings method (how much faster relearning is than original learning). Finding: the forgetting curve — forgetting is steep at first then levels off (most loss happens soon after learning, then the rate slows). He also documented the spacing benefit of distributed practice.

Bartlett — reconstructive memory & schemas

What it claims: remembering is not replay but reconstruction guided by schemas (organised prior knowledge). The study — "War of the Ghosts": Bartlett had English participants read an unfamiliar Native American folk tale, then recall it repeatedly over time. Recall became shorter, more coherent, and distorted toward the participants' own cultural expectations — unfamiliar/supernatural elements were dropped or "rationalised" (e.g. "canoe" became "boat"). This showed memory actively reshapes material to fit existing schemas. Caveat the exam may probe: Bartlett's methods were loosely controlled, but the reconstructive principle is well established and underlies Loftus's later eyewitness work.

Consolidation

What it claims: a new memory is initially fragile and becomes stable over time through consolidation; disruption (e.g. a head injury, ECT) shortly after learning erases the unconsolidated trace, which is why retrograde amnesia is typically worse for recent memories (Ribot's gradient). The hippocampus binds the memory at first; over time (systems consolidation) the memory becomes increasingly independent of the hippocampus and reliant on the neocortex. Sleep aids consolidation.

Patient H.M. (Scoville & Milner)

What happened: to treat severe epilepsy, surgeon Scoville removed H.M.'s medial temporal lobes bilaterally, including most of the hippocampus. The result: dense anterograde amnesia — he could not form new long-term declarative memories — with intact short-term memory (normal digit span), intact procedural learning (he improved at mirror-drawing across days while denying he had ever done it), and largely preserved older memories. Why it matters: H.M. is the founding case that (1) STM and LTM are separate systems, (2) the hippocampus is critical for forming new declarative memories but not for STM or for retrieving old ones, and (3) declarative and procedural memory are dissociable.

Episodic vs semantic: if the fact is tied to when/where you learned it (an event with mental time-travel), it's episodic; if it's free-floating general knowledge, it's semantic. Amnesics often lose episodic while semantic survives.
Anterograde vs retrograde amnesia: antero = can't form new memories after the injury (H.M.); retro = lost memories from before the injury. Mnemonic: antero = "ahead/forward in time"; retro = "back in time." H.M.'s defining deficit is anterograde.
Recap — LTM. Declarative (knowing that, hippocampal) vs procedural (knowing how) — dissociated in H.M. Within declarative: episodic (events, time/place) vs semantic (facts), Tulving. Retrieval = encoding-specificity match (context/state-dependent; Godden & Baddeley divers). Ebbinghaus: nonsense syllables, savings, steep-then-flat forgetting curve. Bartlett: reconstructive, schema-driven, War of the Ghosts. Consolidation stabilises traces (hippocampus → neocortex). H.M. (Scoville & Milner): bilateral hippocampal loss → anterograde amnesia, intact STM and skills.

7. Forgetting & everyday memory

Why memories fail, and how memory works outside the lab. The exam wants the named mechanisms and Loftus's eyewitness findings.

Decay vs interference

Decay theory: the memory trace simply fades with time if not used — a passive process. Hard to test cleanly because time always lets other activity in. Interference theory (better supported for LTM): forgetting is caused by competition between memories. Proactive interference = old learning disrupts new (your old phone number intrudes on the new one). Retroactive interference = new learning disrupts old (learning French makes you lose Spanish). Mnemonic: PRO = prior interferes forward; RETRO = recent reaches back.

Cue-dependent forgetting & TOT

What it claims (Tulving): often the trace is intact but the right retrieval cue is missing — "availability vs accessibility." Provide the cue and the memory returns, which is why recognition often beats free recall. The tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) state is the vivid case: you can report the first letter, number of syllables and similar-sounding words, proving the memory is there but not fully accessible.

Repression / motivated forgetting

What it claims (Freud): threatening or anxiety-laden memories are actively pushed out of conscious awareness. Highly controversial and hard to demonstrate experimentally; mentioned for completeness and because it underlies the "recovered memory" debate.

Loftus — misinformation, leading questions, weapon focus

What it claims: memory is reconstructive and post-event information can rewrite it. The car-crash study (Loftus & Palmer): participants watched a film of a car accident, then were asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed / collided / bumped / hit / contacted" each other. The single verb changed the speed estimate — "smashed" produced the highest estimates — and a week later those given "smashed" were more likely to falsely "remember" broken glass that was never in the film. The misinformation effect: misleading post-event details get incorporated into the memory. Weapon focus: when a weapon is present, attention narrows onto it, worsening memory for other details such as the culprit's face. Together these undermine eyewitness reliability.

Brown & Kulik — flashbulb memory

What it claims: shocking, emotionally significant public events create vivid, detailed, confidently held memories of the moment one heard the news — as if a "flashbulb" captured the scene. The catch the exam wants: flashbulb memories feel highly accurate and are held with great confidence, but research (e.g. on people's memories of disasters) shows they decay and distort like ordinary memories — confidence is not accuracy.

Prospective memory

What it claims: prospective memory is remembering to perform an intended action in the future (take the pill, post the letter), as opposed to retrospective memory (recalling past information). It splits into event-based (do it when a cue occurs — "give Mum the message when you see her") and time-based (do it at a time — "call at 3pm"); time-based is harder because there is no external cue, so it relies more on self-initiated monitoring.

Proactive vs retroactive interference: PROactive — prior (old) learning interferes with new. RETROactive — recent (new) learning interferes with old, reaching back. Direction of the arrow is the trick.
NIMHANS tests harder — prospective memory. Expect the event- vs time-based split, the claim that time-based is harder (no external cue, needs self-initiated retrieval), and that prospective memory has a "remember to remember" component absent from retrospective memory. May be linked clinically to frontal-lobe/executive function and to everyday-memory failures in patients.
Recap — forgetting. Decay (passive fading, weakly supported) vs interference (proactive = old→new, retroactive = new→old). Cue-dependent/TOT = it's there but inaccessible (Tulving). Repression = Freud, controversial. Loftus: misinformation, leading-verb car-crash study (broken glass), weapon focus. Brown & Kulik flashbulb = vivid + confident but not actually accurate. Prospective memory: event- vs time-based, time-based harder.

8. Concepts & knowledge representation

How are categories ("bird," "furniture") stored? The progression: rigid definitions fail, so we move to prototypes, then exemplars, and to network theories of how concepts connect.

Defining-attribute (classical) view

What it claims: a concept is defined by a set of necessary and sufficient features — anything with all of them is a member, anything missing one is not, and all members are equal. Why it fails: most natural categories have fuzzy boundaries and no clean defining set (Wittgenstein's "game"), and people reliably rate some members as more typical than others (a robin is a "better" bird than a penguin) — which a defining-feature view cannot allow, since membership is all-or-none.

Rosch — prototype theory

What it claims: categories are organised around a prototype — an abstract best/average example summarising the central tendency of the category. Membership is graded by similarity to the prototype, which directly explains typicality effects (typical members are categorised faster and named first). Rosch also identified the basic level (e.g. "dog") as the most informative, fastest-named, first-learned level — more useful than the superordinate ("animal") or subordinate ("collie"). Evidence: typicality predicts verification speed, production frequency and learning order.

Medin & Schaffer — exemplar theory

What it claims: we do not abstract a single prototype; instead we store many specific exemplars (remembered instances) and categorise a new item by its similarity to those stored instances. Advantage over prototypes: exemplar models retain information about variability and atypical members, and account for context and frequency effects that a single averaged prototype loses.

Collins & Quillian — hierarchical semantic network

What it claims: concepts are nodes in a hierarchy (canary → bird → animal) and properties are stored at the highest level they apply to — cognitive economy ("has skin" stored once at "animal," not repeated at every animal). Prediction & evidence: sentence-verification time should increase with the distance travelled in the hierarchy — "A canary can sing" (0 levels) is verified faster than "A canary can fly" (1 level) faster than "A canary has skin" (2 levels). Reaction times broadly confirmed this. Problems: the typicality effect ("A robin is a bird" verified faster than "A penguin is a bird," same distance) and familiarity effects break the strict-distance prediction.

Collins & Loftus — spreading activation

What it claims: revised the network to drop the strict hierarchy: concepts are linked by semantic relatedness, with shorter/stronger links between more related concepts. Activating one concept causes activation to spread along links to related concepts, pre-activating them. Evidence — semantic priming: people verify "nurse" faster after seeing the related word "doctor" than after an unrelated word, because "doctor" pre-activated "nurse." This handles typicality and relatedness effects the rigid hierarchy could not.

Schema & script (Bartlett; Schank & Abelson)

What it claims: larger bodies of knowledge are organised as schemas (structured packets of expectations about objects/situations) that guide comprehension, inference and memory. A script (Schank & Abelson) is a schema for a stereotyped event sequence — the restaurant script (enter → seated → order → eat → pay → leave). Evidence: people falsely "recall" script-consistent actions that were never stated (you infer they paid the bill), and read script-typical sentences faster — showing scripts fill gaps with default inferences.

Prototype vs exemplar: prototype = one abstracted average per category, judged by similarity to the ideal. Exemplar = many stored individual instances, judged by similarity to remembered cases. "Compare to the ideal bird" = prototype (Rosch); "compare to all the actual birds I've seen" = exemplar (Medin & Schaffer).
Collins & Quillian vs Collins & Loftus: Quillian = strict hierarchy + cognitive economy, predicts verification time by distance (and is broken by typicality). Loftus = spreading activation over a relatedness web, predicts semantic priming. Same first author "Collins," very different models.
Recap — knowledge. Classical defining-attribute view fails (fuzzy boundaries, typicality). Rosch: prototypes + typicality + basic level. Medin & Schaffer: exemplars (stored instances, keep variability). Networks: Collins & Quillian hierarchy + cognitive economy + verification-by-distance; Collins & Loftus spreading activation + semantic priming. Schemas/scripts (Schank & Abelson restaurant script) fill gaps with default inferences.

9. Language

Language covers acquisition, comprehension (parsing, reading, listening), production, and the brain basis. Each subtopic has a signature model and a signature finding.

Sapir-Whorf — linguistic relativity

What it claims: the language you speak shapes the way you think. Strong form (linguistic determinism): language determines thought and limits what you can conceive — widely rejected (people think about concepts they have no single word for). Weak form (linguistic relativity): language influences habitual thought and perception — has some support (e.g. colour-term boundaries can shift colour discrimination; grammatical gender nudges object descriptions).

Chomsky vs Skinner — language acquisition

What it claims: against Skinner's behaviourist account (language learned by imitation and reinforcement), Chomsky argued grammar is too complex and acquired too fast, on too little/imperfect input, to be learned by reinforcement — the "poverty of the stimulus." He proposed an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD) embodying a Universal Grammar common to all languages. He distinguished surface structure (the actual words/syntax of a sentence) from deep structure (its underlying meaning), linked by transformational rules — which is why one surface form can be ambiguous (two deep structures) and two different surface forms can share a meaning.

Frazier — garden-path model (parsing)

What it claims: syntactic parsing is initially syntax-first and serial: we commit to one structure using simple principles such as minimal attachment (build the simplest tree, fewest nodes) and late closure (attach new words to the current phrase). When that first parse turns out wrong we have to reanalyse — the "garden path." Classic sentence: "The horse raced past the barn fell" — we read "raced" as the main verb and stumble at "fell," which forces reinterpretation ("the horse [that was] raced past the barn fell"). Reading-time spikes at the disambiguating word are the evidence.

Coltheart — dual-route model of reading

What it claims: reading aloud uses two routes from print to sound. The lexical (direct) route looks the whole word up in the mental lexicon — needed for irregular words ("yacht," "pint") that don't follow rules. The non-lexical / grapheme-phoneme (sub-lexical) route applies spelling-to-sound rules — needed for novel words and non-words ("glorp"). The clinching dissociation in acquired dyslexia: surface dyslexia = lexical route damaged, so irregular words are "regularised" (read "pint" to rhyme with "mint") but non-words are fine. Phonological dyslexia = grapheme-phoneme route damaged, so familiar words are read but non-words cannot be sounded out. The double dissociation maps cleanly onto the two routes.

Levelt — speech production

What it claims: producing speech runs through ordered stages: conceptualisation (deciding the message) → formulation (selecting words — lemma/meaning then lexeme/sound — and building syntax) → articulation (motor output), with a self-monitoring loop. Evidence — speech errors: systematic slips reveal the stages — Spoonerisms ("dear old queen" → "queer old dean") show sound-level exchange at formulation, while word-substitution and "freudian slip" errors implicate the lemma-selection stage. The tip-of-the-tongue state shows lemma retrieved but lexeme (sound) not yet available.

McGurk effect & cohort model (perception)

McGurk & MacDonald: speech perception is multimodal — seeing lips say "ga" while hearing "ba" makes you perceive "da," a fusion of the two. Vision overrides audition, proving we integrate seen and heard speech automatically. Marslen-Wilson's cohort model: on hearing a word's onset, all words consistent with it are activated (the "cohort"); as more sound arrives, candidates are eliminated until one remains at the uniqueness point. Context can further narrow the cohort, which is why we recognise spoken words remarkably early.

Aphasias — Broca, Wernicke, conduction

Broca's aphasia (Paul Broca): non-fluent, effortful, halting, agrammatic speech (function words dropped), with comprehension relatively spared. Lesion: left frontal lobe (Broca's area). Wernicke's aphasia (Carl Wernicke): fluent but empty, meaningless "word salad" with neologisms and paraphasias, and poor comprehension; patients often unaware of the deficit. Lesion: left posterior temporal lobe (Wernicke's area). Conduction aphasia: fluent speech and good comprehension but a striking inability to repeat, from damage to the arcuate fasciculus connecting the two areas.

Broca vs Wernicke: Broca = Broken speech — can't produce fluently but understands (frontal). Wernicke = fluent nonsense — speaks easily but can't comprehend (temporal). Conduction = can't repeat (arcuate fasciculus). Mnemonic: Broca/Broken/front; Wernicke/Word-salad/back.
Surface vs phonological dyslexia: surface = lexical route gone → trips on irregular words (regularises "pint"), non-words OK. Phonological = grapheme-phoneme route gone → trips on non-words, real words OK. Map each dyslexia to the route it lost.
Recap — language. Sapir-Whorf: strong (determinism) rejected, weak (relativity) some support. Chomsky LAD/Universal Grammar + deep/surface structure vs Skinner's reinforcement. Frazier garden-path: syntax-first, minimal attachment, "horse raced past the barn fell." Coltheart dual-route: lexical (irregular words, surface dyslexia) vs grapheme-phoneme (non-words, phonological dyslexia). Levelt: conceptualise → formulate → articulate, speech errors reveal stages. McGurk = multimodal; Marslen-Wilson cohort = onset activates many, narrows to uniqueness point. Aphasias: Broca (frontal, non-fluent), Wernicke (temporal, fluent nonsense), conduction (arcuate, can't repeat).

10. Problem solving & expertise

Two traditions meet here: the Gestalt focus on insight and the obstacles to it, and the information-processing focus on search through a problem space (Newell & Simon), plus what makes experts expert.

Gestalt insight & restructuring (Köhler, Sultan)

What it claims: some problems are solved not by gradual search but by a sudden restructuring of how the problem is represented — the "aha" of insight. Evidence: Köhler's chimpanzee Sultan suddenly stacked boxes / joined two sticks to reach a banana after apparent contemplation, rather than by trial-and-error, suggesting a perceptual reorganisation of the situation.

Duncker — functional fixedness

What it claims: a barrier to insight is functional fixedness — the tendency to see an object only in terms of its usual function. The candle problem: given a candle, a box of tacks and matches, people struggle to mount the candle on the wall because they see the box as a container, not as a potential shelf; the solution is to empty the box, tack it to the wall, and stand the candle on it. People solve it faster when the box is presented empty (its "container" function de-emphasised).

Luchins — mental set (Einstellung)

What it claims: a mental set (Einstellung) is the tendency to keep using a previously successful strategy even when a simpler one is available. The water-jar task: after solving several problems with the same multi-step formula, participants kept applying it to later problems that had a far simpler solution, and some failed problems solvable only the simple way. Prior success entrenches a rigid approach.

Newell & Simon — means-ends analysis, problem space, GPS

What it claims: problem solving is search through a problem space — the set of all states (start, goal, intermediate) and the operators that move between them. Because the space is huge, we use heuristics, chiefly means-ends analysis: note the difference between the current state and the goal, find an operator that reduces it, and if that operator can't be applied yet, set a subgoal to enable it. Implemented in their General Problem Solver (GPS) program. Evidence: verbal protocols ("think-aloud") on the Tower of Hanoi and similar well-defined puzzles matched the means-ends, subgoal-setting account.

Well- vs ill-defined problems

Well-defined problems have a clearly specified start state, goal state and operators (chess, Tower of Hanoi) — ideal for the problem-space approach. Ill-defined problems lack one or more of these ("write a good essay," "be happy"); most real-world problems are ill-defined, which is a limit on the laboratory puzzle tradition.

Expertise & chunking (de Groot; Chase & Simon)

What it claims: expert advantage is knowledge-based, not raw memory. The chess studies: de Groot found masters and novices searched similar numbers of moves, but masters perceived the board in better-organised units. Chase & Simon then showed that, briefly shown a real game position, masters recall the pieces far better than novices — but only for meaningful positions. For random arrangements the master's advantage vanishes. Conclusion: experts don't have bigger memories; they encode the board as a few familiar chunks (and templates), which only helps when the configuration is meaningful.

Gick & Holyoak — analogical transfer

What it claims: we can solve a new problem by mapping the solution from a structurally similar one (analogical transfer). The radiation–fortress study: participants who had read the fortress story (a general splits his army to converge on a fort from many roads) were far more likely to solve Duncker's radiation problem (use multiple weak rays converging on a tumour) — but usually only when hinted to use the story. The key finding: spontaneous transfer is poor because people are misled by surface similarity and miss the deeper structural analogy; an explicit cue, or seeing multiple analogues, helps them abstract the shared schema.

Functional fixedness vs mental set: functional fixedness (Duncker) = stuck on an object's usual use (the box is "just a box"). Mental set / Einstellung (Luchins) = stuck on a procedure that used to work (keep using the long water-jar formula). Object vs method.
Recap — problem solving. Gestalt insight/restructuring (Köhler's Sultan). Blocks to insight: Duncker functional fixedness (candle/box), Luchins mental set (water-jar). Newell & Simon: problem space + operators, means-ends analysis + subgoals, GPS, Tower of Hanoi. Well- vs ill-defined problems. Expertise = chunks not memory (de Groot; Chase & Simon — masters beat novices only on real, not random, boards). Gick & Holyoak: analogical transfer is poor without a hint because surface beats structure (radiation/fortress).

11. Judgement & decision making

Tversky & Kahneman showed that people don't compute probabilities normatively; they use fast heuristics that usually work but produce systematic biases. Then prospect theory and dual-process theory explain choice under risk and the two "minds" behind it.

The three heuristics (Tversky & Kahneman)

Availability: judge how likely/frequent something is by how easily examples come to mind. Because vivid, recent or dramatic events come to mind easily, people overestimate plane crashes, shark attacks and homicide relative to commoner but duller risks. Representativeness: judge probability by how much something resembles the stereotype/prototype of the category, ignoring base rates and sample size. Anchoring & adjustment: an initial value (the anchor) biases the final estimate because adjustment from it is insufficient — e.g. estimates of a product (8×7×…×1 vs 1×2×…×8) differed by starting point, and arbitrary numbers (a spun wheel) shifted estimates of unrelated quantities.

Base-rate neglect (the Tom W. / lawyer-engineer problem)

What it shows: when given a personality sketch that sounds like an engineer, people judge "Tom W. is an engineer" as likely regardless of the stated base rate (whether the sample was 70 engineers/30 lawyers or the reverse). The representativeness of the description swamps the prior probability — a failure to combine evidence with base rates (Bayesian reasoning).

Conjunction fallacy (the Linda problem)

What it shows: given a sketch of Linda as a socially-conscious philosophy graduate, people judge "Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement" as more probable than "Linda is a bank teller." That is logically impossible — a conjunction can never be more probable than one of its constituents. The cause is representativeness: the feminist-teller story resembles Linda better, and resemblance overrides probability.

Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky)

What it claims: people evaluate outcomes not as final wealth but as gains and losses relative to a reference point. The value function is S-shaped: concave for gains (risk-averse), convex for losses (risk-seeking to avoid a sure loss), and steeper for losses than gains — this is loss aversion ("losses loom larger than gains," roughly twice as much). Probabilities are also weighted non-linearly (small probabilities overweighted). This explains why the same objective outcome is chosen or rejected depending on framing.

Framing effect (the Asian disease problem)

What it shows: the identical choice flips with wording. When a disease programme is framed in terms of lives saved ("200 of 600 will be saved"), people choose the sure option (risk-averse for gains); when the same outcome is framed as deaths ("400 of 600 will die"), people choose the gamble (risk-seeking for losses). A direct demonstration of prospect theory's reference-dependence and loss aversion.

Simon — bounded rationality & satisficing

What it claims: real decision-makers have limited information, time and computational capacity, so they cannot optimise; they exercise bounded rationality and satisfice — search only until an option that is "good enough" (meets an aspiration level) is found, then stop, rather than evaluating every alternative for the optimum.

Dual-process theory (System 1 / System 2)

What it claims: two modes of thought. System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless, intuitive, parallel and emotional — the source of heuristics. System 2 is slow, controlled, effortful, analytic and rule-based, and can override System 1 when engaged. Many biases (Linda, base-rate neglect) arise because System 1's intuitive answer is accepted without System 2 checking it.

Availability vs representativeness: availability = judging by how easily examples come to mind (overrate plane crashes, recent news). Representativeness = judging by how much it resembles the stereotype (Linda, Tom W.). The conjunction fallacy (Linda) and base-rate neglect (Tom W.) both stem from representativeness, not availability.
Recap — judgement. Heuristics: availability (ease of recall), representativeness (resemblance → base-rate neglect Tom W. and conjunction fallacy Linda), anchoring & adjustment. Prospect theory: gains/losses from a reference point, S-shaped value function, loss aversion. Framing (Asian disease) flips choice between save/die wording. Simon: bounded rationality, satisficing (good enough). Dual-process: System 1 fast/intuitive vs System 2 slow/analytic.

12. Reasoning

Reasoning splits into deductive (drawing certain conclusions from premises) and inductive (generalising probably from cases). The recurring theme is that content and context, not pure logic, drive human reasoning — and that we are biased toward confirming and toward believable conclusions.

Deductive vs inductive

Deductive reasoning moves from general premises to a logically certain conclusion (syllogisms, conditionals); if the premises are true and the form valid, the conclusion must be true. Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to a probable generalisation; the conclusion can be well-supported but never guaranteed (the basis of science and everyday prediction).

Wason — the selection task

What it shows: given four cards showing, say, A, K, 4, 7 and the rule "if a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other," people must say which cards to turn to test the rule. The logically correct answer is the vowel (A) and the odd number (7) — i.e. P and not-Q (turn the case that could falsify the rule). Most people fail the abstract version, typically picking A and 4 (a matching/confirmation bias). Content effect: with a familiar, deontic rule — "if you drink alcohol you must be over 18," cards "beer, coke, 22, 16" — performance leaps, because we readily check the beer-drinker and the 16-year-old. Concrete, real-world (especially permission) content unlocks correct logic.

Wason — the 2-4-6 task & confirmation bias

What it shows: told that "2, 4, 6" fits a rule the experimenter has in mind, participants must discover the rule by proposing their own number-triples and getting yes/no feedback. The actual rule is broad ("any three ascending numbers"), but people fixate on a narrow hypothesis (e.g. "even numbers ascending by 2") and test only triples that confirm it (8-10-12) rather than ones that could falsify it. This confirmation bias — seeking supporting rather than disconfirming evidence — leaves them announcing the wrong rule with confidence.

Evans — belief bias

What it shows: in syllogistic reasoning, people judge an argument's validity by whether they believe the conclusion rather than whether it logically follows. They accept invalid syllogisms with believable conclusions ("Some addictive things are not cigarettes" type errors) and reject valid ones with unbelievable conclusions. Belief in the conclusion overrides logical structure — a key piece of evidence that everyday reasoning is not formal logic.

Johnson-Laird — mental models theory

What it claims: we reason not by applying formal logical rules but by constructing mental models of the situations the premises describe, then reading off conclusions and (ideally) searching for counterexample models. Key prediction: reasoning errors and difficulty increase with the number of models a problem requires — problems needing only one model are easy; those needing several are error-prone because people fail to construct all of them (and tend to consider just one). Explains why some valid inferences are far harder than others of the same logical "type."

Atmosphere effect; pragmatic reasoning schemas

Atmosphere effect (Woodworth & Sells): in syllogisms, the "mood" of the premises biases the conclusion accepted — if premises contain "some," people favour a "some" conclusion; if they contain a negative, they favour a negative conclusion — regardless of validity. Pragmatic reasoning schemas (Cheng & Holyoak): rather than pure logic or mere familiarity, we reason using abstract but content-bound schemas such as permission ("to do X you must satisfy Y") and obligation. This neatly explains the selection-task content effect: the drinking-age version triggers the permission schema and so yields correct "P and not-Q" checking even though it is not a familiar everyday memory.

Wason's two tasks: the selection task (4 cards, P/not-Q) tests conditional/deductive logic and is solved by familiar/permission content. The 2-4-6 task tests hypothesis testing and exposes confirmation bias. Both are Wason — don't conflate them.
Belief bias vs atmosphere effect: belief bias = judging by whether you believe the conclusion (Evans). Atmosphere effect = being swayed by the quantifiers/mood of the premises (some/all/no — Woodworth & Sells). Conclusion-believability vs premise-mood.
Recap — reasoning. Deductive (certain) vs inductive (probable). Wason selection task: P and not-Q is correct, abstract fails, familiar/permission content succeeds. Wason 2-4-6: confirmation bias (seek confirming, not falsifying, triples). Evans belief bias: validity judged by conclusion believability. Johnson-Laird mental models: difficulty ↑ with number of models. Atmosphere effect (premise mood) and Cheng & Holyoak pragmatic schemas (permission/obligation explain the content effect).
NIMHANS tests harder — embodied cognition. Beyond the classical "mind as computer" view, embodied (grounded) cognition holds that cognition is shaped by the body and its sensorimotor systems: abstract concepts are partly grounded in bodily/perceptual experience (understanding "grasp an idea" recruits motor representations), and thought is "for" action. Contrast it with the amodal, symbol-manipulation assumption of classical cognitivism. NIMHANS may pair this with the extended/situated-cognition idea that cognition uses the body and environment as part of the processing system.
NIMHANS tests harder — metacognition. Metacognition = "thinking about thinking": knowledge and regulation of one's own cognitive processes (Flavell). It splits into metacognitive knowledge (what you know about your memory/strategies), monitoring (judgements of learning, feeling-of-knowing, the tip-of-the-tongue state as a metamemory signal) and control (deciding how long to study, when to stop). Expect links to study strategies, to frontal-lobe function, and to clinical insight (e.g. impaired metacognition in some disorders).

★ Key-studies walkthrough

The handful of experiments that examiners describe and ask you to name. Know what was done and what was found.

Patient H.M. (Scoville & Milner)

Bilateral medial-temporal resection (incl. hippocampus) to treat epilepsy → dense anterograde amnesia, intact STM (normal digit span), intact procedural learning (mirror-drawing improves while he denies practising), older memories largely spared. Shows: STM ≠ LTM; hippocampus needed to form new declarative memories; declarative ≠ procedural.

Sperling — partial report

3×4 letter grid flashed 50 ms. Whole report ≈ 4–5 letters; a post-display tone cueing one row → near-perfect for that row, so ~all 9–12 were briefly available. Delaying the cue collapses performance by ~1 s. Shows: iconic memory is high-capacity but decays in ~½ s.

Dichotic listening (Cherry, Broadbent, Moray)

Different messages to each ear; shadow one. Listeners notice physical changes (a tone, a switch to a different voice) on the unattended ear but little of its content — yet they detect their own name (cocktail-party effect). Shows: selection is early but leaky → supports Treisman over strict Broadbent.

Posner — spatial cueing

A cue points to a location; target appears at the cued (valid) or another (invalid) spot. Valid cues speed RT, invalid cues slow it (a cost). Shows: attention can move covertly to a location, independent of the eyes (the spotlight).

Loftus & Palmer — car crash

Watch an accident film; the verb in "how fast when they ___?" (smashed/hit/contacted) raises or lowers the speed estimate; a week later "smashed" subjects falsely recall broken glass. Shows: misinformation/leading questions reshape memory — reconstructive, fallible eyewitness memory.

Wason — selection task

4 cards + a conditional rule; correct answer is P and not-Q (the falsifying cards). Abstract version mostly failed (people pick P and Q); a familiar deontic version (drinking-age) is solved easily. Shows: reasoning depends on content/context, not pure logic (→ pragmatic schemas).

Chase & Simon — chess expertise

Briefly shown a board, masters recall piece positions far better than novices — but only for real game positions, not random ones. Shows: expertise is organised knowledge (chunks), not superior raw memory.

Bartlett — War of the Ghosts

English participants repeatedly recall an unfamiliar Native American tale; recall shortens and distorts toward their own cultural schemas (unfamiliar elements dropped/rationalised). Shows: memory is reconstructive and schema-driven.
