1. Biopsychology as a neuroscience

Biopsychology is the scientific study of the biology of behaviour. That one-line definition hides the thing that makes the field distinctive, so it is worth unpacking slowly. Neuroscience as a whole is the study of the nervous system, and it is enormous: it includes people who study how a single ion channel folds, people who model networks mathematically, and people who develop drugs. Biopsychology is the branch of neuroscience that keeps behaviour at the centre of the question. A biopsychologist does not ask "what does this structure do" in the abstract; the question is always "what does this structure do to what the organism thinks, feels, or does". Pinel's phrase for the discipline's contribution is that biopsychologists bring to neuroscience a knowledge of behaviour and of the methods of behavioural research, which the other neurosciences do not supply on their own. You can know everything about a receptor and still have no idea what an animal will do, because behaviour has its own laws and its own measurement problems.

The name was not always this. Earlier labels included psychobiology, behavioural biology and behavioural neuroscience, and you will still meet all of them. Pinel prefers biopsychology precisely because the word order carries the argument: bio is the adjective and psychology is the noun, so the discipline is a kind of psychology that uses biology, not a kind of biology that happens to notice behaviour.

The founding text: Hebb, 1949

Biopsychology has a reasonably clean origin date. In 1949 Donald O. Hebb published The Organization of Behavior, and this is generally taken as the first comprehensive theory of how complex psychological phenomena (perception, emotion, thought, memory) could be produced by ordinary activity in the brain. Why that mattered is easy to miss now that it is obvious. Before Hebb, the widespread assumption among psychologists was that complex mental events were simply too complex to be traceable to neural activity, so the honest thing was to study behaviour and leave the brain alone. Hebb's move was to show that you could actually specify a mechanism.

His mechanism was the Hebb synapse, usually compressed into the slogan "cells that fire together wire together". Stated properly: if a presynaptic neuron repeatedly participates in firing a postsynaptic neuron, some growth process or metabolic change occurs at that synapse so that the presynaptic neuron's later ability to fire the postsynaptic one is increased. Chain those strengthened synapses together and you get a cell assembly, a set of neurons that has been wired into a functional unit by experience and can now be re-activated as a whole by any part of it. Chain assemblies in sequence and you get a phase sequence, which is Hebb's account of a train of thought. Notice the structure of the argument: a single local, plausible, physical rule, applied repeatedly, yields something that looks psychological. That is the template for essentially every biopsychological explanation that follows in this book.

Hebb built his theory from a genuinely mixed evidence base, and this is the second thing he established as normal practice: experiments on laboratory animals, clinical case studies of human patients, and logical argument all counted. He did not privilege one.

Human subjects and animal subjects

Biopsychology studies both humans and non-human animals, and each has real advantages, so the choice is a research-design decision rather than a matter of taste.

Human participants have three obvious advantages. They follow instructions, which means you can run a task instead of waiting for a behaviour to occur spontaneously. They report subjective experience, which is the only direct access anyone has to conscious content. And they are cheaper: no purpose-built housing, no colony maintenance. There is a fourth advantage that is easy to overlook: they have a human brain, and if the question is about a specifically human capacity such as syntax, only a human brain will do.

Animal subjects have a different and equally real set of advantages. Their nervous systems are simpler, so the effect you are looking for is not buried under the extra machinery. The comparative approach becomes available: studying the same system across species lets you separate the general biological principle from the local human quirk, and it is usually only across species that you can see what a structure is for. It is possible to run genuinely invasive experiments, meaning lesions, direct stimulation, single-cell recording, and selective breeding, which are the only methods that establish causation cleanly. And practically, you can control genetics, rearing, and history in ways that are simply not possible with people.

The six divisions of biopsychology

Pinel divides the field six ways. The division is by method and subject, not by topic, which is the single most common thing students get wrong here. Two people can both be studying memory and belong to different divisions.

PsychopharmacologyHow do drugs change behaviour and the brain?Manipulation by drug rather than by scalpel. Animals and humans. Strongly applied: it is where therapeutic drugs come from.

Trap

Three of these six names are routinely confused, and papers exploit it. Physiological psychology = invasive manipulation, animals, pure research. Psychophysiology = non-invasive measurement, healthy humans, no manipulation of the brain at all. Neuropsychology = pre-existing brain damage in human patients. The give-away words in a question stem are "lesioned rats" (physiological), "skin conductance in volunteers" (psychophysiology), and "patient with left temporal damage" (neuropsychology).

Converging operations: why one method is never enough

Every method in the list above is flawed, and each is flawed in its own particular direction. Lesion studies establish that a structure is necessary but tell you nothing about what it contributes when intact, and a lesion inevitably damages fibres that are merely passing through. Imaging shows correlated activity but cannot show that the activity is doing any work. Human case studies are rich but uncontrolled, since the damage was not assigned to the patient by a researcher. Animal experiments are controlled but raise the question of whether the finding generalises.

The field's response is the strategy Pinel calls converging operations: attack a single problem with several methods whose weaknesses do not overlap, and trust the conclusion only where they agree. The classic worked example is Korsakoff's syndrome. Human case studies of chronic alcoholics showed a devastating amnesia, which invited the conclusion that alcohol is the cause. But converging evidence changed the answer. Some patients with the syndrome had no history of alcohol at all, but did have severe malnutrition. Alcoholics eat badly and alcohol interferes with the absorption of thiamine (vitamin B1). Giving thiamine to Korsakoff patients halted progression, and thiamine-deprived animals developed comparable brain damage without alcohol. The conclusion that survives all the methods is that Korsakoff's syndrome is caused by thiamine deficiency, with alcohol as the usual route to that deficiency and an additional independent toxin in its own right. No single method could have got there.

Scientific inference: how you study something you cannot see

The brain's workings are not directly observable, so biopsychology relies on scientific inference: you measure something you can observe, and reason back to the invisible mechanism that must have produced it. This is not a weakness peculiar to the field. It is what physics does with subatomic particles. What matters is that the inference is disciplined.

The textbook illustration is the perception of movement during eye movement. Your eyes are constantly making rapid saccades, so the image of the world sweeps across your retina many times a second, yet the world does not appear to jump. Two competing inferences are available. Either the brain tracks the eye's position by monitoring feedback from the eye muscles, or it uses the motor command it has just issued to the eye muscles, that is, it knows where it told the eye to go. You cannot see which. But you can design an observation that separates them. If the eye is prevented from moving while the command to move is still issued, a feedback account predicts the world will look stationary (no movement occurred, no feedback), whereas a command-based account predicts the world should appear to jump (a command was issued, so a compensation is applied that nothing cancels). The world does appear to move. The inference is therefore that the visual system compensates using the outgoing motor signal, a copy of the command, rather than sensory feedback from the muscles. Note the form: an unobservable mechanism, two rival accounts, and one observable consequence on which they disagree.

Critical thinking about biopsychological claims

Pinel closes the chapter by teaching a habit rather than a fact, and he does it with two cautionary cases. The habit is to separate what was observed from what was concluded, because the gap between them is where bad science lives.

Case one: Delgado and the bull. José Delgado implanted an electrode in the caudate nucleus of a bull, entered the ring, and stopped a charging bull by radio stimulation. The claim built on it was that the caudate contains a mechanism that inhibits aggression, and by extension that human violence might be treated by stimulating it. What was actually observed is narrower: the stimulation stopped the charge. But the bull did not stop in a way that looked like a loss of aggressive motivation. It turned consistently in circles in one direction. A far more parsimonious reading is that the stimulation elicited a motor response, a forced turning, which happens to be incompatible with charging straight ahead. The observation is real; the conclusion about aggression was never licensed by it.

Case two: the prefrontal lobotomy. Egas Moniz introduced the prefrontal leucotomy as a psychiatric treatment and received a Nobel Prize in 1949; tens of thousands of operations followed, including Walter Freeman's transorbital version, which was fast enough to be performed outside an operating theatre. The evidential base was startlingly thin. It rested substantially on Carlyle Jacobsen's report that a chimpanzee named Becky, who had been extremely upset by errors on a delayed-response task, became placid after frontal-lobe lesions. One primate, one behaviour, in a laboratory task. Against that, another chimpanzee in the same work, Lucy, showed no comparable calming, and the follow-up evaluation of human patients was for years essentially anecdotal and conducted by the surgeons themselves. The lesson is not that psychosurgery is inconceivable; it is that the evidence, the sample, and the outcome measure must be examined before a mechanism is believed, and that the enthusiasm of the person making the claim is not evidence.

High-yield

Delgado's bull = caudate stimulation, the turning-response reinterpretation, the warning about over-reading a dramatic demonstration. Worked example

A researcher records skin conductance and heart rate in undergraduates while they view emotional photographs, and concludes that the amygdala generates the arousal. Which division is the study, and what is wrong with the conclusion? The methods are non-invasive surface measures in healthy humans, so the study is psychophysiology. The conclusion is not licensed: nothing in the study measured the amygdala, and no structure was manipulated. Skin conductance indexes sympathetic activation, which many structures could drive. To get to the amygdala you would need converging evidence, for example fMRI showing amygdala activation in the same task (cognitive neuroscience), plus patients with selective amygdala damage failing to show the response (neuropsychology), plus amygdala lesions abolishing the response in animals (physiological psychology). Once the three agree, the claim stands.

2. Evolution, genetics and experience

This chapter exists to dismantle a question rather than answer it. The question is "is this behaviour due to nature or to nurture", and Pinel's position is that it is not a hard question with a subtle answer, it is a malformed question that cannot have an answer. Understanding why it is malformed is the whole chapter.

The two flawed dichotomies

The first flawed dichotomy is physiological versus psychological, sometimes phrased as brain versus mind. This is Cartesian dualism, from René Descartes, who proposed a material body governed by physical law and a non-material mind, interacting at the pineal gland. It has an appealing tidiness and it is false in a way that matters practically: if some behaviour is caused by a non-physical mind, then that behaviour is permanently outside biological explanation, and the research programme stops. Biopsychology proceeds on monism: there is one kind of stuff, and thinking is something that brains do.

The second flawed dichotomy is nature versus nurture, the assumption that behaviour can be partitioned into an inherited portion and a learned portion. Two historical camps set this up. European ethologists such as Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen studied instinctive behaviours in the wild and emphasised inheritance; North American behaviourists such as John B. Watson and B. F. Skinner studied learning in the laboratory and emphasised experience. Both were describing real phenomena. Both were generalising from a method that could only see one of the two.

Pinel's replacement model has three terms rather than two. Behaviour is the product of the interaction between (1) the organism's genetic endowment, which is a product of its evolution, (2) its experience, and (3) its perception of the current situation. Genes and experience do not contribute separate portions of a behaviour; genes build a nervous system that develops in an environment, and the resulting brain then responds to a situation as it perceives it. Asking which of the two produced the behaviour is like asking whether the area of a rectangle is due to its length or its width.

The one-sentence version

Genes and experience are not two additive sources of behaviour; genes specify how development responds to experience, so every behaviour is 100% genetic and 100% environmental at the same time, and the only sensible questions are about how the interaction works.

Natural selection, and what it actually says

Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859, presenting three lines of evidence: the fossil record's documented change over time, the striking structural similarities among living species (a bat's wing, a whale's flipper and a human hand share a bone plan), and the effects of selective breeding by humans, which showed that heritable variation plus selection produces change within a few generations.

The mechanism, natural selection, needs only three ingredients: individuals vary, some of that variation is heritable, and variants differ in how many offspring they leave. Anything meeting those three conditions will evolve. The unit of accounting is fitness, which in evolutionary biology does not mean health or strength; it means the number of offspring an individual contributes to the next generation relative to others. A frail animal that reproduces more is fitter, in this technical sense, than a magnificent one that does not.

Two refinements matter for behaviour. First, sexual selection, also Darwin's: traits can evolve because they improve mating success even at a cost to survival, which is how you get peacock tails and the general pattern of intrasexual competition (males competing for access) and intersexual selection (choosiness, typically female). Second, evolution is opportunistic and does not design. It works on whatever is already there, so structures often get repurposed: a feature that arose for one function and was later co-opted for another is an exaptation, and a feature that is a structural by-product of something else and was never selected for at all is a spandrel. This is the standing corrective to lazy adaptive storytelling: not everything is for something.

Trap

Evolution is not a ladder and humans are not at its top. Living species are all contemporary end-points of separate branches, not rungs. We did not evolve from chimpanzees; chimpanzees and humans share a common ancestor. Evolution has no goal, "more complex" is not the direction of travel, and most species that ever existed left no descendants. Also: individual differences within a species are typically far larger than the average difference between related species.

The course of human evolution, and the brain

The lineage runs, in the order the textbook uses, from early multicellular water-dwellers to chordates (animals with a dorsal nerve cord), then vertebrates (chordates with a spinal bone casing), then the first vertebrates, the bony fishes, then amphibians, which added life on land while still returning to water to lay eggs, then reptiles, which solved reproduction on dry land with the shelled egg and dry skin, then mammals, which added internal gestation and milk, then primates, and within primates the apes and then the hominins. The genus Homo is recent, and anatomically modern Homo sapiens is very recent indeed on this timescale.

Three things happened to the brain along the way and they are separable. Overall size increased, but size on its own is a poor index because it scales with body size, which is why comparisons use the ratio of brain to body weight, and better still the size of the brain relative to what a body of that size would predict. Second, the increase was not uniform: it was concentrated in the cerebrum and specifically in the neocortex, the structures involved in complex adaptive processes, while the brain stem, which handles reflexes and basic regulation, changed comparatively little. Third, and this is the elegant engineering point, cortex increased in area far more than the skull increased in volume, and it accommodated this by convolution: folding into gyri and sulci. Folding lets you pack a large sheet into a small box, which is why cortical surface area, not brain mass, is the number that tracks capability.

Fundamental genetics, built up

Gregor Mendel got the foundations right in the 1860s by making three good methodological choices: he studied dichotomous traits (either/or, such as brown versus white seeds, with no intermediates), he began from true-breeding lines, and he counted offspring across generations instead of describing them. Crossing brown-seed with white-seed true-breeding lines gave an all-brown first generation, and crossing those offspring with each other gave brown and white in a 3:1 ratio. Whiteness had vanished for a generation and returned unchanged, so it could not have been diluted or blended. Mendel's inference was that inheritance is carried by discrete particles, now called genes, that each organism carries two of them for a trait, and that where the two differ one may be expressed (dominant) and the other not (recessive). Two copies of the same version make a homozygote, two different versions a heterozygote. What is expressed is the phenotype; what is carried is the genotype. The 3:1 ratio falls straight out: one homozygous dominant, two heterozygotes and one homozygous recessive, and only the last shows the recessive phenotype.

Genes sit on chromosomes, which come in matched pairs. Ordinary cell division, mitosis, copies every chromosome so each daughter cell gets the full set. The production of gametes uses meiosis, which halves the number, so that egg and sperm each contribute one chromosome of each pair and the offspring is a genuinely new combination. Meiosis also includes crossing over, in which paired chromosomes exchange segments before separating. Crossing over is the reason inheritance is not simply reshuffling of whole parental chromosomes, and it is why genes physically close together on a chromosome tend to be inherited together, a phenomenon called linkage. Sex is determined by one pair: XX develops as female, XY as male, and the traits carried on the X alone are sex-linked, which is why recessive X-linked conditions appear far more often in males, who have no second X to mask them.

Watson and Crick in 1953 identified the structure that makes all this physical: DNA as a double helix of two complementary strands, with adenine pairing to thymine and guanine to cytosine. Complementarity explains replication in one step: unzip the helix and each strand specifies its partner, so two identical copies result.

Expression runs in two steps and the vocabulary is worth getting exactly right. In transcription, the DNA sequence of a gene is copied into a strand of messenger RNA, which leaves the nucleus. In translation, a ribosome reads the mRNA in three-base units called codons, each codon specifying one amino acid, and assembles the amino acid chain that folds into a protein. Proteins are the point: they are the enzymes and channels and receptors and structural elements, so "a gene does something" always cashes out as "a gene specifies a protein". The human genome contains on the order of twenty thousand protein-coding genes, strikingly few, and most of the genome is not protein-coding but regulatory, which is the modern answer to how so few genes build something so complicated: the same parts, controlled differently.

Control is where epigenetics enters. Every cell has the same DNA but expresses a different subset of it, and expression is regulated by chemical marks that do not change the sequence: DNA methylation, which typically silences a gene, and histone modification, which changes how tightly the DNA is spooled and therefore how accessible it is. These marks respond to experience, and some are transmitted through cell division and in some cases across generations. Epigenetics is the concrete mechanism that makes the nature-nurture dichotomy untenable: experience physically regulates genes.

Behavioural genetics: the experiments that settle the interaction

Two studies carry this section, and both are designed so that the interaction is not merely asserted but measured.

Selective breeding, Tryon. Robert Tryon bred rats for maze performance, mating the best learners with the best learners and the worst with the worst over successive generations. The two lines separated cleanly, which established that maze-learning ability is heritable. That is a real and non-trivial result, and it is also exactly where naive interpretation goes wrong.

Cooper and Zubek (1958). This is the study to remember, because it takes Tryon's maze-bright and maze-dull lines and asks what environment does to them. Rats from both lines were reared in one of three conditions: an enriched environment (large, complex, full of objects), an impoverished environment (bare cage), or a normal laboratory cage. The finding is a textbook interaction. In the impoverished condition, both lines performed poorly and the difference between them shrank to nothing. In the enriched condition, both lines performed well and again barely differed. The genetic difference between "bright" and "dull" rats appeared in full only in the ordinary environment. The heritability of the trait was real, yet whether it made any difference at all depended entirely on the environment. There is no fixed quantity of genetic influence to be measured; the size of the genetic effect is itself a function of the environment.

The same logic gives the cleanest single illustration of gene-environment interaction in humans: phenylketonuria (PKU). PKU is caused by a single recessive gene that leaves the child unable to make the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine, so phenylalanine accumulates and, in the developing brain, causes severe intellectual disability. It is unambiguously a single-gene disorder with a known biochemistry. And its principal consequence is entirely preventable by an environmental manipulation: a low-phenylalanine diet begun early in life. The gene is not the destiny; the gene specifies a vulnerability to a particular dietary environment.

Trap

Heritability estimates are the most misread statistic in psychology. A heritability estimate is a proportion of the variation in a trait within a particular population in a particular set of environments that is attributable to genetic variation. It is not the proportion of the trait in an individual that is due to genes, that quantity is meaningless. It does not tell you the trait is unmodifiable (PKU is highly heritable and fully treatable). And it does not transfer across populations or environments: change the range of environments and the estimate changes, as Cooper and Zubek showed directly.

The human tools are twin and adoption studies. Monozygotic twins come from one zygote and are genetically near-identical; dizygotic twins come from two zygotes and share about half their genes, like ordinary siblings. Comparing the two, and comparing twins reared together against twins reared apart, is the standard way of estimating heritability in humans. The known confound is that monozygotic twins are also treated more alike, so the design overestimates genetic contribution to the extent that it does. Note too that a small amount of DNA, the mitochondrial DNA, is inherited from the mother only, since mitochondria come with the egg.

Worked example

A paper reports that the heritability of a cognitive test score is 0.70 in a sample of middle-class children, and concludes that educational intervention is largely futile. Take it apart. First, 0.70 refers to variance in that sample, not to any individual's score. Second, and decisively, that sample has a narrow range of environments: if nearly everyone is in a similar decent environment, environmental variance is small, so genetic variance must account for most of what is left, and heritability is pushed up. Broaden the sample to include deprived environments and the estimate will fall, because there is now real environmental variance to account for. This is Cooper and Zubek's point in human form: a high heritability inside a narrow environmental range says nothing about what happens when you change the environment. Third, heritability is silent about modifiability in any case. PKU is the counter-example that ends the argument.

3. Anatomy of the nervous system

Anatomy is the chapter students try to memorise and should instead try to organise. Almost everything here follows from two facts: the nervous system is built as a tube that swells at the front end, and it is described using a coordinate system borrowed from four-legged animals. Get those two and the names stop being arbitrary.

The coordinate system, and why it bends in humans

Directions are given in three axes. Anterior means toward the nose and posterior toward the tail, so these are also called rostral and caudal. Dorsal means toward the back surface and ventral toward the belly. Medial means toward the midline and lateral away from it. The catch is that these terms were fixed for an animal whose spinal cord and brain lie along one straight horizontal line. Humans stand upright, so the axis bends about ninety degrees at the top of the brain stem. The practical consequence: in the human brain, dorsal means superior (toward the top of the head) and ventral means inferior, whereas in the human spinal cord dorsal still means toward the back. This single bend is responsible for a large share of confusion, so read the term as belonging to a structure rather than to the body.

Three cutting planes: the sagittal plane runs front to back down the vertical midline (the mid-sagittal section splits left from right), the horizontal plane cuts top from bottom, and the coronal or frontal plane cuts front from back, like a slice across the face. Finally ipsilateral means on the same side of the body and contralateral on the opposite side, which is the phrase you will need constantly once movement and sensation are crossed.

The major divisions

The whole nervous system splits into the central nervous system (CNS), meaning brain and spinal cord, encased in bone, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), everything else. The PNS then splits functionally.

The somatic nervous system (SNS) deals with the external environment and the skeletal muscles. It has afferent nerves carrying sensory signals inward from skin, muscles, joints and the special senses to the CNS, and efferent nerves carrying motor commands outward to the skeletal muscles. Afferent means arriving, efferent means exiting, and the mnemonic worth keeping is that afferent arrives and efferent exits.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) deals with the internal environment: smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands. Its afferents carry interoceptive signals inward. Its efferents divide into two antagonistic branches. The sympathetic branch mobilises: heart rate and blood pressure up, bronchi dilated, blood shunted to skeletal muscle, pupils dilated, digestion suspended, adrenal medulla triggered. This is the fight-or-flight arm, and its fibres leave the CNS from the thoracic and lumbar segments of the spinal cord. The parasympathetic branch conserves and restores: heart rate down, digestion and salivation up, pupils constricted, the rest-and-digest arm, with fibres leaving from the brain and the sacral region (the craniosacral pattern).

There is a second, more mechanical difference between the two branches that examiners like. Autonomic efferent pathways are two-stage: a preganglionic neuron runs from the CNS to a ganglion, and a postganglionic neuron runs from the ganglion to the target organ. In the sympathetic branch the ganglia sit close to the spinal cord, so preganglionic fibres are short and postganglionic fibres long, which suits a system designed to activate the whole body at once in a coordinated burst. In the parasympathetic branch the ganglia sit on or near the target organ, so preganglionic fibres are long and postganglionic ones very short, which suits organ-by-organ regulation. The transmitters follow suit: all preganglionic neurons in both branches release acetylcholine; parasympathetic postganglionic neurons also release acetylcholine (hence cholinergic), whereas sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine (hence adrenergic).

High-yield

Sympathetic = thoracolumbar origin, short preganglionic, long postganglionic, postganglionic transmitter norepinephrine, mass mobilisation. Parasympathetic = craniosacral origin, long preganglionic, short postganglionic, acetylcholine throughout, organ-specific. All preganglionic fibres in both branches are cholinergic.

Twelve pairs of cranial nerves project from the brain, mostly to the head. Some are purely sensory (I olfactory, II optic, VIII vestibulocochlear), some purely motor (III oculomotor, IV trochlear, VI abducens, XI accessory, XII hypoglossal), and some mixed (V trigeminal, VII facial, IX glossopharyngeal, X vagus). The one to know beyond its name is the vagus (X), whose name means wandering: it leaves the head entirely and supplies the heart, lungs and gut, and it carries the bulk of parasympathetic outflow. Because cranial nerves enter the brain at known levels, the pattern of cranial-nerve deficits localises brain-stem damage precisely, which is why the neurological examination tests them one by one.

Protection: meninges, cerebrospinal fluid, and the blood-brain barrier

Three membranes, the meninges, wrap the CNS. Outermost is the tough dura mater. Beneath it is the web-like arachnoid membrane. Innermost, adhering to the brain's surface and following every fold, is the delicate pia mater. Between arachnoid and pia lies the subarachnoid space, which contains large blood vessels and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

CSF fills the subarachnoid space and the internal chambers: the two lateral ventricles, the third ventricle, the cerebral aqueduct connecting third to fourth, and the fourth ventricle. It is continuously produced by the choroid plexus, networks of capillaries protruding into the ventricles, and continuously absorbed into the large veins. Its jobs are mechanical support (the brain effectively floats, which dramatically reduces its effective weight and cushions impact) and clearance of waste. Because production and absorption must balance, a blockage is dangerous: if the flow is obstructed, typically at the narrow cerebral aqueduct, pressure builds and the ventricles expand, which is hydrocephalus, treated by draining the fluid with a shunt.

The blood-brain barrier is not a membrane but a property of the cerebral blood vessels: their endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions, so molecules cannot slip between them and must pass through. Small uncharged molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross freely, fat-soluble molecules cross readily (which is why psychoactive drugs are typically lipid-soluble), and everything else needs an active transporter, as glucose and amino acids have. The barrier protects the brain from toxins and from fluctuations in blood chemistry, and it is the central practical obstacle in neuropharmacology, since a drug that works beautifully in a dish is useless if it cannot get in. The classic solution is to give a precursor that can cross: dopamine cannot cross the barrier, but L-DOPA can, and is converted to dopamine inside, which is the basis of Parkinson's treatment. Note also that the barrier is deliberately weak in a few places, the area postrema among them, so that the brain can detect circulating toxins and trigger vomiting.

The cells: neurons and glia

A neuron is a cell specialised for receiving, integrating and transmitting electrochemical signals. Its parts, in signal order: dendrites, branching processes that receive input and are studded with dendritic spines where synapses land; the cell body or soma, containing the nucleus and the metabolic machinery, where inputs are summed; the axon hillock, the cone-shaped junction where the axon leaves the soma and where the decision to fire is taken; the axon, the long conducting process; and the terminal buttons at its branch tips, which release neurotransmitter into the synapse. Inside, the usual organelles plus two structural elements that matter: microtubules, which are the tracks along which materials are transported up and down the axon, and the mitochondria that supply the very large amounts of energy the neuron needs.

Neurons are classified by shape. Multipolar neurons have many dendrites and one axon and are the commonest type in the CNS. Bipolar neurons have one dendrite and one axon and are typical of sensory systems, for example in the retina. Unipolar neurons have a single process leaving the soma that then divides, and are typical of somatosensory afferents. An interneuron is defined by function, not shape: it has a short axon or none, and integrates activity within a single structure rather than transmitting between structures.

Glia outnumber neurons and are no longer thought of as passive packing. Four kinds. Oligodendrocytes myelinate axons in the CNS, each one wrapping segments of several axons. Schwann cells myelinate axons in the PNS, one cell per segment of one axon, and, crucially, they guide axonal regrowth after injury, which is a large part of why peripheral nerves regenerate and central ones largely do not. Astrocytes are the largest and most numerous: they hold the tissue together structurally, contact both neurons and blood vessels and so participate in the blood-brain barrier and in delivering nutrients, take up and buffer extracellular potassium and transmitters (an active role in signalling, not merely housekeeping), and can trigger blood-flow changes. Microglia are the immune cells of the CNS: small, mobile, they proliferate in response to injury and infection and phagocytose debris, and they also prune synapses during development.

Myelin is worth pausing on because it is structural and functional at once. It is a fatty insulating sheath laid in segments, with small uninsulated gaps between segments called nodes of Ranvier. Insulation prevents current leaking out along the axon, and the exposed nodes are where the signal is regenerated, which produces the fast, jumping conduction described in the next chapter.

Seeing the structures: neuroanatomical techniques

Neural tissue is uniformly translucent, so anatomy depends entirely on stains that make some things visible and everything else invisible. The Golgi stain, silver-based, is capricious in a useful way: it fills a small random subset of neurons completely and blackly, leaving the rest unstained, so you can see the entire shape of individual cells including their processes. The Nissl stain, such as cresyl violet, binds material in cell bodies only, so it stains no processes but does let you count cell bodies and see how their density varies, which is how cortical layers and nuclear boundaries were mapped. Electron microscopy resolves fine structure, including the synapse itself, at the cost of only seeing a tiny field. And tract tracing establishes connections rather than shapes: anterograde tracers are taken up by cell bodies and carried forward to the terminals, revealing where a structure projects to; retrograde tracers are taken up by terminals and carried back to the cell bodies, revealing where a structure receives from.

The spinal cord

In cross-section the cord shows an inner butterfly of grey matter (cell bodies) surrounded by white matter (myelinated axons, white because myelin is fatty). The grey matter's dorsal arms are the dorsal horns and its ventral arms the ventral horns. Sensory afferents enter by the dorsal roots, and their cell bodies lie outside the cord in the dorsal root ganglia. Motor efferents leave by the ventral roots, and their cell bodies lie inside, in the ventral horns. This dorsal-in, ventral-out arrangement is the anatomical fact behind the spinal reflex and behind the localisation of spinal injuries.

The five divisions of the brain

The brain develops from a tube with three swellings, and the adult divisions are just those swellings subdivided. Learning them in developmental order makes the list stop being arbitrary. From the tail forward:

1. Myelencephalon (medulla). The most posterior division, essentially a continuation of the cord, and largely composed of tracts passing through. It contains the nuclei that regulate cardiovascular function, respiration and vomiting, which is why medullary damage is rapidly fatal. Running through it and up into the higher brain stem is the reticular formation, a complex net of about a hundred small nuclei; it is involved in arousal and sleep, attention, movement, and the cardiac and respiratory reflexes. Because it does many separable things, the old label "reticular activating system" is an oversimplification.

2. Metencephalon. Contains the pons ventrally, a bulge of ascending and descending tracts plus part of the reticular formation, and the cerebellum dorsally. The cerebellum is a convoluted structure holding a large proportion of all the brain's neurons; damage to it does not abolish movement but destroys its precision and timing, impairs balance, and disrupts adaptation of movement to changing conditions, along with certain kinds of learning. Note the pattern: it is a coordinator, not an initiator.

3. Mesencephalon (midbrain). Two divisions. The tectum (roof) is the dorsal surface and consists of the superior colliculi, which have a visual function, and the inferior colliculi, which have an auditory one; together they are the colliculi, and in mammals they serve orienting responses to visual and auditory stimuli. The tegmentum (floor) lies ventral to it and contains three structures worth naming: the periaqueductal grey, which surrounds the cerebral aqueduct and mediates the analgesic effects of opioids, the substantia nigra, and the red nucleus, both components of the sensorimotor system. The substantia nigra is the origin of the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway whose degeneration causes Parkinson's disease.

4. Diencephalon. Two structures. The thalamus is the large two-lobed relay sitting at the top of the brain stem, joined across the midline in many brains by the massa intermedia. It is a collection of distinct nuclei, and the important principle is that most sensory information reaching cortex is relayed through a specific thalamic sensory relay nucleus: the lateral geniculate nuclei for vision, the medial geniculate nuclei for audition, and the ventral posterior nuclei for touch. Relay is not passive; these nuclei receive as much input back from cortex as they send to it. The hypothalamus sits beneath the thalamus and is small but disproportionately important: it regulates the motivated behaviours (eating, drinking, sleeping, temperature, sexual behaviour) and it controls the pituitary gland, which hangs from it, thereby controlling the endocrine system. Landmarks on its underside include the optic chiasm, where the optic nerves cross (specifically, the fibres from the medial retina cross, which is what makes the crossing partial, or decussating), and the mammillary bodies at its posterior, which are among the structures damaged in Korsakoff's syndrome.

5. Telencephalon. The largest division, and the one that expanded most in evolution. It comprises the cerebral cortex, the limbic system and the basal ganglia, and it initiates voluntary movement, interprets sensory input, and supports learning, memory, speaking and problem solving.

Cortex, limbic system and basal ganglia

The cerebral cortex is a thin sheet of grey matter, only a few millimetres deep, whose area is greatly expanded by folding. Large folds are fissures, small ones sulci, and the ridges between them are gyri. The landmarks to know: the longitudinal fissure separates the two hemispheres, which are connected by cerebral commissures, the largest being the corpus callosum; the central fissure and the lateral fissure divide each hemisphere into its four lobes, frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital. Four gyri are worth naming because they are functionally defined: the precentral gyrus just anterior to the central fissure is primary motor cortex, the postcentral gyrus just posterior to it is primary somatosensory cortex, the superior temporal gyrus contains auditory cortex, and the cingulate gyrus arcs above the corpus callosum on the medial surface.

Most cortex is neocortex, six-layered, and its two principal cell types are pyramidal cells, large multipolar neurons with a triangular cell body, a long apical dendrite, and an axon that leaves the cortex (these are the output cells), and stellate cells, small star-shaped interneurons with short axons that stay local. The layering has a logic: layer IV is where sensory input from the thalamus arrives and is thickest in sensory cortex, while layer V contains the largest pyramidal output cells and is thickest in motor cortex. Alongside the layers runs a columnar organisation, cells in a vertical column tending to respond to the same thing. A minority of cortex is older and simpler: the hippocampus, on the medial temporal lobe, has three major layers rather than six.

The limbic system is a circuit around the margin (the limbus) of the cerebral hemispheres, classically credited to James Papez, and traditionally associated with the four Fs of motivated behaviour and with emotion and memory. Trace the circuit as a ring: amygdala (anterior temporal lobe, in front of the hippocampus), then hippocampus, then the fornix, the arching tract that leaves the hippocampus and runs to the mammillary bodies and septum, then the cingulate cortex, and back round. The functional split now recognised is that the amygdala is the emotional structure, especially for fear, while the hippocampus is the memory structure.

The basal ganglia are a set of subcortical nuclei for movement, and their naming is the part that trips people. The caudate is a long tail-like structure; the putamen lies beside it; together caudate plus putamen are the striatum, so called because the fibre bundles crossing between them give a striped appearance. Medial to the putamen is the globus pallidus, the pale globe. The nucleus accumbens, in the ventral striatum, is the structure central to reward and addiction (Chapter 15). And the pathway to remember is the nigrostriatal pathway, dopaminergic fibres running from the substantia nigra of the midbrain to the striatum, whose degeneration produces Parkinson's disease. The amygdala is usually listed with the basal ganglia anatomically and with the limbic system functionally, which is why you meet it in both lists.

Trap

Striatum = caudate + putamen, not including globus pallidus. Tectum is the midbrain roof (colliculi, dorsal), tegmentum the midbrain floor (periaqueductal grey, substantia nigra, red nucleus, ventral). Superior colliculi are visual, inferior colliculi auditory. Lateral geniculate = vision, medial geniculate = audition, ventral posterior = somatosensation. Oligodendrocytes myelinate in the CNS, Schwann cells in the PNS.

Worked example

A patient has a resting tremor, rigidity and difficulty initiating movement, but normal sensation and normal memory. Reason from the anatomy. Initiation of movement plus tremor plus rigidity, with sensation intact, points to the motor loop rather than to sensory cortex or cerebellum (cerebellar damage gives ataxia and intention tremor during movement, not a tremor at rest). The basal ganglia are the movement-initiation structures, and the input that drives the striatum is dopaminergic and comes from the substantia nigra of the midbrain tegmentum. So the lesion is degeneration of the nigrostriatal pathway, which is Parkinson's disease. Now predict the treatment from Chapter 3's other section: dopamine itself cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, so you give its precursor L-DOPA, which has a transporter, and let the brain convert it.

4. Neural conduction and synaptic transmission

This is the load-bearing chapter of the book. Everything later, drugs, memory, sleep, psychosis, is ultimately a statement about what happens at a membrane or at a synapse. So it is worth going slowly and building it in the order the signal actually travels: the resting state, the small local signals that arrive on the dendrites, the decision taken at the axon hillock, the action potential, its conduction down the axon, and finally the synapse where it is handed to the next cell.

The resting potential, and the four forces that produce it

Push a fine glass microelectrode filled with salt solution into a neuron, put a reference electrode outside, and you measure a steady voltage difference of about −70 millivolts, inside negative relative to outside. That is the resting membrane potential, and the cell is said to be polarised.

Why does it exist? Because four things are true at once, and the resting potential is the equilibrium they settle into.

First, the ions are unevenly distributed. Sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−) are more concentrated outside the cell; potassium (K+) is more concentrated inside; and there are large negatively charged proteins inside that cannot leave at all.

Second, random thermal motion pushes every ion species down its own concentration gradient, so Na+ tends to enter and K+ tends to leave.

Third, electrostatic pressure pushes ions according to charge: because the inside is negative, positive ions are drawn in and negative ions pushed out. Notice that for K+ the two forces oppose each other (concentration pushes it out, charge pulls it in), while for Na+ they point the same way (both push it in). This is why Na+ is the ion poised to do something dramatic: it is under enormous combined pressure to enter, and the only thing stopping it is the membrane.

Fourth, the membrane is differentially permeable. At rest, K+ and Cl− pass through their channels fairly easily; Na+ passes only with difficulty; and the internal proteins do not pass at all.

Those four would still slowly run down, because a trickle of Na+ does leak in and K+ does leak out. The gradient is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, an active transporter that spends metabolic energy to exchange three Na+ out for every two K+ in. Two implications. Because the exchange is unequal, the pump is itself mildly electrogenic, contributing directly to the negativity. And because it costs ATP continuously, a large fraction of the brain's energy budget goes on simply staying ready to fire, which is why the brain is so intolerant of interrupted glucose or oxygen.

Vocabulary that must be exact

Depolarisation = the membrane potential moves toward zero (less negative, for example −70 to −60 mV), which makes firing more likely. Hyperpolarisation = the potential becomes more negative (−70 to −80 mV), making firing less likely. Do not read "hyper" as "more active"; it means more polarised, therefore more inhibited.

Postsynaptic potentials and integration

When transmitter binds at a synapse on a dendrite or soma, the local membrane potential changes. If the change is depolarising, it is an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP); if hyperpolarising, an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP). Three properties of these signals matter, and they are exactly the properties the action potential will not have.

They are graded: their size is proportional to the strength of the signal that produced them, so they carry an analogue quantity. They are fast, transmitted almost instantaneously. And they are decremental: they get smaller as they travel, dying out within a couple of millimetres. Conduction of this kind is passive, meaning it involves no additional energy input, exactly like the spread of current down a leaky wire.

Because they are graded and decremental, the many thousands of signals arriving on one neuron at any moment do not each get a vote. They mix. The mixing is called integration, and it happens in two ways. Spatial summation combines signals arriving at different locations at the same time: two simultaneous EPSPs at different synapses add into a larger depolarisation, and an EPSP and an IPSP of similar size cancel. Temporal summation combines signals arriving at the same location at different times: if a second EPSP arrives before the first has decayed, they add. Temporal summation is why rate of presynaptic firing is a meaningful signal at all.

The place where the summed total is read is the axon hillock, or more precisely the adjacent initial segment of the axon, and the reason it is read there is that the density of voltage-gated sodium channels is highest at that spot. If the summed potential at the hillock reaches the threshold of excitation, roughly −65 mV, an action potential is fired.

The action potential, step by step

Unlike the postsynaptic potentials, the action potential (AP) is all-or-none: it either happens at full size or does not happen at all, and a stronger stimulus does not produce a larger AP. It is a massive, brief reversal, from about −70 mV to about +50 mV and back, lasting roughly a millisecond.

The mechanism rests on voltage-activated ion channels: channels whose opening is controlled by the membrane potential itself. That single fact makes the event self-propelling.

  1. Depolarisation reaches threshold at the hillock, and voltage-activated sodium channels open.
  2. Na+ rushes in, driven by both its concentration gradient and electrostatic attraction (the two forces that were pointing the same way at rest). The inside becomes rapidly less negative, then positive. Here is the crucial bit: the entry of Na+ depolarises the membrane further, which opens still more sodium channels. This is positive feedback, and it is why the rising phase is explosive and why the AP is all-or-none. Once it starts, it cannot be half-done.
  3. At the peak, the sodium channels inactivate: they close and, importantly, cannot be reopened until the membrane has returned to its resting state.
  4. Slower voltage-activated potassium channels, which opened when threshold was reached, are now fully open, and K+ flows out, driven by concentration and by the now-positive interior. The efflux of positive charge repolarises the cell.
  5. The potassium channels close sluggishly, so slightly too much K+ leaves and the membrane briefly overshoots into hyperpolarisation. Then the resting distribution is restored.

Note what does not do the work: the sodium-potassium pump plays no part in the action potential itself. Very few ions actually cross during a single AP, far too few to disturb the concentration gradients. The pump's job is long-term maintenance, not moment-to-moment signalling.

Two refractory periods follow, and each explains something important. During the absolute refractory period, roughly 1 to 2 ms, another AP simply cannot be triggered, because the sodium channels are inactivated. During the following relative refractory period, an AP can be triggered but only by a stronger-than-usual stimulus. Together these produce three consequences worth stating explicitly. They set a maximum firing rate, in the region of a thousand per second. They are the reason APs travel in one direction only: the stretch of membrane just behind the advancing spike is refractory, so the signal cannot reverse. And because the AP is all-or-none, the refractory periods are what allow intensity to be coded at all: a strong stimulus cannot make a bigger spike, so it makes more spikes per second. Rate coding is a direct consequence of the all-or-none law.

High-yield

Resting potential −70 mV; threshold about −65 mV; peak about +50 mV. Rising phase = Na+ in; falling phase = K+ out; hyperpolarising overshoot = slow K+ channel closing. Postsynaptic potentials are graded, decremental and passive; action potentials are all-or-none, non-decremental and active. Absolute refractory period = sodium channel inactivation, and it gives unidirectional conduction plus a ceiling firing rate. Intensity is coded by rate, not amplitude.

Conduction along the axon, and why myelin matters

Conduction down the axon is active and therefore non-decremental: at each patch of membrane the AP is regenerated at full size, so the signal arrives at the terminal exactly as large as it started. The comparison worth holding is that a postsynaptic potential fades like a whisper down a corridor, whereas an AP is more like a line of gunpowder, re-igniting at full intensity all the way along.

In an unmyelinated axon this regeneration happens at every point, which is reliable but slow. Myelinated axons do something cleverer. The myelin insulates the membrane so current cannot leak out, and the AP therefore spreads passively and very fast along the insulated segment, weakening slightly as it goes but arriving at the next node of Ranvier still above threshold, where it is regenerated at full strength. The spike appears to jump from node to node, which is why this is called saltatory conduction (from saltare, to leap). It is both faster and much more metabolically economical, because ions cross only at the nodes. Conduction speed also rises with axon diameter, so the fastest fibres in the body are thick and heavily myelinated. This is also why demyelinating disease such as multiple sclerosis is so disabling: the axon is intact, but current now leaks across the bared internodal membrane, and the signal may fail to reach the next node above threshold at all.

Two more terms. Conduction in the natural direction, from hillock to terminals, is orthodromic; stimulate an axon in the middle and the spike will also travel backward toward the soma, which is antidromic. And the classic experimental preparation behind all of this is the squid giant axon, used by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley, whose quantitative model of the sodium and potassium conductances earned the 1963 Nobel Prize. The axon was chosen because it is enormous, which made it possible to get electrodes inside it in the 1950s.

The synapse: structure

Classify synapses by what contacts what. Axodendritic synapses, terminal button onto dendrite (often onto a dendritic spine), and axosomatic synapses, onto the soma, are the two commonest. Axoaxonic synapses, onto another neuron's terminal, are functionally special: they do not excite or inhibit the target cell directly, they modulate how much transmitter that target's terminal releases, which is presynaptic facilitation or inhibition. Dendrodendritic synapses exist and are often capable of transmission in both directions.

Not all transmission is at a tidy point-to-point junction. Directed synapses have the release site and the receptor site close together, the arrangement you picture by default. Non-directed synapses release transmitter into the general extracellular space to act on whatever receptors it reaches, and the anatomical signature is the string-of-beads axon, whose swellings, called varicosities, release along its length rather than only at its tip. Many monoamine neurons work this way, which is why a single monoamine cell can influence a very wide territory diffusely. Finally, a minority of junctions are gap junctions, or electrical synapses, in which the membranes are bridged by pores so that ions pass directly; these are far faster than chemical synapses and, unlike them, are typically bidirectional.

The synapse: the transmission cycle

Follow one cycle from synthesis to clearance.

Synthesis and storage. The two great classes of transmitter are handled differently. Small-molecule transmitters are synthesised in the terminal button itself, by enzymes present there, and packaged into synaptic vesicles by the Golgi complex. Neuropeptides, being chains of amino acids, must be assembled on ribosomes in the cell body and then transported down the axon in larger vesicles, which is slower and less renewable.

Release. The arriving action potential opens voltage-activated calcium channels in the terminal. Ca2+ entry is the actual trigger: it causes vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and empty their contents into the synaptic cleft, a process called exocytosis. Small-molecule transmitters sit in vesicles clustered right at the membrane and are released in bursts by each AP; peptides are typically released more diffusely and in response to sustained firing.

Receptor action. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to receptors, and a transmitter's effect is determined not by the transmitter but by which receptor subtype it lands on. This is the single most useful idea in the chapter, because it explains how one substance can excite in one place and inhibit in another. Two receptor families:

  • Ionotropic receptors are themselves ion channels. Binding opens the channel immediately and the ion flows. They are fast, short-lived, and produce the classic EPSP or IPSP. Whether the result is excitation or inhibition depends on the ion: open a Na+ channel and you depolarise (EPSP); open a Cl− or K+ channel and you hyperpolarise (IPSP).
  • Metabotropic receptors are not channels. Binding activates an attached G protein, part of which detaches inside the cell and either acts on a nearby channel or, more often, triggers the synthesis of a second messenger that diffuses through the cytoplasm. Effects are slower to start, longer-lasting, more diffuse and more varied, and because second messengers can enter the nucleus and change gene expression, metabotropic signalling is the route by which a synaptic event produces a durable structural change. Hold on to that: it is the bridge to learning in Chapter 11.

A special case: autoreceptors are metabotropic receptors on the presynaptic membrane that bind the neuron's own transmitter. They function as a thermostat, monitoring how much has been released and reducing further synthesis and release. This negative feedback loop is why some drugs have paradoxical initial effects.

Deactivation. Transmitter must be cleared or the signal never ends. Two routes. Reuptake is the commoner: transporter proteins in the presynaptic membrane draw the intact transmitter back for reuse, which is efficient. Enzymatic degradation breaks it down in the cleft, the standard example being acetylcholinesterase destroying acetylcholine. Astrocytes also take up transmitter, notably glutamate.

The transmitters themselves

Learn them in classes, because the drugs follow the classes.

Amino acid transmitters do the fast, bulk signalling of the CNS: glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter of the brain, GABA the main inhibitory one, with aspartate and glycine (inhibitory, mainly in the spinal cord and brain stem) alongside them. GABA is synthesised from glutamate in one step, which is a pleasing detail: the brain's brake is made from its accelerator.

Monoamines are the diffuse modulators, made by small numbers of brain-stem neurons with vast projections. They split into the catecholamines, which share a synthesis chain, and the indolamines. The chain is worth memorising in order because it is examined and because it explains three drugs: tyrosine (an amino acid from the diet) becomes L-DOPA, which becomes dopamine, which becomes norepinephrine, which becomes epinephrine. A neuron is dopaminergic or noradrenergic simply according to which enzymes it possesses, that is, where it stops. The indolamine is serotonin (5-HT), synthesised from the amino acid tryptophan.

Acetylcholine stands on its own. It is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction, at all autonomic preganglionic synapses, at parasympathetic postganglionic synapses, and in cortical projections important for arousal and memory. Its two receptor subtypes are named after the drugs that revealed them: nicotinic receptors (ionotropic, at the neuromuscular junction and autonomic ganglia) and muscarinic receptors (metabotropic, in the CNS and at parasympathetic targets).

Unconventional transmitters break the rules deliberately. Soluble gases such as nitric oxide are not stored in vesicles at all: they are synthesised on demand and diffuse straight through membranes, act, and are gone in seconds. Endocannabinoids, the class the active ingredient of cannabis mimics, are synthesised from fats in the postsynaptic membrane and travel backwards to act on the presynaptic terminal, which is retrograde transmission: the receiving cell talking back to the sending one to tell it to release less.

Neuropeptides are the large, slow, modulatory class, of which the best known are the endorphins (endogenous morphine-like peptides) acting at opioid receptors, which is how the brain has its own analgesia system.

A drug that increases transmission at a synapse is an agonist; one that decreases it is an antagonist. The value of the framework is that there are only a handful of places in the cycle to interfere, so any drug's mechanism can be located.

Worked example

Why does a single amphetamine dose feel stimulating while chronic use leaves the person flat? Trace the cycle. Amphetamine causes stored monoamines to leak from vesicles into the terminal and then reverses the transporters, so transmitter is pumped out into the cleft, and it also blocks reuptake. Acutely, that is a large agonist effect at dopamine and norepinephrine synapses. But three feedback processes now engage. Autoreceptors detect the flood and cut synthesis and release. Postsynaptic receptors downregulate, decreasing in number and sensitivity. And the vesicular stores are being depleted faster than they are refilled, since synthesis is enzyme-limited. So on the drug you get progressively less effect for the same dose (tolerance), and off it you have fewer receptors, depleted stores and suppressed synthesis, which is a functionally hypodopaminergic state: the crash. Every element of that explanation is a point on the transmission cycle. Chapter 15 turns this into the full account of addiction.

5. The research methods of biopsychology

Methods chapters get skipped and then cost marks, because most exam questions about the brain are secretly questions about how somebody could possibly know. Organise the chapter by what a method lets you conclude, which sorts it into four groups: seeing structure, measuring activity, manipulating the brain, and measuring behaviour. Only the third group licenses causal claims, and that fact is the spine of the chapter.

Visualising the living human brain

Plain X-rays are useless for the brain because brain tissue and the fluid around it absorb X-rays almost identically. Every technique below is a way around that problem.

Contrast X-ray procedures inject a substance that absorbs X-rays differently from the surrounding tissue. In cerebral angiography a radio-opaque dye is injected into an artery, making the cerebral vasculature visible; it is used to localise vascular damage and also tumours, which distort vessels.

Computed tomography (CT) takes X-ray images through the head from many angles in one horizontal plane, then reconstructs a slice computationally, and stacks slices into a three-dimensional volume. It is fast, widely available, and good for gross lesions, bleeds and skull fractures.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) uses no X-rays. Placed in a strong magnetic field, hydrogen nuclei in the tissue align; a radio pulse knocks them out of alignment and the signal they emit as they relax back is measured. Because the signal depends on the local chemical environment, MRI resolves soft tissue with much higher spatial resolution and contrast than CT, and it can produce images in any plane. Its variant diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) measures the direction in which water diffuses, which in white matter is along the fibres, and therefore maps tracts: it is the only non-invasive way to visualise the brain's wiring in a living person.

Everything so far shows structure only. The next two show function.

Positron emission tomography (PET) injects a radioactive labelled substance, classically 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), and images where it accumulates. 2-DG is taken up by active neurons as though it were glucose but cannot be metabolised, so it accumulates in proportion to activity: the resulting image is a map of glucose uptake, that is, of activity, not of structure. PET can also be used with labelled ligands to map receptor distributions, which is its enduring advantage. Its costs are the injection of radioactivity and poor temporal and spatial resolution.

Functional MRI (fMRI) exploits the fact that oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin have different magnetic properties, so the scanner can detect increases in oxygenated blood flowing to active regions, the BOLD signal (blood-oxygen-level-dependent). fMRI has largely displaced PET because it requires no injection, provides both structural and functional images from the same session, has better spatial resolution, and can be analysed three-dimensionally. Its limitation is temporal: the haemodynamic response is sluggish, unfolding over seconds, whereas the neural events it reflects take milliseconds. And a conceptual caution that examiners like: fMRI measures blood flow, which is a proxy for activity, and increased activity in a region during a task does not establish that the region is necessary for the task.

Two more methods complete the set. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures the tiny magnetic fields produced by neural activity at the scalp; its temporal resolution is excellent, complementing fMRI's spatial resolution. And transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) uses a magnetic coil over the scalp to turn off, or disrupt, activity in a small area of cortex for a brief period. TMS matters out of proportion to its precision because it creates a temporary, reversible virtual lesion in a healthy human volunteer: it converts a correlational question into an experimental one, which is exactly what imaging cannot do.

High-yield

Structure: CT (X-ray, fast, gross), MRI (magnetic, high-resolution soft tissue), DTI (white-matter tracts). Function: PET (radioactive tracer, 2-DG, can map receptors), fMRI (BOLD, no injection, good spatial, poor temporal). MEG = excellent temporal resolution. TMS = reversible virtual lesion, and the one non-invasive human method that supports a causal claim.

Recording human psychophysiological activity

These are the non-invasive surface measures of psychophysiology, all recorded from healthy intact people.

The electroencephalogram (EEG) records gross electrical activity from scalp electrodes. What it captures is not individual action potentials but the summed postsynaptic potentials of large populations of similarly oriented neurons, which is why EEG is a good index of global state and a poor index of local computation. Its signatures are frequency bands: alpha waves, regular 8 to 12 Hz activity, appear in relaxed wakefulness with the eyes closed, and Chapter 14 uses the rest of the bands to stage sleep.

The important trick applied to EEG is signal averaging. The response to a single stimulus is buried in background noise. But if the stimulus is presented many times and the EEG segments following each presentation are averaged, the random background noise averages toward zero while the response, which is time-locked to the stimulus, survives. The result is an event-related potential (ERP), and its early components are the sensory evoked potentials. The best-known later component is the P300, a positive wave about 300 ms after a stimulus that the participant finds meaningful or unexpected; because its latency tracks the time taken to categorise a stimulus rather than its physical properties, it is used as an index of cognitive processing. Note also far-field potentials: because the skull conducts, activity generated in the brain stem can be recorded at the scalp, which is how brain-stem auditory function is tested in newborns.

The other four measures: muscle tension via the electromyogram (EMG), which is normally rectified and integrated to give a smooth index of tension; eye movement via the electro-oculogram (EOG), exploiting the steady voltage difference between front and back of the eyeball; skin conductance, indexing sympathetic arousal through sweat-gland activity, reported either as the tonic skin conductance level (SCL) or the phasic skin conductance response (SCR) to a discrete stimulus; and cardiovascular activity, comprising heart rate (from the ECG), blood pressure (measured with a sphygmomanometer and expressed as systolic over diastolic), and local blood volume, measured by plethysmography.

Invasive physiological methods

Now the methods that manipulate, used in animals, and therefore the ones that establish causes.

Stereotaxic surgery is the enabling technology. A stereotaxic atlas gives the three-dimensional coordinates of every structure relative to a fixed reference point on the skull, conventionally bregma, the junction of the skull sutures. A stereotaxic instrument has a head holder that fixes the skull in the standard orientation and a micromanipulator that carries an electrode or cannula to the specified coordinates. This is how a structure millimetres deep in an intact brain is reached reproducibly.

Lesion methods destroy tissue and then ask what behaviour is lost. Aspiration uses a fine pipette to draw off tissue on the surface. Radio-frequency lesions pass high-frequency current through an electrode to destroy tissue with heat, and their size and shape are determined by the current and the electrode tip. Knife cuts sever connections rather than removing a structure, which distinguishes a structure's function from that of its pathway. Cryogenic blockade is the important one conceptually: cooling tissue enough to stop neurons firing, but not enough to kill them, so the block is reversible and each animal can serve as its own control.

Two interpretive cautions carry heavy weight. First, most brain structures are bilateral, and a unilateral lesion typically produces much milder effects than a bilateral one because the intact side compensates, so a study that lesions one side and finds nothing has not shown the structure is unimportant. Second, and this is the classic criticism of the whole method, a lesion inevitably damages fibres of passage, axons merely travelling through the region on their way elsewhere. A deficit after a lesion may therefore be caused by disconnecting two other structures rather than by removing the target's own function. This is precisely why converging operations matter.

Electrical stimulation is the complement to lesioning: it typically has the opposite effect and often elicits a whole behavioural sequence, such as eating or attack, which is strong evidence that a coordinated program is being triggered rather than a muscle being twitched.

Invasive recording comes in four grains. Intracellular unit recording puts the electrode inside a single neuron and gives the moment-to-moment membrane potential, the method behind Chapter 4. Extracellular unit recording places the tip beside a neuron and records the timing of its action potentials but not the graded potential; its practical problem is that a moving animal displaces the electrode, solved by implanting fine wires that move with the tissue. Multiple-unit recording uses a larger tip to pick up many neurons at once and reports the summed rate. Invasive EEG uses implanted electrodes for gross signals, either cortical or deep.

Pharmacological and genetic methods

Drugs can be given peripherally, but to test whether a particular structure mediates an effect the drug is delivered there directly, through an implanted cannula by microinjection. Selective neurotoxins refine lesioning by killing only one kind of cell: 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) is taken up by catecholaminergic neurons and destroys them selectively, and kainic acid and similar excitotoxins destroy cell bodies while sparing fibres of passage, which directly addresses the interpretive problem above.

Several techniques label chemicals in tissue. Autoradiography exposes a slice to photographic emulsion to reveal where a radioactive ligand bound, mapping receptors. The 2-DG technique is the autoradiographic version of the PET logic, giving a high-resolution activity map. Immunocytochemistry uses labelled antibodies to a specific protein to show where that protein is, and is used to locate transmitters, enzymes and receptors. In situ hybridisation uses a labelled complementary strand of nucleic acid to show where a particular mRNA is, that is, where a gene is being expressed. Cerebral dialysis is the one method that measures transmitter release in a behaving animal: a fine tube with a semipermeable section is implanted, fluid is perfused through it, and the transmitter that diffuses in is collected and assayed, so you get a chemical time-course alongside behaviour.

The genetic methods are the newest layer. Gene knockout animals have a target gene inactivated, so you can ask what a single protein contributes; the standing caution is that development compensates, so a knockout tells you about an animal built without the gene, not about the gene's normal moment-to-moment role. Gene knock-in inserts a gene, often a human one, to model a disorder. Antisense oligonucleotides bind a target mRNA and block its translation, giving a temporary and dose-controlled knockdown. Green fluorescent protein (GFP), borrowed from a jellyfish, is expressed alongside a gene of interest so that the cells expressing it glow and can be traced in living tissue. Optogenetics is the transformative one: a light-sensitive ion channel such as channelrhodopsin is expressed in one genetically specified population of neurons, and a pulse of light then switches exactly that population on (or, with a different opsin, off) within milliseconds, in a behaving animal. It combines the causal power of stimulation with cell-type specificity and millisecond timing, which no earlier method had together. CRISPR gene-editing extends the same logic to precise sequence changes.

Behavioural research methods

The measurement of behaviour is not the soft end of the chapter; a study is only as good as its behavioural measure. A paradigm is a standard set of procedures for investigating a phenomenon, and the reason paradigms matter is comparability: results across laboratories are only combinable if the behaviour was elicited the same way.

Human neuropsychological testing has passed through three approaches, and the sequence is examinable. The single-test approach looked for one test that would detect brain damage, and failed, because damage is not one thing. The standardised-test-battery approach administers a fixed battery to everyone, the Halstead-Reitan and Luria-Nebraska batteries being the standard examples; this reliably detects whether there is damage but is weak at specifying what is wrong. The customised-test-battery approach is the modern one and works in two phases: a common battery of general tests is given to everyone to identify the general area of difficulty, and then a second set of tests is selected specifically to characterise that difficulty in detail. The customised approach depends on theory, since you can only design a follow-up test if you have a model of the function.

In practice the common first phase usually begins with an intelligence test such as the WAIS, used not for the IQ score but because the pattern of subtest scores flags where to look next. Individual tests worth naming: digit span for short-term verbal memory (the usual span is around seven digits), the token test for language comprehension, and the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test for frontal-lobe function, where the participant must infer a sorting rule that then changes without warning, and the diagnostic error is perseveration, continuing to use a rule that has stopped working. Memory testing must separate explicit memory (conscious recollection, which the patient can report) from implicit memory (memory expressed through improved performance without recollection), because amnesic patients characteristically lose the first and keep the second. The standard demonstration is repetition priming: shown a list of words and later given fragments to complete, amnesic patients complete the previously seen words at above-chance rates while denying having seen the list.

Animal behavioural paradigms come in families. For species-typical behaviour: the open-field test measures activity and fearfulness in a large empty enclosure, with fearful animals showing thigmotaxis (staying near the walls) and more defecation; the elevated plus maze pits exploration against the aversiveness of open exposed arms and is a standard anxiety assay; and the colony-intruder paradigm elicits naturalistic aggression and defence. For learning: Pavlovian conditioning, operant conditioning in a Skinner box, conditioned taste aversion (a single pairing of a novel flavour with illness produces long-lasting avoidance, a demonstration that learning is not equipotential across stimuli), self-stimulation of reward sites, and conditioned defensive burying. For spatial memory specifically, the two workhorses are the Morris water maze, where an animal must learn the location of a hidden platform in opaque water using only distal cues, and the radial arm maze, where food is placed at the ends of arms radiating from a centre and efficient performance requires remembering which arms have already been visited. Both reappear in Chapter 11, because both are exquisitely sensitive to hippocampal damage.

Worked example

Claim: "the hippocampus stores spatial memories", based on an fMRI study showing hippocampal activation while people navigate a virtual town. Ask what each method could add. The fMRI shows correlation only, and the activation might reflect a related process such as attention to landmarks. TMS over the region during navigation would test necessity in humans, though depth is a problem for the hippocampus. Human patients with selective hippocampal damage (neuropsychology) failing a comparable navigation task would strengthen it considerably. Bilateral hippocampal lesions impairing Morris water maze performance in rats would establish necessity causally, though unilateral lesions would be expected to show little, and excitotoxic lesions with kainic acid rather than aspiration would rule out fibres of passage. Optogenetic silencing of hippocampal cells only during the retrieval phase would establish the specific stage at which the structure is required. The claim is credible in proportion to how many of these agree, which is Chapter 1's converging operations turned into a research plan.

6. The visual system

Vision is the model system of sensory neuroscience: it is the sense we know most about, and Pinel uses it to teach a general lesson about how brains work. The lesson is that perception is not transmission. The visual system does not deliver a picture to the cortex; it takes a spatially and temporally structured pattern of light, throws most of it away, extracts a small number of specific features, and constructs a representation that is useful. Almost every counter-intuitive fact in the chapter follows from that.

Light, and the two properties that matter

Light can be described as waves, and two properties of the wave correspond to two properties of experience. Wavelength relates to perceived hue (the visible range being roughly 380 to 760 nanometres) and intensity relates to perceived brightness. The word "relates" is doing real work: the correspondence is not one-to-one, because what you see also depends on the surrounding light and on your own history, which is the point of colour constancy later.

The eye's optics contribute two adjustments. The pupil sets the trade-off between sensitivity and acuity: constricted, it admits less light but gives a sharper image with greater depth of focus; dilated, it admits more light at the cost of sharpness. So in bright light the system takes the sharp option and in dim light it sacrifices sharpness for sensitivity. And accommodation is the adjustment of the lens's shape by the ciliary muscles to focus near or far objects on the retina: for near objects the lens is allowed to become fatter and more powerful.

The eyes also converge, and because they are set apart in the head each receives a slightly different image, a difference called binocular disparity. Disparity is greater for near objects than far ones, and the visual system uses it to construct three-dimensional depth. This is a good early example of the chapter's theme: depth is not in the image, it is computed from the relation between two images.

The retina, and its counter-intuitive design

The retina has five layers of cells, and the transmission chain runs: receptors (rods and cones) to bipolar cells to retinal ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve. Two further cell types run laterally rather than vertically: horizontal cells connect receptors to each other and amacrine cells connect bipolars and ganglion cells to each other. The lateral cells are the reason the retina computes rather than merely relays; they mediate the inhibition described below.

The design has a striking oddity: the retina is built inside-out. Light must pass through the ganglion cells and bipolar cells before reaching the receptors, which are at the back. This creates two problems, and the solutions to both are instructive.

The first problem is that the axons must leave the eye, and they do so through a hole, the optic disc, which is therefore a blind spot with no receptors. You never notice it. The visual system fills it in by completion, using the surrounding information to construct what is presumably there. Completion is the strongest everyday evidence that what you see is a construction: the blind spot is not experienced as a gap because the brain does not represent gaps, it represents its best guess.

The second problem is that overlying cells blur the image. The solution is local: at the fovea, the high-acuity centre of the retina, the overlying layers are thinned and pushed aside, forming an indentation. The system accepts blur across most of the retina and buys sharpness at the one place that is pointed at whatever you are attending to. Which is why you must move your eyes to inspect anything.

Duplexity theory: two systems in one retina

Duplexity theory holds that the retina contains two functionally different systems, and once you see the trade-off each makes, every property follows.

The single idea that generates the whole table is convergence. When many receptors funnel into one ganglion cell, their weak individual signals sum, so very little light is needed to make that ganglion cell respond: high sensitivity. But the cell can no longer tell which of its many receptors was stimulated, so spatial detail is lost: low acuity. When one cone drives one ganglion cell, the reverse holds. Sensitivity and acuity are not two independent design goals; they are the two ends of one trade-off, and having two systems is how the retina gets both, at different times of day and in different parts of the visual field.

Two consequences worth knowing. Dark adaptation follows a two-part curve, with a fast cone component and a slower, much larger rod component that continues for twenty to thirty minutes, which is simply the two systems adapting at their own rates. And the Purkinje effect: because the rod and cone systems have different spectral sensitivity curves, with rods most sensitive at shorter wavelengths than cones, a red flower and a blue flower that look equally bright in daylight will look very different at dusk, the blue appearing relatively brighter as vision shifts from cones to rods.

Trap

To see a very faint star, look slightly beside it. The fovea is all cones and has almost no rods, so it is the least sensitive part of the retina in dim light. This also means acuity in the dark is genuinely poor, not just subjectively vague. And note that rods are not "worse" receptors: they are the ones that let you see at all at night.

From eye to cortex: the retina-geniculate-striate pathway

About 90 per cent of retinal ganglion axons take this route: retina to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus, then to primary visual cortex, also called striate cortex or V1, in the posterior occipital lobe. The pathway has three properties to hold on to.

It is retinotopic: neighbouring points on the retina project to neighbouring points in the LGN and in the cortex, so the cortex contains a map of the retina. The map is heavily distorted, with a disproportionate area given to the fovea, which is the cortical expression of the same acuity priority.

It is contralateral by visual field, not by eye. This distinction is the most-tested point in the chapter. At the optic chiasm the fibres from the medial (nasal) half of each retina cross and those from the lateral (temporal) half do not. Because the lens inverts the image, the left half of the visual field falls on the right half of each retina. The result is that all information from the left visual field, from both eyes, ends up in the right hemisphere, and vice versa. So cutting one optic nerve blinds one eye, but a lesion behind the chiasm produces a loss of one visual field in both eyes.

It is parallel. Two channels run side by side from the start. The P (parvocellular) channel, which receives mostly from cones, is sensitive to colour, fine detail and stationary or slowly moving stimuli. The M (magnocellular) channel, receiving mostly from rods, is colour-blind and specialised for movement and rapid change. They terminate in different layers of the six-layered LGN, and this early split is the ancestor of the two cortical streams at the end of the chapter.

Receptive fields: what a visual neuron is actually for

The receptive field of a visual neuron is the area of the retina within which light influences its firing. The great research programme here belongs to David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, who won the 1981 Nobel Prize for it, and their method was simple in principle: record from one cell, move a spot or bar of light around the visual field, and find out what makes the cell fire.

At the level of retinal ganglion cells and LGN cells, receptive fields are small, circular and centre-surround: they have a centre and a doughnut-shaped surround that have opposite effects. An on-centre cell is excited by light in the centre and inhibited by light in the surround; an off-centre cell does the reverse. The functional consequence is the important part. Such a cell responds weakly to uniform illumination across its whole field, because centre and surround cancel, and strongly to a difference between centre and surround. In other words, these cells do not report brightness, they report contrast. The retina is throwing away information about absolute light level, which is largely irrelevant, and keeping information about edges, which is where objects are.

The mechanism is lateral inhibition, implemented by the horizontal and amacrine cells: an active receptor inhibits its neighbours. Lateral inhibition also produces contrast enhancement, the perceptual exaggeration of edges, whose classic demonstration is Mach bands, the illusory bright and dark stripes seen at the boundaries between adjacent grey strips of uniform shade. The stripes are not in the stimulus; they are in the computation. Pinel's point is that this is not a flaw. Edges are what matter for identifying objects, and the visual system deliberately exaggerates them.

In V1, Hubel and Wiesel found two further classes, and the progression is a progression in abstraction.

  • Simple cells have rectangular rather than circular receptive fields with excitatory and inhibitory regions side by side. They respond to a bar or edge of a particular orientation at a particular location. They are still position-specific.
  • Complex cells respond to a bar of a particular orientation anywhere within a larger receptive field, and many respond best when it moves in one direction. They have abstracted away from position: the cell now signals "a vertical edge is present" rather than "light is at this spot". Many complex cells are also binocular, responding to input from either eye, and typically respond best to slightly different retinal positions in the two eyes, which is the cellular basis of binocular disparity and hence depth.

V1 is also organised in columns running through its depth. All neurons in one column share a preferred orientation (orientation columns), and adjacent columns alternate in which eye drives them more strongly (ocular dominance columns). Combine the columnar organisation with the retinotopic map and V1 turns out to be a bank of local edge-detectors, replicated for every orientation and every eye at every point in the visual field.

High-yield

Hubel and Wiesel (Nobel 1981): centre-surround fields in retina and LGN, simple cells (orientation plus location) and complex cells (orientation, position-invariant, often direction-selective and binocular) in V1, plus orientation and ocular dominance columns. Lateral inhibition gives contrast enhancement, demonstrated by Mach bands. Centre-surround cells report contrast, not brightness.

Deprivation, and the critical period

Hubel and Wiesel's second great contribution was developmental. If one eye of a kitten is deprived of patterned vision during the first months of life, the cortical territory driven by that eye shrinks dramatically and few cortical cells respond to it afterwards, while the territory driven by the open eye expands. The deprived eye is not damaged; the cortex has been rewired. Two controls make the result precise. Depriving both eyes produces far less severe cortical reorganisation than depriving one, which shows that the mechanism is competition between the two eyes' inputs rather than simple disuse. And the effect only occurs if the deprivation falls within an early critical period; the same deprivation in an adult leaves ocular dominance largely intact. This is the origin of the clinical practice of treating childhood cataract and strabismus early, and it is the sharpest single demonstration in the book that experience physically shapes neural circuitry within a bounded developmental window. Chapter 9 generalises it.

Colour vision: two theories, both correct, at different levels

Two historical theories competed, and the resolution is the interesting part.

Component (trichromatic) theory, from Thomas Young and developed by Hermann von Helmholtz, proposed three kinds of receptor with different spectral sensitivities, with colour coded by the ratio of activity in the three. Its strongest support is behavioural: any colour can be matched by mixing three suitably chosen wavelengths, which is exactly what a three-receptor system predicts.

Opponent-process theory, from Ewald Hering, proposed instead two chromatic classes of cell each signalling opposite colours in opposite directions (red versus green, blue versus yellow) plus one achromatic black-versus-white class. Its strongest support is phenomenological: you never see a reddish green or a bluish yellow, and the complementary afterimage of staring at red is green, both of which follow if the pairs are opposed in a single channel.

The resolution, and this is the examinable point, is that both are true at different levels of the system. The receptors are trichromatic, three cone types with different peak sensitivities, as Young and Helmholtz said. The cells downstream, in the retina and LGN, are opponent: they are excited by one range of wavelengths in the centre of their field and inhibited by another, as Hering said. The opponent organisation is built out of trichromatic input. A hundred-year argument dissolved because the two sides were describing different stages.

Colour is then not determined by wavelength alone. Colour constancy is the fact that an object's perceived colour stays roughly the same under very different illuminations, even though the wavelengths reflected into the eye change substantially: a white shirt looks white indoors under yellow light and outdoors in blue shade. Edwin Land's retinex theory accounts for it by proposing that the system compares the light reflected from an object with the light reflected from its surroundings across the three cone types, so what is computed is a relative reflectance, which is a property of the object rather than of the illumination. Constancy, once again, is a construction, and it is the useful thing to construct because it is the object and not the lighting that you need to recognise.

Beyond V1: two streams, and what their lesions look like

Cortical visual areas are conventionally grouped as primary (V1, receiving most of the geniculate input), secondary (receiving mainly from primary), and association (receiving from more than one sensory system). The flow is largely from primary to secondary to association, but with substantial feedback in the reverse direction, which is why perception is influenced by expectation.

Beyond the secondary areas the pathway divides into two functional streams, and the division goes back to the P and M channels of the LGN.

  • The dorsal stream runs from V1 into the parietal lobe. It is fed heavily by the M channel and handles spatial location and the visual guidance of action. It was originally labelled the where stream and is now more often called the how stream, because its function seems to be less about knowing where things are than about controlling movements toward them.
  • The ventral stream runs from V1 into the temporal lobe. It is fed heavily by the P channel and handles the identification of objects: the what stream.

The evidence that convinced people is a double dissociation between two kinds of patient. Patients with visual agnosia from ventral-stream damage cannot identify or describe an object, and cannot report the orientation of a slot, yet can reach out and post a card through that same slot with an accurately oriented, well-formed grasp. Patients with optic ataxia from dorsal-stream damage show the mirror pattern: they can describe and identify the object perfectly well but cannot guide the hand to it accurately. Conscious visual identification and visually guided action are handled by different systems, and each can be lost while the other is intact. Pinel notes the further implication that the dorsal stream's contribution is largely unconscious.

Damage to specific ventral areas produces specific losses: prosopagnosia is a selective inability to recognise faces, with object recognition relatively spared and, tellingly, with the patient still showing physiological signs of recognising familiar faces they cannot consciously identify. Akinetopsia, from damage to the motion area, is a deficiency in seeing movement: the world is perceived as a series of static snapshots. And a lesion of V1 produces a scotoma, an area of blindness in the corresponding part of the visual field, which patients often do not notice because of completion. Some such patients show blindsight: an ability to respond to visual stimuli in the scotoma, for example by pointing at them or correctly guessing their orientation, while denying seeing anything at all. The usual explanation is that the residual ten per cent of retinal projections that bypass the geniculostriate route, going to structures such as the superior colliculi, can support responses without supporting conscious vision.

Worked example

A patient reports normal vision but keeps bumping into things on the left and does not notice food on the left of the plate; on testing, the left visual field is lost in both eyes. Where is the lesion, and why is it not in the eye? Loss confined to one eye means the damage is at or in front of the optic nerve; loss of the same visual field in both eyes means the damage is behind the chiasm, where fibres have been regrouped by field rather than by eye. Left field loss in both eyes therefore means a right-sided lesion of the optic tract, LGN, or striate cortex. Now the second half of the question: why does the patient say vision is normal? Because there is no experience of a gap. The blind region is filled by completion, the same mechanism that hides the blind spot in every healthy eye, so a scotoma is characteristically detected by testing rather than by complaint. If the patient can also point above chance to lights presented in the blind field while insisting they see nothing, you have blindsight, and the inference is that the surviving retinal projections to the superior colliculi are doing the work.
