Biopsychology (Pinel & Barnes), in full depth

The whole of Pinel's Biopsychology, all eighteen chapters, rewritten as real explanatory prose instead of bullet fragments. Every concept is built from scratch in full sentences, every mechanism is walked through step by step, the experiment or the patient behind each claim is named, the theorist is named wherever there is one, and worked examples are given where a concept only clicks once you push a number or a case through it. Read a chapter cold and you should finish it understanding the concept, not merely recognising the words.

1. Biopsychology as a neuroscience

Biopsychology is the scientific study of the biology of behaviour. That one-line definition hides the thing that makes the field distinctive, so it is worth unpacking slowly. Neuroscience as a whole is the study of the nervous system, and it is enormous: it includes people who study how a single ion channel folds, people who model networks mathematically, and people who develop drugs. Biopsychology is the branch of neuroscience that keeps behaviour at the centre of the question. A biopsychologist does not ask "what does this structure do" in the abstract; the question is always "what does this structure do to what the organism thinks, feels, or does". Pinel's phrase for the discipline's contribution is that biopsychologists bring to neuroscience a knowledge of behaviour and of the methods of behavioural research, which the other neurosciences do not supply on their own. You can know everything about a receptor and still have no idea what an animal will do, because behaviour has its own laws and its own measurement problems.

The name was not always this. Earlier labels included psychobiology, behavioural biology and behavioural neuroscience, and you will still meet all of them. Pinel prefers biopsychology precisely because the word order carries the argument: bio is the adjective and psychology is the noun, so the discipline is a kind of psychology that uses biology, not a kind of biology that happens to notice behaviour.

The founding text: Hebb, 1949

Biopsychology has a reasonably clean origin date. In 1949 Donald O. Hebb published The Organization of Behavior, and this is generally taken as the first comprehensive theory of how complex psychological phenomena (perception, emotion, thought, memory) could be produced by ordinary activity in the brain. Why that mattered is easy to miss now that it is obvious. Before Hebb, the widespread assumption among psychologists was that complex mental events were simply too complex to be traceable to neural activity, so the honest thing was to study behaviour and leave the brain alone. Hebb's move was to show that you could actually specify a mechanism.

His mechanism was the Hebb synapse, usually compressed into the slogan "cells that fire together wire together". Stated properly: if a presynaptic neuron repeatedly participates in firing a postsynaptic neuron, some growth process or metabolic change occurs at that synapse so that the presynaptic neuron's later ability to fire the postsynaptic one is increased. Chain those strengthened synapses together and you get a cell assembly, a set of neurons that has been wired into a functional unit by experience and can now be re-activated as a whole by any part of it. Chain assemblies in sequence and you get a phase sequence, which is Hebb's account of a train of thought. Notice the structure of the argument: a single local, plausible, physical rule, applied repeatedly, yields something that looks psychological. That is the template for essentially every biopsychological explanation that follows in this book.

Hebb built his theory from a genuinely mixed evidence base, and this is the second thing he established as normal practice: experiments on laboratory animals, clinical case studies of human patients, and logical argument all counted. He did not privilege one.

High-yield

Hebb (1949), The Organization of Behavior = the founding of biopsychology as a discipline, and the first real theory of how psychological phenomena arise from brain activity. Hebbian learning: co-active neurons strengthen their synapse. Cell assembly (a wired-together group) then phase sequence (a chain of assemblies, i.e. a thought).

Human subjects and animal subjects

Biopsychology studies both humans and non-human animals, and each has real advantages, so the choice is a research-design decision rather than a matter of taste.

Human participants have three obvious advantages. They follow instructions, which means you can run a task instead of waiting for a behaviour to occur spontaneously. They report subjective experience, which is the only direct access anyone has to conscious content. And they are cheaper: no purpose-built housing, no colony maintenance. There is a fourth advantage that is easy to overlook: they have a human brain, and if the question is about a specifically human capacity such as syntax, only a human brain will do.

Animal subjects have a different and equally real set of advantages. Their nervous systems are simpler, so the effect you are looking for is not buried under the extra machinery. The comparative approach becomes available: studying the same system across species lets you separate the general biological principle from the local human quirk, and it is usually only across species that you can see what a structure is for. It is possible to run genuinely invasive experiments, meaning lesions, direct stimulation, single-cell recording, and selective breeding, which are the only methods that establish causation cleanly. And practically, you can control genetics, rearing, and history in ways that are simply not possible with people.

The six divisions of biopsychology

Pinel divides the field six ways. The division is by method and subject, not by topic, which is the single most common thing students get wrong here. Two people can both be studying memory and belong to different divisions.

DivisionThe defining questionMethod and subjects
Physiological psychologyWhat neural mechanism produces this behaviour?Direct manipulation of the brain: lesions, electrical stimulation, surgery. Almost always laboratory animals. Pure research.
PsychopharmacologyHow do drugs change behaviour and the brain?Manipulation by drug rather than by scalpel. Animals and humans. Strongly applied: it is where therapeutic drugs come from.
NeuropsychologyWhat are the psychological effects of brain damage?Human patients with existing brain damage. Case studies plus standardised test batteries. Never experimental brain damage. The most applied division.
PsychophysiologyHow does physiological activity relate to behaviour in intact people?Non-invasive recording from the surface of healthy human volunteers: EEG, heart rate, blood pressure, skin conductance, pupil diameter, EMG, EOG.
Cognitive neuroscienceWhat is the neural basis of cognition?Predominantly functional brain imaging (fMRI, PET) in healthy humans performing cognitive tasks. Highly collaborative and interdisciplinary.
Comparative psychologyHow and why did this behaviour evolve?Comparison across species, including ethology (behaviour in the wild) and evolutionary/behavioural genetics.
Trap

Three of these six names are routinely confused, and papers exploit it. Physiological psychology = invasive manipulation, animals, pure research. Psychophysiology = non-invasive measurement, healthy humans, no manipulation of the brain at all. Neuropsychology = pre-existing brain damage in human patients. The give-away words in a question stem are "lesioned rats" (physiological), "skin conductance in volunteers" (psychophysiology), and "patient with left temporal damage" (neuropsychology).

Converging operations: why one method is never enough

Every method in the list above is flawed, and each is flawed in its own particular direction. Lesion studies establish that a structure is necessary but tell you nothing about what it contributes when intact, and a lesion inevitably damages fibres that are merely passing through. Imaging shows correlated activity but cannot show that the activity is doing any work. Human case studies are rich but uncontrolled, since the damage was not assigned to the patient by a researcher. Animal experiments are controlled but raise the question of whether the finding generalises.

The field's response is the strategy Pinel calls converging operations: attack a single problem with several methods whose weaknesses do not overlap, and trust the conclusion only where they agree. The classic worked example is Korsakoff's syndrome. Human case studies of chronic alcoholics showed a devastating amnesia, which invited the conclusion that alcohol is the cause. But converging evidence changed the answer. Some patients with the syndrome had no history of alcohol at all, but did have severe malnutrition. Alcoholics eat badly and alcohol interferes with the absorption of thiamine (vitamin B1). Giving thiamine to Korsakoff patients halted progression, and thiamine-deprived animals developed comparable brain damage without alcohol. The conclusion that survives all the methods is that Korsakoff's syndrome is caused by thiamine deficiency, with alcohol as the usual route to that deficiency and an additional independent toxin in its own right. No single method could have got there.

Scientific inference: how you study something you cannot see

The brain's workings are not directly observable, so biopsychology relies on scientific inference: you measure something you can observe, and reason back to the invisible mechanism that must have produced it. This is not a weakness peculiar to the field. It is what physics does with subatomic particles. What matters is that the inference is disciplined.

The textbook illustration is the perception of movement during eye movement. Your eyes are constantly making rapid saccades, so the image of the world sweeps across your retina many times a second, yet the world does not appear to jump. Two competing inferences are available. Either the brain tracks the eye's position by monitoring feedback from the eye muscles, or it uses the motor command it has just issued to the eye muscles, that is, it knows where it told the eye to go. You cannot see which. But you can design an observation that separates them. If the eye is prevented from moving while the command to move is still issued, a feedback account predicts the world will look stationary (no movement occurred, no feedback), whereas a command-based account predicts the world should appear to jump (a command was issued, so a compensation is applied that nothing cancels). The world does appear to move. The inference is therefore that the visual system compensates using the outgoing motor signal, a copy of the command, rather than sensory feedback from the muscles. Note the form: an unobservable mechanism, two rival accounts, and one observable consequence on which they disagree.

Critical thinking about biopsychological claims

Pinel closes the chapter by teaching a habit rather than a fact, and he does it with two cautionary cases. The habit is to separate what was observed from what was concluded, because the gap between them is where bad science lives.

Case one: Delgado and the bull. José Delgado implanted an electrode in the caudate nucleus of a bull, entered the ring, and stopped a charging bull by radio stimulation. The claim built on it was that the caudate contains a mechanism that inhibits aggression, and by extension that human violence might be treated by stimulating it. What was actually observed is narrower: the stimulation stopped the charge. But the bull did not stop in a way that looked like a loss of aggressive motivation. It turned consistently in circles in one direction. A far more parsimonious reading is that the stimulation elicited a motor response, a forced turning, which happens to be incompatible with charging straight ahead. The observation is real; the conclusion about aggression was never licensed by it.

Case two: the prefrontal lobotomy. Egas Moniz introduced the prefrontal leucotomy as a psychiatric treatment and received a Nobel Prize in 1949; tens of thousands of operations followed, including Walter Freeman's transorbital version, which was fast enough to be performed outside an operating theatre. The evidential base was startlingly thin. It rested substantially on Carlyle Jacobsen's report that a chimpanzee named Becky, who had been extremely upset by errors on a delayed-response task, became placid after frontal-lobe lesions. One primate, one behaviour, in a laboratory task. Against that, another chimpanzee in the same work, Lucy, showed no comparable calming, and the follow-up evaluation of human patients was for years essentially anecdotal and conducted by the surgeons themselves. The lesson is not that psychosurgery is inconceivable; it is that the evidence, the sample, and the outcome measure must be examined before a mechanism is believed, and that the enthusiasm of the person making the claim is not evidence.

High-yield

Delgado's bull = caudate stimulation, the turning-response reinterpretation, the warning about over-reading a dramatic demonstration. Moniz = prefrontal lobotomy, Nobel 1949, built on Jacobsen's chimpanzee Becky; Freeman = transorbital lobotomy. Korsakoff's = thiamine deficiency, and the standing example of converging operations.

Worked example

A researcher records skin conductance and heart rate in undergraduates while they view emotional photographs, and concludes that the amygdala generates the arousal. Which division is the study, and what is wrong with the conclusion? The methods are non-invasive surface measures in healthy humans, so the study is psychophysiology. The conclusion is not licensed: nothing in the study measured the amygdala, and no structure was manipulated. Skin conductance indexes sympathetic activation, which many structures could drive. To get to the amygdala you would need converging evidence, for example fMRI showing amygdala activation in the same task (cognitive neuroscience), plus patients with selective amygdala damage failing to show the response (neuropsychology), plus amygdala lesions abolishing the response in animals (physiological psychology). Once the three agree, the claim stands.

2. Evolution, genetics and experience

This chapter exists to dismantle a question rather than answer it. The question is "is this behaviour due to nature or to nurture", and Pinel's position is that it is not a hard question with a subtle answer, it is a malformed question that cannot have an answer. Understanding why it is malformed is the whole chapter.

The two flawed dichotomies

The first flawed dichotomy is physiological versus psychological, sometimes phrased as brain versus mind. This is Cartesian dualism, from René Descartes, who proposed a material body governed by physical law and a non-material mind, interacting at the pineal gland. It has an appealing tidiness and it is false in a way that matters practically: if some behaviour is caused by a non-physical mind, then that behaviour is permanently outside biological explanation, and the research programme stops. Biopsychology proceeds on monism: there is one kind of stuff, and thinking is something that brains do.

The second flawed dichotomy is nature versus nurture, the assumption that behaviour can be partitioned into an inherited portion and a learned portion. Two historical camps set this up. European ethologists such as Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen studied instinctive behaviours in the wild and emphasised inheritance; North American behaviourists such as John B. Watson and B. F. Skinner studied learning in the laboratory and emphasised experience. Both were describing real phenomena. Both were generalising from a method that could only see one of the two.

Pinel's replacement model has three terms rather than two. Behaviour is the product of the interaction between (1) the organism's genetic endowment, which is a product of its evolution, (2) its experience, and (3) its perception of the current situation. Genes and experience do not contribute separate portions of a behaviour; genes build a nervous system that develops in an environment, and the resulting brain then responds to a situation as it perceives it. Asking which of the two produced the behaviour is like asking whether the area of a rectangle is due to its length or its width.

The one-sentence version

Genes and experience are not two additive sources of behaviour; genes specify how development responds to experience, so every behaviour is 100% genetic and 100% environmental at the same time, and the only sensible questions are about how the interaction works.

Natural selection, and what it actually says

Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859, presenting three lines of evidence: the fossil record's documented change over time, the striking structural similarities among living species (a bat's wing, a whale's flipper and a human hand share a bone plan), and the effects of selective breeding by humans, which showed that heritable variation plus selection produces change within a few generations.

The mechanism, natural selection, needs only three ingredients: individuals vary, some of that variation is heritable, and variants differ in how many offspring they leave. Anything meeting those three conditions will evolve. The unit of accounting is fitness, which in evolutionary biology does not mean health or strength; it means the number of offspring an individual contributes to the next generation relative to others. A frail animal that reproduces more is fitter, in this technical sense, than a magnificent one that does not.

Two refinements matter for behaviour. First, sexual selection, also Darwin's: traits can evolve because they improve mating success even at a cost to survival, which is how you get peacock tails and the general pattern of intrasexual competition (males competing for access) and intersexual selection (choosiness, typically female). Second, evolution is opportunistic and does not design. It works on whatever is already there, so structures often get repurposed: a feature that arose for one function and was later co-opted for another is an exaptation, and a feature that is a structural by-product of something else and was never selected for at all is a spandrel. This is the standing corrective to lazy adaptive storytelling: not everything is for something.

Trap

Evolution is not a ladder and humans are not at its top. Living species are all contemporary end-points of separate branches, not rungs. We did not evolve from chimpanzees; chimpanzees and humans share a common ancestor. Evolution has no goal, "more complex" is not the direction of travel, and most species that ever existed left no descendants. Also: individual differences within a species are typically far larger than the average difference between related species.

The course of human evolution, and the brain

The lineage runs, in the order the textbook uses, from early multicellular water-dwellers to chordates (animals with a dorsal nerve cord), then vertebrates (chordates with a spinal bone casing), then the first vertebrates, the bony fishes, then amphibians, which added life on land while still returning to water to lay eggs, then reptiles, which solved reproduction on dry land with the shelled egg and dry skin, then mammals, which added internal gestation and milk, then primates, and within primates the apes and then the hominins. The genus Homo is recent, and anatomically modern Homo sapiens is very recent indeed on this timescale.

Three things happened to the brain along the way and they are separable. Overall size increased, but size on its own is a poor index because it scales with body size, which is why comparisons use the ratio of brain to body weight, and better still the size of the brain relative to what a body of that size would predict. Second, the increase was not uniform: it was concentrated in the cerebrum and specifically in the neocortex, the structures involved in complex adaptive processes, while the brain stem, which handles reflexes and basic regulation, changed comparatively little. Third, and this is the elegant engineering point, cortex increased in area far more than the skull increased in volume, and it accommodated this by convolution: folding into gyri and sulci. Folding lets you pack a large sheet into a small box, which is why cortical surface area, not brain mass, is the number that tracks capability.

Fundamental genetics, built up

Gregor Mendel got the foundations right in the 1860s by making three good methodological choices: he studied dichotomous traits (either/or, such as brown versus white seeds, with no intermediates), he began from true-breeding lines, and he counted offspring across generations instead of describing them. Crossing brown-seed with white-seed true-breeding lines gave an all-brown first generation, and crossing those offspring with each other gave brown and white in a 3:1 ratio. Whiteness had vanished for a generation and returned unchanged, so it could not have been diluted or blended. Mendel's inference was that inheritance is carried by discrete particles, now called genes, that each organism carries two of them for a trait, and that where the two differ one may be expressed (dominant) and the other not (recessive). Two copies of the same version make a homozygote, two different versions a heterozygote. What is expressed is the phenotype; what is carried is the genotype. The 3:1 ratio falls straight out: one homozygous dominant, two heterozygotes and one homozygous recessive, and only the last shows the recessive phenotype.

Genes sit on chromosomes, which come in matched pairs. Ordinary cell division, mitosis, copies every chromosome so each daughter cell gets the full set. The production of gametes uses meiosis, which halves the number, so that egg and sperm each contribute one chromosome of each pair and the offspring is a genuinely new combination. Meiosis also includes crossing over, in which paired chromosomes exchange segments before separating. Crossing over is the reason inheritance is not simply reshuffling of whole parental chromosomes, and it is why genes physically close together on a chromosome tend to be inherited together, a phenomenon called linkage. Sex is determined by one pair: XX develops as female, XY as male, and the traits carried on the X alone are sex-linked, which is why recessive X-linked conditions appear far more often in males, who have no second X to mask them.

Watson and Crick in 1953 identified the structure that makes all this physical: DNA as a double helix of two complementary strands, with adenine pairing to thymine and guanine to cytosine. Complementarity explains replication in one step: unzip the helix and each strand specifies its partner, so two identical copies result.

Expression runs in two steps and the vocabulary is worth getting exactly right. In transcription, the DNA sequence of a gene is copied into a strand of messenger RNA, which leaves the nucleus. In translation, a ribosome reads the mRNA in three-base units called codons, each codon specifying one amino acid, and assembles the amino acid chain that folds into a protein. Proteins are the point: they are the enzymes and channels and receptors and structural elements, so "a gene does something" always cashes out as "a gene specifies a protein". The human genome contains on the order of twenty thousand protein-coding genes, strikingly few, and most of the genome is not protein-coding but regulatory, which is the modern answer to how so few genes build something so complicated: the same parts, controlled differently.

Control is where epigenetics enters. Every cell has the same DNA but expresses a different subset of it, and expression is regulated by chemical marks that do not change the sequence: DNA methylation, which typically silences a gene, and histone modification, which changes how tightly the DNA is spooled and therefore how accessible it is. These marks respond to experience, and some are transmitted through cell division and in some cases across generations. Epigenetics is the concrete mechanism that makes the nature-nurture dichotomy untenable: experience physically regulates genes.

High-yield

Mendel: dichotomous traits, true-breeding lines, 3:1 ratio, dominant/recessive, genotype/phenotype. Meiosis halves and includes crossing over (source of linkage); mitosis copies. Watson and Crick 1953, double helix, A-T and G-C complementarity. Transcription (DNA to mRNA) then translation (codons to amino acids to protein). Epigenetics: methylation and histone remodelling change expression without changing sequence.

Behavioural genetics: the experiments that settle the interaction

Two studies carry this section, and both are designed so that the interaction is not merely asserted but measured.

Selective breeding, Tryon. Robert Tryon bred rats for maze performance, mating the best learners with the best learners and the worst with the worst over successive generations. The two lines separated cleanly, which established that maze-learning ability is heritable. That is a real and non-trivial result, and it is also exactly where naive interpretation goes wrong.

Cooper and Zubek (1958). This is the study to remember, because it takes Tryon's maze-bright and maze-dull lines and asks what environment does to them. Rats from both lines were reared in one of three conditions: an enriched environment (large, complex, full of objects), an impoverished environment (bare cage), or a normal laboratory cage. The finding is a textbook interaction. In the impoverished condition, both lines performed poorly and the difference between them shrank to nothing. In the enriched condition, both lines performed well and again barely differed. The genetic difference between "bright" and "dull" rats appeared in full only in the ordinary environment. The heritability of the trait was real, yet whether it made any difference at all depended entirely on the environment. There is no fixed quantity of genetic influence to be measured; the size of the genetic effect is itself a function of the environment.

The same logic gives the cleanest single illustration of gene-environment interaction in humans: phenylketonuria (PKU). PKU is caused by a single recessive gene that leaves the child unable to make the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine, so phenylalanine accumulates and, in the developing brain, causes severe intellectual disability. It is unambiguously a single-gene disorder with a known biochemistry. And its principal consequence is entirely preventable by an environmental manipulation: a low-phenylalanine diet begun early in life. The gene is not the destiny; the gene specifies a vulnerability to a particular dietary environment.

Trap

Heritability estimates are the most misread statistic in psychology. A heritability estimate is a proportion of the variation in a trait within a particular population in a particular set of environments that is attributable to genetic variation. It is not the proportion of the trait in an individual that is due to genes, that quantity is meaningless. It does not tell you the trait is unmodifiable (PKU is highly heritable and fully treatable). And it does not transfer across populations or environments: change the range of environments and the estimate changes, as Cooper and Zubek showed directly.

The human tools are twin and adoption studies. Monozygotic twins come from one zygote and are genetically near-identical; dizygotic twins come from two zygotes and share about half their genes, like ordinary siblings. Comparing the two, and comparing twins reared together against twins reared apart, is the standard way of estimating heritability in humans. The known confound is that monozygotic twins are also treated more alike, so the design overestimates genetic contribution to the extent that it does. Note too that a small amount of DNA, the mitochondrial DNA, is inherited from the mother only, since mitochondria come with the egg.

Worked example

A paper reports that the heritability of a cognitive test score is 0.70 in a sample of middle-class children, and concludes that educational intervention is largely futile. Take it apart. First, 0.70 refers to variance in that sample, not to any individual's score. Second, and decisively, that sample has a narrow range of environments: if nearly everyone is in a similar decent environment, environmental variance is small, so genetic variance must account for most of what is left, and heritability is pushed up. Broaden the sample to include deprived environments and the estimate will fall, because there is now real environmental variance to account for. This is Cooper and Zubek's point in human form: a high heritability inside a narrow environmental range says nothing about what happens when you change the environment. Third, heritability is silent about modifiability in any case. PKU is the counter-example that ends the argument.

3. Anatomy of the nervous system

Anatomy is the chapter students try to memorise and should instead try to organise. Almost everything here follows from two facts: the nervous system is built as a tube that swells at the front end, and it is described using a coordinate system borrowed from four-legged animals. Get those two and the names stop being arbitrary.

The coordinate system, and why it bends in humans

Directions are given in three axes. Anterior means toward the nose and posterior toward the tail, so these are also called rostral and caudal. Dorsal means toward the back surface and ventral toward the belly. Medial means toward the midline and lateral away from it. The catch is that these terms were fixed for an animal whose spinal cord and brain lie along one straight horizontal line. Humans stand upright, so the axis bends about ninety degrees at the top of the brain stem. The practical consequence: in the human brain, dorsal means superior (toward the top of the head) and ventral means inferior, whereas in the human spinal cord dorsal still means toward the back. This single bend is responsible for a large share of confusion, so read the term as belonging to a structure rather than to the body.

Three cutting planes: the sagittal plane runs front to back down the vertical midline (the mid-sagittal section splits left from right), the horizontal plane cuts top from bottom, and the coronal or frontal plane cuts front from back, like a slice across the face. Finally ipsilateral means on the same side of the body and contralateral on the opposite side, which is the phrase you will need constantly once movement and sensation are crossed.

The major divisions

The whole nervous system splits into the central nervous system (CNS), meaning brain and spinal cord, encased in bone, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), everything else. The PNS then splits functionally.

The somatic nervous system (SNS) deals with the external environment and the skeletal muscles. It has afferent nerves carrying sensory signals inward from skin, muscles, joints and the special senses to the CNS, and efferent nerves carrying motor commands outward to the skeletal muscles. Afferent means arriving, efferent means exiting, and the mnemonic worth keeping is that afferent arrives and efferent exits.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) deals with the internal environment: smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands. Its afferents carry interoceptive signals inward. Its efferents divide into two antagonistic branches. The sympathetic branch mobilises: heart rate and blood pressure up, bronchi dilated, blood shunted to skeletal muscle, pupils dilated, digestion suspended, adrenal medulla triggered. This is the fight-or-flight arm, and its fibres leave the CNS from the thoracic and lumbar segments of the spinal cord. The parasympathetic branch conserves and restores: heart rate down, digestion and salivation up, pupils constricted, the rest-and-digest arm, with fibres leaving from the brain and the sacral region (the craniosacral pattern).

There is a second, more mechanical difference between the two branches that examiners like. Autonomic efferent pathways are two-stage: a preganglionic neuron runs from the CNS to a ganglion, and a postganglionic neuron runs from the ganglion to the target organ. In the sympathetic branch the ganglia sit close to the spinal cord, so preganglionic fibres are short and postganglionic fibres long, which suits a system designed to activate the whole body at once in a coordinated burst. In the parasympathetic branch the ganglia sit on or near the target organ, so preganglionic fibres are long and postganglionic ones very short, which suits organ-by-organ regulation. The transmitters follow suit: all preganglionic neurons in both branches release acetylcholine; parasympathetic postganglionic neurons also release acetylcholine (hence cholinergic), whereas sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine (hence adrenergic).

High-yield

Sympathetic = thoracolumbar origin, short preganglionic, long postganglionic, postganglionic transmitter norepinephrine, mass mobilisation. Parasympathetic = craniosacral origin, long preganglionic, short postganglionic, acetylcholine throughout, organ-specific. All preganglionic fibres in both branches are cholinergic.

Twelve pairs of cranial nerves project from the brain, mostly to the head. Some are purely sensory (I olfactory, II optic, VIII vestibulocochlear), some purely motor (III oculomotor, IV trochlear, VI abducens, XI accessory, XII hypoglossal), and some mixed (V trigeminal, VII facial, IX glossopharyngeal, X vagus). The one to know beyond its name is the vagus (X), whose name means wandering: it leaves the head entirely and supplies the heart, lungs and gut, and it carries the bulk of parasympathetic outflow. Because cranial nerves enter the brain at known levels, the pattern of cranial-nerve deficits localises brain-stem damage precisely, which is why the neurological examination tests them one by one.

Protection: meninges, cerebrospinal fluid, and the blood-brain barrier

Three membranes, the meninges, wrap the CNS. Outermost is the tough dura mater. Beneath it is the web-like arachnoid membrane. Innermost, adhering to the brain's surface and following every fold, is the delicate pia mater. Between arachnoid and pia lies the subarachnoid space, which contains large blood vessels and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

CSF fills the subarachnoid space and the internal chambers: the two lateral ventricles, the third ventricle, the cerebral aqueduct connecting third to fourth, and the fourth ventricle. It is continuously produced by the choroid plexus, networks of capillaries protruding into the ventricles, and continuously absorbed into the large veins. Its jobs are mechanical support (the brain effectively floats, which dramatically reduces its effective weight and cushions impact) and clearance of waste. Because production and absorption must balance, a blockage is dangerous: if the flow is obstructed, typically at the narrow cerebral aqueduct, pressure builds and the ventricles expand, which is hydrocephalus, treated by draining the fluid with a shunt.

The blood-brain barrier is not a membrane but a property of the cerebral blood vessels: their endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions, so molecules cannot slip between them and must pass through. Small uncharged molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross freely, fat-soluble molecules cross readily (which is why psychoactive drugs are typically lipid-soluble), and everything else needs an active transporter, as glucose and amino acids have. The barrier protects the brain from toxins and from fluctuations in blood chemistry, and it is the central practical obstacle in neuropharmacology, since a drug that works beautifully in a dish is useless if it cannot get in. The classic solution is to give a precursor that can cross: dopamine cannot cross the barrier, but L-DOPA can, and is converted to dopamine inside, which is the basis of Parkinson's treatment. Note also that the barrier is deliberately weak in a few places, the area postrema among them, so that the brain can detect circulating toxins and trigger vomiting.

The cells: neurons and glia

A neuron is a cell specialised for receiving, integrating and transmitting electrochemical signals. Its parts, in signal order: dendrites, branching processes that receive input and are studded with dendritic spines where synapses land; the cell body or soma, containing the nucleus and the metabolic machinery, where inputs are summed; the axon hillock, the cone-shaped junction where the axon leaves the soma and where the decision to fire is taken; the axon, the long conducting process; and the terminal buttons at its branch tips, which release neurotransmitter into the synapse. Inside, the usual organelles plus two structural elements that matter: microtubules, which are the tracks along which materials are transported up and down the axon, and the mitochondria that supply the very large amounts of energy the neuron needs.

Neurons are classified by shape. Multipolar neurons have many dendrites and one axon and are the commonest type in the CNS. Bipolar neurons have one dendrite and one axon and are typical of sensory systems, for example in the retina. Unipolar neurons have a single process leaving the soma that then divides, and are typical of somatosensory afferents. An interneuron is defined by function, not shape: it has a short axon or none, and integrates activity within a single structure rather than transmitting between structures.

Glia outnumber neurons and are no longer thought of as passive packing. Four kinds. Oligodendrocytes myelinate axons in the CNS, each one wrapping segments of several axons. Schwann cells myelinate axons in the PNS, one cell per segment of one axon, and, crucially, they guide axonal regrowth after injury, which is a large part of why peripheral nerves regenerate and central ones largely do not. Astrocytes are the largest and most numerous: they hold the tissue together structurally, contact both neurons and blood vessels and so participate in the blood-brain barrier and in delivering nutrients, take up and buffer extracellular potassium and transmitters (an active role in signalling, not merely housekeeping), and can trigger blood-flow changes. Microglia are the immune cells of the CNS: small, mobile, they proliferate in response to injury and infection and phagocytose debris, and they also prune synapses during development.

Myelin is worth pausing on because it is structural and functional at once. It is a fatty insulating sheath laid in segments, with small uninsulated gaps between segments called nodes of Ranvier. Insulation prevents current leaking out along the axon, and the exposed nodes are where the signal is regenerated, which produces the fast, jumping conduction described in the next chapter.

Seeing the structures: neuroanatomical techniques

Neural tissue is uniformly translucent, so anatomy depends entirely on stains that make some things visible and everything else invisible. The Golgi stain, silver-based, is capricious in a useful way: it fills a small random subset of neurons completely and blackly, leaving the rest unstained, so you can see the entire shape of individual cells including their processes. The Nissl stain, such as cresyl violet, binds material in cell bodies only, so it stains no processes but does let you count cell bodies and see how their density varies, which is how cortical layers and nuclear boundaries were mapped. Electron microscopy resolves fine structure, including the synapse itself, at the cost of only seeing a tiny field. And tract tracing establishes connections rather than shapes: anterograde tracers are taken up by cell bodies and carried forward to the terminals, revealing where a structure projects to; retrograde tracers are taken up by terminals and carried back to the cell bodies, revealing where a structure receives from.

The spinal cord

In cross-section the cord shows an inner butterfly of grey matter (cell bodies) surrounded by white matter (myelinated axons, white because myelin is fatty). The grey matter's dorsal arms are the dorsal horns and its ventral arms the ventral horns. Sensory afferents enter by the dorsal roots, and their cell bodies lie outside the cord in the dorsal root ganglia. Motor efferents leave by the ventral roots, and their cell bodies lie inside, in the ventral horns. This dorsal-in, ventral-out arrangement is the anatomical fact behind the spinal reflex and behind the localisation of spinal injuries.

The five divisions of the brain

The brain develops from a tube with three swellings, and the adult divisions are just those swellings subdivided. Learning them in developmental order makes the list stop being arbitrary. From the tail forward:

1. Myelencephalon (medulla). The most posterior division, essentially a continuation of the cord, and largely composed of tracts passing through. It contains the nuclei that regulate cardiovascular function, respiration and vomiting, which is why medullary damage is rapidly fatal. Running through it and up into the higher brain stem is the reticular formation, a complex net of about a hundred small nuclei; it is involved in arousal and sleep, attention, movement, and the cardiac and respiratory reflexes. Because it does many separable things, the old label "reticular activating system" is an oversimplification.

2. Metencephalon. Contains the pons ventrally, a bulge of ascending and descending tracts plus part of the reticular formation, and the cerebellum dorsally. The cerebellum is a convoluted structure holding a large proportion of all the brain's neurons; damage to it does not abolish movement but destroys its precision and timing, impairs balance, and disrupts adaptation of movement to changing conditions, along with certain kinds of learning. Note the pattern: it is a coordinator, not an initiator.

3. Mesencephalon (midbrain). Two divisions. The tectum (roof) is the dorsal surface and consists of the superior colliculi, which have a visual function, and the inferior colliculi, which have an auditory one; together they are the colliculi, and in mammals they serve orienting responses to visual and auditory stimuli. The tegmentum (floor) lies ventral to it and contains three structures worth naming: the periaqueductal grey, which surrounds the cerebral aqueduct and mediates the analgesic effects of opioids, the substantia nigra, and the red nucleus, both components of the sensorimotor system. The substantia nigra is the origin of the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway whose degeneration causes Parkinson's disease.

4. Diencephalon. Two structures. The thalamus is the large two-lobed relay sitting at the top of the brain stem, joined across the midline in many brains by the massa intermedia. It is a collection of distinct nuclei, and the important principle is that most sensory information reaching cortex is relayed through a specific thalamic sensory relay nucleus: the lateral geniculate nuclei for vision, the medial geniculate nuclei for audition, and the ventral posterior nuclei for touch. Relay is not passive; these nuclei receive as much input back from cortex as they send to it. The hypothalamus sits beneath the thalamus and is small but disproportionately important: it regulates the motivated behaviours (eating, drinking, sleeping, temperature, sexual behaviour) and it controls the pituitary gland, which hangs from it, thereby controlling the endocrine system. Landmarks on its underside include the optic chiasm, where the optic nerves cross (specifically, the fibres from the medial retina cross, which is what makes the crossing partial, or decussating), and the mammillary bodies at its posterior, which are among the structures damaged in Korsakoff's syndrome.

5. Telencephalon. The largest division, and the one that expanded most in evolution. It comprises the cerebral cortex, the limbic system and the basal ganglia, and it initiates voluntary movement, interprets sensory input, and supports learning, memory, speaking and problem solving.

Cortex, limbic system and basal ganglia

The cerebral cortex is a thin sheet of grey matter, only a few millimetres deep, whose area is greatly expanded by folding. Large folds are fissures, small ones sulci, and the ridges between them are gyri. The landmarks to know: the longitudinal fissure separates the two hemispheres, which are connected by cerebral commissures, the largest being the corpus callosum; the central fissure and the lateral fissure divide each hemisphere into its four lobes, frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital. Four gyri are worth naming because they are functionally defined: the precentral gyrus just anterior to the central fissure is primary motor cortex, the postcentral gyrus just posterior to it is primary somatosensory cortex, the superior temporal gyrus contains auditory cortex, and the cingulate gyrus arcs above the corpus callosum on the medial surface.

Most cortex is neocortex, six-layered, and its two principal cell types are pyramidal cells, large multipolar neurons with a triangular cell body, a long apical dendrite, and an axon that leaves the cortex (these are the output cells), and stellate cells, small star-shaped interneurons with short axons that stay local. The layering has a logic: layer IV is where sensory input from the thalamus arrives and is thickest in sensory cortex, while layer V contains the largest pyramidal output cells and is thickest in motor cortex. Alongside the layers runs a columnar organisation, cells in a vertical column tending to respond to the same thing. A minority of cortex is older and simpler: the hippocampus, on the medial temporal lobe, has three major layers rather than six.

The limbic system is a circuit around the margin (the limbus) of the cerebral hemispheres, classically credited to James Papez, and traditionally associated with the four Fs of motivated behaviour and with emotion and memory. Trace the circuit as a ring: amygdala (anterior temporal lobe, in front of the hippocampus), then hippocampus, then the fornix, the arching tract that leaves the hippocampus and runs to the mammillary bodies and septum, then the cingulate cortex, and back round. The functional split now recognised is that the amygdala is the emotional structure, especially for fear, while the hippocampus is the memory structure.

The basal ganglia are a set of subcortical nuclei for movement, and their naming is the part that trips people. The caudate is a long tail-like structure; the putamen lies beside it; together caudate plus putamen are the striatum, so called because the fibre bundles crossing between them give a striped appearance. Medial to the putamen is the globus pallidus, the pale globe. The nucleus accumbens, in the ventral striatum, is the structure central to reward and addiction (Chapter 15). And the pathway to remember is the nigrostriatal pathway, dopaminergic fibres running from the substantia nigra of the midbrain to the striatum, whose degeneration produces Parkinson's disease. The amygdala is usually listed with the basal ganglia anatomically and with the limbic system functionally, which is why you meet it in both lists.

Trap

Striatum = caudate + putamen, not including globus pallidus. Tectum is the midbrain roof (colliculi, dorsal), tegmentum the midbrain floor (periaqueductal grey, substantia nigra, red nucleus, ventral). Superior colliculi are visual, inferior colliculi auditory. Lateral geniculate = vision, medial geniculate = audition, ventral posterior = somatosensation. Oligodendrocytes myelinate in the CNS, Schwann cells in the PNS.

Worked example

A patient has a resting tremor, rigidity and difficulty initiating movement, but normal sensation and normal memory. Reason from the anatomy. Initiation of movement plus tremor plus rigidity, with sensation intact, points to the motor loop rather than to sensory cortex or cerebellum (cerebellar damage gives ataxia and intention tremor during movement, not a tremor at rest). The basal ganglia are the movement-initiation structures, and the input that drives the striatum is dopaminergic and comes from the substantia nigra of the midbrain tegmentum. So the lesion is degeneration of the nigrostriatal pathway, which is Parkinson's disease. Now predict the treatment from Chapter 3's other section: dopamine itself cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, so you give its precursor L-DOPA, which has a transporter, and let the brain convert it.
