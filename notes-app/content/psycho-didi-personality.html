What this covers. The major approaches to personality — psychoanalytic/psychodynamic (Freud), the neo-Freudians and analytical psychology (Jung, Horney, Adler, Fromm), humanistic (Rogers), existential and transpersonal approaches (Heidegger, Rollo May, Wilber), and Murray & Morgan's personology — followed by the theories of motivation (instinct, arousal, drive-reduction, and the need theories of Maslow, Alderfer, McClelland, Deci & Ryan, and Zuckerman).

1 · Psychoanalytic & Psychodynamic (Freud)

Theory → Theorist. Psychosexual / psychodynamic personality theory → Sigmund Freud.

Freud — the psychodynamic model

Freud used a topographical model of the mind and held that behaviour springs from innate instincts and unconscious conflict.

Life and death instincts — both are innate:

  • Life instinct (eros) — libidinal energy that pushes us to function; survival-related instincts.
  • Death instinct (thanatos) — instincts to hurt; destructive energy.

The Id, Ego and Superego

StructurePrincipleDevelops in
IdPleasure principle — seeks immediate gratificationOral phase
EgoReality principleAnal phase
SuperegoMorality principlePhallic stage

The superego consists of two parts:

  • Ego ideal — rewards ideal behaviour.
  • Conscience — punishes with guilt.

Three types of anxiety (Freud)

AnxietyNatureDescription
Realistic anxietyRealistic fearThe fear of an actual item.
Moral anxietySocial fearFear of punishment; represented by shame and guilt.
Neurotic anxietyNervous fearThe fear of being overwhelmed; feeling that you are about to lose it; fear of the id taking over.

Defence mechanisms

  • Repression — keeping unpleasant memories or thoughts buried in the unconscious. Primal: innately part of the id. Proper: unacceptable derivatives of original id impulses.
  • Denial — refusing to recognize an unpleasant reality.
  • Rationalization — creating a socially acceptable excuse to justify unacceptable behaviour.
  • Reaction formation — not acknowledging unacceptable impulses and over-emphasizing their opposite.
  • Projection — transferring one's unacceptable qualities or impulses to others.
  • Displacement — diverting one's impulses to a more acceptable target.
  • Sublimation — channelling socially unacceptable impulses into acceptable activities.
  • Regression — reverting to immature ways of responding.
  • Identification — enhancing self-esteem by imagining or forming alliances with others.
  • Intellectualization — ignoring troubling emotional aspects by focusing on abstract ideas or thoughts.
  • Suppression — actively choosing to ignore something to do what is better and more important at the moment.
Key. Id = pleasure principle (oral); Ego = reality principle (anal); Superego = morality principle (phallic), made of the ego ideal (rewards) and conscience (guilt). Repression is the "primal" defence; suppression is conscious, repression is unconscious.

2 · Neo-Freudian & Analytical Psychology

Jung — Analytical Psychology

Theory → Theorist. Analytical psychology → Carl Jung.
Point of differenceFreudJung
Role of libidoSexual energyBroad psychic energy
Forces that influence personalityPresent in the past onlyPresent in the past as well as the future
The unconsciousPersonal unconsciousPersonal and collective unconscious

Structures of the psyche:

  • Ego — is in control of itself; the conscious mind.
  • Personal unconscious — accessible once in a while; personal memories that have lost their intensity.
  • Collective unconscious — shared memories (patterns of behaviour), motifs, primordial patterns. Contains complexes, formed if a collective unconscious element completely takes over our personality.

Energy principles:

  • The principle of opposites — every wish or feeling has its opposite.
  • The principle of equivalence — there is a constant amount of energy in our psyche.
  • The principle of entropy — redistribution of energy within our psyche.

Four functions: thinking, feeling, sensing, intuiting.

Psychological types — attitudes: introversion / extraversion; rational / irrational.

Archetypes and the anima/animus:

  • Anima — the female archetype in the male psyche.
  • Animus — the male archetype in the female psyche.
  • Syzygy — anima and animus together.
  • Self — finding a balance between the different aspects of ourselves.
  • Persona — a mask, a facade to meet society's demands; a compromise between your needs and society's demands.
  • Shadow — deepest, darkest instincts; comparable to the id.
  • Other major archetypes: Joker, Wise Old Man, Hero, Witch, Villain.

Horney

Karen Horney gave the concept of womb envy. When children are born they have basic anxiety; if they are met with love and care this anxiety subsides and a healthy personality develops. In the neurotic personality there is a huge difference between the ideal and the real self, because of a rigid ideal self.

Neurotic needs — three orientations:

OrientationNormalNeuroticNeeds
Towards peopleFriendlyCompliantSeeks acceptance and help all the time; need for affection and approval; need for a partner who will control their life.
Against peopleCompetitiveAggressiveNeed for power, prestige, personal achievement; need to exploit other people; need to compete all the time.
Away from peopleAutonomousDetachedNeed to move away from people; independence; self-reliance; perfection; narrow limits.

Adler — Individual Psychology

Theory → Theorist. Individual psychology → Alfred Adler.

Adler's approach is future-oriented, non-deterministic and idiographic.

  • Fictional finalism — dreams that may not come true and are fictional, but motivate the child to do well in life.
  • Masculine protest — when female children feel male children within the family are being treated better, they behave more like boys as a protest.
  • Organ inferiority — experienced by each child when they are young.

Types of inferiority:

TypeDescription
Primary inferiorityThe "original and normal feeling" of inferiority maintained by an infant. This feeling is productive, as it provides motivation for the child to develop.
Secondary inferiorityThe inferiority feeling in the adult that results when the child develops an exaggerated feeling of inferiority. These feelings in the adult are what are harmful, and they comprise the inferiority complex.

Adler's interpretation of birth order:

PositionTypical characteristics
First bornPerfectionist, achiever, leader, bossy, responsible, motivated, conscientious, controlling, cautious, reliable.
Middle bornAdaptable, independent, go-between, people pleaser, can be rebellious, feels left out, peacemaker, social.
Last bornSocial, charming, outgoing, uncomplicated, manipulative, seeks attention, self-centred, fun, confident.
The only childConscientious, responsible, perfectionist, centre of attention, mature for their age, seek approval, sensitive, leader.

Three types of problems: problems of occupation; problems of love; problems involving our behaviour towards others.

Four styles of life: the dominant type; the getting type; the avoiding type; the socially useful type.

Erich Fromm

Erich Fromm was a Neo-Freudian. He identified two fundamental needs: freedom and belonging.

Character orientations:

TypeDescription
ProductiveNurturing, loving, meaningful; a good spouse and friend; the only healthy approach.
ExploitativeDeceives; manipulative; disregard for others' well-being.
MarketingRelationship is considered a transactional exchange; shallow; opportunistic.
ReceptiveCompliant; dependent.
HoardingPossession.

Needs:

  • Relatedness — connection.
  • Transcendence — meaning.
  • Rootedness — belonging to a safe space; if too much, causes inflexibility.
Don't confuse — Freud vs Jung vs Adler. Freud: libido = sexual energy, personality shaped by the past, only a personal unconscious. Jung: libido = broad psychic energy, past and future, personal and collective unconscious (archetypes). Adler: future-oriented, striving from inferiority, birth order and social interest, non-deterministic and idiographic. Horney = womb envy + three interpersonal orientations; Fromm = freedom vs belonging + five character types.

3 · Humanistic Approach (Rogers)

Theory → Theorist. Person-centred / self theory → Carl Rogers.

Humanists hold that humans want to reach their highest potential. Actualization is the process of becoming a fully functioning individual.

Rogers

Phenomenology argues that the only reality of which we can be sure is our own subjective world of experience — our inner perception of reality. The phenomenal field is the accumulation of a person's experiences.

Research technique — Q-sort (mixed method):

  • Qualitative: ideal self, through preference arrangement.
  • Quantitative: factor analysis.

The Fully Functioning Person: open to experience; existential living; trusts feelings; creativity and spontaneity; a fulfilled life; freedom of choice; a continual need to grow.

Congruence vs incongruence — the degree of overlap between the self-concept and experience: congruence = they match; incongruence = a gap between them.

Conditional vs unconditional positive regard:

ConditionalUnconditional
Having conditions that need to be met in order to receive love.Love without any condition.
An effort to manipulate the actions of individuals.No effort to manipulate or control an individual.
Child develops conditions of worth.Congruent personality; child develops the organismic valuing process; does not need therapy.
Key. Unconditional positive regard → organismic valuing process → congruent, healthy personality. Conditional regard → conditions of worth → incongruence.

4 · Existential & Transpersonal Approaches

Existential approach

Proponents: Rollo May, Viktor Frankl, Martin Heidegger. Meaning in life is what keeps you grounded. Dasein = "to be in the world".

Heidegger

Martin Heidegger's modes of being-in-the-world:

ModeMeaning
UmweltOne's relationship with the natural world or environment.
MitweltOur relationship with other people and the meanings given to those relationships.
EigenweltOne's relationship with oneself and how we interpret things based on self.
UberweltSpirituality.

Existential anxiety — the fear that we won't be alive. It can motivate us to achieve our best potential (proportionate), or demotivate us so that we wish to give up, thinking nothing really matters (neurotic).

Rollo May

Types of anxiety: Normal; Neurotic (disproportionate to the situation).

Four stages of development:

  • Innocence — physical and psychological dependence.
  • Rebellion — people define themselves in opposition to or defiance of another.
  • Ordinary — the normal adult ego; a healthy, mature personality.
  • Creative — transcending our usual limited viewpoint and catching a glimpse of ultimate reality.

Transpersonal psychology

Founded by Abraham Maslow and Stanislav Grof; it also has roots in earlier work by psychologists such as William James and Carl Jung.

Methods: psychedelic; transpersonal therapy; dreamwork; mindfulness.

Altered state of consciousness: connectedness beyond ego boundaries; altruism, creativity, intuitive wisdom; a journey into self-exploration.

Groups: Personal (concerns of the ego); Transpersonal (concerns beyond the ego). Division — Ego: the central organizer of behaviour.

Ken Wilber's transpersonal stages of development:

  • Pre-personal — when you don't understand self fully.
  • Vision logic — integration of mind and body.
  • Psychic — empathetic understanding.
  • Subtle — across archetypal forms.
  • Causal — non-dual awareness.
  • Final — freely transverse between all stages.

5 · Personology (Murray & Morgan)

Theory → Theorist. Personology → Henry Murray & Christina Morgan.

Principles

  1. Personality is rooted in the brain.
  2. The process of tension reduction is satisfying, but a tension-free state is a source of distress.
  3. An individual's personality develops over time.
  4. Personality is not fixed or static.
  5. Each person is unique.

Structures: Id — innate impulses that society considers unacceptable; Superego — internalization of cultural values.

Needs

CategoryDescription
PrimarySurvival-related.
SecondaryEmotional or psychological.
ProactiveSpontaneous; achievement-related.
ReactiveResponse to object; harm-avoidance.

Types of needs: materialistic, affiliation, information, power, achievement, sensual.

Press, thema and complexes

  • Press — the influence of environment and past events on the current activation of a need.
  • Thema — need × press + how they affect personality = unity thema. Subsidation: one need will activate another, and the second need will fulfil the first need.

Complexes:

ComplexDescription
Claustral (womb)The desire to reinstate the conditions experienced in the mother's womb.
OralSame as Freud's.
AnalSame as Freud's.
UrethralCharacterized by excessive ambition and a distorted, enlarged sense of self-esteem.
GenitalCastration complex.

6 · Theories of Motivation & Emotion

Instinct theory

Behaviour is motivated by instincts that are inborn and that are activated by environmental stimuli.

  • Freud's object relations theory — basic instincts from eros motivate us; explains motivation in terms of primary needs.
  • Homeostatic approach — disturbance of homeostasis creates drive.

Instincts have four basic characteristics (S-A-I-O):

  • Source — source of deficit (internal).
  • Aim — what we want to do.
  • Impetus — the drive that propels us to act.
  • Object — that through which the instinct achieves its aim.

Arousal theory

Behaviour is motivated by the need to achieve optimum levels of arousal.

Yerkes-Dodson law — arousal increases performance up to a point, after which additional arousal impedes performance.

Alderfer's ERG theory

One does not necessarily start from the lowest level.

  • Moving from a lower level to an upper level of the hierarchy is called succession progression.
  • Moving from a higher level to a lower level is called frustration regression.

Maslow's need hierarchy

Theory → Theorist. Hierarchy of needs → Abraham Maslow.
  • Lower needs come from deficiencies; higher needs come from satisfaction.
  • Measured with the Personal Orientation Inventory.
  • Progression is only upward, but not automatic.
  • Jonah complex — fear of your own best potential.

Self-determination theory

Three basic needs:

  • Autonomy — the need to control the course of our lives.
  • Competence — the need to be effective in dealing with the environment.
  • Relatedness — the need to have close, affectionate relationships with others.

Causality orientations:

Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull)

Theory → Theorist. Drive-reduction theory → Clark Hull.

A homeostatic approach. A bodily need (e.g. hydration) creates a drive; the organism is motivated to satisfy the drive through goal-directed behaviour, restoring homeostasis (drive reduced, need satisfied).

McClelland's theory of needs

Theory → Theorist. Need for achievement / acquired-needs theory → David McClelland.

All three needs are learned: Power (nPow), Achievement (nAch), Affiliation (nAff).

Zuckerman's sensation-seeking theory

Theory → Theorist. Sensation-seeking theory → Marvin Zuckerman.

Four types:

  • Thrill and adventure seeking
  • Experience seeking
  • Disinhibition
  • Boredom susceptibility
Key. Need theories to keep straight: Maslow (hierarchy, deficiency vs growth needs), Alderfer (ERG — succession progression vs frustration regression), McClelland (learned nAch/nPow/nAff), Deci & Ryan (self-determination — autonomy, competence, relatedness). Hull's drive-reduction is the homeostatic model; Yerkes-Dodson governs the optimum-arousal model.
Don't confuse — the need theorists. Maslow = a fixed upward hierarchy driven by deficiency then growth; Alderfer's ERG = flexible, allows frustration regression; McClelland = three learned needs measured by projective tests, no hierarchy. And in McClelland's model, high-nAch people take internal credit for success while low-nAch people show an external locus for success and an internal one for failure.
