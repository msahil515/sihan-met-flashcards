1. The official syllabus (all 10 units)

This is the University Grants Commission (UGC) NET Bureau syllabus for Psychology, Code No. 04, reproduced in full. The paper draws its questions from these ten units. Topics are listed exactly as in the official syllabus.

High-yield

Every NET Psychology question maps to one of these ten units. Knowing the unit a question belongs to is half the battle: it tells you which body of theory the examiner is testing. Units 1 (Emergence/systems), 2 (Methodology & Statistics) and 3 (Testing) are disproportionately heavy in the papers in this book.

Unit 1. Emergence of Psychology

  • Eastern systems: Psychological thought in major Eastern systems — Bhagavad Gita, Buddhism, Sufism and Integral Yoga.
  • Academic psychology in India: Pre-independence era; post-independence era; 1970s (the move to addressing social issues); 1980s (Indigenization); 1990s (paradigmatic concerns, disciplinary identity crisis); 2000s (emergence of Indian psychology in academia). Issues: the colonial encounter; post-colonialism and psychology; lack of distinct disciplinary identity.
  • Western: Greek heritage, medieval period and modern period. Structuralism, Functionalism, Psychoanalytical, Gestalt, Behaviorism, Humanistic-Existential, Transpersonal, Cognitive revolution, Multiculturalism. Four founding paths of academic psychology — Wundt, Freud, James, Dilthey. Issues: crisis in psychology due to strict adherence to the experimental-analytical paradigm (logical empiricism); Indic influences on modern psychology.
  • Knowledge paradigms: Essential aspects — ontology, epistemology, and methodology. Paradigms of Western Psychology: Positivism, Post-Positivism, Critical perspective, Social Constructionism, Existential Phenomenology, and Co-operative Enquiry. Paradigmatic controversies. Significant Indian paradigms on psychological knowledge: Yoga, Bhagavad Gita, Buddhism, Sufism, and Integral Yoga. Science and spirituality (avidya and vidya). The primacy of self-knowledge in Indian psychology.

Unit 2. Research Methodology and Statistics

  • Research: Meaning, purpose, and dimensions. Research problems, variables and operational definitions, hypothesis, sampling. Ethics in conducting and reporting research.
  • Paradigms of research: Quantitative, Qualitative, Mixed methods approach.
  • Methods of research: Observation, Survey (interview, questionnaires), Experimental, Quasi-experimental, Field studies, Cross-cultural studies, Phenomenology, Grounded theory, Focus groups, Narratives, Case studies, Ethnography.
  • Statistics: Measures of central tendency and dispersion. Normal probability curve. Parametric (t-test) and non-parametric tests (Sign test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Mann-Whitney test, Kruskal-Wallis test, Friedman). Power analysis. Effect size.
  • Correlational analysis: Correlation (Product Moment, Rank Order), partial correlation, multiple correlation. Special correlation methods: Biserial, Point-biserial, tetrachoric, phi coefficient. Regression: simple linear regression, multiple regression. Factor analysis: assumptions, methods, rotation and interpretation.
  • Experimental designs: ANOVA (one-way, factorial), Randomized Block Designs, Repeated Measures Design, Latin Square, Cohort studies, Time series, MANOVA, ANCOVA. Single-subject designs.

Unit 3. Psychological Testing

  • Types of tests.
  • Test construction: Item writing, item analysis.
  • Test standardization: Reliability, validity and norms.
  • Areas of testing: Intelligence, creativity, neuropsychological tests, aptitude, personality assessment, interest inventories.
  • Attitude scales — Semantic differential, Stapel, Likert scale.
  • Computer-based psychological testing.
  • Applications of psychological testing across settings: Clinical, Organizational and business, Education, Counseling, Military, Career guidance.

Unit 4. Biological Basis of Behavior

  • Sensory systems: general and specific sensations, receptors and processes.
  • Neurons: structure, functions, types, neural impulse, synaptic transmission. Neurotransmitters.
  • The Central and Peripheral Nervous Systems — structure and functions. Neuroplasticity.
  • Methods of Physiological Psychology: Invasive methods (anatomical methods, degeneration techniques, lesion techniques, chemical methods, microelectrode studies); Non-invasive methods (EEG, scanning methods).
  • Muscular and glandular system: types and functions.
  • Biological basis of motivation: hunger, thirst, sleep and sex.
  • Biological basis of emotion: the limbic system, hormonal regulation of behavior.
  • Genetics and behavior: chromosomal anomalies; nature-nurture controversy (twin studies and adoption studies).

Unit 5. Attention, Perception, Learning, Memory and Forgetting

  • Attention: Forms of attention, models of attention.
  • Perception: Approaches to the study of perception (Gestalt and physiological). Perceptual organization (Gestalt, figure and ground, laws of organization). Perceptual constancy (size, shape, colour); illusions. Perception of form, depth and movement. Role of motivation and learning in perception. Signal detection theory: assumptions and applications. Subliminal perception and related factors, information processing approach, culture and perception, perceptual styles, pattern recognition, ecological perspective on perception.
  • Learning process: Fundamental theories (Thorndike, Guthrie, Hull). Classical conditioning: procedure, phenomena, related issues. Instrumental learning: phenomena, paradigms, theoretical issues. Reinforcement: basic variables and schedules; behaviour modification and its applications. Cognitive approaches in learning: latent learning, observational learning. Verbal learning and discrimination learning. Recent trends: neurophysiology of learning.
  • Memory and forgetting: Memory processes (encoding, storage, retrieval). Stages of memory (sensory, short-term/working, long-term — declarative: episodic and semantic; procedural). Theories of forgetting (interference, retrieval failure, decay, motivated forgetting).

Unit 6. Thinking, Intelligence and Creativity

  • Theoretical perspectives on thought processes: Associationism, Gestalt, Information processing, Feature integration model.
  • Concept formation: rules, types, strategies; role of concepts in thinking. Types of reasoning. Language and thought.
  • Problem solving: types, strategies, obstacles. Decision-making: types and models.
  • Metacognition: metacognitive knowledge and metacognitive regulation.
  • Intelligence: Spearman; Thurstone; Jensen; Cattell; Gardner; Sternberg; Goleman; Das, Kar & Parrila.
  • Creativity: Torrance, Getzels & Jackson, Guilford, Wallach & Kogan. Relationship between intelligence and creativity.

Unit 7. Personality, Motivation, Emotion, Stress and Coping

  • Determinants of personality: biological and socio-cultural.
  • Approaches to personality: Psychoanalytical, Neo-Freudian, Social learning, Trait and Type, Cognitive, Humanistic, Existential, Transpersonal. Other theories: Rotter's Locus of Control, Seligman's explanatory styles, Kohlberg's theory of moral development.
  • Basic motivational concepts: instincts, needs, drives, arousal, incentives, motivational cycle. Approaches to motivation: Psychoanalytical, Ethological, S-R, Cognitive, Humanistic.
  • Exploratory behavior and curiosity. Zuckerman's sensation seeking. Achievement, affiliation and power. Motivational competence. Self-regulation. Flow.
  • Emotions: physiological correlates. Theories of emotion: James-Lange, Cannon-Bard, Schachter and Singer, Lazarus, Lindsley. Emotion regulation.
  • Conflicts: sources and types. Stress and coping: concept, models, Type A/B/C/D behaviors, stress-management strategies (Biofeedback, Music therapy, Breathing exercises, Progressive Muscular Relaxation, Guided Imagery, Mindfulness, Meditation, Yogasana, Stress Inoculation Training).

Unit 8. Social Psychology

  • Nature, scope and history of social psychology.
  • Traditional theoretical perspectives: Field theory, Cognitive Dissonance, Sociobiology, Psychodynamic approaches, Social Cognition.
  • Social perception (communication, attributions); attitude and its change within cultural context; prosocial behavior.
  • Group and social influence (Social Facilitation; Social Loafing). Social influence (Conformity, Peer Pressure, Persuasion, Compliance, Obedience, Social Power, Reactance). Aggression. Group dynamics, leadership style and effectiveness. Theories of intergroup relations (Minimal Group Experiment and Social Identity Theory, Relative Deprivation Theory, Realistic Conflict Theory, Balance Theories, Equity Theory, Social Exchange Theory).
  • Applied social psychology: Health, Environment and Law; personal space, crowding, and territoriality.

Unit 9. Human Development and Interventions

  • Developmental processes: nature, principles, factors, stages of development. Successful aging.
  • Theories of development: Psychoanalytical, Behavioristic, Cognitive. Various aspects of development: sensory-motor, cognitive, language, emotional, social and moral.
  • Psychopathology: concept, Mental Status Examination, classification, causes.
  • Psychotherapies: Psychoanalysis, Person-centered, Gestalt, Existential, Acceptance Commitment Therapy, Behavior therapy, REBT, CBT, MBCT, Play therapy, Positive psychotherapy, Transactional Analysis, Dialectical Behavior Therapy, Art therapy, Performing Art Therapy, Family therapy.
  • Applications of theories of motivation and learning in school. Factors in educational achievement. Teacher effectiveness. Guidance in schools: needs, organizational set-up and techniques.
  • Counselling: process, skills, and techniques.

Unit 10. Emerging Areas

  • Issues of gender, poverty, disability, and migration: cultural bias and discrimination. Stigma, marginalization, and social suffering; child abuse and domestic violence.
  • Peace psychology: violence, non-violence, conflict resolution at the macro level, role of media in conflict resolution.
  • Wellbeing and self-growth: types of wellbeing (Hedonic and Eudemonic), character strengths, resilience and post-traumatic growth.
  • Health: health-promoting and health-compromising behaviors, lifestyle and chronic diseases (Diabetes, Hypertension, Coronary Heart Disease), psychoneuroimmunology (Cancer, HIV/AIDS).
  • Psychology and technology interface: digital learning; digital etiquette; cyber bullying; cyber pornography (consumption, implications); parental mediation of digital usage.

2. How the exam works

UGC-NET (the National Eligibility Test) is conducted by the National Testing Agency (NTA) on behalf of the University Grants Commission. It determines eligibility for Assistant Professor only, or for Assistant Professor and Junior Research Fellowship (JRF) both, in colleges and universities. Below is the practical guidance distilled from the official NTA FAQ document.

Format and mode

  • Mode: Computer Based Test (CBT) only.
  • Sittings: Two shifts on exam day — roughly 09:00–12:00 and 15:00–18:00. The date and slot are allotted randomly by computer (needed for the normalization process); a candidate cannot choose them.
  • Two papers in one session: Paper 1 (general teaching/research aptitude, common to all subjects) and Paper 2 (the Psychology subject paper based on the 10-unit syllabus above). Both are attempted in the same sitting.
  • Subjects: NET is conducted in roughly 80+ subjects; Psychology is subject code 04.
High-yield

There is NO negative marking in UGC-NET. The official FAQ states plainly: "No. There is no negative marking for wrong answers." So you should attempt every question — leaving a question blank can only cost you, never protect you. (This is the opposite of many other entrance tests, so do not carry a "skip if unsure" habit into NET.)

Marking and result

  • Negative marking: None. Every question carries positive marks only; wrong answers are not penalised.
  • Qualifying criterion: Per UGC policy, the top 6% of candidates who appear in both papers and obtain the minimum qualifying marks (in aggregate of both papers) are declared NET-qualified.
  • No all-India rank: The FAQ states "Rank is not assigned in UGC-NET." Results are pass/qualify based, with JRF allocated subject-wise and category-wise.
  • Challenging the key: After the test, the question paper, recorded responses and the provisional answer key are released. Candidates may challenge an answer by paying a non-refundable fee (Rs 200 per question challenged) within the given window. The final result uses the answer key vetted by subject experts. No re-evaluation or re-checking is done after results.

Eligibility and age

  • Subject: Candidates should appear in the subject of their post-graduation. If their PG subject is not in the NET list, they may appear in a related subject.
  • JRF age limit: Not more than 31 years (as on the first day of the month in which the exam concludes), with relaxations for reserved categories. There is no upper age limit for the Assistant Professor eligibility.
  • Category concessions: Candidates in the non-creamy layer whose category is in the central OBC list, and SC/ST/PwD candidates, get fee and (where applicable) qualifying-mark concessions.

Fees (as per the cited cycle)

CategoryFee

Payment is online only (debit/credit card or net banking). Fees are non-refundable and applications cannot be withdrawn. Documents and most details can be edited later through the correction window.

Mnemonic

Remember the three numbers that define NET: 0 (zero negative marking), 6 (top 6% qualify), 2 (two papers in one session). Attempt everything, aim for the top slice, and budget time across both papers.

Trap

NET does not give you a "rank" or a "score out of 100" that ranks you nationally for admission the way a CET does. Qualifying is a percentile-style cut-off (top 6%). Do not confuse a NET qualification with an entrance-exam merit rank.

  1. In what sequence are the Social Combination rules followed for group success?

    • 1Additive, disjunctive, compensatory, conjunctive
    • 2Additive, compensatory, disjunctive, conjunctive
    • 3Disjunctive, compensatory, conjunctive, additive
    • 4Compensatory, conjunctive, disjunctive, additive
    ✓ Best answer: (2) Additive, compensatory, disjunctive, conjunctive
    Ivan Steiner, typology of group tasks (1972)
    The concept

    Why this oneThis is the standard order of Ivan Steiner's combination rules, from the most to the least group success: additive first, conjunctive last.

    What they areSteiner classified tasks by how members' inputs are combined. Additive tasks sum efforts (tug of war), so the group beats its best member; compensatory tasks average judgements, so the group beats most members.

    The other twoDisjunctive tasks need one correct answer, so the group can only equal its best member. Conjunctive tasks need everyone to finish, so the weakest member sets the pace, like a roped climbing team.

    Exam tipPooling (additive, compensatory) helps groups most; conjunctive tasks expose the weakest link. Steiner also named discretionary tasks, where the group picks its own rule.

    Not certainIf 'equal to the best member' (disjunctive) is ranked above 'better than most members' (compensatory), disjunctive moves to second place, which gives (1).

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (1) Additive, disjunctive, compensatory, conjunctiveSwaps compensatory and disjunctive; the standard sequence places compensatory (averaging judgements) second and disjunctive (relying on the best member) third.
    • (3) Disjunctive, compensatory, conjunctive, additiveReverses the scale by ending with additive; additive tasks, where every member's effort adds up, give the highest group output and come first.
    • (4) Compensatory, conjunctive, disjunctive, additiveEnds with additive and puts conjunctive second; additive tasks should head the list, and conjunctive tasks, limited by the weakest member, come last.

  3. Which of the following was described by R.B. Cattell using P-Technique of Factor Analysis?

    • 1Differential effects of stimulus situations on personality
    • 2Group formation
    • 3Social interactions
    • 4Individual differences among people
    ✓ Answer: (1) Differential effects of stimulus situations on personality
    Raymond Cattell, P-technique factor analysis
    The concept

    Why this oneP-technique factor-analyses many measures taken on one person across many occasions, so it shows how that person's personality shifts with changing situations.

    How it worksOrdinary R-technique correlates tests across many people, giving individual differences. Q-technique correlates people across tests to find types. P-technique stays inside one individual over time.

    Exam tipRaymond Cattell used P-technique to separate fluctuating states from stable traits. P = Person over occasions; R = many people; Q = people as variables.

    Old keyThis page used to show (2) here, from a key that does not line up with this paper. See below for why it is wrong.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (2) Group formationGroup formation is studied through sociometry and small-group research, not by factor-analysing one person's scores over time.
    • (3) Social interactionsPatterns of social interaction are mapped by observation methods such as Robert Bales' interaction process analysis, not by P-technique.
    • (4) Individual differences among peopleDifferences between people come from R-technique, which correlates traits across a large sample; P-technique deliberately studies a single person.

  4. Which of the following would be the best method for studying the relative contribution of heredity and environment?

    • 1Comparing identical and fraternal twins reared together
    • 2Comparing identical and fraternal twins reared apart
    • 3Comparing siblings reared together and reared apart
    • 4Comparing identical and fraternal twins, half of whom have been reared apart
    ✓ Answer: (4) Comparing identical and fraternal twins, half of whom have been reared apart
    Twin and adoption designs (behaviour genetics)
    The concept

    Why this oneUsing identical and fraternal twins, with half reared apart, varies genes and environment separately, so the effect of each can be pulled apart.

    How it worksIdentical (MZ) twins share all their genes, fraternal (DZ) twins about half. If MZ twins stay more alike than DZ twins even when reared apart, heredity matters; if twins reared together are more alike than those reared apart, shared environment matters.

    Exam tipThe fullest design has four groups: MZ together, MZ apart, DZ together, DZ apart. Each comparison isolates one source of similarity.

    Old keyThis page used to show (2) here, from a key that does not line up with this paper. See below for why it is wrong.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (1) Comparing identical and fraternal twins reared togetherTwins reared together share their home as well as their genes, so environment never varies; greater MZ similarity could partly reflect more similar treatment.
    • (2) Comparing identical and fraternal twins reared apartSeparating twins varies environment, but without a reared-together comparison you cannot gauge how much a shared upbringing adds.
    • (3) Comparing siblings reared together and reared apartOrdinary siblings all share about half their genes, so genetic similarity never varies and the role of heredity cannot be estimated.

  5. Which one of the following statements is true regarding the relationship of test length with reliability and validity?

    • 1As test length increases, only reliability improves.
    • 2As test length increases, only validity improves.
    • 3As test length increases, neither reliability nor validity improves.
    • 4As test length increases, both reliability and validity improve.
    ✓ Answer: (4) As test length increases, both reliability and validity improve.
    Spearman-Brown prophecy formula
    The concept

    Why this oneAdding more comparable items shrinks the share of random error, so reliability rises; because validity is limited by reliability, validity rises too, though more slowly.

    How it worksThe Spearman-Brown formula predicts the new reliability: rnew = nr / [1 + (n - 1)r], where n is how many times longer the test becomes.

    Exam tipA validity coefficient cannot exceed the square root of reliability, so a more reliable test has a higher validity ceiling. Gains level off as a test gets very long.

    Old keyThis page used to show (3) here, from a key that does not line up with this paper. See below for why it is wrong.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (1) As test length increases, only reliability improves.Validity also improves with length, because the ceiling on validity is set by reliability; reliability does not rise on its own.
    • (2) As test length increases, only validity improves.Reliability is the quantity most directly raised by lengthening (Spearman-Brown), so it cannot be that only validity improves.
    • (3) As test length increases, neither reliability nor validity improves.Lengthening with comparable items does improve both; only adding poor or unrelated items fails to help.

    • (b) well-being refers to "striving for perfection"
    • (c) subjective well-being focuses on bodily pleasures like focus on appetite and self-interests

  9. Which are the two basic features of Schema?

    • (a) Influencing memory
    • (b) Promoting forgetting
    • (c) Resisting change
    • (d) Promoting change

    Choose the correct option:

    • 1(a) and (b)
    • 2(a) and (c)
    • 3(a) and (d)
    • 4(b) and (c)
    ✓ Answer: (2) (a) and (c)
    Frederic Bartlett; Robert Baron and Donn Byrne, social cognition
    The concept

    Why this oneSchemas influence memory (what we notice, store and recall), and they resist change, even when evidence contradicts them.

    Shaping memoryFrederic Bartlett's 'War of the Ghosts' study showed people reshaping an unfamiliar story to fit their own cultural schemas when they recalled it.

    Resisting changeThis stubbornness is the perseverance effect: schemas and beliefs persist even after the information that built them has been discredited.

    Exam tipSchemas can also act as self-fulfilling prophecies, leading people to behave in ways that make the expectation come true.

    Why the other options are wrong

    • (3) (a) and (d)Schemas do the opposite of promoting change: they are stable and tend to persist against contradicting evidence.
    • (4) (b) and (c)Resisting change is right, but promoting forgetting is not a core feature; the other basic feature is their influence on memory.

  10. Which among the following are the levels of processing proposed by Craik and Lockhart?

    • 1Symbolic, Semantic and Acoustics
    • 2Physical, behavioral and Acoustics
    • 3Behavioral, Acoustics and Semantic
    • 4Physical, Acoustics and Semantic
    ✓ Answer: (4) Physical, Acoustics and Semantic
    Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart, levels of processing (1972)
    The concept

    Why this oneFergus Craik and Robert Lockhart described processing from shallow to deep: physical (structural, how a word looks), acoustic (phonemic, how it sounds) and semantic (what it means).

    ✓ Answer: (3) 0.875
    Spearman-Brown prophecy formula
    The concept

    Why this oneGoing from 25 to 75 similar items lengthens the test by a factor of n = 75 ÷ 25 = 3. The Spearman-Brown prophecy formula predicts the new reliability.

    Workingrnew = nr ÷ (1 + (n - 1)r) = (3 × 0.7) ÷ (1 + 2 × 0.7) = 2.1 ÷ 2.4 = 0.875.

    Exam tipLonger tests are more reliable, with diminishing returns, as long as the added items are parallel. Halving the test (n = 0.5) would drop 0.7 to 0.35 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 0.54.

    Why the other options are wrong

    • (2) 0.824This is what doubling gives: (2 × 0.7) ÷ (1 + 0.7) = 1.4 ÷ 1.7 ≈ 0.824. The test is tripled, not doubled.
    • (4) 0.931Overshoots: reaching 0.931 would need a test nearly six times as long (about 145 items); tripling gives 2.1 ÷ 2.4 = 0.875.

  98. Which one of the following correlations is most suitable for computing item-remainder correlations?

    • 1Tetrachoric Correlations
    • 2Phi Coefficient
    • 3Contingency Coefficient
    • 4Point biserial Correlation
    ✓ Answer: (4) Point biserial Correlation
    Point-biserial correlation in item analysis
    The concept

    Why this oneAn item-remainder correlation relates each item, scored 0/1 (a true dichotomy: right or wrong), with the total of the remaining items, a continuous score. Dichotomous with continuous → point-biserial r.

    Formularpb = ((Mp - Mq) ÷ st) × √(pq), where Mp and Mq are the mean remainder scores of those who passed and failed the item.

    Why 'remainder'The item is removed from the total first, so it is not correlated with itself. This stops the item-total correlation from being inflated, which matters most in short tests.

    Why the other options are wrong
