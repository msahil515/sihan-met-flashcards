1 · Development & Organization of the Nervous System

The Nervous System is divided into the Central Nervous System (CNS) and the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS).

  • Central Nervous System (CNS) — the Brain and the Spinal Cord.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) — includes the Autonomic Nervous System (with its Sympathetic and Parasympathetic divisions) and the Somatic Nervous System (with its Afferent and Efferent nervous systems).
  • Enteric Nervous System — controls the smooth muscle and glandular tissue in the digestive system.

Development from the neural tube

The brain develops from the neural tube, forming the following divisions:

Forebrain (Prosencephalon)TelencephalonCerebral hemispheres / four lobes
DiencephalonThalamus, hypothalamus, epithalamus

Don't confuse. Afferent nerves carry sensory information toward the CNS; Efferent nerves carry motor commands away from the CNS. Similarly, the Sympathetic system drives "fight or flight" while the Parasympathetic system drives "rest and digest."

4 · The Basal Ganglia

The Basal Ganglia handle motor coordination and inhibition; they prevent unnecessary motor movements and activity in the white matter of the frontal lobe. Dysfunction: tremors, involuntary muscle movements, abnormal increase in tone, difficulty initiating movements, and abnormal posture.

Components of the basal ganglia: caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, substantia nigra, nucleus accumbens, and subthalamic nuclei.

10 · Techniques to Study the Brain

History

  • Trephination: creating a hole in the skull or nail to relieve pressure or treat a health condition.
  • Autopsy: a medical examination of a body after death.
  • Ablation: complete removal of a part of the brain (mostly for treatment).
  • Gene knockout: use of genetic engineering to inactivate or remove one or more specific genes from an organism.

Imaging techniques

  • Computed Axial Tomography (CAT): introduced in 1960; 360-degree X-ray; 3D/2D structural image.
  • EEG: electrical activity.
  • EMG: magnetic activity.
  • MEG: muscle tension.
  • EOG: sleep studies, eye movement.
  • PET: introduced in 1973; functional image of the brain (2D); fluorodeoxyglucose injected in carotid artery.
  • MRI: traces radioactive field of hydrogen present in brain; 3D image of structure; high spatial resolution.
  • fMRI: traces BOLD (Blood Oxygen Level Dependent) signal; gives structural as well as functional image. BOLD contrast: checks difference between active and inactive areas.
  • Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS): non-invasive; uses magnetic pulses to stimulate the brain and treat conditions like depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder.
  • Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT): 2D structural, 3D functional image; location of seizures can be checked.
  • Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS): optical imaging technique; light measures blood flow changes; good temporal resolution.
  • Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI): better spatial resolution than MRI.
Note the labels as given. This source lists EMG as "magnetic activity," MEG as "muscle tension," and MRI as tracing a "radioactive field of hydrogen." These are the definitions exactly as printed in the source.

Surgical & stimulation techniques

  • Stereotaxic surgery: uses an atlas and an instrument; useful for lesion and surgery; invasive.
  • Optogenetic stimulation: a genetic technique that enables scientists to activate or inhibit the activity of specific neuron populations using light.
  • Neurosurgery: done for treatment.

Lesions (controlled destruction)

  • Aspiration lesion: for suction action; only for shallow areas and cerebral cortex.
  • Knife cut lesion: severing, tissue sectioning (for biopsy).
  • Reversible lesion: functional lesion; cryogenic (put freezing agents); uses a cryoprobe (cryogenic blockade).
  • Chemical lesion: excitotoxic lesions performed through stereotaxis — kainic acid (glutamate neuron), ibotenic acid, 6-hydroxy-dopamine (monoamine).
  • Radiofrequency lesion: bilateral lesions show better effect than unilateral.

11 · Dysfunctions of the Brain

Lateralization and Localization

Specific parts of the brain control specific functions.

Who → what. Franz Gall and Johann Casper — studies of localization. Sperry and Gazzaniga — the Split Brain Experiment: severing the corpus callosum leads to the binding problem. Karl Lashley — controlled lesioning; concept of equipotentiality: each area of the brain has an equal role in each function.

Lateralization = functional dominance of one hemisphere over the other.

Lobes

Occipital

  • Cortical blindness — people confabulate because they can't see.
  • Anton syndrome: lack of insight
  • Scotoma
  • Nystagmus
  • Agnosia

  • Recent memory impairments
  • Reduced concentration
  • Behaviour disorder

  • Lack of inhibition
  • Flat affect
  • Contralateral plegia
  • Paresis
  • Dysfunctional executive functioning
  • Decreased fluency of speech
  • Apathy

Parietal

  • Hemineglect / Contralateral / Unilateral (in extreme cases, person forgets half of his/her body)
  • Anosognia: not aware of deficit (hemineglect)
  • Apraxia: unable to remember motor sequence
  • Decreased proprioception
  • Balint's syndrome: simultagnosia, ocular apraxia, optic ataxia

Temporal

  • Decreased auditory processing
  • Amnesia

Hindbrain

Hypothalamus

Based on structure:

  • Glucocorticoids — immunosuppression (dementia, PTSD)

14 · Glial Cells

CNS glial cells

  • Astrocytes: maintain the structure of the cell; control concentration of neurotransmitters; maintain blood brain barrier
  • Ependymal cells: produce CSF
  • Oligodendrocytes: myelinate neurons of the CNS
  • Microglia: brain's immune cell; soldiers of the nervous system

PNS glial cells

  • Satellite cells: similar function to astrocytes, but in the PNS
  • Schwann cells: myelinate neurons of the PNS
Don't confuse. Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS neurons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS neurons.

Stress axes

HPA Axis

  • CRF → Anterior Pituitary
  • ACTH → Adrenal cortex
  • Cortisol → metabolism increase; insulin resistance

SAM pathway

  • Hypothalamus → SNS → Adrenal Medulla → Epinephrine and Norepinephrine

Don't confuse. Sympathetic = Thoracic and Lumbar nerves, "fight or flight," pupils dilate, heart rate up, digestion inhibited, GSR increases. Parasympathetic = Cranial and Sacral nerves, "rest and digest," slows heartbeat, stimulates saliva, GSR decreases.
Mechanism. In sympathetic arousal, galvanic skin response increases because sweat gland activity rises, which decreases skin resistance. Under parasympathetic dominance, GSR decreases as skin resistance increases.
