Effects, Phenomena, Theories & Originators

One consolidated lookup for every named effect, phenomenon, illusion, theory, complex and the people behind them. The five old sweeps overlapped heavily (an "effect" carries an originator; a theory carries a theorist), so each item now lives in exactly one entry: the definition, who coined it, the classic study, and the confusable lookalike, together. Drill it both directions: cover the "Who" column and name the person; cover the description and name the term.

High-yield

NIMHANS / MET ask this two ways. (1) Name the "____ effect / theory / complex" and ask who proposed it (answer = a person). (2) Describe a scene/finding with no name and ask you to name it (answer = the effect/theory). Almost every trap is a same-family lookalike (halo↔horn, Pygmalion↔Golem, placebo↔nocebo, James-Lange↔Cannon-Bard, McCall's T↔Student's t). Always learn each item with its twin.

1. Named effects

Every named "____ effect," its originator, and the one-line hook. Grouped by domain so the good-twin / bad-twin pairs sit together.

1.1 Memory & learning effects

EffectWhoWhat it is
Zeigarnik effectBluma ZeigarnikInterrupted / unfinished tasks are remembered better than completed ones.
Von Restorff (isolation) effectHedwig von RestorffThe odd-one-out item in a list stands out and is recalled best.
Serial position effectEbbinghaus (named by Murdock)First + last items in a list recalled best (= primacy + recency together).
Primacy effect—First items recalled well (rehearsed into long-term store).
Recency effect—Last items recalled well (still in short-term store). A delay/distractor kills recency, not primacy.
Spacing (distributed practice) effectEbbinghausSpread-out study beats cramming for long-term retention.
Testing effect—Retrieving (self-testing) cements memory better than re-reading.
Generation effectSlamecka & GrafInfo you generate yourself is remembered better than info you just read.
Self-reference effectRogers, Kuiper & KirkerMaterial related to yourself is encoded and recalled better.
Misinformation effectElizabeth LoftusPost-event misleading info distorts the original memory (eyewitness work).
Picture superiority effectAllan Paivio (dual-coding)Pictures are remembered better than words.
Encoding-specificity / context effectTulving & ThomsonRecall is best when retrieval cues match the encoding context (state/context-dependent memory).
Levels-of-processing effectCraik & LockhartDeeper (semantic) processing → better memory than shallow (visual/acoustic).
Trap

Remove the delay/distractor and it's the recency end that vanishes (STM decays); primacy survives. Zeigarnik = unfinished remembered; von Restorff = distinctive remembered — don't merge them.

1.2 Attention & perception effects

EffectWhoWhat it is
Stroop effectJ. Ridley StroopSlower to name ink colour when the word spells a different colour (automatic reading interferes).
Cocktail-party effectColin CherrySelective attention to one voice in a crowd, yet you still catch your own name.
McGurk effectMcGurk & MacDonaldWhat you see the mouth do changes what you hear (audio-visual speech fusion).
Ventriloquist effect—Sound is mislocated toward a plausible visual source.
Troxler effect (fading)Ignaz TroxlerFixate steadily and unchanging peripheral stimuli fade from awareness.
Motion aftereffect (waterfall illusion)—After staring at motion, a static scene appears to drift the opposite way.
Bouba/kiki effectKöhler (later Ramachandran)People map "bouba" to round shapes, "kiki" to spiky ones (sound symbolism).
Tetris effect—Heavy repeated activity intrudes into imagery/dreams.
Trap

The phi phenomenon and autokinetic phenomenon are labelled "phenomena," not "effects" — they live in §2.1. Same content, the label decides where the examiner files it.

1.3 Social & expectancy effects

EffectWhoWhat it is
Halo effectEdward ThorndikeOne good trait (e.g. looks) bleeds into an overall positive judgement.
Horn(s) effect—The negative mirror of halo: one bad trait darkens the whole impression.
Pygmalion (Rosenthal) effectRosenthal & JacobsonHigh teacher/leader expectations → the person actually performs better (self-fulfilling prophecy).
Golem effect—Low expectations → worse performance (the dark twin of Pygmalion).
Hawthorne effectElton Mayo (Western Electric studies)People change behaviour because they know they're being observed.
John Henry effectSaretskyA control group tries extra hard because it knows it's the comparison (a research artefact).
Mere-exposure effectRobert ZajoncRepeated exposure alone increases liking (also a persuasion lever: familiarity breeds liking).
Bystander effectLatané & DarleyMore onlookers → less likely any one helps (diffusion of responsibility). After Kitty Genovese. 5-step decision model: notice → interpret → take responsibility → know how → act. (Theory form in §3.6.)
Ringelmann effectMax RingelmannPer-person effort drops as group size grows (precursor to social loafing).
Pratfall effectElliot AronsonA competent person becomes more likeable after a small blunder.
Spotlight effectThomas GilovichOverestimating how much others notice you.
False-consensus effectLee RossOverestimating how many people share your views/choices.
Lake Wobegon / above-average effect—Most people rate themselves above average (illusory superiority).
Cheerleader effect—Faces look more attractive in a group than alone.
Overjustification effectLepper, Greene & NisbettPaying for an already-enjoyed activity kills the intrinsic motivation.
Boomerang effectHovland tradition (akin to Brehm)Persuasion backfires, shifting the attitude the opposite way (cousin of reactance, §3.6).
Trap

Same-study, different name: Pygmalion = Rosenthal & Jacobson (high expectation lifts), Golem = low expectation drops, Hawthorne = Mayo (being watched changes behaviour). Examiners swap these three constantly.

1.4 Judgment & decision effects

EffectWhoWhat it is

Dunning–Kruger effectDunning & KrugerLow-skill people overestimate their own competence.

Framing effectTversky & KahnemanSame facts, different wording (gain vs loss) flips the choice.

IKEA effectNorton, Mochon & ArielyYou over-value things you partly built yourself.

High-yield

Anchoring, framing and availability are written up both as effects (here) and as cognitive biases/heuristics (§2.6). If the stem says "bias/heuristic," it's §2.6; if it says "effect," it's here. Same content, two labels — scan both.

1.5 Persuasion, media & population effects

EffectWhoWhat it is
Sleeper effectCarl Hovland (Yale)A message from a low-credibility source persuades more over time once you forget the source.
Werther effectDavid PhillipsSpike in copycat suicides after a publicised one.
Papageno effectNiederkrotenthalerResponsible media coverage of coping can reduce suicide (the protective opposite of Werther).
Mozart effectRauscher et al.A brief, small spatial-reasoning bump after Mozart (popularly overstated).
Flynn effectJames FlynnPopulation IQ scores have risen generation on generation (forces test re-norming).
Matthew effectRobert Merton"Rich get richer": early advantage compounds (reading, science credit).
Kuleshov effectLev KuleshovViewers read emotion into a neutral face based on the adjacent shot (film editing).

1.6 Biological & clinical effects

EffectWhoWhat it is
Placebo effect—Improvement from an inert treatment driven by expectation.
Nocebo effect—Negative symptoms from an inert treatment (the dark twin of placebo).
Garcia effect (taste aversion)John GarciaOne-trial conditioned aversion to a taste; biological preparedness; defies classic CC timing rules.
Coolidge effectFrank Beach (named it)Renewed sexual interest when a novel partner is introduced.
Westermarck effectEdvard WestermarckReverse sexual imprinting: people raised together in early childhood feel no attraction (incest avoidance).
Kindling effectGoddard (seizure model)Repeated sub-threshold stimulation lowers the threshold for episodes (mood/seizure recurrence).
Tolerance / sensitisation—Same drug dose gives less effect (tolerance) or more (sensitisation) with repetition.
Trap

Six "good twin / bad twin" pairs: placebo↔nocebo, Werther↔Papageno, Pygmalion↔Golem, halo↔horn, primacy↔recency, tolerance↔sensitisation. Learn only one of each and the distractor will be the other.

2. Phenomena, illusions, biases & clinical signs

Named things the mind/brain does: illusions, attention failures, memory glitches, group phenomena, biases and neuropsych signs. The stem usually describes the experience and wants the name (or who described it).

2.1 Perceptual illusions

IllusionWhoWhat you see
Müller-LyerFranz Müller-LyerTwo equal lines look unequal depending on arrow-head direction (inward vs outward fins).
PonzoMario PonzoTwo equal lines on converging "railway" lines look unequal (depth misread).
Ebbinghaus (Titchener) circlesEbbinghausA circle ringed by big circles looks smaller than the same circle ringed by small ones.
ZöllnerJohann ZöllnerParallel lines look slanted because of crossing hatch marks.
Kanizsa triangleGaetano KanizsaYou "see" a white triangle edge that isn't drawn (illusory/subjective contours).
Necker cubeLouis NeckerA wire cube flips between two 3-D orientations (bistable perception).
Rubin's vaseEdgar RubinFaces-or-vase: figure–ground reversal.
Ames roomAdelbert AmesA distorted room makes equal-size people look giant vs tiny.
Moon illusion—The moon looks larger near the horizon than overhead.
Hermann grid—Ghostly grey blobs appear at the white intersections of a black grid.
Café wallGregory & HeardStraight mortar lines look wedge-shaped between offset tiles.
Phi phenomenonMax WertheimerLights flashing in sequence look like one moving light (the founding Gestalt demo of apparent motion).
Autokinetic phenomenonused by Muzafer SherifA still point of light in the dark seems to drift; Sherif used it to study norm formation.
Trap

Three "size of equal lines" illusions: Müller-Lyer = arrow fins; Ponzo = converging rails; Ebbinghaus = circle surrounded by other circles. Examiners mix these three.

2.2 Gestalt grouping principles

From Wertheimer, Koffka & Köhler. "The whole is other than the sum of its parts." Master law = Prägnanz (we see the simplest, most stable form). The Gestalt theory of perception itself is in §3.11.

PrincipleWhat groups together
ProximityThings close together are seen as a group.
SimilarityThings that look alike are grouped.
Continuity (good continuation)We follow smooth, continuous lines over abrupt changes.
ClosureWe fill gaps to see a complete figure.
Figure–groundWe split a scene into an object and its background.
Common fateThings moving together are grouped.
Prägnanz (good form)The overarching law: perceive the simplest, most regular organisation.

2.3 Attention & perception phenomena

PhenomenonWhoWhat it is
Inattentional blindnessMack & Rock; Simons & Chabris (gorilla)You miss an obvious unexpected object while attending elsewhere.
Change blindnessSimons & LevinYou fail to notice a large change across a visual interruption.
Attentional blinkRaymond, Shapiro & ArnellBrief inability to detect a 2nd target ~200–500 ms after the 1st.
Cocktail-party phenomenonColin CherryTracking one voice in noise, yet still catching your own name (effect form: §1.2).
Weber's law / JNDErnst WeberThe just-noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the stimulus.
Fechner's lawGustav FechnerSensation grows with the log of stimulus intensity (founded psychophysics).
Sensory adaptation—Constant stimulation → declining response (you stop feeling your clothes).
Pareidolia—Seeing meaningful patterns (faces) in random stimuli (clouds, toast).
Synaesthesia—One sense automatically triggers another (hearing colours, tasting words).
BlindsightLawrence WeiskrantzResponding to visual stimuli without conscious sight (V1 damage).
Perceptual constancy—Objects seen as stable in size/shape/colour despite changing retinal image.

2.4 Memory phenomena

PhenomenonWhoWhat it is
Tip-of-the-tongue (TOT)Brown & McNeillKnowing a word is there but not retrieving it (you often get its first letter/length).
Flashbulb memoryBrown & KulikVivid, confident memory of where you were during a shocking event (often inaccurate).
Déjà vu / jamais vu—Novel scene feels familiar / familiar scene feels alien.
Reminiscence bump—Older adults recall disproportionately many memories from ages ~10–30.
Childhood (infantile) amnesia—Few/no episodic memories before ~age 3–4.
Proactive interference—Old learning blocks new (old password keeps coming up).
Retroactive interference—New learning blocks old (new password erases the old one).
Forgetting curveHermann EbbinghausRetention drops sharply then levels off; relearning is faster (savings).
Misattribution / source-monitoring errorDaniel SchacterRight memory, wrong source (cryptomnesia: feeling you invented something you read).

PhenomenonWhoWhat it is

Social loafingLatané; Ringelmann rootsIndividuals slack in groups when effort isn't identifiable.

Group polarisationMoscovici; Stoner (risky shift)Group discussion pushes members to a more extreme version of their leaning.

ConformitySolomon AschGoing along with a group's wrong judgement (line task); normative conformity.

Bystander apathyLatané & DarleyThe umbrella phenomenon: less help as the crowd grows (effect form: §1.3).
Risky shiftStonerGroups make riskier decisions than individuals (early name for polarisation).

2.6 Cognitive biases & heuristics

Heuristics = mental shortcuts (largely Tversky & Kahneman). Biases = the systematic errors they cause.

Bias / heuristicWhoWhat it is
Availability heuristicTversky & KahnemanJudge frequency by how easily examples come to mind.
Representativeness heuristicTversky & KahnemanJudge by resemblance to a prototype (ignores base rates → conjunction fallacy).
Anchoring & adjustmentTversky & KahnemanCling to an initial value when estimating (effect form: §1.4).
Confirmation biasPeter WasonSeeking/weighting evidence that fits your belief.
Hindsight biasBaruch Fischhoff"I knew it all along" after the outcome is known.
Fundamental attribution errorcoined by Lee RossOver-attributing others' behaviour to character, under-weighting situation.
Self-serving bias—Credit success to self, blame failure on circumstance.
Sunk-cost fallacy—Continuing because of past investment that can't be recovered.
Gambler's fallacy—Believing past random outcomes change future odds.
Loss aversion / framingKahneman & Tversky (prospect theory)Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good.
Trap

Tells: "judged by ease of recall" = availability; "judged by stereotype/prototype" = representativeness; "blamed the person not the situation" = FAE (Ross). Don't swap the two heuristics.

Phenomenon / signWhoWhat it is

Capgras delusionJoseph CapgrasBelief that a loved one has been replaced by an identical impostor.
Fregoli delusion—Belief that different people are one person in disguise.
Cotard delusionJules CotardBelief that one is dead, doesn't exist, or is rotting (nihilistic delusion).

Confabulation—Filling memory gaps with fabricated events without intent to lie (Korsakoff).

Trap

Delusional misidentification trio: Capgras = loved one is an impostor; Fregoli = strangers are one disguised person; Cotard = "I am dead." A classic NIMHANS distractor set. (More eponymous syndromes in §4.)

3. Theories & models

Every named theory/model, its author and the one-line claim, grouped so rival theories (each other's distractors) sit side by side. Learn the contrast, not the theory alone.

3.1 Learning theories

TheoryAuthorCore claim
Classical conditioningIvan PavlovNeutral stimulus paired with a reflex-evoking one comes to evoke the reflex.
Connectionism / Law of EffectEdward ThorndikeResponses followed by satisfaction are stamped in (trial-and-error, puzzle box).
Operant conditioningB. F. SkinnerBehaviour is shaped by its consequences (reinforcement/punishment).
Contiguity theoryEdwin GuthrieOne-trial learning by sheer stimulus–response pairing in time.
Insight learningWolfgang KöhlerSudden restructuring of a problem ("aha"), shown in chimps (Sultan).
Latent learning / cognitive mapsEdward TolmanLearning happens without reinforcement and shows up later (maze rats).
Social / observational learningAlbert BanduraWe learn by watching and modelling others (Bobo doll).
Rescorla–Wagner modelRescorla & WagnerConditioning depends on surprise / prediction error, not mere pairing.
Premack principleDavid PremackA high-frequency activity can reinforce a low-frequency one ("grandma's rule").
Conditions / hierarchy of learningRobert GagnéEight learning types from signal learning up to problem-solving; later five learned capabilities + nine instructional events.
Meaningful learning / advance organizersDavid AusubelNew material is anchored to existing knowledge; pre-teach an advance organizer first. Reception, not discovery.
Discovery learningJerome BrunerLearn by exploring and figuring it out yourself; spiral curriculum; enactive → iconic → symbolic representation.
Two-factor (avoidance) theoryO. H. MowrerAvoidance = classical conditioning of fear + operant reinforcement from escaping it. Explains why phobias persist.

3.3 Emotion theories (the order of events is the trap)

TheoryAuthorSequence
James–LangeJames & LangeStimulus → bodily arousal → emotion ("we're afraid because we tremble"). Emotion = reading your own physiology.
Cannon–BardCannon & BardStimulus → arousal and emotion simultaneously (thalamic); body feedback not needed.
Schachter–Singer two-factorSchachter & SingerArousal + cognitive label → emotion (same racing heart becomes fear or excitement by context).
Cognitive appraisalRichard LazarusAppraisal of the event comes first; cognition precedes emotion.
Affective primacyRobert ZajoncEmotion can occur before and without cognition ("preferences need no inferences"; mere-exposure is his evidence).
Facial-feedback hypothesisTomkins; EkmanFacial expressions feed back to intensify/trigger the felt emotion.
Basic emotionsPaul EkmanSix universal, cross-cultural facial emotions.
Wheel of emotionsRobert PlutchikEight primary emotions in opposing pairs, combining into others.
Trap

The classic swap: James-Lange = body first; Cannon-Bard = both at once; Schachter-Singer = arousal + label; Lazarus = appraisal first; Zajonc = feeling before thinking. Lazarus vs Zajonc is the famous "primacy of cognition vs affect" debate.

Mnemonic

Compliance techniques cluster with reactance: foot-in-the-door (Freedman & Fraser, small→big), door-in-the-face and low-ball (Cialdini et al.), and Cialdini's 6 principles of influence (reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity — scarcity works because of reactance).

Trap

Scenario tell: "told he can't have X, so he wants it more / does it anyway" = reactance (Brehm). "Tried, failed repeatedly, gave up even when he could win" = learned helplessness (Seligman, §3.10). Don't swap them.

6.2 Statistical tests & who gave them

High-yield

Student's t = William Sealy Gosset. "Student" is a pseudonym, not a person. Gosset worked at the Guinness brewery in Dublin and published the t-test in 1908 under the pen name because Guinness barred staff from publishing. "Who was Student / who gave the t-test" → Gosset. The t-test compares means when the sample is small and the population SD is unknown.

TestWho gave itWhat it's for
Student's t-test / t-distributionW. S. Gosset ("Student", 1908)Compare means, small n, σ unknown. Flavours: one-sample (df = n−1), independent two-sample (df = n₁+n₂−2), paired (before/after, same people).
ANOVA / F-testRonald Fisher (the F named for Fisher by Snedecor)Compare 3+ group means at once. F = between-group var ÷ within-group var. Also: null hypothesis, p < .05, experimental design.
Chi-square (χ²)Karl PearsonAssociation / goodness-of-fit for categorical data.
Pearson rKarl PearsonLinear correlation, interval data.
Spearman rho (ρ)Charles SpearmanRank correlation (ordinal / non-normal).
Mann–Whitney UMann & WhitneyNonparametric twin of the independent t (two unrelated groups).
Wilcoxon signed-rankFrank WilcoxonNonparametric twin of the paired t.
Kruskal–Wallis HKruskal & WallisNonparametric twin of one-way ANOVA.
Friedman testMilton FriedmanNonparametric repeated-measures ANOVA.
McNemar testQuinn McNemarPaired nominal data (before/after yes-no).
Trap

"Student" is Gosset, not a real student, and McCall's T (a standard score) ≠ Student's t (a test statistic) — the shared letter is the bait. 2 groups → t-test; 3+ groups → ANOVA (don't run repeated t-tests). Parametric → nonparametric: independent t → Mann–Whitney, paired t → Wilcoxon, one-way ANOVA → Kruskal–Wallis. F-test/ANOVA = Fisher; chi-square = Pearson.

Definition

t vs z: use z when the population SD is known and/or n is large; use t when SD is estimated from a small sample. As df → ∞ the t-distribution becomes the normal (z) curve. t is shorter and fatter-tailed than z.
