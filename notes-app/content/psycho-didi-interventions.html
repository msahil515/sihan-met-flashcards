1 · Psychodynamic Psychotherapy

Psychodynamic psychotherapy → Sigmund Freud. Worked with Joseph Breuer (the case of Anna 'O'); influenced by Charcot (hypnosis) and Breuer (catharsis).

Freud — early life & career

  • Was his mother's favourite child; observed his own life; was attracted to his mother and scared of his father.
  • Started teaching at the University of Vienna in 1873.
  • Died in Britain from jaw cancer.
  • Career milestones: cocaine exploration; influence of Charcot (hypnosis) and Breuer (catharsis); movement from resistance to suggestion → concentration technique → free association; publication of Studies on Hysteria.

Defence mechanisms

DefenceCore idea
RepressionKeeping unpleasant memories or thoughts buried in the unconscious. Primal: innately part of the Id. Proper: unacceptable derivatives of original id impulses.
DenialRefusing to recognise an unpleasant reality.
RationalizationCreating a socially acceptable excuse to justify unacceptable behaviour.
Reaction formationNot acknowledging unacceptable impulses and over-emphasising their opposite.
ProjectionTransferring one's unacceptable qualities or impulses to others.

Important processes

ProcessMeaning
ResistanceReluctance to discuss certain topics; conscious defence mechanisms.
TransferenceThe patient projects feelings meant for others onto the therapist.
Counter-transferenceThe therapist develops emotional reactions towards the patient.
Working throughDiscussing emotionally charged material over many therapy sessions.
Trap — transference vs counter-transference. Transference = patient → therapist (patient projects feelings for others onto the therapist). Counter-transference = therapist → patient (therapist develops emotional reactions towards the patient).

Dreams

  • Manifest content: the clearly identifiable stories, characters, events and actions.
  • Latent content: the unconscious thoughts, desires, fears and conflicts.

Dream work

MechanismFunction
CondensationCondensing multiple thoughts into a single dream element or symbol.
DisplacementShifting emotional significance from one object to another.
SymbolizationRepresenting an action or idea through symbols.
Secondary revisionRationalizing latent content.

2 · Transactional Analysis

Transactional Analysis → Eric Berne.

Transactional Analysis is a psychotherapeutic method characterised by a creative combination of cognitive and psychodynamically oriented psychotherapy, enriched by aspects of communication therapy and gestalt therapy processes.

Types of analysis

  • Structural analysis: individual personality analysis.
  • Transactional analysis: dialogue analysis.
  • Game analysis: saying something and meaning something else (analysis of ulterior transactions).
  • Script analysis: compulsive, rigid transactions that people act out compulsively.

Ego states

  • Parent ego — Nurturing parent and Critical parent.
  • Adult ego.
  • Child ego — Free child and Adapted child.

Transactions

  • Complementary transactions.
  • Crossed transactions.
  • Ulterior transactions — e.g. saying "You don't love me" while meaning "You love my brother so much!"

3 · Behaviour Therapy

Behaviour therapy is the application of experimentally derived principles of learning to the treatment of psychological disorders.

Assumptions

  • An empirical approach.
  • Most behaviour is learned and therefore can be unlearned.
  • Maladaptive behaviours are learned in the same manner as adaptive ones.
  • The historical account of the development of a problem behaviour is less relevant than the present factors.
  • It rejects the assumptions of traditional trait theory.

Features

  • Focus on present factors.
  • Functional assessment and behavioural analysis.
  • Recognition of individual–environment interaction.
  • Active client participation.
  • Two core features: extinction and counter-conditioning.
  • Goal is to reduce maladaptive behaviours.

Applications

Used in treating anxiety, depression, PTSD, substance abuse, eating and sexual disorders, and hypertension. Applied across diverse fields: developmental disability, mental illness, education and special education, and community psychology.

Techniques based on classical conditioning

Systematic desensitization

Systematic desensitization → Joseph Wolpe (1958).

Based on reciprocal inhibition: the process in which maladaptive responses learned due to prior conditioning with aversive events are eliminated by developing antagonistic responses.

Steps:

  1. Jacobson's Progressive Muscle Relaxation (JPMR).
  2. Development of an anxiety hierarchy and exposure in vitro.
  3. Desensitization (Subjective Unit of Distress < 25).

Flooding

Involves therapist-controlled, prolonged exposure to anxiety-provoking conditioned stimuli (CS), simultaneously blocking the individual's chance of escape and avoidance.

Implosion

Implosion → Thomas Stampfl (1966).

A variant of flooding, but it takes place at the imaginary level.

Trap — systematic desensitization vs flooding. Systematic desensitization (Wolpe) is graded: it climbs an anxiety hierarchy with relaxation, keeping distress low (reciprocal inhibition). Flooding is maximal: prolonged full-intensity exposure with escape/avoidance blocked, no gradual hierarchy. Implosion (Stampfl) = flooding done in imagination.

Aversion therapy

An avoidance-learning method in which the individual is required to perform a response to avoid punishment. Example: alcohol + disulfiram causes vomiting, so the person learns to avoid alcohol. Used for treating OCD, paraphilia and substance-use disorders; also applied in clinical psychology, rehabilitation, business, self-management and sports-psychology interventions.

Techniques based on operant conditioning (1st wave)

Differential reinforcement

Refers to the implementation of two clearly different degrees of reinforcement for two behaviours, one of which is to be replaced by the other.

TypePurpose
DRO — of other behaviour (or zero-rate behaviour)Reinforcing the non-occurrence of the target behaviour.
DRL — of low-rate behaviourDesigned to decrease a behaviour to an acceptable level.
DRI — of incompatible behaviourReinforcing responses that are topographically incompatible with the possibility of the target behaviour.
DRA — of alternate behaviourThe reinforced behaviour need not be topographically incompatible; it may be any desirable alternate behaviour.

Other operant techniques

TechniqueCore idea
Token economyA reinforcement system based on the delivery of tokens. Tokens earned for specific target behaviour can be spent on different things within a defined therapeutic environment, or saved for future purchases.
Response costWithdrawal of acquired positive reinforcers following undesirable or problematic behaviour.
Time outTemporarily separating a person from an environment where an unacceptable behaviour has occurred. Example: a player getting a red card in a football match.
ShapingReinforcing behaviours that are successively closer approximations of the desired or targeted behaviour.
ChainingTeaching a behaviour using behaviour chains — sequences of individual behaviours that, when linked together, form a terminal behaviour.

Techniques based on modelling

Modelling → Albert Bandura (S-O-R theory).

Process of learning: attention → retention → motor reproduction → motivation.

Functions: teaching through demonstration, promoting behaviour, motivating behaviour, anxiety reduction, and discouraging behaviours.

Variants:

  • Covert — live modelling, symbolic modelling, participant modelling, self-modelling.
  • Overt — imaginative.

4 · Cognitive-Behaviour Therapy (Waves 1–3)

Key — the three waves. 1st wave = behaviour therapy (operant/classical conditioning). 2nd wave = cognitive therapy — Beck's CT and Ellis's REBT. 3rd wave = acceptance/mindfulness-based therapies (DBT, MBCT, MBSR, ACT, and others).

Beck's Cognitive Therapy (2nd wave)

Cognitive therapy → Aaron Beck.

Based on the concept that our thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and actions are interconnected, and that negative thoughts and feelings can trap us in a vicious cycle.

Cognitive triad

  • Self — "I am a failure."
  • World — "Everybody is unfair."
  • Future — "I will never succeed."

Cognitive distortions

DistortionMeaning
All or nothingDichotomous, extremes of language such as "always".
Mental filterOnly paying attention to certain evidence, skewing it as meaning more than it does.
Mind readingAssuming you know what someone else is thinking or feeling.
OvergeneralizingSeeing a pattern based on a single event, or being overly broad in your conclusions.
Disqualifying the positiveA type of mental filter discounting the good things that have happened or that you have done.
Fortune tellingAssuming you know what will happen in the future.
MagnificationBlowing things out of proportion.
Emotional reasoning"Because I'm thinking or feeling it, it must be true."
LabellingAssigning labels to ourselves or others, putting people in a box so they can't and won't change.
MinimizationInappropriately shrinking something to make it seem less important; rationalization, justification, blaming.
Shoulds and mustsUnrealistic expectations.
PersonalizationTaking responsibility for something that is not yours to own.

Self-instruction training

Self-instruction training → Donald Meichenbaum.
  • Modelling: demonstrating assertive behaviour.
  • Practice: role playing.
  • Self-instruction: overt, then covert.
  • Practice in real life.

Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT)

REBT → Albert Ellis (a directive therapy).

Ellis coined the term musterbation to indicate that irrational beliefs are usually expressed as musts, shoulds, ought-tos, have-tos and got-tos.

Three major irrational clusters:

  1. "I must do well and win approval for my performances."
  2. "Others must treat me kindly and considerately."
  3. "Conditions under which I live must be arranged so that I get practically everything I want comfortably, quickly and easily."

Techniques: Socratic questioning, confrontation, role playing, and shame-attacking exercises (homework outside of therapy).

Trap — Beck vs Ellis. Beck (Cognitive Therapy) targets cognitive distortions and the cognitive triad (self–world–future) through collaborative empiricism and Socratic guided discovery. Ellis (REBT) targets irrational beliefs ("musterbation": musts/shoulds) and is more directive/confrontational, using the ABC framework and shame-attacking exercises.

Third-wave therapies (3rd wave)

Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)

DBT → Marsha M. Linehan.

Emerged as a treatment for chronically parasuicidal women with Borderline Personality Disorder.

Three main principles:

  1. Everything is interconnected or interrelated.
  2. Reality is not static.
  3. The truth (which is always evolving) can be found by integrating or synthesizing differing (and possibly opposite) views.

Four core skill modules: core mindfulness skills, interpersonal effectiveness skills, emotion regulation skills, and distress tolerance skills.

Steps / stages:

  1. Identifying safety and life-threatening behaviours.
  2. Addressing therapy-interfering behaviours.
  3. Enhancing quality of life.
  4. Adaptation and growth.

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)

MBCT → Zindel Segal, Mark Williams & John Teasdale.

Features: thought-process focused; promotes a new way of being with painful affect and challenging circumstances; distinguishing thoughts as thoughts (vs statements of fact); noticing and allowing thoughts and feelings without fixing, changing or avoiding; behavioural interventions focused on developing present-moment awareness; and the therapist also embodies the approach.

Structure: 8 consecutive weekly sessions of about 2 hours each — Phase 1 (teaching), Phase 2 (application).

Used for: anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, depression associated with medical illness, low mood, unhappiness, depression-relapse prevention, and treatment-resistant depression.

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)

Structure: 8 consecutive weekly sessions, 5 hours per week — Phase 1 (teaching), Phase 2 (application).

Features: focuses on cultivating mindfulness, contemplation, listening and speaking; a briefer integrative practice.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

ACT → Steven Hayes.

The ACT acronym:

  • A: Accept your thoughts and feelings, and be present.
  • C: Choose a valued direction.
  • T: Take action.

Six core processes (grouped as Open up / Be present / Do what matters):

  • Contact with the present moment (be here now).
  • Defusion (watch your thinking).
  • Acceptance (open up).
  • Self as context (pure awareness).
  • Values (know what matters).
  • Committed action (doing what it takes).

Other third-wave therapies

  • Functional Analytic Psychotherapy (FAP).
  • Metacognitive Therapy (MCT).
  • Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT).

5 · Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR)

EMDR → Francine Shapiro (1987).
  • Considered a behaviour therapy by some authors.
  • Used in PTSD.
  • Not falsifiable.
  • Book: Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing: Basic Principles, Protocols.

Procedure

  1. History taking.
  2. Desensitization: focus on emotion and bodily sensation.
  3. Bilateral stimulation: eye movement, tactile, auditory stimulation.
  4. Reprocessing: the trauma that occurred in the past.
  5. Installation: of positive beliefs.

6 · Gestalt Therapy

Gestalt therapy → Fritz Perls and Laura Perls (1940s).
  • Influenced by the existential school, phenomenology and field theory.
  • Focuses on re-owning disowned parts of the self.
  • Self: internal and external (one dominates or is dominated).
  • Real change comes when we try to be our true self rather than being someone else.
  • Increasing awareness enables informed choices and leads to a more meaningful life.
  • Insight comes through the help of therapy.

A directional, confrontational therapy

  • Emphasizes that one must understand the field-theory diagram in order to understand oneself.
  • Clients should take responsibility for their life problems.
  • Movement from environmental support to self-support.
  • A confrontational therapy style — Perls intentionally frustrated clients (skilful frustration).
  • Focuses on restoring awareness.

Types of awareness (Polster & Polster, 1973)

  • Sensation and action.
  • Feeling.
  • Wants.
  • Values and assessment.

Focus on current experience: the here and now; the what and how.

Contact and levels

Contact: interacting meaningfully with others. Fusion: fusing with others.

Level of contactDescription
Phony layerPeople complimenting when they want something from you.
Phobic layerAvoidance of pain, grief.
Impasse layerUnable to change and unable to solve.
Implosive layerBecoming aware of self but keeping feelings inside.
Explosive layerTaking action and expression.

When contact is not genuine and authentic (Polster & Polster)

DisturbanceMeaning
IntrojectionSwallowing whole, or accepting others' views without questioning.
RetroflectionDoing to ourselves what we would like to do to other people.
ProjectionAssigning aspects of ourselves onto others.
DeflectionAvoidance.
ConfluenceThe boundary between oneself and others becomes muted.

Integration sequence

  1. Discovery: self-realization prompting newfound awareness.
  2. Accommodation: skills to navigate challenging situations.
  3. Assimilation: learning to influence the environment.

Boundaries

Boundaries are rigid and must be challenged and crossed; language must be used as a method to challenge boundaries. Types of boundaries: body boundaries, value boundaries, familiarity boundaries, expressive boundaries.

Gestalt therapy techniques

TechniqueDescription
The Internal Dialogue Exercise (top dog vs underdog)The topdog is the voice of what the person should do; the underdog is the rebellious part that resists the topdog's demands. The client converses as both.
The Empty-Chair Technique (by Joseph Moreno)A role-playing technique in which all the parts are played by the client, so introjects can surface and the client can experience the conflict more fully.
Future Projection TechniqueAn anticipated event is brought into the present moment and acted out.
Making the RoundsAsking a person in a group to go up to others and either speak to or do something with each person — to confront, to risk, to disclose the self, to experiment with new behaviour, and to grow and change.
The Reversal ExerciseClients take the plunge into the very thing fraught with anxiety and make contact with submerged, denied parts of themselves.
The Rehearsal ExerciseRehearsing aloud in a group so that others can experience the internal thinking process.
The Exaggeration ExerciseRepeating and exaggerating a movement or gesture to help clients become more aware of their emotions.
Staying With the FeelingAt key moments when clients wish to flee an unpleasant feeling, the therapist urges them to stay with and go deeper into the feeling or behaviour they wish to avoid.
Dream WorkThe Gestalt approach does not interpret and analyze dreams; instead, it brings dreams back to life and relives them as though happening now.
"I" statementsClients are encouraged to use the phrase "I" to take responsibility for their own feelings and actions.

7 · Humanistic & Existential Therapy

Carl Rogers — Client-Centred Therapy

Client-centred therapy → Carl Rogers.

Rogers believed that people can consciously and rationally change their thoughts and behaviour from undesirable to desirable.

Conditions of therapy:

  • A congruent therapist.
  • Unconditional positive regard for the client.
  • Accurate empathy.

After therapy, the person gets closer to becoming a congruent and fully functioning person.

Karl Jaspers — Boundary Situations

Boundary situations are conditions of human life that are difficult to overcome. They are basic facts about human life, such as death, suffering, guilt and struggle. They are unchanging and unalterable, although the way they appear to humans may change; they are experienced at the limit of one's existence.

Significance of boundary situations:

  • Reveal being as such.
  • Reveal the potential for self-realization.
  • Remove the cover of a private world view.
  • Expose people to the reality of opposites, contradictions and death.

Jean-Paul Sartre — Existential Psychoanalysis

  • Believed there is no inherent meaning to life; any meaning you find is your own.
  • People are "condemned to be free".
  • In existential psychoanalysis, the therapist's role is to confront excuses for misery.

Viktor Frankl — Logotherapy

Logotherapy → Viktor Frankl.

Emphasis on existential and spiritual questions. Meaning is found through the "meaning triangle" of Attitude, Experience and Creativity. Books: Man's Search for Meaning; Recollections: An Autobiography.

Techniques for transcending problems:

  • Attitude modulation: changing negative to positive.
  • Dereflection: redirecting focus from our issue to other, greater issues.
  • Paradoxical intention: cracking jokes about difficult situations.
  • Socratic dialogue (by Guttman): guided questioning — the approach questions beliefs, the guidance busts misunderstanding, and the outcome is reduced self-absorption.

Irvin Yalom

Focus: death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness.

Themes:

  • Death and existence — live authentically.
  • Freedom and responsibility.
  • Existential isolation.
  • Meaning and purpose.
  • Authenticity and self-awareness.

8 · Stress and Coping

Stress is a state brought on by any situation that threatens, or appears to threaten, a person's sense of well-being, thus challenging the person's ability to cope.

Types of stressors and stress

  • Acute: a short-term stressor that has a definite endpoint.
  • Chronic: a long-term stressor that often lacks a definite endpoint.
  • Eustress: an optimal amount of stress required to motivate you.
  • Distress: stress that occurs when we are unable to meet the demands of our environment with our limited resources.

Personality & stress

Type A / Type B personality → Meyer Friedman & Raymond Rosenman (cardiologists, late 1950s).
TypeTraitsProne to
Type ASelf-driven, competitive, achievers, risk-takers, show hostility.Coronary heart disease.
Type BCharismatic, easy-going, not easily stressed, lacking a sense of urgency.—
Type CIntroverts, lone workers, sticklers for details, perfectionists, unassertive.Cancer.
Type DNegative affectivity (worry, gloominess) and social inhibition; distressed.Depression.

The Social Readjustment Rating Scale

Social Readjustment Rating Scale → Thomas Holmes & Richard Rahe (1967).

They compiled a list of 43 events likely to change a person's life and therefore cause stress. Based on participant ratings, each event was assigned a point value from 1 to 100; these units were called life change units.

Types of stressors (Holmes & Rahe): catastrophic/cataclysmic events, and daily hassles.

Physiological responses to stress

  • HPA axis: CRF → anterior pituitary → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol → metabolism increase and insulin resistance.
  • SAM pathway: hypothalamus → SNS → adrenal medulla → epinephrine and norepinephrine.
  • Allostasis is a process of adaptation to acute stress by releasing stress hormones in the event of a challenge, with the goal of restoring homeostasis; it occurs because the HPA axis is activated during stress. Constant cortisol release causes immunosuppression.

Cognitive appraisal / mediation theory

Cognitive appraisal (mediation) theory → Richard Lazarus.
  • Primary appraisal: we examine the stressor and assess how severe it is — identifying it as a threat (a future danger), a challenge (a current situation to be confronted and overcome), or a harm or loss.
  • Secondary appraisal: we evaluate our own resources and ability to cope with the threat, challenge or loss.

General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)

General Adaptation Syndrome → Hans Selye.
  1. Stage 1: alarm.
  2. Stage 2: resistance.
  3. Stage 3: exhaustion.

Response to stress & coping

  • Defending vs Coping; Adaptive vs Maladaptive.
  • Problem-focused coping (source: external) vs Emotion-focused coping (source: internal).

Explanatory styles

Explanatory styles → Martin Seligman.

The characteristic manner in which we explain events; explanatory styles make a difference in how we appraise and respond to stressors.

DimensionOptimistic stylePessimistic style
DurationTemporaryPermanent
ScopeSpecificGlobal
SourceExternalInternal

Interventions

Physiological level:

  • Simple breathing exercises — pranayam, box breathing.
  • Guided imagery: a method of treating stress and anxiety in which one replaces disturbing memories with positive mental energy.
  • Biofeedback: a self-regulation technique through which patients learn to voluntarily control what were once thought to be involuntary body processes.
  • Progressive Muscle Relaxation — introduced by Edmund Jacobson (1920), also called JPMR; types of relaxation: top-down and bottom-up.

Psychological level:

  • Mindfulness meditation: slows racing thoughts, living in the present, letting go of negativity to calm body and mind — attitude, attention and intention.
  • Stress inoculation (by Donald Meichenbaum, 1985): stages of education/conceptualization → rehearsal/skill acquisition → implementation/skill application and follow-through.
  • Social support: an effective, active, problem-focused network of friends; ask for help.

9 · Counselling and Guidance

Dimensions

A good counselling relationship, therapeutic intervention, and psychological process.

Essential features

TheoristEssential features for counselling
RogersUnconditional positive regard, empathy, congruence.
CarkhuffRespect, confrontation, immediacy, concreteness, self-disclosure.

Need & goals

Need: when stress-related symptoms are out of proportion, loneliness, job issues, career problems.

Goals:

  1. Healthy development of personality.
  2. Providing support.
  3. Emotional release.
  4. Awareness.
  5. Value clarification and change.

Process & steps

Process: pre-counselling (see if the client is suitable) → identify the need for counselling → prepare for counselling → conduct counselling → follow-up.

Steps of counselling: Identify → Prepare → Conduct → Termination → Follow-up.

Important steps: background information → identifying core issues → case formulation → goal setting → intervention implementation → evaluation → closure.

Length and intensity of counselling depend on the severity of the problem, motivation of the client, type of problems, and age of the client.

Stages of counselling: Beginning → Middle → Ending

  • Developing a relationship / beginning: establish a safe environment, help people express, active listening, paraphrasing, focus on feelings.
  • Middle: transform the problem into a goal, explore approaches to the goal, help the person choose a way towards the goal, make a contract to fulfil the plan, summarize and get verification, get feedback and confirmation.
  • Analysing fears: abandonment, rejection, not being enough. It is important that clients gain recognition for their skills and experience; being confronted from a caring position; re-experiencing past events in the present.
  • Analysis of transference and countertransference — the reaction to the counsellor–client relationship.

Termination of a relationship

  • The client no longer requires help.
  • The counsellor cannot provide any more benefit to the client.
  • Premature termination: termination before it is due.
  • Referral: when the client needs to be referred to another professional.
  • Follow-up.

Factors affecting counselling

  • Structure: the psychological and physical atmosphere.
  • Initiative: whether the client himself wants to change or not (difficult if the client is reluctant).
  • Settings.

Qualities of counsellor and client

Qualities of a counsellor: self-aware, honest, congruent, good communication skills, knowledgeable.

Trap — YAVIS vs HOUND clients. YAVIS = Young, Attractive, Verbal, Intelligent, Successful → tend to get better help. HOUND = Homely, Old, Unattractive, Nonverbal, Dumb → less likely to be helped.

Working in a relationship

  1. Leading: redirecting the conversation towards change.
  2. Accurate empathy — components: empathic rapport, communication attunement. Types: primary; advanced (making implicit explicit, identifying themes, connecting islands).
  3. Self-disclosure: the counsellor's problems, facts about the counsellor's role, and reaction to the client.
  4. Analysis of transference and countertransference.
