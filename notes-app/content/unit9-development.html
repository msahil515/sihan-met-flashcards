1. Developmental processes, domains & periods

The one idea. Developmental science studies change and stability across the lifespan. Scientists care not only about how people change but about the underlying stability or constancy of personality and behaviour over time.

Two kinds of change

  • Quantitative change — a change in number or amount: growth in height, weight, vocabulary, aggressive behaviour, or frequency of communication.
  • Qualitative change — a change in kind, structure, or organisation. Not "more of the same" but a different form altogether.

Three domains of development

Change and stability occur in various domains (dimensions) of the self. Scientists discuss them separately but treat development as a single unified process.

DomainWhat changes
Physical developmentGrowth of body and brain; change or stability in sensory capacities, motor skills, and health.
Cognitive developmentChange or stability in mental abilities: learning, attention, memory, language, thinking, reasoning, and creativity.
Psychosocial developmentChange and stability in emotions, personality, and social relationships.

Periods of the lifespan

The division of the lifespan into periods (infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, old age) is a social construction: a concept about the nature of reality based on societally shared perceptions or assumptions, not a fixed biological fact.

High-yield

Quantitative = change in amount (height, vocabulary). Qualitative = change in kind/structure. Three domains: physical, cognitive, psychosocial. Lifespan periods are a social construction.

2. Theories of development

The psychoanalytic viewpoint — Freud

Freud concluded that human development is a conflictual process: as biological creatures we have basic sexual and aggressive instincts that must be served, yet society dictates that many of these drives be restrained. The ways parents manage these urges in the first few years play a major role in shaping personality. Freud held personality was fixed in childhood — essentially formed by age 5 or 6.

Erikson's theory of psychosocial development

Erikson renounced Freud's idea of fixity, arguing that identity formation is a lifelong process. Personality (Erikson focused on identity) develops through eight stages as life unfolds. The outcome of each stage depends on the outcome of the previous one, and successful negotiation of each ego crisis is essential for optimal growth. He used Freud's psychosexual stages as his basis; his first five stages reflect ego crises tied to Freud's stages.

Trap

Freud: personality fixed by age 5–6, five psychosexual stages. Erikson: identity is lifelong, eight psychosocial stages built on Freud's first five. Don't merge the two.

The learning viewpoint — Watson & the operant view

Watson believed that well-learned associations between external stimuli and observable responses (called habits) are the building blocks of development. From this perspective children do not progress through distinct biologically dictated stages; development is a continuous process of behavioural change shaped by each person's unique environment, and may differ dramatically from person to person. Operant learning theory claims development depends on external stimuli (reinforcers and punishers) rather than internal forces such as instincts, drives, or maturation. Modern developmentalists agree habits can emerge and disappear over a lifetime depending on their positive or negative consequences.

The cognitive viewpoint — Jean Piaget

Piaget (1950) defined intelligence as a basic life process that helps an organism adapt to its environment. As children mature they acquire ever more complex cognitive structures. A cognitive structure — what Piaget called a scheme (or schema) — is an organised pattern of thought or action used to cope with or explain some aspect of experience. Cognitive development occurs neither solely through maturation nor solely through learning: through interaction with the environment, psychological structures become reorganised. Infants are born with simple schemata such as sucking and grasping.

Definition

Assimilation = actively organising new information so it fits with what is already perceived and thought. Accommodation = changing what is already perceived or thought so it fits new information. Learning occurs through these two basic mental operations.

3. Piaget's cognitive stages

Piaget described four stages through which all of us pass. Each new experience requires cognitive organisation or reorganisation in a mental structure (schema).

Sensorimotor stage (0–2 years)

Infants know the world through senses and motor action, building from inborn reflexes (sucking, grasping) toward intentional behaviour and, eventually, object permanence and mental representation.

Preoperational stage (2–7 years)

Marked by the appearance of the symbolic function — the ability to make one thing (a word or object) stand for or represent something else. The knowledge that an entity can stand for something other than itself is representational insight. Language is the most obvious symbolism; pretend (symbolic) play blossoms in the early preoperational period. 2½-year-olds lack dual representation — the ability to think about an object in two different ways at the same time.

Deficits in preoperational reasoning

  • Animism — attributing life and lifelike qualities to inanimate objects.
  • Egocentrism — viewing the world from one's own perspective; difficulty recognising another's point of view.
  • Centration — attending to one aspect of a situation to the exclusion of others (contrast with decentration).
  • Lack of conservation — failure to recognise that properties of an object/substance do not change when its appearance is altered superficially.
  • Lack of reversibility — inability to reverse or negate an action by mentally performing the opposite (negation).

Theory of Mind (ToM)

ToM develops during the preoperational period: children's developing concepts of mental activity — an understanding that humans are cognitive beings whose mental states are not always shared with or accessible to others. The most frequently used tool is the false-belief task, where the child must infer that another person does not possess knowledge that the child possesses (holds a false belief). Children usually make an egocentric response.

Concrete-operational stage (7–11 years)

  • Can solve conservation problems.
  • Mental seriation — mentally arranging items along a quantifiable dimension (height, weight).
  • Transitivity — recognising necessary relations among elements in a series (if A > B and B > C, then A > C).
  • Horizontal décalage — Piaget's term for uneven cognitive performance: inability to solve certain problems even though one can solve similar problems requiring the same operations (e.g., solving some conservation tasks but not others).

Formal-operational stage (11–12 years and beyond)

Formal operations are mental actions performed on ideas and propositions. The benchmark is hypothetico-deductive reasoning. Such thinkers are not restricted to previously acquired facts but can generate hypotheses, and can think inductively (from specific observations to broad generalisations). For them, what is possible is more important than what is real.

Mnemonic

Some People Can Fly — Sensorimotor, Preoperational, Concrete-operational, Formal-operational. Conservation arrives at concrete; abstract hypotheticals arrive at formal.

4. Vygotsky & the information-processing viewpoint

Vygotsky's sociocultural theory

Vygotsky focused on how culture — the beliefs, values, traditions, and skills of a social group — is transmitted across generations. Cognitive growth is a socially mediated activity: children acquire new ways of thinking through cooperative dialogues with more knowledgeable members of society. He rejected the notion that all children progress through the same universal stages, arguing the new skills children master are often specific to their culture rather than universal structures.

Four interrelated levels of analysis

  • Microgenetic — changes over very brief periods (seconds, minutes, days).
  • Ontogenetic — development of the individual over their lifetime.
  • Phylogenetic — development over evolutionary time.
  • Sociohistorical — changes that have occurred in one's culture and the values, norms, and technologies its history generated.

Core constructs

  • Tools of intellectual adaptation — each culture provides children tools that let them use basic mental functions more adaptively.
  • Zone of proximal development (ZPD) — the range of tasks too complex to be mastered alone but accomplishable with guidance and encouragement from a more skilful partner.
  • Scaffolding — the process by which an expert, instructing a novice, responds contingently to the novice's behaviour so the novice gradually increases understanding. Less able children elicit more support than more capable children.
  • Guided participation — adult–child interactions in which children's cognitions are shaped as they participate with or observe adults in culturally relevant activities (e.g., learning weaving or hunting without formal schooling). Contrast with context-independent learning ("knowledge for knowledge's sake") that prepares children for formal schooling.
Trap

Piaget = child as a lone scientist discovering through self-directed interaction; universal stages. Vygotsky = child as a social apprentice; learning is mediated by culture and more skilful partners (ZPD, scaffolding); skills are culture-specific.

The information-processing viewpoint

The human mind is likened to a computer into which information flows, is operated on, and is converted to output (answers, inferences, solutions). Theorists view cognitive development as age-related changes in the mind's hardware (brain and peripheral nervous system) and software (mental processes such as attention, perception, memory, and problem-solving strategies). Crucially, development is a continuous process that is not stage-like — involving small quantitative rather than large qualitative changes.

5. Sensorimotor & motor development

Basic trends of locomotor development

  • Cephalocaudal — development proceeds head downward (head, neck, upper extremities precede legs and lower extremities).
  • Proximodistal — development proceeds centre outward (trunk and shoulders before hands and fingers).

Three views of motor sequencing/timing

ViewpointClaim
MaturationalMotor development is the unfolding of a genetically programmed sequence in which nerves and muscles mature in a downward and outward direction.
Experiential (practice)Maturation is necessary but not sufficient; infants physically capable of sitting/crawling/walking won't be proficient without opportunities to practise.
Dynamical systemsEach new skill is a construction that emerges as infants actively reorganise existing motor capabilities into new, more complex action systems; a real world of interesting objects and events gives infants reasons (purposes, motives) to reach, sit, crawl, walk, and run. New patterns are at first tentative, inefficient, uncoordinated, then become smooth and coordinated.

Fine motor development — voluntary reaching & manipulatory skills

  • Newborns come equipped with a grasping reflex and make uncoordinated prereaches (primitive thrusts) at objects in the visual field.
  • By 2 months, reaching/grasping may seem to deteriorate as the reflexive palmar grasp disappears and prereaching occurs less often.
  • From 3 months, voluntary reaching is a learned motor skill that does not simply "unfold"; infants make in-flight corrections, improving in accuracy.
  • By ~4–5 months, an infant able to sit and reach inward across the body can grasp interesting objects with both hands.
  • The palmar grasp (clumsy, claw-like grip allowing little tactile exploration) is replaced near the end of the first year by the ulnar grasp.
  • The pincer grasp (thumb + forefinger) transforms the child from a fumbler into a skilful manipulator; by 16 months they use thumbs and forefingers to lift and explore. By the end of the second year they scribble with a crayon, copy a simple line, and build towers of five or more blocks.
  • Fingering activity may be the primary method by which 4- to 6-month-olds gain information about objects, since unimanual grasping is still poor.

6. Language: components, theories & periods

Definition

Language = a small number of individually meaningless symbols (sounds, letters, gestures) combined by agreed-on rules to produce an infinite number of messages. Communication = the process by which one organism transmits information to and influences another. Vocables = unique patterns of sound a prelinguistic infant uses to represent objects, actions, or events.

Five components of language

ComponentWhat it covers
PhonologyThe sound system of a language and rules for combining sounds to produce meaningful units. Phonemes = the basic units of sound used in a spoken language.
MorphologyThe basic units of meaning. Morphemes = smallest meaningful language units. Free morphemes stand alone (e.g., "dog"); bound morphemes cannot stand alone but change meaning when attached to a free morpheme.
SemanticsThe expressed meaning of words and sentences.
SyntaxThe structure of a language; rules specifying how words and grammatical markers combine into meaningful sentences.
PragmaticsPrinciples underlying effective, appropriate use of language in social contexts; involves sociolinguistic knowledge (culturally specific rules for how language should be structured and used).

Theories of language development

  • Learning theorists (empiricist) — language is obviously learned. Emphasise imitation and reinforcement. Skinner: adults shape a child's speech by selectively reinforcing babbling that resembles words, then withhold reinforcement until the child combines words into longer grammatical utterances. Social-learning theorists add that children acquire much linguistic knowledge by listening to and imitating older companions.
  • Nativists — language acquisition is a biologically programmed activity, possibly involving specialised processing that operates most efficiently early in childhood. Evidence: linguistic universals. Chomsky proposed a language acquisition device (LAD) — an inborn linguistic processor activated by verbal input, containing a universal grammar (rules common to all languages). Dan Slobin proposed an inborn language-making capacity (LMC) — cognitive/perceptual abilities specialised for language learning. The sensitive-period hypothesis was prompted by observations that child aphasics often recover lost language without therapy, whereas adult aphasics usually require extensive therapy.
  • Interactionists — language acquisition reflects a complex interplay among biological predispositions, cognitive development, and the unique linguistic environment (not a dedicated LAD or LMC). Like nativists they believe children are biologically prepared, but the preparation is a powerful, slowly maturing human brain. Elizabeth Bates (1999) argued grammatical speech arises out of social necessity as vocabularies grow beyond 100–200 words. Adults give children repeated opportunities to learn that conversing involves turn-taking, that things have names, and proper ways to ask and answer.
Definition

Child-directed speech (motherese) = the short, simple, high-pitched, often repetitive sentences adults use with young children. Adults provide negative evidence — responding to ungrammatical speech in ways that subtly signal an error: expansions (responding with a grammatically improved form) and recasts (responding with a nonrepetitive, grammatically correct statement).

Periods of language development

  • Prelinguistic period. Infants are well prepared: sensitive to intonational cues from birth, able to discriminate a wider variety of phonemes than adults. They begin cooing at ~2 months, babbling at 4–6 months, and by 7–10 months are already segmenting others' speech into phrases and wordlike units. They learn turn-taking and that gestures communicate. Receptive language is ahead of productive language.
  • Holophrase (one-word) period. Infants speak in holophrases (one-word utterances that carry a whole sentence's meaning) and expand vocabulary one word at a time, mostly about moving/manipulable objects. A vocabulary spurt (naming explosion) occurs between 18 and 24 months. Western children mostly develop a referential style; a smaller number, and many from social-harmony cultures, adopt an expressive style. Toddlers use social/contextual cues to fast map words, aided by processing constraints (object scope constraint, mutual exclusivity, lexical context, syntactic bootstrapping), and make semantic errors of overextension and underextension.
  • Telegraphic period. At 18–24 months toddlers produce two- and three-word sentences called telegraphic speech (omitting grammatical markers and smaller words). These follow word-order rules and express consistent semantic relations; toddlers grow sensitive to pragmatic constraints and sociolinguistic prescriptions (e.g., politeness in requests).
  • Preschool period (ages 2–5). Language becomes much more adult-like. Children add grammatical morphemes (-s plural, -ed past, -ing progressive, articles, prepositions, auxiliaries), may overregularise, and learn transformational grammar (turning declaratives into questions, negations, imperatives, relative clauses, compound sentences).
  • Middle childhood & adolescence. A period of refinement: subtle exceptions to grammatical rules, complex syntax, rapid vocabulary growth, growing metalinguistic awareness, and much better referential communication skills.

7. The concept of abnormality (psychopathology)

No single indicator is sufficient in and of itself to define abnormality. The more a person has difficulties across the following areas, the more likely they have a mental disorder.

Seven indicators of abnormality

  • Suffering — psychological pain or distress.
  • Maladaptiveness — behaviour that interferes with well-being and functioning.
  • Statistical deviancy — being statistically rare or atypical.
  • Violation of the standards of society — breaking social/moral norms.
  • Social discomfort — making others uneasy.
  • Irrationality and unpredictability.
  • Dangerousness — to self or others.
Definition

DSM-5 mental disorder = a syndrome present in an individual that involves clinically significant disturbance in behaviour, emotion regulation, or cognitive functioning, reflecting a dysfunction in biological, psychological, or developmental processes necessary for mental functioning.

8. Describing & assessing disorders

Epidemiology & prevalence terms

Mental health epidemiology studies the distribution of mental disorders across settings or population groups (epidemiology more broadly studies the distribution of diseases, disorders, and health-related behaviours).

TermMeaning
Point prevalenceEstimated proportion of actual, active cases of a disorder at a given point in time.
1-year prevalenceEveryone who experienced the disorder at any point throughout the entire year.
Lifetime prevalenceNumber of people who have had a disorder at any time in their lives (even if now recovered).
IncidenceNumber of new cases over a given period (typically 1 year). Lower than prevalence because it excludes preexisting cases.
ComorbidityPresence of two or more disorders in the same person. Especially high in people with severe forms; in the NCS-R study, half of those with a disorder rated as serious had two or more additional disorders.

Other descriptions of disorders

  • Sex ratio — percentage of males vs females with the disorder; and age of onset, which differs across disorders.
  • Course — most disorders follow an individual pattern: chronic (lasting a long time, sometimes a lifetime, e.g., schizophrenia); episodic (recovery within months then recurrence, e.g., mood disorders); time-limited (improves without treatment in a short period).
  • Onset — acute (sudden) vs insidious (gradual, over an extended period).
  • Prognosis — the anticipated course of a disorder.

The mental status examination (MSE)

Systematic observation of an individual's behaviour across five categories:

  1. Appearance and behaviour — general appearance, gait, grooming, posture, facial expression.
  2. Thought processes — rate/flow of speech (continuity of speech), delusions, hallucinations, obsessions.
  3. Mood and affect — mood is the predominant feeling state; affect is the feeling state accompanying what we say at a given point, judged "appropriate" or "inappropriate".
  4. Intellectual functioning — attention, concentration, memory.
  5. Sensorium — general awareness of surroundings; orientation to place, time, and person.
Trap

Mood vs affect: mood is the sustained, predominant feeling state (the "climate"); affect is the moment-to-moment expressed feeling (the "weather"), rated as appropriate or inappropriate. Prevalence vs incidence: prevalence counts all active cases; incidence counts only new cases.

Culture-specific disorders (culture-bound syndromes) are recognised patterns of distress tied to particular cultural contexts, reminding clinicians that the expression of abnormality is shaped by culture.

9. Classification (DSM & ICD) & causes of disorders

History & the two major systems

  • The 19th-century German physician Emil Kraepelin was the first modern theorist to develop a comprehensive classification model on the basis of distinctive features (symptoms) of abnormal behaviour patterns.
  • DSM — Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, published by the American Psychiatric Association; an outgrowth of Kraepelin's work. Introduced in 1952; the latest, DSM-5, was published in 2013. Used widely in the United States.
  • ICD — International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, published by the WHO; now in its 11th revision (ICD-11). A compendium of both mental and physical disorders and the most widely used diagnostic manual worldwide.

DSM-5 vs DSM-IV — major changes

  • Dispensed with the multiaxial framework. The old five axes comprised: Axis I & II — inventory of mental disorders; Axis III — medical conditions and diseases; Axis IV — psychosocial and environmental problems/sources of stress affecting functioning; Axis V — a rating scale (GAF) of overall level of functioning.
  • Adopted a dimensional component. DSM-5 did not abandon the categorical model but expanded it to include a dimensional component for many disorders.
  • New, reclassified, and consolidated disorders:
    • Asperger's disorder and autistic disorder reclassified under autism spectrum disorder.
    • Trichotillomania (hair-pulling) moved from impulse-control disorders to Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders.
    • Pathological (compulsive) gambling moved from impulse-control to Substance Use and Addictive Behaviors.
    • PTSD moved from anxiety disorders to a new category, Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders.

Causes of disorders — the diathesis-stress model

Definition

Diathesis = a predisposition toward developing a disorder, deriving from biological, psychological, or sociocultural causal factors. Stress = the response/experience to demands perceived as taxing or exceeding one's personal resources.

Many disorders develop when a stressor operates on a person who has a diathesis (vulnerability). The model accounting for both is the diathesis-stress (stress-diathesis) model. Protective factors such as intelligence and inoculation to stress reduce the chances of disorder. Two kinds exist: the integrative stress-diathesis model and the additive stress-diathesis model.

10. Psychoanalytic therapy

Goals & process

The two goals of Freudian psychoanalytic therapy are to make the unconscious conscious and to strengthen the ego so behaviour is based more on reality and less on instinctual cravings or irrational guilt. Analysts assume an anonymous, nonjudgmental "blank-screen" stance, avoiding self-disclosure and maintaining neutrality to foster a transference relationship in which clients make projections onto them. Analysis of transference is the very essence of therapeutic work. Counter-transference — the therapist projecting feelings onto the client — is detrimental and to be avoided.

Six basic techniques

TechniqueWhat it is
Maintaining the analytic frameworkThe range of procedural/stylistic factors: analyst's relative anonymity, neutrality and objectivity, regularity and consistency of meetings, starting/ending on time, clarity on fees, basic boundary issues.
Free associationClients say whatever comes to mind, however painful, silly, trivial, illogical, or irrelevant. Gives access to the unconscious.
InterpretationThe analyst points out, explains, and even teaches the meanings of behaviour in dreams, free association, resistances, defences, and the therapeutic relationship. Functions: enable the ego to assimilate new material and speed up uncovering further unconscious material.
Dream analysisKey procedure for uncovering unconscious material and giving insight into unresolved problems. During sleep defences are lowered and repressed feelings surface.
Analysis of resistanceResistance is anything that works against the progress of therapy — the client's reluctance to bring repressed unconscious material to awareness.
Analysis of transferenceThe transference situation is valuable because its manifestations let clients re-experience feelings that would otherwise be inaccessible.

11. Person-centred, Gestalt & existential therapy

Person-centred therapy (Carl Rogers)

Rogers held that three therapist attributes create a growth-promoting climate:

  • Congruence — genuineness, realness.
  • Unconditional positive regard — acceptance and caring.
  • Accurate empathic understanding — deeply grasping the subjective world of another.

The actualizing tendency is a directional process of striving toward realization, fulfilment, autonomy, and self-determination. Goals: help clients achieve greater independence and integration, and increase congruence. It is the therapist's attitude, not knowledge/theories/techniques, that facilitates change; the therapist is a catalyst. Immediacy (addressing what is going on between client and therapist) is highly valued. Offshoots: Motivational Interviewing (MI) by William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick (early 1980s), a humanistic, client-centred, modestly directive approach matched to the stages of change (precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance); and Person-Centred Expressive Arts Therapy (Natalie Rogers).

Gestalt therapy (Fritz Perls)

Arnie Beisser (1970) proposed the paradoxical theory of change: authentic change occurs more from being who we are than from trying to be who we are not. Key concepts:

  • Holism — "Gestalt" is German for a whole/completion, a form that cannot be separated into parts without losing its essence.
  • Field theory — the organism must be seen in its environment/context as part of a constantly changing field; emphasis on the figure (most salient experience) vs the ground (out of awareness). Therapists practise "attending to the obvious" via gestures, tone, demeanour.
  • Figure-formation process — how the individual organises experience moment to moment as something emerges from the background into focal attention.
  • Organismic self-regulation — equilibrium is "disturbed" by the emergence of a need, sensation, or interest.
  • Contact & resistances to contact — contact is made by seeing, hearing, smelling, touching, moving; boundaries both connect and separate. Resistances ("contact boundary phenomena"): introjection (uncritically accepting others' beliefs), projection (disowning aspects of self by assigning them to the environment), retroflection (turning back on ourselves what we'd do to others, or doing to ourselves what we'd want others to do), deflection (distraction/veering off), confluence (blurring the self–environment differentiation).
  • The NOW — phenomenological inquiry attends to what is occurring now; therapists ask "what" and "how" questions, rarely "why".
  • Unfinished business — uncompleted figures leave unexpressed feelings (resentment, rage, pain, anxiety, grief, guilt). The impasse (stuck point) occurs when external support is unavailable; the therapist accompanies clients through it without rescuing or frustrating them.
  • Energy and blocks to energy — blocked energy manifests as bodily tension, restricted voice, shallow breathing, avoiding contact.

Goal: greater awareness and with it greater choice (increased awareness is itself curative). Therapists work within I/Thou dialogue in a here-and-now framework, attending to body language and speech habits ("It" talk, "You" talk, questions, language that denies power, metaphors). Miriam Polster (1987) described a three-stage integration sequence: discovery, accommodation, assimilation.

Gestalt techniques

  • Experiments vs exercises — exercises are ready-made techniques; experiments grow out of the client–therapist interaction (the cornerstone of experiential learning).
  • Confrontation — inviting clients to examine incongruities, especially verbal–nonverbal gaps.
  • Internal dialogue exercise — the top dog (righteous, authoritarian, "shoulds"/"oughts", the critical parent) vs the underdog (victim: defensive, apologetic, helpless, feigning powerlessness).
  • Empty-chair technique — given by Jacob Moreno (founder of psychodrama); a vehicle for role reversal.
  • Future projection, making the rounds, the reversal exercise, the rehearsal exercise, the exaggeration exercise, staying with the feeling, and Gestalt dream work (acting out the dream in the present; each part is a projection of the self).

Existential therapy

The existential tradition balances the limits and tragic dimensions of human existence against its possibilities. Schneider and Krug (2010) name three values: the freedom to become within limits; the capacity to reflect on the meaning of our choices; the capacity to act on those choices. Sartre (1971) called the failure to accept personal responsibility inauthenticity. Existential guilt grows from a sense of incompleteness — awareness of having evaded a commitment or chosen not to choose. Authenticity is living true to one's own evaluation of a valuable existence — the courage to be who we are.

Four ways of being (Dasein)

  • Umwelt — the biological world / environment.
  • Mitwelt — "with-world"; the area of human relationships.
  • Eigenwelt — "own-world"; the relationship individuals have with themselves.
  • Überwelt — relationship with spiritual/religious values (added by van Deurzen; the first three were introduced by Binswanger).

Yalom (1980) distinguished three types of isolation: interpersonal (distance from others), intrapersonal (separating parts of oneself via defences), and existential (being separated from the world — a profound aloneness). Other themes: meaning and meaninglessness (Frankl emphasised the search for meaning) and self-transcendence (rising above immediate situation and self-interest). Authenticity is the basic goal; for May, "the aim of therapy is that the patient experiences his existence as real" — May (1958) asked not "How are you?" but "Where are you?" Phases: initial (clarifying assumptions about the world), middle (examining the source/authority of one's value system), and final (putting self-knowledge into action).

12. Behaviour therapy

Foundations — four major areas of development

AreaKey ideas
Classical conditioningUCS → UCR; UCS + NS → UCR; CS → CR. Learning by association.
Operant conditioningPositive & negative reinforcement; positive & negative punishment; schedules of reinforcement (intermittent vs fixed; ratio vs interval).
Social learning theoryTriadic reciprocal interaction (environmental factors, personal factors, individual behaviour); self-efficacy.
Cognitive behaviour therapyEmphasises the interaction among affective, behavioural, and cognitive dimensions.

Basic characteristics & assumptions

  • Focus on observable behaviour, current determinants, learning experiences that promote change, tailoring strategies to the individual, and rigorous assessment/evaluation.
  • Based on the principles and procedures of the scientific method; uses functional assessment (behavioural analysis).
  • Clients assume an active role; BT is action-oriented and educational.
  • Insight may not be necessary for behaviour change.
  • The focus is on assessing overt and covert behaviour directly, identifying the problem, and evaluating change; treatment is individually tailored.
Definition

Functional assessment / behavioural analysis uses the ABC model — Antecedents, Behaviours, Consequences — to identify the events that influence or are functionally related to a behaviour.

Techniques & procedures

  • Applied behaviour analysis (ABA) — operant techniques applied across settings: positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, extinction (withholding reinforcement from a previously reinforced response), positive punishment, negative punishment.
  • Relaxation training — Jacobson (1938) developed progressive muscle relaxation; used for stress, anxiety, asthma, headache, hypertension, insomnia, irritable bowel syndrome, panic disorder.
  • Exposure therapies — systematic confrontation with a feared stimulus (imaginal or in vivo/live). Variants of systematic desensitization: in vivo exposure (exposure to actual events), self-managed in vivo exposure (clients expose themselves when a therapist can't be present), and flooding — intense, prolonged exposure (in vivo flooding to actual stimuli; imaginal flooding in the client's imagination).
  • Systematic desensitization — based on classical conditioning, developed by Joseph Wolpe. Clients imagine successively more anxiety-arousing situations while engaging in a behaviour (relaxation) that competes with anxiety, becoming gradually desensitized. Steps (McNeil & Kyle, 2009): relaxation training → development of the anxiety hierarchy → systematic desensitization proper. Also used for anger, asthmatic attacks, insomnia, motion sickness, nightmares, sleepwalking.
  • Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) — a form of exposure therapy combining imaginal flooding, cognitive restructuring, and rapid rhythmic eye movements / bilateral stimulation for traumatic stress; developed by Francine Shapiro (2001).
  • Social skills training — psychoeducation, modeling, reinforcement, behavioural rehearsal, role playing, feedback; includes anger management and assertion training.
  • Self-modification (self-management) programs — basic steps (Watson & Tharp, 2007): selecting goals → translating goals into target behaviours → self-monitoring (behavioural diary) → working out a plan for change → evaluating the action plan. Strategies include self-monitoring, self-reward, self-contracting, stimulus control, and self-as-model. Advantage: treatment can be extended to the public.
  • Multimodal therapy (Arnold Lazarus) — an open system encouraging technical eclecticism, grounded in social learning and cognitive theory. Personality is divided into seven areas — BASIC I.D.: Behaviour, Affective responses, Sensations, Images, Cognitions, Interpersonal relationships, Drugs (biological functions, nutrition, exercise). Premise: breadth is often more important than depth.

13. REBT & Beck's cognitive therapy

Albert Ellis's Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT)

The basic assumption is that people contribute to their own psychological problems by the way they interpret events. Cognitions, emotions, and behaviours interact significantly with a reciprocal cause-and-effect relationship. REBT's basic hypothesis: our emotions stem mainly from our beliefs, evaluations, interpretations, and reactions. CBT (combining cognitive and behavioural principles in a short-term approach) has generated more empirical research than any other psychotherapy model. The focus is on working with thinking and acting rather than primarily expressing feelings; therapy is an educational process.

View of emotional disturbance & the three basic musts

It is our own repetition of early-indoctrinated irrational thoughts that keeps dysfunctional attitudes alive. Blame is at the core of most emotional disturbance. We escalate desires and preferences into dogmatic shoulds, musts, and oughts. The three basic irrational "musts":

  • "I must do well and win the approval of others for my performances."
  • "Other people must treat me considerately, fairly, kindly, and exactly the way I want."
  • "I must get what I want, when I want it; and I must not get what I don't want."
High-yield

ABCDEF framework: A (activating event) → B (belief about A) → C (emotional/behavioural consequence) → D (disputing intervention) → E (effective new philosophy) → F (new feeling). The disputing process has three components: detecting, debating, discriminating. Cognitive restructuring = replacing faulty cognitions with constructive beliefs (monitor self-talk, identify maladaptive self-talk, substitute adaptive self-talk).

Goals (Ellis, 2001b): help clients achieve unconditional self-acceptance (USA) and unconditional other-acceptance (UOA). Techniques span three classes: cognitive (disputing irrational beliefs, cognitive homework, changing one's language, psychoeducation, coping self-statements, cost-benefit analysis); emotive (rational emotive imagery, humour, role playing, shame-attacking exercises, forceful self-statements/self-dialogue); and behavioural (operant conditioning, self-management, systematic desensitization, relaxation, modeling, real-life behavioural homework).

Aaron Beck's Cognitive Therapy (CT)

Beck developed CT from his research on depression, observing that depressed clients had a negative bias in interpreting life events, producing cognitive distortions. CT sees problems as stemming from commonplace processes: faulty thinking, incorrect inferences from inadequate information, and failing to distinguish fantasy from reality. Assumptions: internal communication is accessible to introspection; clients' beliefs have highly personal meanings; these meanings can be discovered by the client.

Cognitive distortions (logical errors)

  • Arbitrary inferences — conclusions without supporting evidence; includes catastrophizing.
  • Selective abstraction — conclusions based on an isolated detail.
  • Overgeneralization — extreme beliefs from a single incident applied to dissimilar settings.
  • Magnification and minimization — perceiving a situation as greater or lesser than it deserves.
  • Personalization — relating external events to oneself without basis.
  • Labeling and mislabeling — defining identity by past imperfections/mistakes.
  • Dichotomous thinking — either-or, all-or-nothing extremes.

The frequent question is "Where is the evidence for ___?" The Socratic (inductive) questioning method leads the client through questions to become aware of thoughts, identify distortions, and find adaptive replacements. Automatic thoughts are personalised notions triggered by particular stimuli that lead to emotional responses. In depression, Beck described the cognitive triad: a negative view of (1) themselves, (2) experience (interpreting in a negative manner — selective abstraction), and (3) the future (gloomy projections). Beck (1967) designed the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). In family work, CT emphasises schema (core beliefs) and "family schemata".

Trap

Ellis (REBT) centres on irrational beliefs / the three musts and disputing (the D in ABCDEF), with a directive, even confrontational style. Beck (CT) centres on cognitive distortions and the cognitive triad, using collaborative Socratic questioning ("where is the evidence?"). Both restructure cognition; the flavour differs.

14. Transactional Analysis (Eric Berne)

TA is a psychological theory developed by Eric Berne in the 1960s explaining why we think, act, and feel as we do. Philosophical assumptions: People are OK; everyone has the capacity to think; people decide their own destiny, and these decisions can be changed.

Ego-state model (PAC) & transactions

An ego-state is a set of related behaviours, thoughts, and feelings. We all have three: Parent, Adult, Child.

  • Adult — thinking, feeling, behaving in response to the here-and-now, using all available resources.
  • Parent — behaving, thinking, feeling as one's parents did.
  • Child — returning to ways of behaving/thinking/feeling as one did as a child.

Capital letters P, A, C indicate ego-states; small letters p, a, c indicate real-life parents, adults, children. Structural analysis uses the PAC model to understand personality. A transaction is the exchange of communications between the ego-states of two people; transactional analysis proper analyses sequences of transactions. Complementary transactions (ego-states sympathetic to one another) are effective; crossed transactions occur when a response comes from a different ego-state than expected. Any signal of recognition is a stroke.

Decisional model, life-script & games

TA holds that self-defeating behaviour follows strategies decided in childhood — the best way of surviving then. Because the person is responsible for these early decisions, she can redecide. Each of us writes a life-story; the unaware version is the life-script. Parents give script-messages (how to be / not to be); negative messages are injunctions. Script analysis shows how people unawarely set up or solve problems. Distorting one's perception so it fits the script is redefining; selectively ignoring information is discounting. Permitted childhood feelings used to cover authentic feelings are racket feelings; storing one instead of expressing it is saving a stamp; a game is a repetitive sequence where both parties end up with racket feelings. Symbiosis is where a person and partner together display only three ego-states (one supplies Parent and Adult, the other Child).

Therapy & autonomy

Berne urged that the proper goal is not insight but cure, progressing through four stages: social control → symptomatic relief → transference cure → script cure. The goal of personal change is autonomy, whose components are awareness, spontaneity, and the capacity for intimacy. Client and therapist enter a contract (mutual consent, valid consideration, competency, lawful object). Therapy involves closing escape hatches (an unconditional decision never to kill/harm self or others or go crazy). The therapist's crucial function is to give permission, protection, and potency. Three schools: Classical (strengthen Adult), Redecision (do so from a Child ego-state), Cathexis (emphasis on Child as well as Adult; the client also assumes a Parent role).

15. Family & systems therapy

General systems theory

  • Feedback — the communication pattern within the units of a system, in two basic patterns: linear and circular. Two types: negative feedback (equilibrium is achieved) and positive feedback (change occurs in the system).
  • Homeostasis — systems tend to seek stability and equilibrium; a family regulates itself to maintain stability, achieved through feedback from its units.
  • Equifinality — many different ways to reach the same destination (related to complexity).

Pathological communication patterns (schizophrenia research)

  • Double bind — Bateson, Jackson, Haley & Weakland (1956): a person receives two related but contradictory messages, one often clear and the other unclear (nonverbal), creating a "no-win" paradox.
  • Marital schism & skew — Lidz and colleagues: marital schism = undermining the worth of the other parent by competing for the children's sympathy; marital skew = one parent's psychological disturbance dominates the home while the other accepts it, distorting reality for the children.

Bowen's intergenerational (family systems) theory

Based on the individual's ability to differentiate intellectual functioning from feelings. Eight core concepts:

  • Differentiation of self — being able to separate one's intellectual processes from one's feeling processes.
  • Triangulation — when two people are under stress they bring in a third member to dilute the anxiety/tension.
  • Nuclear family emotional systems — when partners have relatively low differentiation, they may project their stress onto a child (the family projection process).
  • Family projection process — projecting parental stress onto one child.
  • Emotional cutoff — managing unresolved attachment by emotional distance.
  • Multigenerational transmission process — after six or seven generations of increasingly fused couples, an observer could find highly dysfunctional families vulnerable to stress and lacking differentiation.
  • Sibling position — Bowen believed birth order affects functioning within the family.
  • Societal regression — the broader lack of differentiation between thoughts and feelings at a societal level.

Techniques: evaluation interview (any combination of members; a single member can suffice if willing to work on differentiating their own feelings rather than blaming others), genograms, interpretation (information from genograms interpreted so members understand dynamics; the therapist maintains objectivity to see patterns reflecting the family of origin), and detriangulation (Bowen worked with the healthiest, most differentiated member to make changes; he tried to separate parts of a triangle directly).

Structural family therapy (Salvador Minuchin)

Traces to the early 1960s with delinquent boys from poor families at the Wiltwyck School in New York. Central idea: an individual's symptoms are best understood from interactional patterns within a family. Goals: (1) reduce symptoms of dysfunction; (2) bring about structural change by modifying transactional rules and establishing more appropriate boundaries. Structural-strategic approaches were the most-used models; interventions became synonymous with the systems approach — joining, boundary setting, unbalancing, reframing, ordeals, paradoxical interventions, and enactments.

Concepts

  • Family structure — rules developed over years determining who interacts with whom (temporary or long-standing).
  • Family subsystems — husband–wife, parents–children, siblings.
  • Boundary permeability — rules about who participates in interactions and how.
  • Alignments and coalitions — alignments = ways members join with or oppose each other; coalitions = alliances against another member. Minuchin uses triangle more specifically than Bowen: a coalition where "each parent demands that the child side with him against the other parent".

Techniques

  • Family mapping — diagrams of current ways families relate.
  • Enactment — instructing the family to act out a conflict so the therapist works with it in the present.
  • Intensity — repeating the message or changing interaction length to facilitate change.
  • Changing boundaries — rearranging seating, changing distance, unbalancing the structure (e.g., siding with a parent in an enmeshed family to restore parental power).
  • Reframing — offering a different explanation so a constructive change can occur.
Trap

Bowen vs Minuchin: Bowen is intergenerational (differentiation of self, eight concepts, genograms, detriangulation across generations). Minuchin is structural and present-focused (family structure, subsystems, boundaries, enactment, unbalancing). Both use "triangle", but Minuchin means it more narrowly (a parent–parent–child coalition).

16. Successful aging: biological theories

Definition

Life span = the upper boundary of life, the maximum number of years an individual can live (for humans ~120–125 years). Life expectancy = the number of years that will probably be lived by the average person born in a particular year.

Genes matter for reaching extreme old age, but other factors do too; what usually contributes to surviving into the 100s is the ability to cope successfully with stress.

Genetic-programming theories

TheoryClaim
Cellular clock theory (Leonard Hayflick, 1977)Cells can divide a maximum of ~75–80 times (the Hayflick limit); ability to divide decreases with age. The answer to why cells stop may lie at the tips of chromosomes — telomeres (DNA sequences that cap chromosomes) shorten with each division; after ~70–80 replications they are dramatically reduced and the cell can no longer reproduce. Injecting the enzyme telomerase can substantially extend cell life.
Programmed senescence theoryAging results from the sequential switching on and off of certain genes. Senescence is the time when age-associated deficits become evident.
Immunological theoryA programmed decline in immune-system function leads to increased vulnerability to infectious disease and thus to aging and death.
Hormonal stress theoryAging in the body's hormonal system can lower resilience to stress and increase disease likelihood. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is a main regulatory system for responding to stress; with aging, stress hormones remain elevated longer than in youth. Prolonged elevations are linked to cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, and hypertension.

Variable-rate theories

  • Free-radical theory — normal metabolism produces unstable oxygen molecules (free radicals) that ricochet around cells, damaging DNA and other structures, leading to disorders including cancer and arthritis.
  • Wear-and-tear theory — cells and tissues have vital parts that simply wear out.
  • Rate-of-living theory — the greater an organism's rate of metabolism, the shorter its life span.
  • Autoimmune theory — the immune system becomes confused and attacks the body's own cells.

17. Socioemotional aging & late-life disorders

Theories of socioemotional development in aging

TheoryCore idea
Disengagement theoryTo cope effectively, older adults should gradually withdraw from society. Older adults develop increasing self-preoccupation, decrease emotional ties, and show less interest in society's affairs; by disengaging they were believed to enjoy enhanced life satisfaction. (Largely discredited but examinable.)
Activity theoryThe more active and involved older adults are, the more likely they are to be satisfied with their lives; many achieve greater satisfaction by continuing their middle-adulthood roles into late adulthood.
Socioemotional selectivity theory (Laura Carstensen)Older adults become more selective about social networks: placing high value on emotional satisfaction, they spend more time with familiar, rewarding individuals and consciously decrease total social contacts. Two classes of goals: (1) knowledge-related and (2) emotional.
Selective Optimization with Compensation (SOC) (Paul Baltes & colleagues)Successful aging is linked with three factors: Selection (reduced capacity requires reducing performance in most life domains), Optimization (maintaining performance in some areas through continued practice and new technologies), and Compensation (when tasks demand a level beyond current potential — e.g., memorising fast, reacting quickly when driving — older adults compensate, especially under high mental/physical demand).
High-yield

Disengagement = withdraw. Activity = stay engaged. Socioemotional selectivity (Carstensen) = prune to emotionally rewarding relationships. SOC (Baltes) = Select, Optimize, Compensate.

Mental problems in old age

Definition

Dementia = the general term for physiologically caused cognitive and behavioural decline sufficient to affect daily life.

  • Alzheimer's disease (AD) — a progressive, degenerative brain disorder. The brain shows excessive neurofibrillary tangles (twisted masses of collapsed protein fibres) and large waxy chunks of amyloid plaque (nonfunctioning tissue formed in the spaces between neurons by an abnormal variant of the protein beta amyloid).
  • Parkinson's disease — the second most common disorder involving progressive neurological degeneration, characterised by tremor, stiffness, slowed movement, and unstable posture.
