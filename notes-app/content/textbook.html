C.2 Sensation vs perception, and psychophysics

  • Sensation = the raw signal a receptor sends up (bottom-up). Perception = the brain organising and interpreting it (adds top-down expectation, context, schema). Same retinal image, two perceptions = perception > sensation.
  • Absolute threshold: the smallest intensity detectable 50% of the time.
  • Difference threshold = JND (just noticeable difference): the smallest change you can detect 50% of the time.
  • Sensory adaptation: constant unchanging stimulus, receptors stop responding (you stop feeling your watch).
LawWhat it saysThe hook
Weber's lawThe JND is a constant proportion of the original stimulus, not a fixed amountAdd 1 candle to 1, you notice; add 1 to 100, you do not
Fechner's lawSensation grows with the logarithm of stimulus intensityBuilt psychophysics on Weber's finding
Stevens' power lawSensation is a power function of intensity (fits more senses than Fechner)The modern refinement
Signal Detection Theory: detection is not just sensitivity, it is a decision. Four outcomes: Hit (signal there, said yes), Miss (signal there, said no), False alarm (no signal, said yes), Correct rejection (no signal, said no). d′ = true sensitivity; the criterion (β) = how willing you are to say "yes". A tired or motivated observer shifts criterion, not sensitivity.
TRAP: Weber = constant ratio (JND). Fechner = log. Do not say Weber gives a fixed amount. SDT separates sensitivity (d′) from response bias (criterion); a question about "willingness to report" is about the criterion, not d′.

C.3 Gestalt organisation, constancies, depth cues

  • Gestalt grouping laws: figure-ground, proximity (near things group), similarity (alike things group), closure (fill the gap), continuity / good continuation (smooth lines), common fate (things moving together group), Prägnanz (we see the simplest possible form). The umbrella principle is Prägnanz, "the law of good figure".
  • Perceptual constancies: size, shape, colour, brightness stay stable even as the retinal image changes. A door is still rectangular when open (shape constancy).
  • Depth cues: binocular = retinal disparity + convergence (need both eyes, work near). Monocular = relative size, interposition/overlap, linear perspective, texture gradient, aerial perspective, motion parallax, light & shadow (work with one eye, mostly for distance).
TRAP: Retinal disparity and convergence are the only two binocular cues; everything else is monocular. Motion parallax is monocular even though it sounds complex.

C.6 Emotion: the theories side by side

Every emotion question turns on the order of three events: the stimulus, the bodily arousal, and the felt emotion. Learn the sequence each theory claims.

TheoryThe sequence it claims
James-LangeStimulus → bodily arousal → emotion. "We are afraid because we tremble." Emotion follows the body.
Cannon-BardStimulus → arousal AND emotion at the same time (thalamus routes to both). Body and feeling are simultaneous, not causal.
Schachter-Singer two-factorStimulus → arousal → cognitive label of that arousal → emotion. Same arousal, different label, different emotion (the adrenaline study).
Lazarus appraisalStimulus → cognitive appraisal comes first → arousal + emotion. Thinking precedes feeling.
Facial feedbackThe face itself feeds emotion: holding a smile can lift mood.
TRAP: James-Lange = body first, then emotion. Cannon-Bard = simultaneous. Schachter-Singer adds a cognitive label to arousal. Lazarus puts appraisal first. A stem about "interpreting your racing heart as either fear or excitement" is Schachter-Singer.

C.7 Memory

  • Multi-store (Atkinson-Shiffrin): sensory register (iconic ~0.5 s, echoic ~2–4 s) → short-term (~7±2 items, ~15–30 s, lost by displacement/decay) → long-term (effectively unlimited). Rehearsal moves STM → LTM.
  • Working memory (Baddeley): the central executive directs three slaves: the phonological loop (verbal), the visuospatial sketchpad (visual/spatial), and the episodic buffer (binds + links to LTM). This replaced the passive STM "box".
  • LTM types: Explicit/declarative (conscious) splits into episodic (events, what/where/when) and semantic (facts). Implicit/non-declarative includes procedural (skills), priming, and conditioning.
  • Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart): deeper, semantic processing yields better recall than shallow (visual/phonemic) processing.
  • Serial position: first items recalled well (primacy, rehearsed into LTM) and last items recalled well (recency, still in STM). A delay before recall wipes recency but not primacy.
  • Forgetting: decay (trace fades), interference, cue-dependent (encoding-specificity, state- and context-dependent recall), motivated forgetting (repression). Ebbinghaus = the forgetting curve (steep then levels off).
  • Memory is reconstructive: Bartlett's schemas ("War of the Ghosts"), Loftus's misinformation effect (leading questions reshape eyewitness memory).
TRAP: Proactive interference = old learning blocks new ("pro" = forward, old pushes ahead). Retroactive = new learning blocks old ("retro" = backward). Recall (produce it) is harder than recognition (pick it out). Episodic = tied to a time and place; semantic = a free-floating fact.

C.9 Statistics & research the common section quietly tests

  • Central tendency: mean (pulled toward the tail by skew/outliers), median (robust, best for skewed income-type data), mode (the only one usable for nominal data). In a positive (right) skew the order is mode < median < mean; in a negative skew it reverses. The mean is dragged toward the longer tail.
  • Normal curve: symmetrical, mean = median = mode. 68 / 95 / 99.7 of cases fall within 1 / 2 / 3 SD. A z-score = how many SDs a value sits from the mean.
  • Correlation: r ranges −1 to +1; the sign is direction, the absolute value is strength. r = 0 means no linear relationship. r² (coefficient of determination) = proportion of variance shared. Correlation does not equal causation (third-variable, directionality problems).
  • Reliability (consistency) vs validity (accuracy). Reliability types: test-retest, inter-rater, internal consistency (Cronbach's α, split-half). Validity types: content, construct, criterion (concurrent + predictive). A test can be reliable but not valid (a consistent bathroom scale that reads 5 kg heavy). It cannot be valid without being reliable.
  • Levels of measurement (NOIR): Nominal (categories), Ordinal (ranked), Interval (equal gaps, no true zero, e.g. Celsius/IQ), Ratio (true zero, e.g. weight, reaction time).
  • Variables: the independent variable is manipulated; the dependent variable is measured. A confound varies with the IV and offers a rival explanation. Random assignment (to groups) controls confounds; random sampling gives generalisability. They are not the same thing.
TRAP: Reliable ≠ valid: consistency is not accuracy. In a positively skewed distribution the mean is the highest of the three averages (pulled right). Random assignment ≠ random sampling. Interval has no true zero; ratio does.

1.2 The action potential (the single most testable neurophysiology topic)

An AP is a ~1 ms electrical pulse travelling down the axon. The whole story is "Na+ in, K+ out": Na+ rushing in depolarises; K+ leaving repolarises.

  • Resting membrane potential ~ −70 mV. Set by K+ leak channels (membrane ~50× more permeable to K+), NOT by the Na/K pump. Threshold ~ −55 mV.
  • Na/K pump: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per ATP. Electrogenic. Maintains the gradient but does not set RMP.
  • Phase 1 Depolarisation: voltage-gated Na+ channels open → Na+ in → spike to ~ +30 mV.
  • Phase 2 Repolarisation: Na+ channels inactivate, voltage-gated K+ channels open (slower) → K+ out.
  • Phase 3 Hyperpolarisation: K+ channels stay open briefly → undershoot below −70 mV.
  • Phase 4 Return: K+ channels close, Na/K pump restores ions.
Ion concentrations: "K is in, Na is out, Cl is out, Ca is way out." Equilibrium potentials: K+ −90 mV, Na+ +60 mV, Cl− −65 mV, Ca²⁺ +120 mV.

All-or-none law: once threshold is reached the AP fires at constant amplitude; stimulus intensity is coded by frequency, not amplitude.

Refractory periods: Absolute = Na+ channels inactivated, no stimulus however strong can fire another AP (ensures one-way propagation). Relative = membrane hyperpolarised (K+ still open), a stronger stimulus can fire one.

TRAP: Inactivated ≠ closed. The Na+ channel has 3 states: closed (resting), open (during AP), inactivated (after, "ball and chain" blocks the pore). Resets only after repolarisation.

Propagation: continuous (unmyelinated C fibres, 0.5–2 m/s) vs saltatory (myelinated, AP jumps between Nodes of Ranvier, up to 120 m/s in A-alpha). Conduction velocity rises with myelination > axon diameter > temperature. Demyelination (MS, Guillain-Barré) slows/blocks conduction.

Synaptic transmission: AP arrives → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, Ca²⁺ enters → vesicles fuse (SNARE proteins, synaptotagmin = Ca²⁺ sensor) → NT released → binds postsynaptic receptors → EPSP (Na+/Ca²⁺ in, depolarising) or IPSP (Cl− in or K+ out, hyperpolarising) → cleared by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion. Summation (spatial + temporal) at the axon hillock decides if a new AP fires.

TRAP: Ca²⁺ is the trigger ion for NT release at the terminal. Without Ca²⁺ entry, no transmitter is released.

1.3a Neurotransmitters — the deep note, one per transmitter

The table above is the cheat-sheet. This is the full read: for each transmitter — what it is, where it's made, its receptors, what it does in brain and body, what too much or too little looks like, and the drugs that hit it. If a question asks "which NT" or "which drug," the answer is in one of these boxes.

The two-minute frame: Glutamate is the gas (excitatory), GABA is the brake (inhibitory) — together they run ~90% of fast CNS signalling. The monoamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin, plus histamine) are the slow, mood/arousal modulators that nearly every psych drug targets. ACh straddles muscle, the parasympathetic body, and memory. Everything else (peptides, glycine, gases) fills in the edges.

ReceptorsSeven families (5-HT1–7), almost all GPCRs. 5-HT3 is the only ionotropic one (cation channel → nausea/vomiting). 5-HT1A (anxiety/depression autoreceptor), 5-HT2A (psychedelics; atypical-antipsychotic target).
Cleared byReuptake via SERT, then MAO → 5-HIAA (the urinary metabolite).
Brain & bodyMood, sleep, appetite/satiety, impulse control, temperature; in the body: gut motility, vasoconstriction, platelet aggregation.
Too muchSerotonin syndrome (agitation, hyperthermia, clonus, autonomic instability — often SSRI + MAOI or + tramadol); carcinoid syndrome.
Too littleDepression, anxiety, OCD, increased aggression/impulsivity.
Drugs that hit itSSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline), SNRIs (venlafaxine), TCAs, MAOIs; buspirone (5-HT1A partial agonist); triptans (5-HT1B/1D, migraine); ondansetron (5-HT3 block, antiemetic); LSD/psilocybin (5-HT2A agonists); atypical antipsychotics (5-HT2A antagonism).
TRAP: 5-HT3 is the odd one out — it's the only ligand-gated ion channel in the serotonin family, and blocking it (ondansetron) stops vomiting. Every other 5-HT receptor is a G-protein receptor.

⑤ Acetylcholine (ACh) — muscle, parasympathetic, memory

AspectDetail
What it isAn ester; the first neurotransmitter ever discovered (Otto Loewi's "Vagusstoff").
Where it's madeCholine + acetyl-CoA via choline acetyltransferase (ChAT). Found at the neuromuscular junction, all autonomic ganglia, all parasympathetic postganglionic neurons (+ sympathetic to sweat glands), and the basal forebrain (nucleus basalis of Meynert) for memory.
ReceptorsNicotinic (ionotropic, ligand-gated Na⁺/K⁺ — NMJ, ganglia, CNS) and Muscarinic (M1–M5, GPCR — parasympathetic targets and CNS).
Cleared byAcetylcholinesterase → choline + acetate (choline is recycled).
Brain & bodyMuscle contraction (NMJ), "rest and digest" parasympathetic tone, arousal, attention, and learning/memory.

Too littleAlzheimer's (degeneration of basal-forebrain cholinergic neurons → memory loss); myasthenia gravis (autoantibodies vs nicotinic AChR → fatigable weakness).
Drugs that hit itAChE inhibitors: donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine (Alzheimer's); pyridostigmine/neostigmine (myasthenia); physostigmine. Anticholinergics: atropine, scopolamine, benztropine. Neuromuscular blockers: curare, succinylcholine.
TRAP: "Dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter" = anticholinergic (muscarinic-block) toxicity. The opposite — wet everywhere (SLUDGE) — is cholinergic excess. Atropine treats the wet one.

1.7 Sensory systems

  • Vision: rods (peripheral, low light, no colour) + cones (foveal, colour, acuity). Cones densest at the fovea centralis. Optic disc = blind spot. Pathway: retina → optic nerve → chiasm (nasal fibres cross) → optic tract → LGN → V1. Theories: trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz, retinal) + opponent-process (Hering, post-retinal). Dorsal stream "where/how" (parietal); ventral stream "what" (temporal).
  • Audition: outer → middle (malleus, incus, stapes) → cochlea → hair cells on basilar membrane → CN VIII → cochlear nuclei → MGN → Heschl's gyrus. Place theory (Békésy): base = high frequencies, apex = low; best for high. Frequency theory: firing rate = frequency, works <500 Hz. Volley principle bridges mid-range.
  • Somatosensation: dorsal column-medial lemniscus (touch, vibration, proprioception, decussates at medulla) vs spinothalamic (pain, temp, decussates at spinal level of entry).
  • Taste: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami. CN VII / IX / X. Insula = primary gustatory cortex.
  • Smell: olfactory epithelium → bulb → piriform cortex (no thalamus relay), closest to limbic system.

1.11 Sleep (full primer)

Sleep = NREM (3 stages) + REM. One cycle ~ 90 min; 4–6 cycles/night. N3 dominates the first half; REM dominates the second half and lengthens through the night.

  • Neural control: sleep pressure from adenosine (caffeine blocks adenosine receptors); VLPO = GABAergic "sleep switch"; wake driven by the ARAS (histamine, orexin, NE, 5-HT, ACh). SCN = master circadian clock, entrained by light via the retinohypothalamic tract → pineal → melatonin in darkness. REM-ON = cholinergic (PPT/LDT); REM-OFF = aminergic (NE, 5-HT).
  • Memory: declarative consolidated in NREM (delta replay); procedural in REM + spindles; emotional in REM.
  • Disorders: Narcolepsy = orexin/hypocretin deficiency; cataplexy is pathognomonic. RBD = loss of atonia in REM, older men, α-synucleinopathy warning (Parkinson/Lewy body). RLS = urge to move legs at rest (dopamine/iron). Kleine-Levin = the 3 H's (hypersomnia + hyperphagia + hypersexuality), young males. Insomnia DSM-5: 3 nights/wk × 3 months.

1.12 Emotion, arousal, learning & LTP

  • Theories of emotion: James-Lange (body first, "we feel sad because we cry"); Cannon-Bard (body + emotion simultaneously, thalamic); Schachter-Singer (two-factor: arousal + cognitive label); Lazarus (appraisal precedes emotion).
  • Yerkes-Dodson law: inverted-U of arousal vs performance. Optimum is lower for complex tasks, higher for simple/well-learned tasks.
  • Memory substrates: hippocampus (declarative consolidation), amygdala (emotional/fear), striatum (procedural/habit), cerebellum (motor conditioning). LTP strengthens synapses (NMDA receptor critical, Ca²⁺ entry, AMPA insertion); LTD weakens them.
  • Papez circuit: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary → anterior thalamus → cingulate → entorhinal → hippocampus.

2. Developmental Psychology MET 15 Q · NIMHANS 10 Q

2.1 Foundational principles

  • Cephalocaudal (head → toe) · Proximodistal (centre → periphery).
  • Critical period = rigid biological window, lost if missed (Lorenz imprinting). Sensitive period = optimal but flexible (language acquisition).
  • Continuity vs discontinuity; nature vs nurture (interactionist consensus); active vs passive child (bidirectionality).

Prenatal stages: Germinal (0–2 wk), Embryonic (2–8 wk, most vulnerable to teratogens, organogenesis), Fetal (8 wk–birth, growth). Teratogens: alcohol (FAS, most common preventable ID), tobacco, thalidomide (limbs), rubella (1st trimester), lead/mercury, Zika (microcephaly).

2.3 Vygotsky — sociocultural

  • ZPD = gap between what a child can do alone vs with help from a more knowledgeable other (MKO).
  • Scaffolding = temporary tailored support, withdrawn as competence grows.
  • Private speech → inner speech, guides cognition. Language is the master tool, internalised from social interaction.
TRAP: "Scaffolding" is Bruner's term, applied to Vygotsky's ZPD. Vygotsky's own terms are ZPD and MKO.
One line: Piaget = child as lone scientist (development → learning). Vygotsky = child as apprentice (learning → development, mediated by culture).

4. Psychological Assessment & Tests MET 15 Q · NIMHANS 10 Q
The whole map: a psychological test is a measuring instrument that must be (a) consistent = reliability and (b) accurate = validity. Everything else is a way to estimate one of those, interpret scores against norms, or an example of a real test.

4.1 Item analysis

  • Difficulty index p = proportion who got the item right (0–1). Sweet spot 0.50 for free-response, ~0.625 for a 4-option MCQ (halfway between guessing 0.25 and ceiling). p > 0.80 too easy, < 0.20 too hard.
  • Discrimination index D = p(top 27%) − p(bottom 27%), range −1 to +1. D > 0.40 good. Negative D = mis-keyed/ambiguous item, discard immediately.

4.2 Reliability (consistency)

Classical test theory: Observed = True + Error. Reliability = proportion of variance that is true.

  • Test-retest (across time; standard window 2–4 wks).
  • Parallel forms (across forms).
  • Split-half + Spearman-Brown correction (r_full = 2·r_half / (1 + r_half)); Cronbach α (≥0.7 acceptable, ≥0.8 good) = mean of all possible split-halves; KR-20 for dichotomous (0/1) items.
  • Inter-rater: Cohen κ (categorical, Landis & Koch: 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81+ almost perfect), ICC (continuous).
  • SEM = SD·√(1 − r_xx). Higher reliability → smaller SEM → tighter confidence band around a score.
TRAP: Cronbach α is NOT a measure of unidimensionality — a test can have high α and still measure two constructs. And κ is NOT internal consistency.

4.3 Validity (accuracy)

  • Content: covers the domain (expert panels, Lawshe's CVR). Face validity = looks valid to a layperson, the weakest form.
  • Criterion: Concurrent (criterion now) vs Predictive = Postponed (criterion later).
  • Construct: convergent (correlates with similar) + discriminant (not with unrelated) + nomological network. Campbell & Fiske's multitrait-multimethod matrix (MTMM, 1959).
Validity ceiling: max validity = √(reliability). Unreliable tests cannot be strongly valid. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

5.7 Learning & conditioning

FamilyTerms
Classical (Pavlovian)Extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination

Cognitive / socialLatent (Tolman), insight (Köhler), observational (Bandura)

  • Shaping = reinforce successive approximations of a new behaviour. Chaining = link existing behaviours (each is the SD for the next; backward chaining preferred clinically). Fading = remove the prompt (extinction removes the reinforcer). Prompting hierarchy: physical → modelling → gestural → verbal → visual.
  • Extinction: watch for the extinction burst; extinction suppresses, doesn't erase. Spontaneous recovery proves the memory is intact. Generalization is the default; discrimination is trained by differential reinforcement.
  • Schedules: VR = highest, steadiest rate, most resistant to extinction (gambling). VI = steady moderate (checking phone). FR = post-reinforcement pause then burst (piece-work). FI = scallop (studying before a weekly quiz). Resistance: VR > VI > FR > FI > continuous.
  • Latent learning (Tolman, cognitive map — learning without reinforcement, shown later). Insight learning (Köhler's Sultan, sudden "aha"). Observational learning (Bandura Bobo doll; 4 processes ARRM: Attention → Retention → Reproduction → Motivation; vicarious reinforcement). Habituation vs sensitization (Kandel, Aplysia, non-associative).
Escape vs avoidance (your mock guess): Escape = behaviour terminates an aversive stimulus that is already present (turn off a loud alarm). Avoidance = behaviour prevents the aversive stimulus from starting (leave before the alarm). Both are negative reinforcement.

6.3 Hypothesis testing, errors & p-values

  • α = type I rate (false positive, conventionally 0.05). β = type II rate (false negative). Power = 1 − β (aim ≥ 0.80; rises with larger n, larger effect, larger α, lower variability, one-tailed).

  • 95% CI: if repeated, 95% of intervals would contain the true parameter. Excludes 0 (difference) or 1 (OR/RR) → significant at p < 0.05.

Diagnostic stats: Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN), high-sensitivity test rules a condition OUT when negative (SnNout). Specificity = TN/(TN+FP), rules a condition IN when positive (SpPin). PPV/NPV depend on prevalence; likelihood ratios don't. ROC plots sensitivity vs (1−specificity).

TRAPS: Cohort can be prospective or retrospective; case-control is always retrospective. Random sampling (external validity) ≠ random assignment (internal validity). r = 0 means no linear relationship (a U-shape has r ≈ 0). A large biased sample is precisely biased.

7. Language & Communication Skills MET 15 Q · NIMHANS 5 Q · highest fast-marks ratio

7.1 Subject-verb agreement

The verb agrees with the subject in number, not the nearest noun.

  • Each, every, either, neither, one, anyone, everyone, nobody → singular ("Each of the participants was given…").
  • Either/or, neither/nor → verb agrees with the nearer subject ("Neither the patient nor the family members were informed").
  • "A number of" = plural; "the number of" = singular. None defaults singular in formal writing.
  • "Data are" — data is technically plural (datum singular); treat as plural in exam contexts.
  • Singular-looking: news, mathematics, physics, politics, statistics. Plural-only: scissors, trousers, glasses.

7.2 Voice & conditionals

7.3 Punctuation & prepositions

  • Restrictive vs non-restrictive clauses: non-restrictive (extra info, removable) → commas + "who"; restrictive (defines which) → no commas, "that". "The patient, who was anxious, refused" (one patient) vs "The patient who was anxious refused" (picks out one of several).
  • Semicolon joins two independent clauses; colon introduces a list/explanation. It's = it is; its = possessive.
  • Prepositions: insist on, depend on, comply with, consist of, refer to, suffer from, object to, accuse of, married to (not with), prefer X to Y, superior/inferior to (not than), discuss (no preposition). "Between you and me."

7.4 Modifiers, parallelism & common corrections

  • Dangling modifier: "Walking down the street, the building looked tall" (the building isn't walking). Parallelism: "reading, writing, and swimming" (not "to swim").
  • Like (before a noun) vs as (before a clause). Who (subject) vs whom (object). Less (uncountable) vs fewer (countable). Between (two) vs among (3+). "The reason is that" (not "because"). Predicate adjective follows a linking verb ("The data were inconclusive").

9.1a Theorist detail — the big ones explained

Not just the name → the setup, the finding, the number, the trap. Social psych first since that is where the named-experiment questions cluster.

TheoristWhat they actually did · the trap
Social psychology
AschConformity. Line-judgement task: pick which of 3 lines matches a standard. Confederates deliberately gave a wrong answer; about 75% of real subjects conformed at least once, ~33% on average. Conformity is normative (fit in / avoid disapproval), not informational. Trap: Asch = group peer pressure; Milgram = authority. Conformity dropped sharply with one dissenting ally.
MilgramObedience. "Teacher" told by experimenter to shock a "learner" for wrong answers, up to 450V. 65% went to the maximum shock. Showed ordinary people obey a legitimate authority. Trap: obedience = following an authority's order; conformity (Asch) = matching peers.
ZimbardoStanford Prison Experiment. Students randomly assigned guard/prisoner roles; "guards" turned abusive fast, study stopped after 6 days. Power of the situation/assigned role over personality (deindividuation). Trap: situational, not dispositional.
SherifRobbers Cave / realistic conflict theory. Two boys' camp groups; competition bred hostility, and superordinate goals (shared tasks neither group could solve alone) reduced it. Contact alone was not enough.
TajfelSocial identity theory + minimal group paradigm. Even arbitrary grouping (e.g. coin toss) produced in-group favouritism. Identity comes from group membership; basis of prejudice.
FestingerCognitive dissonance + social comparison theory. Festinger & Carlsmith: people paid $1 to lie about a boring task changed their attitude more than those paid $20 (less external justification → more internal change). Trap: the smaller reward causes the bigger attitude shift.
Latane & DarleyBystander effect / diffusion of responsibility (Kitty Genovese). More bystanders → less likely any one helps. Steps: notice → interpret as emergency → assume responsibility → help.
Lewin"Father of social psychology." Field theory (B = f(P,E): behaviour is a function of person and environment); leadership styles; unfreeze–change–refreeze model of change.
HeiderAttribution theory + balance theory. We explain behaviour by internal (dispositional) vs external (situational) causes. Related: fundamental attribution error = over-blaming the person, under-weighting the situation.
KelleyCovariation model of attribution: we judge cause using consensus, consistency, distinctiveness.
BemSelf-perception theory: we infer our own attitudes by observing our own behaviour (the reverse of dissonance).
Learning
PavlovClassical conditioning. Neutral stimulus (bell) paired with food (UCS) becomes a conditioned stimulus producing salivation (CR). Terms: acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation, discrimination.
WatsonBehaviourism founder. Little Albert: conditioned a baby to fear a white rat (paired with a loud noise); fear generalised to furry things.
ThorndikeLaw of effect (cat puzzle box): responses followed by satisfaction are stamped in. Precursor to operant conditioning.
SkinnerOperant conditioning. Behaviour shaped by consequences. Reinforcement schedules: variable-ratio gives the highest, most extinction-resistant response rate. Positive/negative reinforcement both increase behaviour; punishment decreases it.
BanduraSocial learning / Bobo doll: children imitated aggression they observed, no direct reward needed. Also self-efficacy and reciprocal determinism.
TolmanLatent learning + cognitive maps (purposive behaviourism). Tolman & Honzik maze: the group given a reward only from day 11 suddenly matched the always-rewarded group, proving they'd learned the maze all along without reinforcement. Behaviour is goal-directed; animals build a mental "map." Trap: learning ≠ performance.
RotterSocial learning theory + locus of control. Behaviour potential = expectancy × reinforcement value. Internal LOC ("I control my outcomes") vs external LOC ("luck/fate/others do"); measured by his I–E Scale. Cognitive bridge between behaviourism and personality.
Memory
EbbinghausForgetting curve using nonsense syllables; most forgetting happens fast then levels off. Also the spacing effect.
Miller"Magic number 7 ± 2": short-term memory holds about 5–9 chunks.
BaddeleyWorking memory model (4 parts): central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer. Replaced the single STM box.
TulvingLong-term memory split: episodic (events) vs semantic (facts) vs procedural (skills).
LoftusMisinformation effect: leading questions distort eyewitness memory ("smashed" vs "hit" changed speed estimates). Memory is reconstructive.
BartlettReconstructive memory + schemas ("War of the Ghosts"): recall is rebuilt to fit existing schemas, not replayed.
Development
Piaget4 cognitive stages: sensorimotor (object permanence), preoperational (egocentrism, no conservation), concrete operational (conservation), formal operational (abstract). Assimilation vs accommodation.
VygotskySociocultural: learning is social. Zone of proximal development = gap between what a child can do alone vs with help; scaffolding.
Erikson8 psychosocial stages, each a crisis (e.g. trust vs mistrust, identity vs role confusion in adolescence). Spans the whole lifespan.
KohlbergMoral development: 3 levels (pre-conventional, conventional, post-conventional), 6 stages. Trap: Gilligan criticised it as male-biased and added the ethic of care.
Bowlby / AinsworthAttachment. Ainsworth's Strange Situation → secure, anxious-ambivalent, avoidant (+ disorganised). Harlow's monkeys: contact comfort beats food.
Personality
FreudPsychoanalysis: id–ego–superego; 5 psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital); defence mechanisms; unconscious.
JungAnalytical psychology: collective unconscious, archetypes, introversion/extraversion.
AdlerIndividual psychology: inferiority complex, striving for superiority, birth order.
Eysenck / Cattell / AllportTrait theorists: Eysenck = PEN (3 dims); Cattell = 16 PF; Allport = cardinal/central/secondary. Big Five (OCEAN) is the dominant model now.
Rogers / MaslowHumanistic: Rogers = client-centred, unconditional positive regard, congruence; Maslow = hierarchy of needs → self-actualisation.
Intelligence & therapy
SpearmanTwo-factor: general g + specific s; invented factor analysis.
Gardner / SternbergGardner = multiple intelligences (8); Sternberg = triarchic (analytical, creative, practical).
Binet / Terman / WechslerBinet = first practical test (mental age); Terman = Stanford-Binet, IQ ratio; Wechsler = WAIS/WISC, deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15).
Beck / EllisBeck = cognitive triad + CBT; Ellis = REBT, the ABC model. Both target distorted thinking.
Selye / LazarusSelye = General Adaptation Syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion); Lazarus = cognitive appraisal of stress (primary/secondary).
