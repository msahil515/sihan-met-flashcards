In the early 1950s Eugene Aserinsky (1921 to 1998) was a graduate student in physiology at the University of Chicago, nearly thirty years old, short of money and living with his wife and young son in a converted army barracks. His supervisor, Nathaniel Kleitman (1895 to 1999), was the most senior sleep researcher in the world, and he had given Aserinsky a modest job: watch the eyes of sleepers and see what they do. Most scientists of the day thought that sleep was simply the brain switching off, a long quiet night of nothing, so the project did not look promising. Aserinsky found an old, temperamental recording machine in a basement, repaired it as best he could and began taping electrodes around the eyes of anyone who would sleep in his laboratory.

One of his first volunteers was his eight-year-old son, Armond. As the boy slept, the pens of the machine scratched out slow, lazy lines, exactly as expected. Then, deep into the night, the pens began to jerk sharply from side to side, the tracing of eyes darting about as if the sleeper were wide awake and watching something. Aserinsky assumed that his old machine had failed again. But the boy was asleep, and the jerking pens came back, in bursts, at regular intervals through the night. They came back in adult volunteers too.

Aserinsky then did the obvious thing that nobody had done before: he woke people up during these bursts of eye movement and asked them what had been going on in their minds. Most of them described vivid, story-like dreams. Woken at other times, they usually reported nothing much. In 1953, Aserinsky and Kleitman published their discovery in the journal Science: sleep contains recurring periods of rapid eye movement, and these periods are when we dream.

That short paper did something larger than add a fact to physiology. It showed that consciousness does not simply switch on in the morning and off at night. The mind changes state, many times a night, and each state has its own brain activity and its own kind of experience. This chapter is about those states. It begins with attention, the process that decides which tiny part of the world reaches awareness at all, and then moves to consciousness itself, the body's daily clocks, sleep, dreams, hypnosis, meditation and the changes that drugs produce. Keep Armond's dancing eyes in mind; you will meet them again in Section 5.7.

5.1 Attention: what it is, how it behaves and what captures itLower yield · 1 Q

Sit for a moment in a busy chai stall near the Mysuru bus stand. Buses roar past, the stove hisses, two men argue about cricket, a radio plays a film song, your tea is too hot, your shoe pinches and a crow is calling from the roof. All of this reaches your sense organs at once, yet at any instant you are clearly aware of only a small part of it. If your friend starts telling you about tomorrow's exam, the cricket argument fades into a murmur. Then someone at the next table says your name, and suddenly you are listening to them instead. The process that is doing this choosing is attention.

Defining attention

The most quoted definition comes from William James (1842 to 1910), the American philosopher-psychologist, in his Principles of Psychology of 1890: "Everyone knows what attention is. It is the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought. Focalization, concentration, of consciousness are of its essence. It implies withdrawal from some things in order to deal effectively with others." Notice the two halves of that definition. Attention is a process of selection (one object out of several) and of concentration (that object is held clearly and vividly while others are withdrawn from). Modern textbooks say much the same thing in plainer words: attention is the process by which we select some information from the vast amount available to the senses and to memory, and concentrate mental resources on it.

Why do we need such a process at all? Because the brain's capacity to process information fully is limited. You can register a great deal at the level of the sense organs, but you cannot analyse it all for meaning, remember it all or act on it all. Attention is the gatekeeper between the flood of sensation and the narrow channel of awareness, which is why Indian exam notes often summarise the sequence as sensation, then attention, then perception. (Sensation and perception have their own chapters, Chapters 3 and 4.)

Psychologists have classified attention in several ways. A classic classification, set out by Edward Bradford Titchener (1867 to 1927), separates involuntary attention (his "primary" attention), which is pulled by the stimulus without effort (a sudden bang), from voluntary attention (his "secondary" attention), which you direct deliberately and with effort (forcing yourself to read a dull chapter), and adds a third, habitual attention (his "derived primary" attention), which began as voluntary but has become effortless through long practice and interest (the cricket fan who notices a score flashing on a distant shop television without trying). A clinical classification, proposed by McKay Moore Sohlberg and Catherine Mateer in 1987 for rehabilitating brain-injured patients, describes five levels: focused attention (responding to a single stimulus at all), sustained attention (keeping that response going over time), selective attention (holding focus in the presence of distractors), alternating attention (switching back and forth between tasks) and divided attention (responding to two tasks at the same moment). You will meet each of these again in this chapter.

The characteristics of attention

Classical general psychology describes attention by five characteristics. Each is a simple observation about how attention behaves, and each is a favourite source of exam questions.

  • Selectivity. Attention picks out a few stimuli and neglects the rest. The mother asleep through traffic noise but awake at her baby's faintest whimper shows selectivity at work even during sleep.
  • Shifting. Attention moves from one object to another, often several times a second, as when your eyes and mind move between the teacher, the blackboard and your notes. Shifting can be voluntary or forced by a new stimulus.
  • Span. Attention can take in only a limited number of items in a single glance. This limit is called the span of attention, and it is examined in detail below.
  • Fluctuation. Even when you try to keep attention on one unchanging stimulus, its clarity waxes and wanes. This is different from shifting: in fluctuation the object stays the same but your hold on it rises and falls.
  • Division. Under some conditions attention can be shared between two activities at once, such as walking and talking. How far it can be divided, and why it often cannot, is the subject of Section 5.4.

The span of attention

The span of attention (also called the span of apprehension) is the number of separate objects that can be clearly perceived in a single brief exposure, too short for the eyes to move or for counting. The question is old. The Scottish philosopher Sir William Hamilton (1788 to 1856) remarked in his lectures that if you throw a handful of marbles on the floor, you will find it difficult to see more than six, or seven at most, at one view without confusion.

Key study: Jevons and the beans (1871)

The English economist and logician William Stanley Jevons (1835 to 1882) turned Hamilton's remark into an experiment, with himself as the only participant. He threw random handfuls of black beans into a shallow box, glanced at them for an instant, called out an estimate of how many there were, and then counted them. Over about a thousand trials he was never wrong with three or four beans; errors began at five, and his accuracy fell steadily as the number rose. He published the result in Nature in 1871. Later laboratory work used the tachistoscope, an apparatus that flashes a display for a fraction of a second, and arrived at the figure that general psychology textbooks still give: for unrelated items, the adult span of attention is about six to eight, usually summarised as "about seven". For perfectly accurate, effortless enumeration the limit is lower, about four; this rapid, error-free judgement of small numbers was named subitising by E. L. Kaufman and colleagues in 1949.

The span of attention explains a lot of everyday design. An Indian vehicle registration number is not printed as one long string such as KA09AB1234. It is broken into short groups (a two-letter state code, a two-digit district code, a letter series and a number of no more than four digits), so that a witness to an accident can take in each group at a glance. The same reason lies behind short telephone groupings and four-digit PINs. The closely related limit on how much we can hold in mind for a few seconds, George Miller's "magical number seven, plus or minus two" of 1956, belongs to short-term memory and is taught in Chapter 7.

Exam trap

When a question asks why number plates carry only about four digits with a few letters, the answer is span of attention, not selective, divided or sustained attention. Do not confuse the span of attention (how many items can be apprehended in one glance; Jevons, tachistoscope studies) with the memory span of Miller (how many items can be held and repeated back). The numbers are similar; the processes are not.

Fluctuation of attention

Hold a watch at the distance where its ticking is just audible and listen carefully. You will notice that the ticking seems to fade out and come back, although the watch has not changed. The classical laboratory demonstration of this fluctuation of attention used the Masson disc, a white disc carrying a broken line of small grey segments that, when the disc is spun, forms faint grey rings at the threshold of visibility. A person who fixes the disc steadily reports that the faint rings appear and disappear, again and again. Ambiguous figures, such as Rubin's vase-faces figure (see Chapter 4), show something similar: attention cannot be held on one organisation for long and keeps flipping between them. Part of the fluctuation has peripheral causes (small eye movements and the adaptation of receptors), and part is central. The broader lesson is that attention is never static. It needs constant renewal, and when a task gives it nothing new, it drifts. That drift, as you will see in Section 5.5, is where daydreams come from.

The determinants of attention

What decides which stimulus wins? The determinants of attention are traditionally divided into two groups. External determinants (also called objective factors) are properties of the stimulus itself, and they explain why advertisers do what they do. Internal determinants (subjective factors) are properties of the person: motives, interests, expectations and attitudes. Table 5.1 sets them out.

Exam trap

Learn which list each determinant belongs to. Intensity, size, contrast, change, movement, novelty, repetition, colour and position are external (objective). Motives, interests, mental set, attitudes, emotions and habit are internal (subjective). "Fluctuation" and "span" are not determinants at all; they are characteristics of attention. Also keep shifting (attention moves to a different object) apart from fluctuation (attention to the same object waxes and wanes).

5.2 Selective listening: filters, attenuators and late selectionMedium yield · 5 Qs

The chai stall showed you a puzzle. You can follow your friend's voice through a wall of noise, and yet your name, spoken by a stranger, still gets through. How can the mind be ignoring the other voices and also listening to them? This puzzle drove the first great theoretical debate of cognitive psychology, and it was fought with headphones.

The cocktail party problem and dichotic listening

The British engineer Colin Cherry (1914 to 1979), working on problems of communication, named the puzzle in 1953. He called it the cocktail party problem: how do we manage to follow one conversation when many people are talking at once? The everyday experience of hearing your own name across a crowded room, even though you were not listening to that conversation, is called the cocktail party phenomenon (or cocktail party effect).

Key study: Cherry's shadowing experiments (1953)

Cherry invented the method that dominated attention research for twenty years. In dichotic listening, a person wears headphones and a different message is played to each ear. To make sure they really attend to one ear, they must repeat the attended message aloud, word for word, as it arrives, a technique called shadowing. Afterwards they are asked what they noticed in the other, unattended ear. The results were striking. People could report almost nothing about the content of the unattended message. They did not notice when it switched from English to German, or when the speech was played backwards. But they almost always noticed physical changes: a switch from a male to a female voice, or a pure tone in place of speech. Cherry concluded that the unattended message is processed only for its physical features, not for meaning.

A few years later the British psychologist Neville Moray added two findings that pulled in opposite directions. First, in 1959 he showed that a short list of words repeated 35 times in the unattended ear left no trace in memory: people did not recognise the words afterwards. That supported Cherry. Second, about a third of his participants noticed when an instruction in the unattended ear was preceded by their own name. Your name, it seems, can break through a channel you are not listening to. This is the laboratory version of the cocktail party phenomenon, and any theory of attention has to explain both halves of Moray's result.

Broadbent's filter model (1958): early selection

The first theory came from Donald Eric Broadbent (1926 to 1993), a British psychologist who had served in the Royal Air Force and studied the problems of pilots and air traffic controllers swamped with radio messages. In his book Perception and Communication (1958) he proposed the filter model of attention. This was the first major theory to treat the mind explicitly as an information-processing system, a sequence of stages through which information flows, like messages through a telephone exchange, and it is often described as the starting point of the information-processing approach in cognitive psychology.

Broadbent's model has three parts (see the top row of Figure 5.1). All incoming messages first enter a sensory buffer, a brief store that holds them for a second or two. Next comes a selective filter, which lets through only one message, chosen on the basis of a physical characteristic such as which ear it arrives at, the pitch of the voice or its loudness. Everything else is blocked. Finally, the selected message passes into a limited-capacity channel where it is analysed for meaning and can enter awareness and memory. Because the filter works on physical features before any analysis of meaning, Broadbent's is the classic early selection model, and because the filter passes one channel and blocks the other completely, like a railway switch, it is sometimes called an all-or-none or "switch" model. The filter is also the first bottleneck in attention theory: a narrow point in the system through which only a little information can pass at once.

Broadbent supported his model with the split-span experiment (1954). Three pairs of digits were played simultaneously, one digit of each pair to each ear (for example 7 to the left ear and 2 to the right, then 4 and 9, then 3 and 6). People found it much easier to recall the digits ear by ear (743, then 296) than pair by pair (72, 49, 36). Broadbent's explanation was that the filter is set to one ear at a time; recalling by ear means switching the filter only once, while recalling by pairs means switching it back and forth.

The model fitted Cherry's findings well, but it could not survive long. If the unattended message is blocked before meaning is analysed, how could Moray's participants hear their own names? And there was more trouble. Jeffrey Gray and A. A. I. Wedderburn (1960) played "Dear 7 Jane" to one ear and "9 Aunt 6" to the other; people reported "Dear Aunt Jane", grouping by meaning across the ears, not by ear as the filter predicts. Anne Marie Treisman (1935 to 2018) found in 1960 that when the story being shadowed suddenly switched to the other ear, people often followed the story into the "wrong" ear for a word or two before catching themselves: meaning in the unattended ear was guiding them. And in 1972 Robert Corteen and B. Wood conditioned people to expect a mild electric shock when they heard certain city names; later, when those names (and even other city names never paired with shock) were played in the unattended ear, the participants showed a galvanic skin response, although they could not report hearing the words. Something in the unattended channel was being analysed for meaning.

Treisman's attenuation model (1964)

Treisman's answer, set out fully in 1964, kept Broadbent's idea of an early stage that works on physical features but changed what that stage does. In her attenuation model, the filter is not a switch but a volume control, an attenuator. The attended message passes at full strength; unattended messages are not blocked but turned down, so that a weakened version still travels on to the next stage (middle row of Figure 5.1).

The next stage is a mental dictionary of dictionary units, one for each word you know, and each unit has its own threshold, the amount of activation it needs before it fires and the word reaches awareness. Thresholds are not equal. Words that are personally important (your own name, "fire", "help") have permanently low thresholds, so even an attenuated signal is enough to fire them. Words that are made probable by the context of the attended message have their thresholds temporarily lowered too, which is why Treisman's participants followed the story into the wrong ear: the next word of the story was expected, so its unit fired even from the weakened channel. Treisman also proposed that analysis runs through a hierarchy, from physical cues to syllable patterns to individual words to grammar and meaning, and that when capacity runs short, the later stages of analysis are simply not carried out for the unattended message.

The attenuation model explains everything that troubled Broadbent: Moray's own-name effect, Gray and Wedderburn's grouping by meaning, Corteen and Wood's skin responses and Treisman's own ear-switching. That is why it is the standard answer when a question asks which model best explains the cocktail party phenomenon. It is also why Treisman's theory is called a filter-attenuation theory: it is a modified filter theory, still early selection in spirit, but leaky.

Exam trap

"Filter-attenuation theory" is Treisman, not Broadbent (pure filter), Johnston or Heinz (multimode theory). The attenuation model is dated 1964 (her ear-switching experiment was 1960). Some Indian notes give 1980 for the attenuation model; 1980 is the year of a different Treisman theory, feature integration theory (Treisman and Gelade), taught in Section 5.3. If a question asks which model "better explains the cocktail party effect", the answer is the attenuator model; Broadbent's switch model cannot explain why your name gets through.

Late selection: Deutsch and Deutsch (1963) and Norman (1968)

At the opposite extreme, J. Anthony Deutsch and Diana Deutsch argued in 1963 that there is no early filter at all. In their late selection model, every incoming message is fully analysed, including for meaning, automatically and without capacity limits. The bottleneck comes only at the end, when the system must decide which analysed message to respond to, remember or become aware of (bottom row of Figure 5.1). Selection is based on importance: the most pertinent message wins the response. On this view you do understand the cricket argument at the next table; you simply do not respond to it, and you will not remember it, unless it becomes more important than your friend's voice.

Donald Arthur Norman (born 1935) refined the late-selection view in 1968. He proposed that what gets selected depends on two things added together: the strength of the sensory input (how loud and clear it is) and its pertinence (how relevant it is to current goals and expectations). A faint word that is highly pertinent can beat a loud word that is irrelevant. Norman's idea gives a late-selection account of the own-name effect: your name is so pertinent that it is selected even though the voice saying it is faint.

So which is right, early or late? The evidence pointed both ways. Treisman and Riley (1969) found that people detected target words far more often in the shadowed message than in the unattended one, which is awkward for a theory in which both are fully analysed. Yet the skin-response and own-name findings show some analysis of meaning in the unattended channel. The debate was eventually resolved not by declaring a winner but by asking a better question: when does selection happen early, and when late?

Flexible selection: Johnston and Heinz (1978) and Lavie's perceptual load theory

William A. Johnston and Steven P. Heinz proposed in 1978 that the bottleneck has no fixed position. In their multimode theory (flexible multimode model), selection can occur at any of three stages: at the stage of sensory representation (early, on physical features), at the stage of semantic representation (late, on meaning), or after both have been built and are entering consciousness. The listener chooses the mode, but there is a price: the later the selection, the more mental effort and capacity it consumes. Johnston and Heinz showed that when two messages differed physically (one male voice, one female), people selected early and had capacity left for a secondary task; when the messages could be told apart only by meaning, selection had to be late and secondary-task performance suffered. Attention is, in their phrase, flexible but costly.

The most influential modern resolution is the perceptual load theory of Nilli Lavie, developed in the mid-1990s (with Yehoshua Tsal in 1994, and in her own paper of 1995). Lavie argued that perception has limited capacity and that it runs automatically until that capacity is full. When the main task carries a high perceptual load (many items, hard discriminations), it uses up all the capacity, so nothing is left over for distractors and they are not processed at all: selection looks early. When the main task is easy (low perceptual load), spare capacity spills over automatically onto the distractors, which are then processed and can interfere: selection looks late. The early-late debate, on this view, was a debate about task difficulty. A practical prediction follows: if you want to stop being distracted, a harder, more absorbing task helps more than willpower does. (Lavie added that a high load on working memory, a cognitive load, has the opposite effect and makes distraction worse, because it weakens the control needed to ignore distractors.) 5.3 Visual attention: spotlights, binding and blindness to the obviousLower yield · 1 Q

The early debate was conducted with the ears, but vision is our dominant sense, and from the 1970s most attention research moved to the eyes. The questions changed shape. What is visual attention like: a beam that sweeps across the scene, or something more flexible? What is it for, beyond picking one thing out? And how much of the visual world do we actually see when we are not attending to it? The answer to the last question turned out to be: far less than you think.

The spotlight and the zoom lens

You can attend to something without looking straight at it. Fix your eyes on this word, and without moving them, notice the margin of the page. Moving attention with the eyes is overt attention; moving attention while the eyes stay still is covert attention. Michael I. Posner (born 1936), whose experiments are described below, likened covert attention to a spotlight: a beam of limited width that illuminates one region of space, so that whatever falls inside it is processed faster and better, and that can be moved from place to place independently of the eyes. William James had anticipated the idea in 1890 when he described consciousness as having a bright focus, surrounded by a dimmer margin or fringe.

A real spotlight has a fixed beam. Charles Eriksen and James St James argued in 1986 that visual attention is more like a zoom lens: you can widen the attended area to take in a whole scene or narrow it to a single letter, but there is a trade-off. The wider the area, the lower the resolution; the same amount of processing power is spread more thinly. A batsman watching the bowler's hand narrows his zoom lens to a tiny patch of space; a fielder at deep midwicket keeps it wide to watch the whole ground. Later work found that under some conditions attention can even be split between two separate locations, like two spotlights, so both metaphors are best treated as approximations of a flexible system.

Posner's cueing task: endogenous and exogenous attention

Key study: Posner's spatial cueing paradigm (1980)

Posner asked people to keep their eyes on a central point and press a key as soon as a light appeared in a box to the left or right. Before the light, a cue appeared. Sometimes it was a central cue, an arrow at fixation pointing left or right; sometimes it was a peripheral cue, a brief brightening of one of the boxes. Most cues were valid (the target appeared where the cue indicated), some were invalid (the target appeared on the other side) and on some trials a neutral cue gave no information. Responses were fastest after valid cues, slowest after invalid cues and in between after neutral cues. Because the eyes never moved, the benefit and the cost could only mean that attention itself had moved, covertly, to the cued location.

The two kinds of cue revealed two systems. An arrow at fixation must be interpreted and used deliberately; this is endogenous attention (also called voluntary, goal-directed or top-down attention), controlled by the person's intentions. It takes a few hundred milliseconds to deploy and, crucially, people ignore central arrows if they learn the arrows are usually wrong. A flash in the periphery grabs attention whether you want it to or not; this is exogenous attention (reflexive, stimulus-driven or bottom-up), and it is fast and automatic, pulling attention even when the flash predicts nothing. You use endogenous attention when you search a crowded railway platform for your friend's red bag; exogenous attention takes over when a pigeon suddenly flaps up beside you. Later imaging work by Maurizio Corbetta and Gordon Shulman (2002) mapped these onto a dorsal fronto-parietal network for goal-directed attention and a right-hemisphere ventral network that acts as a "circuit breaker", redirecting attention to unexpected events. Posner himself, with Steven Petersen in 1990, went on to describe three attention networks: alerting (getting ready), orienting (moving attention to a location) and executive control (resolving conflict).

The peripheral flash has one more surprise. If the target appears very soon after an uninformative flash (within about a quarter of a second), responses at the flashed location are faster. If it appears later than about 300 milliseconds, responses at that location become slower than elsewhere. Attention, having been pulled to the spot and found nothing, is now biased against going back. Posner and Yoav Cohen described this inhibition of return in 1984. Its likely function is to make visual search efficient: once you have checked a location, you are discouraged from wasting time checking it again, rather like a person searching a house who does not reopen a cupboard she has just looked in.

Feature integration theory (Treisman and Gelade, 1980)

Look at a ripe red tomato. Its redness is analysed by colour-sensitive neurons, its roundness by shape-sensitive neurons, its position by yet other circuits, in different parts of the visual brain. Yet you see one red, round object in one place, not a floating redness next to a floating roundness. How are the separate features put back together? This is the binding problem, and Anne Treisman's second great theory, developed with Garry Gelade in 1980, proposed that attention is the solution.

Feature integration theory divides visual processing into two stages. In the first, preattentive stage, simple features (colour, orientation, size, curvature, motion) are registered automatically, in parallel, across the whole visual field, each in its own feature map. No attention is needed and no effort is felt. In the second, focused attention stage, attention is directed to one location at a time and acts as a kind of glue: it combines the features found at that location into a single object. Because the glue can be applied to only one location at a time, this stage is serial.

The theory makes a clear prediction about visual search, tested by Treisman and Gelade. Suppose you must find a target among distractors. In feature search the target differs from every distractor by a single feature: a red letter among green letters, a vertical line among horizontals. The target is found at the preattentive stage and seems to leap out of the display, a phenomenon called pop-out, and search time hardly changes whether there are 5 distractors or 30. In conjunction search the target is defined only by a combination of features, say a green T among green X's and brown T's. No single feature map can find it; attention must visit items one by one, binding features at each, so search time rises in a straight line with the number of items. Figure 5.2 shows the pattern. Treisman and Gelade also found that when the target was absent, the conjunction slope was about twice as steep as when it was present, exactly what a serial, self-terminating search predicts: on average you find a present target halfway through, but you must check every item to be sure it is absent.

If attention is the glue, then features that are not attended should sometimes be glued wrongly. They are. Treisman and Hilary Schmidt (1982) flashed displays of coloured letters very briefly while people's attention was occupied by a second task. Participants often reported seeing, with confidence, combinations that were not there, such as a red X when the display contained a red T and a green X. These illusory conjunctions are strong evidence that features are registered separately and combined only with attention. Patients with damage to both parietal lobes (Balint's syndrome), who have severe difficulty directing spatial attention, produce illusory conjunctions even with long, leisurely displays. Later research qualified the theory (Jeremy Wolfe's guided search model showed that feature information can guide the serial stage, so many conjunction searches are faster than pure serial search), but the distinction between parallel feature detection and attention-dependent binding remains one of the most important ideas in the field.

Exam trap

Anne Treisman gave us two theories: the attenuation model of auditory selection (1964) and feature integration theory of visual binding (1980, with Gelade). Questions mixing them up are common. In feature integration theory, the preattentive stage is automatic and parallel (features), the focused attention stage is serial and binds features into objects; pop-out belongs to feature search, linear search slopes to conjunction search, and illusory conjunctions are the error that occurs when attention is unavailable.

Blind to the obvious: inattentional blindness and change blindness

Most people believe that they see everything in front of their eyes and that anything unusual would immediately catch their attention. Two families of experiments show that this belief is false.

Key study: the invisible gorilla (Simons and Chabris, 1999)

Daniel J. Simons and Christopher F. Chabris, then at Harvard University, filmed two teams of students, one in white shirts and one in black, passing basketballs among themselves. Viewers were asked to count the passes made by the white team, a task that demands close attention. Partway through the clip a woman in a full gorilla suit walks into the middle of the players, turns to the camera, thumps her chest and walks off; she is in full view for several seconds. Around half of the viewers did not see her at all, and many refused to believe she had been there until the clip was replayed. When viewers counted the black team's passes instead, far more of them (83 per cent) noticed the black gorilla, because her colour now matched what they were attending to.

The gorilla study is the best-known demonstration of inattentional blindness: the failure to notice a fully visible but unexpected object or event because attention is engaged elsewhere. The term was introduced by Arien Mack and Irvin Rock, whose 1998 book reported that many people failed to see an unexpected shape flashed near the point they were fixating while they judged which arm of a cross was longer. The lesson is that seeing is not just a matter of light reaching the retina; without attention, an object may never reach awareness at all.

Change blindness is a related but different failure: the failure to notice that something in a scene has changed, even a large change, when the change happens during a brief interruption such as an eye movement, a blink, a cut in a film or a flicker. Ronald Rensink, Kevin O'Regan and James Clark (1997) showed that people can stare for many seconds at two alternating photographs, separated by a brief blank, without spotting that a large object (a jet engine on an aeroplane, for example) appears in one and not the other; this is the flicker paradigm. In a famous field version by Simons and Daniel Levin (1998), an experimenter stopped pedestrians on a university campus to ask for directions; while they talked, two workmen carried a door between them, and behind the door the experimenter was replaced by a different person in different clothes. About half the pedestrians carried on giving directions to the new person without noticing the switch. Continuity errors in films, which audiences almost never spot, are everyday change blindness.

Keep the two apart. In inattentional blindness, an unexpected object is present and is not seen at all. In change blindness, the person may well see both versions of the scene but fails to notice the difference between them, because noticing a change requires attending to the changed item and comparing it across time. Neither should be confused with perceptual constancy, the tendency to see objects as having stable size, shape and colour although their image on the retina changes (taught in Chapter 4); constancy is a success of perception, not a failure of attention.

Exam trap

"Failing to notice significant changes in the environment, even obvious ones" is change blindness (Rensink and colleagues; the Simons and Levin door study). Failing to see an unexpected object that is in plain view while busy with another task is inattentional blindness (Mack and Rock; the gorilla). If the question stresses change, choose change blindness; if it stresses an unexpected object, choose inattentional blindness.

The attentional blink

Attention is limited not only in space but in time. In 1992 Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro and Karen Arnell used rapid serial visual presentation: letters flashed one after another in the same place at about ten per second. Participants had to identify a first target (say, a white letter among black ones) and then report whether a second target (say, the letter X) appeared later in the stream. If the second target came within roughly 200 to 500 milliseconds after the first, it was very often missed; before and after that window it was seen well. This brief "blindness" after a target is called the attentional blink. The usual explanation is that attention is still busy consolidating the first target into memory when the second arrives, so the second decays before it can be processed. (Curiously, a second target that comes immediately after the first, with no item between, is often spared, apparently because it slips into the same attentional episode.)

5.4 Divided attention, automaticity, the Stroop effect and vigilanceHigh yield · 10 Qs

Selective attention asks how we keep one thing in and other things out. Divided attention asks the reverse question: how far can we do two things at once? You can walk and talk, and an experienced driver can drive and hold a conversation. But a learner driver cannot, and nobody can hold two conversations at the same time. Why do some pairs of tasks combine easily and others not at all? Three answers have been proposed: that we have one limited pool of capacity, that we have several pools, and that practice makes some processes stop needing capacity at all.

Kahneman's capacity model (1973)

In his book Attention and Effort (1973), Daniel Kahneman (1934 to 2024), later a Nobel laureate in economics, replaced the bottleneck with a different picture. Attention, he proposed, is a single, limited pool of mental effort, or capacity, that can be shared out among activities. This is the capacity model (also called resource or central capacity theory). Two tasks can be performed together as long as their combined demand does not exceed the capacity available; when it does, performance on one or both suffers. The amount of capacity is not fixed: it rises and falls with arousal, so that a drowsy person has less to share out and a moderately alert one has more. How the capacity is divided depends on an allocation policy, which is shaped by four influences: enduring dispositions (built-in tendencies to attend to novel or moving stimuli, or to one's own name), momentary intentions (what you are trying to do right now), the evaluation of demands (the system's judgement of how much each task needs) and the current level of arousal. Kahneman measured effort through the size of the pupil, which widens as a mental task becomes harder.

The capacity model explained why difficult tasks interfere more than easy ones, and why practice, which makes tasks easier, makes them easier to combine. But it had a weakness: it predicts that two equally difficult tasks should interfere equally with a third, and they do not. What matters is how similar they are.

Multiple resource theory and dual-task interference

The study of what happens when people perform two tasks together is called the dual-task paradigm, and its main finding is that interference depends heavily on similarity. In a striking demonstration, Alan Allport, Barbara Antonis and Patricia Reynolds (1972) found that skilled pianists could sight-read music while shadowing speech with little loss on either task, although shadowing two spoken messages would have been impossible. Christopher Wickens captured this in his multiple resource theory (1984, refined in later work). Instead of a single pool, Wickens proposed several separate pools of resources, defined along a few dimensions: the stage of processing (perceptual and cognitive processing versus responding), the modality of input (visual versus auditory), the code of processing (spatial versus verbal) and, within vision, focal versus peripheral (ambient) vision. Two tasks that draw on different pools can be time-shared well; two tasks that compete for the same pool interfere. Driving (visual, spatial, manual) combines reasonably well with listening to the radio (auditory, verbal), and badly with reading a text message (visual, verbal, manual).

Practice also changes what can be combined. In a famous study, Elizabeth Spelke, William Hirst and Ulric Neisser (1976) trained two students for several months to read short stories while writing down words dictated to them. At first their reading speed and comprehension collapsed; after weeks of practice they read as fast and understood as well as when reading alone, while writing to dictation. Practice had apparently turned one of the tasks into something that no longer needed much capacity.

Multitasking and the phone in the car

What people call multitasking is, for most tasks, rapid switching between them. Every switch has a switch cost: responses are slower and more error-prone just after a switch than when the same task is repeated, as Robert Rogers and Stephen Monsell showed in 1995. The real-world consequences are serious. David Strayer and William Johnston (2001) had people perform a simulated driving task while talking on a mobile phone; they missed nearly twice as many red lights and braked more slowly than when driving alone, and a hands-free phone was no better than a hand-held one. Listening to the radio or a recorded book did not have this effect. The problem is the conversation, not the hands. Later work by Strayer's group found that drivers talking on phones were impaired about as much as drivers at the legal limit for alcohol, and that they looked straight at roadside objects and then failed to remember them: inattentional blindness at the wheel. A two-wheeler rider in Bengaluru with a phone wedged inside his helmet is not doing two things well; he is doing two things badly.

Automatic versus controlled processing

Spelke's students point to the third answer. Some mental processes demand attention and effort; others run off by themselves. In 1975 Michael Posner and Charles Snyder proposed three criteria for an automatic process: it occurs without intention, it occurs without conscious awareness, and it does not interfere with other ongoing mental activity. Two years later Richard M. Shiffrin and Walter Schneider (1977) made the distinction the basis of a major theory. Controlled processes are slow, serial, effortful, limited by capacity, open to awareness and flexible: you can change them at once when the situation changes. Automatic processes are fast, run in parallel, need little or no capacity, are hard to stop once triggered and are inflexible. Table 5.3 lists the contrasts.

Key study: Shiffrin and Schneider's search experiments (1977)

Participants memorised a set of up to four characters and then searched rapidly presented displays for any of them. In the consistent mapping condition, targets and distractors never swapped roles (for example, targets were always consonants and distractors always digits). In the varied mapping condition, the same characters served as targets on some trials and distractors on others. With consistent mapping, search speed hardly depended on how many items had to be compared: search had become automatic and parallel. With varied mapping, every extra item added time: search was controlled and serial. When participants were trained for 2,100 trials with one set of letters as targets and another as distractors, search became automatic; when the roles were then reversed, it took nearly 1,000 trials before performance recovered even to its starting level. Automatic processes, once learned, are fast but stubborn.

The distinction reaches well beyond the laboratory. Patricia Devine (1989) applied it to prejudice: because cultural stereotypes are learned by everyone in a society, they are activated automatically in high- and low-prejudiced people alike when a member of the group is encountered; what distinguishes low-prejudiced people is that they use controlled processing to override the activated stereotype and replace it with their own egalitarian beliefs. (The social psychology of prejudice is taught in the Social Psychology book.) And the stubbornness of automatic processes produces the most famous interference effect in psychology.

The Stroop effect (1935)

Key study: Stroop's colour-word experiments (1935)

John Ridley Stroop (1897 to 1973), an American psychologist, published "Studies of interference in serial verbal reactions" in 1935, based on his doctoral research at George Peabody College. In one experiment, people simply read aloud colour words printed in conflicting ink colours (the word RED printed in green ink) and were barely slower than when reading the same words printed in black. In another, they had to name the ink colour of such words, saying "green" to RED printed in green, and here performance collapsed: naming the ink colours of 100 conflicting words took about 74 per cent longer than naming the colours of plain coloured patches, and errors rose. A third experiment showed that days of practice reduced the interference but did not abolish it. Stroop himself soon left experimental work to teach the Bible, never knowing that his dissertation would become one of the most cited studies in psychology.

The Stroop effect is the slowing of colour naming (and the rise in errors) when the word and the ink colour conflict. Notice its asymmetry. Words interfere strongly with naming colours, but colours hardly interfere with reading words. Why? The most widely accepted explanation is in terms of automaticity. For a literate adult, reading is so overlearned that it is automatic: you cannot look at a familiar word and not read it. Naming an ink colour, by contrast, is a controlled process that needs attention. When the two conflict, the automatically produced word ("red") competes with the deliberately produced colour name ("green") at the stage of choosing a response, and resolving that conflict takes time. A related account, the relative speed of processing explanation, says that words are simply processed faster than colours, so the word's name arrives at the response stage first and must be suppressed. Later connectionist models (Jonathan Cohen, Kevin Dunbar and James McClelland, 1990) recast both ideas in terms of the strength of the pathways involved: the word-reading pathway, trained by years of practice, is stronger.

What, then, does the Stroop effect demonstrate? Above all, a limit on selective attention: we cannot completely filter out a stimulus dimension whose processing is automatic, even when we are told to ignore it and it hurts our performance to attend to it. Within a single stimulus, one attribute (the word) dominates the other (the ink colour) in controlling the response. It is also a measure of interference in reaction time due to conflicting information, and of the ability to inhibit a dominant response, which is why the Stroop task is one of the standard tests of the inhibition component of executive function and why it is used clinically to assess frontal lobe and attention problems. The emotional Stroop task applies the same logic: people name the ink colour of emotional and neutral words, and anxious individuals are slower with words related to their worries (a person with a spider phobia slows down on "web" and "hairy"), revealing an attentional bias toward threat.

Exam trap

The Stroop effect is an attention phenomenon: specifically, a failure of selective attention caused by interference from automatic word reading. It is not primarily about long-term memory retrieval, working memory, procedural memory, perception or language development, which are the usual distractors, and it does not show improved recall for related items or better accuracy under divided attention. One past paper (RML 2024) asks what the Stroop effect is "an account of" and keys stimulus dominance: one dimension of the stimulus (the printed word) dominates the other (its ink colour). "Semantic dominance" is the tempting wrong option because the intruding word is meaningful, but the keyed term describes one attribute of the stimulus overpowering another; "instructional" and "conceptual" dominance are not terms used for this effect.

Vigilance and sustained attention

Some jobs require attention not to many things at once but to one thing for a very long time, while waiting for something rare to happen. Air traffic controllers watching radar screens, radar operators, airport baggage screeners, lifeguards and nurses monitoring intensive-care displays all depend on sustained attention, the ability to keep attention on a task over a prolonged period; when the events to be detected are infrequent and unpredictable, it is usually called vigilance.

Key study: the Mackworth clock (1948)

During the Second World War, the Royal Air Force noticed that radar operators hunting for enemy submarines missed more signals as their watches went on. Norman Mackworth built a laboratory model of the problem. A pointer moved around a blank clock face in small regular jumps; occasionally, and unpredictably, it made a double jump, and the observer had to press a key whenever this happened. Over a two-hour watch, detection fell sharply, with the largest drop within the first half hour; a short break or a telephone message restored it. Mackworth had demonstrated the vigilance decrement: the decline in detection over time on a monotonous monitoring task.

Two kinds of explanation have been offered for the decrement. One is that boring, understimulating tasks lower arousal, so the observer becomes less alert. The other, now more favoured, is that vigilance is hard mental work that steadily depletes attentional resources; people rate vigilance tasks as surprisingly effortful and stressful. Signal detection analysis (Chapter 3) adds that part of the decrement is a shift in criterion: tired observers become more cautious about saying "yes, there it is". Clinically, sustained attention is measured with the Continuous Performance Test (respond to a rare target letter in a long stream), and it is one of the first functions to suffer from sleep loss, as you will see in Section 5.8. Brain recordings provide another window: when a rare target appears among frequent non-targets (the oddball task), the brain produces a positive event-related potential about 300 milliseconds later, the P300, which indexes the allocation of attention to the target and is reduced in conditions such as schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease.

Exam trap

Air traffic controllers and radar readers are the textbook example of sustained attention (vigilance), not selective attention (choosing one input among several), divided attention (two tasks at once) or "perceptual attention" (not a standard category). The classic experiment is Mackworth's clock test (1948).

Clinical link

Disorders of attention are among the commonest problems a clinical psychologist assesses. In attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity begin in childhood (the disorder itself is covered in the Disorders book). In hemispatial neglect, usually after damage to the right parietal lobe, patients ignore the left side of space: they eat from only the right half of the plate, shave half the face and bisect a horizontal line far to the right, although their vision is intact. Delirium is defined by an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, and that attentional disturbance, together with its acute onset, fluctuating course and usually identifiable (often reversible) medical cause, is the most reliable feature separating it from dementia, which begins insidiously and progresses slowly, with attention relatively preserved early. Attention is measured with tests such as the Continuous Performance Test, the Test of Everyday Attention, the Stroop and the Trail Making Test (taught in the Psychological Assessment book).

5.5 Consciousness: definitions, levels and the brainHigh yield · 18 Qs

Everything in the last four sections was about how information gets selected. But selected for what? The usual answer is: for awareness. The gorilla that was not attended to was not experienced; the word that broke through the attenuator was. Attention is, very often, the gateway to consciousness. It is time to ask what lies on the other side of the gate.

What consciousness is

Consciousness is one of those words that everyone understands until asked to define it. A workable definition, used in most introductory texts, is that consciousness is our awareness of ourselves and of our environment: of our sensations, perceptions, thoughts, feelings and memories at any given moment. It helps to separate two meanings. The first is consciousness as a level or state of wakefulness, running from coma through drowsiness to alert wakefulness; this is what a doctor checks at a road accident. The second is consciousness as content: what, in particular, you are aware of right now. The two can come apart. A patient in a vegetative state opens the eyes and has sleep-wake cycles (wakefulness is present) but shows no sign of awareness, whereas a patient with locked-in syndrome, after damage to the brainstem, is fully aware but almost completely paralysed, able to communicate only with eye movements.

The level of consciousness is measured clinically with the Glasgow Coma Scale, published by the neurosurgeons Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett in 1974. It scores three responses: eye opening (1 to 4), verbal response (1 to 5) and motor response (1 to 6), giving a total from 3 (deep coma) to 15 (fully alert). A score of 8 or less is generally taken to mean coma. The scale measures consciousness, not attention, memory or intelligence, and it is used every day in emergency rooms across India.

William James and the stream of consciousness

The founders of experimental psychology tried to study consciousness by breaking it into elements. Wilhelm Wundt and Edward Titchener trained observers to introspect, to report the sensations, images and feelings that made up an experience, much as a chemist breaks a compound into its elements. Titchener described introspection as a highly specialised form of self-observation, and his structuralism set out to describe the components of consciousness, their combinations and their connections (Chapter 1). William James thought this was a mistake. Consciousness, he wrote in 1890, "does not appear to itself chopped up in bits ... It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described. In talking of it hereafter, let us call it the stream of thought, of consciousness, or of subjective life." The phrase stream of consciousness has belonged to James ever since, and it later gave its name to a style of novel-writing that imitates the flow of thought.

James listed five characteristics of this stream. Every thought is part of a personal consciousness: it belongs to someone. Within each person, consciousness is always changing: no state, once gone, can recur exactly. It is sensibly continuous: even after sleep, you know that your thoughts this morning belong to the same self as last night's. It deals with objects independent of itself: thought is always about something. And it is selective: it is interested in some parts of its objects to the exclusion of others, which is James's link between consciousness and attention.

One feature of the stream of consciousness has received particularly intense research attention: daydreaming, the spontaneous drift of thought away from the task and the surroundings toward fantasies, memories and plans. From the 1960s the American psychologist Jerome L. Singer (1924 to 2010) showed that daydreaming is not a sign of laziness or disorder but a normal, almost universal part of mental life, and that it can serve planning, creativity and self-reflection. More recently, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert (2010) used a smartphone app to interrupt thousands of people at random moments; minds were wandering in nearly half of the samples, and people were less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused, whatever they were doing. Brain imaging has linked mind wandering to the default mode network, a set of midline brain regions that become more active when we are not engaged in an external task.

Exam trap

"Stream of consciousness" was coined by William James (Principles of Psychology, 1890), not by Jung, Wundt or Skinner. Wundt studied the elements of consciousness by introspection; James opposed this with the image of a flowing stream, which is why he is linked with functionalism. The feature of the stream that has received the most research attention is daydreaming, not gender differences, brain-wave patterns or eye movements.

Levels of consciousness: Freud's iceberg and the modern unconscious

James described the stream as it appears to the person having it. Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) insisted that most of mental life never appears in the stream at all. In The Interpretation of Dreams (1900) he set out what is now called his topographic model of the mind, which divides it into three regions, usually drawn as an iceberg (Figure 5.3). The conscious is whatever you are aware of at this moment, a small tip above the water. The preconscious holds material that is not in awareness right now but can be recalled at will without difficulty: your mobile number, what you ate yesterday, the name of your school. The unconscious, by far the largest region, holds wishes, impulses and memories that are actively kept out of awareness by repression because they would cause anxiety; they cannot be recalled at will and are known only indirectly, through dreams, slips of the tongue, symptoms and the free associations of the analytic patient. Freud's principle of psychic determinism held that nothing in mental life is accidental: even a slip of the tongue (a parapraxis, popularly a "Freudian slip") is caused by unconscious motives. In 1923 Freud added a second, structural model of id, ego and superego (the id, wholly unconscious, works on the pleasure principle; the ego works on the reality principle; the superego is the internalised conscience); that model, and psychoanalysis as a theory of personality, belong to the Personality book. The picture of behaviour driven by unconscious inner forces that lie largely beyond the person's control, and shaped by early childhood experience, is the heart of the psychodynamic perspective (Chapter 2).

Freud's specific claims about repression and the Oedipus complex have fared poorly in research, but the general idea that much mental activity goes on outside awareness has fared very well; it is the part of his thought that modern research most clearly supports. Modern psychologists, however, usually distinguish the dynamic, motivated unconscious of Freud from two other things. The nonconscious refers to processes that are never accessible to awareness at all, however hard you try: the regulation of blood pressure and body temperature, or the early stages of visual processing that compute depth from the two eyes. The cognitive unconscious, a term popularised by John Kihlstrom in 1987, refers to the mental processes that operate outside awareness but are not repressed, simply automatic. You met examples in Section 5.2 (Corteen and Wood's skin responses to unattended words) and Section 5.4 (automatic reading in the Stroop task). Others include priming and implicit memory (Chapter 7), and three findings deserve special mention:

  • Blindsight. Lawrence Weiskrantz (1926 to 2018) studied patients with damage to the primary visual cortex who insist that they see nothing in part of the visual field, yet, when forced to guess, point to objects there and discriminate their orientation far better than chance. Visual information is processed and used without being consciously seen.
  • The "low road" to fear. Joseph LeDoux showed that a fast pathway from the thalamus to the amygdala, bypassing the cortex, allows rapid, crude detection of threat and the start of a defensive response before the threat is consciously recognised (Chapter 11).
  • Implicit attitudes. The Implicit Association Test (Anthony Greenwald and colleagues, 1998) measures how quickly people pair concepts (for example, social groups with good or bad words) and is used to reveal automatic, often unacknowledged stereotyping (taught in the Social Psychology book).
Exam trap

Accessible memories that can be recalled at will are preconscious, not unconscious. The unconscious cannot be reached at will; that is its defining feature. Psychic determinism is Freud's principle that all mental events are caused, not a level of consciousness. "Subconscious" is a popular word that Freud did not use as a technical term; in exam options prefer conscious, preconscious and unconscious. The model of three levels is the topographic model (1900); id, ego and superego form the later structural model (1923).

Consciousness and the brain, and the hard problem

Where does consciousness come from in the brain? The first clue about the level of consciousness came in 1949, when Giuseppe Moruzzi and Horace Magoun found that electrically stimulating the reticular formation in the brainstem of a sleeping or anaesthetised cat immediately changed its EEG to the pattern of alert wakefulness. The reticular activating system, a network running from the brainstem through the thalamus to the cortex, maintains arousal; damage to it produces coma. The contents of consciousness, in contrast, seem to depend on widespread, coordinated activity across the cortex and thalamus. Francis Crick and Christof Koch made the search for the neural correlates of consciousness, the minimal brain activity sufficient for a specific conscious experience, a respectable scientific programme from 1990, and Bernard Baars's global workspace theory (1988) proposed that information becomes conscious when it is "broadcast" widely across the brain, so that many specialised systems can use it at once, rather like an actor stepping into the spotlight on a stage watched by a large audience. (The split-brain studies of Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga, which bear on the unity of consciousness, are taught in the Physiological Psychology book.)

Even a complete map of these correlates would leave one question open. In 1995 the Australian philosopher David Chalmers (born 1966) distinguished the "easy problems" of consciousness, explaining how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, focuses attention and reports on its states, from the hard problem of consciousness: why any of this processing is accompanied by subjective experience at all. Why does the activity of neurons responding to a certain wavelength feel like the redness of a tomato, instead of going on "in the dark"? The easy problems are hard in practice but are problems of mechanism; the hard problem asks why mechanism should produce experience, and there is no agreed answer.

Altered states of consciousness

Your ordinary waking consciousness is not the only state your mind can be in. An altered state of consciousness (ASC) is a temporary, qualitative change in the overall pattern of mental functioning, such that the person feels that consciousness is radically different from its ordinary state. The definition is associated with the American psychologist Charles Tart (born 1937), whose edited book Altered States of Consciousness (1969) made the term standard; the psychiatrist Arnold Ludwig (1966) listed the features such states tend to share, including disturbed thinking, a changed sense of time, loss of control, changes in emotional expression and body image, perceptual distortion, a sense that experiences are deeply meaningful or indescribable, and increased suggestibility. Some altered states occur naturally every day (sleep and dreaming); others are induced psychologically (hypnosis, meditation, sensory deprivation, rhythmic drumming or chanting) or physiologically (fever, lack of oxygen, fasting and psychoactive drugs). The rest of this chapter examines the most important of them, beginning with the one you enter every night. But sleep is governed by a clock, so we start with the clock.

Indian context

Indian philosophy analysed states of consciousness long before Western psychology. The Mandukya Upanishad describes four states. In jagrat (waking) consciousness is directed outward to the physical world; in svapna (dreaming) it is directed inward to subtle images; in sushupti (deep, dreamless sleep) there is neither object nor desire, only an undifferentiated rest (the self in this state is called prajna). The fourth state, turiya (literally "the fourth"), is not a further condition alongside the others but pure consciousness itself, the silent witness present in all three and identified with the Atman, the true self or soul, understood as eternal, unchanging consciousness (as distinct from the perishable body and the passing states of mind). Vedanta texts often speak of avastha-traya, "the three states", with turiya as what lies beyond them. Indian psychology, whose primary focus is the integration of these Indian philosophical traditions with psychological concepts, is taught in Chapter 2.

5.6 Biological rhythms: the body's clocksLower yield · 4 Qs

If you have ever travelled overnight by train from Mysuru to Delhi and back, you know that the body has opinions about time. You feel sleepy at certain hours whatever you are doing, and alert at others. These opinions come from biological rhythms, regular cyclical changes in physiology and behaviour, and the most important of them is set by a tiny clock deep in the brain.

Circadian rhythms

A circadian rhythm is a biological cycle of about 24 hours (from the Latin circa diem, "about a day"; the term was coined by the chronobiologist Franz Halberg in 1959). The sleep-wake cycle is the obvious example, but almost every bodily process has one. Body temperature falls through the night and is lowest in the early morning hours, which is why night-shift workers feel coldest and most error-prone around four or five in the morning; it rises through the day. The stress hormone cortisol peaks shortly after waking. The hormone melatonin rises in the evening and is highest in the middle of the night.

Are these rhythms just responses to the day outside, or does the body keep time by itself? The test is to remove every time cue. Volunteers have lived for weeks in caves and underground bunkers with no daylight, no clocks and no news, eating and sleeping whenever they wished. Their rhythms persisted, but drifted: under constant conditions human rhythms run with a period slightly longer than 24 hours (about 24.2 hours in careful modern studies), so that each "day" they went to bed a little later. A rhythm that runs on in the absence of time cues is called free-running, and its persistence proves that there is an internal clock. Under normal conditions the clock is reset every day by environmental cues called zeitgebers (German, "time givers"). Light is by far the most powerful; meals, exercise and social activity are weaker zeitgebers. The process of being kept in step with the 24-hour day is called entrainment.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus and melatonin

The master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a pair of small clusters of neurons in the hypothalamus, sitting just above the optic chiasm (hence "supra-chiasmatic"). The evidence that it is the clock is unusually complete. When the SCN is destroyed in rats and hamsters, circadian rhythms of sleep, activity, eating and drinking disappear: the animals sleep about the same total amount, but at random times of day and night. SCN neurons removed from the brain and kept alive in a dish continue to fire in a 24-hour rhythm. And when Martin Ralph and colleagues (1990) transplanted the SCN from hamsters with a mutant short-period clock into normal hamsters whose own SCN had been destroyed, the recipients took on the donor's rhythm. The clock itself is molecular: in each SCN cell, "clock genes" switch each other on and off in a feedback loop that takes about a day to complete, a discovery recognised by the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2017.

How does light reach the clock? Not through the ordinary visual pathway. A special class of retinal ganglion cells containing the photopigment melanopsin responds to overall brightness and sends signals directly to the SCN along the retinohypothalamic tract. (Some blind people whose rods and cones are destroyed still have entrained rhythms because these cells survive.) The SCN, in turn, controls the pineal gland, which secretes melatonin in darkness; light suppresses it. Melatonin, sometimes called the hormone of darkness, signals "night" to the rest of the body and promotes sleepiness, and melatonin taken at bedtime in the new location can help reset the clock after long flights.

Exam trap

The SCN is the master circadian pacemaker, entrained by light through the retinohypothalamic tract; SCN lesions disrupt circadian rhythms, not total sleep amount. Do not confuse it with neighbouring hypothalamic regions that are popular distractors: the lateral and ventromedial hypothalamus (hunger and satiety, Chapter 10), the preoptic area (temperature regulation and, in its ventrolateral part, sleep onset; Section 5.7), the paraventricular nucleus (stress hormones), the hypothalamic control of anterior pituitary hormones, and the mammillary bodies (memory). Memory consolidation belongs to the hippocampus, language to Broca's and Wernicke's areas, and long-term memory is not "stored" in the SCN.

Jet lag, shift work, larks and owls

The clock works well as long as life keeps to a regular schedule. Problems arise when the zeitgebers change suddenly (jet lag, after flying across time zones) or when work forces us to be awake at the wrong phase of the clock (shift work). Both produce sleep disturbance, fatigue, poor concentration and digestive upset, because the internal clock and the external day are out of step, and because different bodily rhythms re-adjust at different speeds. Flying east is harder than flying west. Flying east from London to Bengaluru shortens your day, so you must fall asleep and wake earlier than your clock wants (a phase advance); flying west lengthens it (a phase delay), and since the free-running clock naturally runs a little longer than 24 hours, delaying it is easier. Full adjustment takes roughly a day for each time zone crossed. For the same reason, rotating shifts are easier to tolerate when they move forward, from morning to evening to night, than backward; Charles Czeisler and colleagues showed in 1982 that switching a group of industrial workers to forward rotation improved their health and productivity.

People also differ in the phase of their clocks. "Larks" (morning types) wake early and peak early; "owls" (evening types) are sluggish in the morning and alert at night. These differences, called chronotypes, are partly genetic, and they shift with age: adolescents drift strongly toward owl-ness, which is why a 7 a.m. coaching class is a biological ordeal for many sixteen-year-olds, while older adults drift back toward larkishness.

Sleepiness, finally, is not set by the clock alone. The Swiss researcher Alexander Borbély proposed in 1982 the influential two-process model of sleep regulation. Process S (sleep pressure) is homeostatic: it builds up the longer you are awake (partly through the accumulation of the chemical adenosine in the brain) and dissipates during sleep. Process C is the circadian rhythm from the SCN, which promotes wakefulness during the biological day. You fall asleep easily when sleep pressure is high and the circadian alerting signal is low. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors, hiding sleep pressure for a few hours without removing it.

Ultradian and infradian rhythms

Not all rhythms are daily. Ultradian rhythms are cycles shorter than a day, repeating more than once in 24 hours. The best known is the roughly 90-minute cycle of sleep stages that you will meet in the next section; Kleitman suggested that a similar 90-minute basic rest-activity cycle continues through the day as well, as fluctuations in alertness, and pulses of many hormones follow ultradian rhythms. Infradian rhythms are cycles longer than a day: the human menstrual cycle of about 28 days is the classic example. Cycles of about a year, called circannual rhythms, include hibernation, bird migration and, in humans, seasonal affective disorder, a form of depression that recurs in the dark months of winter and can be treated with bright-light therapy. 5.7 The stages of sleepHigh yield · 11 Qs

We spend about a third of our lives asleep. For most of history that third was a blank: you closed your eyes, and some hours later you opened them. Sleep could not be studied because the sleeper could not report on it. What changed this was a machine that could listen to the brain.

Listening to the sleeping brain: the EEG

In 1929 the German psychiatrist Hans Berger (1873 to 1941) published the first recordings of electrical activity from the human scalp, the electroencephalogram (EEG). The EEG picks up the summed activity of millions of cortical neurons. When those neurons fire in an unsynchronised way, each doing its own work, their activity largely cancels out and the EEG shows small, fast waves; when large numbers fire together in rhythm, the waves become large and slow. Waves are described by their frequency (cycles per second, or hertz, Hz) and their amplitude (height, which reflects voltage). The first rhythm Berger described, a regular wave of about 10 Hz seen when a person rests with eyes closed, is still sometimes called the Berger rhythm; we now call it alpha.

A modern sleep laboratory records three measures together, a procedure called polysomnography: the EEG, the electrooculogram (EOG), which records eye movements from electrodes beside the eyes, and the electromyogram (EMG), which records muscle tension, usually from under the chin. The discovery of REM sleep

Now return to Eugene Aserinsky and the jerking pens. What Aserinsky and Kleitman reported in 1953 was that the bursts of rapid eye movement coincided with periods of low-voltage, fast EEG, like that of a waking person, and that people woken from these periods usually reported dreams. Their student William Charles Dement (1928 to 2020), then a medical student and later the founder of the world's first sleep disorders clinic at Stanford, joined the work, and in 1957 Dement and Kleitman described the full pattern: the stages of sleep follow one another in a regular cycle of about 90 minutes, repeated through the night, with a period of rapid-eye-movement sleep at the end of each cycle. In their studies, about 80 per cent of awakenings from REM periods produced vivid, story-like dream reports, compared with only a small fraction of awakenings from other stages. In 1959 the French researcher Michel Jouvet (1925 to 2017) found that in cats this state is accompanied by a complete loss of muscle tone and called it paradoxical sleep, because the brain looks awake while the body is more deeply relaxed than at any other time.

Sleep researchers therefore divide sleep into two basic kinds. REM sleep (rapid eye movement sleep) is the paradoxical stage. NREM sleep (non-REM sleep) is everything else, and it is subdivided into stages by its EEG. The standard scoring manual of Allan Rechtschaffen and Anthony Kales (1968) recognised four NREM stages, numbered 1 to 4, with stages 3 and 4 together called slow-wave sleep. In 2007 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine merged stages 3 and 4 into a single stage, so that modern texts (including Pinel) describe three NREM stages, N1, N2 and N3, plus REM (sometimes labelled R). Many exam questions still use the older numbering, so you must know both.

A night's journey through the stages

Picture a student going to bed after a long day of revision. As she lies with her eyes closed and relaxes, her EEG shows regular alpha waves. Then, over a few minutes, she passes through the stages of sleep.

Stage N1 is the brief transition from waking to sleep, lasting only a few minutes. Alpha waves give way to slower, irregular theta waves of low amplitude, and the eyes may roll slowly. She is easy to wake, and if woken she may insist she was not asleep. This is the stage of the hypnic jerk, the sudden muscle twitch that sometimes jolts us awake with a feeling of falling, and of dreamlike hypnagogic images (Section 5.9). N1 makes up only about 5 per cent of a night's sleep.

Stage N2 is light but definite sleep. The EEG is mostly theta, punctuated by two signature waveforms: sleep spindles, short waxing and waning bursts of 11 to 15 Hz waves, and K-complexes, single large sharp waves, a large negative (upward) deflection followed at once by a large positive (downward) one, which often appear when a sound occurs during sleep. Heart rate and breathing slow, body temperature falls and the eyes are still. N2 is the most common stage, about half of an adult's total sleep.

Stage N3 is deep sleep. The EEG is dominated by delta waves, the largest and slowest of all, which is why N3 is called slow-wave sleep (SWS). In the older system, stage 3 had 20 to 50 per cent delta and stage 4 more than 50 per cent; together they formed slow-wave sleep. This is the hardest stage to wake from: a person roused from N3 is groggy and confused for several minutes. Breathing and heart rate are slow and regular, the release of growth hormone is at its daily peak, and the brain uses less energy than at any other time. N3 is also when sleepwalking and sleep terrors occur.

After N3 the sleeper climbs back up through N2, and then, about 70 to 90 minutes after falling asleep, something remarkable happens. The EEG becomes fast, low-voltage and desynchronised, much like that of an alert, waking person, with bursts of rapid eye movements under the closed lids. This is REM sleep. Its features are a list of paradoxes (Table 5.6 sets them against those of NREM sleep):

  • The brain is highly active: its oxygen use and blood flow in many regions rise to waking levels, and the EEG shows beta and theta waves with characteristic sawtooth waves.
  • The eyes dart rapidly from side to side in bursts.
  • The large skeletal muscles of the body core are almost completely paralysed, a state called REM atonia, produced by brainstem circuits that actively inhibit motor neurons in the spinal cord, although small muscles of the fingers and face may twitch.
  • Heart rate, blood pressure and breathing become irregular, and there is genital arousal (penile erection or clitoral engorgement) in nearly every REM period, whatever the content of the dream; even infants show it.
  • Most vivid, emotional, story-like dreams occur in REM sleep.
  • Despite the waking-like EEG, a person in REM sleep is hard to wake by meaningless sounds, although meaningful ones (their own name, a baby's cry) can wake them easily.

Exam trap

REM sleep is not characterised by EEG synchrony or delta waves; it has a desynchronised, low-voltage, mixed-frequency EEG. Delta waves and the growth hormone surge belong to N3; spindles and K-complexes belong to N2; dominant alpha over the back of the head belongs to relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed, and beta to alert wakefulness. If a question lists rapid eye movements, atonia, dreams and genital tumescence and asks for the exception, the odd one out is anything describing slow, synchronised waves.

The 90-minute cycle and its changes across the night

One sequence of NREM stages followed by a REM period is a sleep cycle. Each cycle lasts about 90 minutes (anywhere from about 70 to 110 minutes), so a typical adult night of seven to eight hours contains four to six cycles. But the cycles are not identical, and the way they change is a favourite exam point (Figure 5.5). Early in the night, cycles contain long periods of N3 and very short REM periods; the first REM period may last only about 10 minutes. As the night goes on, N3 shrinks and often disappears altogether in the last cycles, while REM periods lengthen to half an hour or more. Slow-wave sleep is concentrated in the first third of the night; REM sleep in the last third. This is why you are most likely to be woken from a dream by your morning alarm, and why sleepwalking and sleep terrors (N3 events) happen within a few hours of falling asleep. Brief awakenings, usually forgotten by morning, are normal.

Exam trap

How many NREM-REM cycles in a night? The standard answer is four to six (about 90 minutes each over seven to eight hours), and that is the keyed answer when it is offered (RML 2024). Some sources give "four to five" or "about five", and one past paper (RML 2022) keys 4 to 7 because 4 to 6 was not among its options; choose the option that contains four to six and excludes the extremes (2 to 4 is too few, 8 to 12 too many). Also remember the direction of change: N3 decreases and REM increases across the night, not the reverse.

Sleep across the lifespan

Sleep changes dramatically with age, as Howard Roffwarg and his colleagues Joseph Muzio and William Dement showed in a classic paper in 1966 (Figure 5.6). A newborn baby sleeps about 16 hours a day, and about half of that is REM sleep; newborns also often enter REM directly at sleep onset. Total sleep and the proportion of REM fall steeply through infancy and childhood. By adolescence REM makes up about 20 to 25 per cent of sleep, and it stays near that level through adulthood, declining slightly in old age. Roffwarg proposed that the huge amount of REM sleep in infancy provides internally generated stimulation that helps the developing brain mature, an idea known as the ontogenetic hypothesis. In older adults total sleep falls to about six hours, slow-wave sleep shrinks greatly (in many elderly people N3 almost disappears), sleep becomes lighter and more broken by awakenings, and its timing shifts earlier in the evening.

What switches sleep on and off

You read in Section 5.5 that the reticular activating system keeps the cortex awake. Sleep is not simply this system running down. It is actively switched on. A group of inhibitory neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO) of the hypothalamus, which use the transmitter GABA, become active at sleep onset and shut down the arousal systems of the brainstem and hypothalamus; the arousal systems, in turn, inhibit the VLPO. Because each side suppresses the other, the brain flips quickly between sleep and wakefulness, like a light switch, instead of drifting between them; Clifford Saper and his colleagues called this the "flip-flop switch". The switch is stabilised by orexin (also called hypocretin), a peptide made by neurons in the lateral hypothalamus that keeps the system firmly in the waking position; its loss causes narcolepsy (Section 5.8).

REM sleep is generated in the brainstem, particularly the pons. "REM-on" neurons in a region called the sublaterodorsal nucleus trigger REM sleep and, through circuits in the medulla, the atonia that goes with it; "REM-off" neurons in the ventrolateral periaqueductal grey hold REM in check. During REM, waves of activity called PGO waves (ponto-geniculo-occipital) travel from the pons through the visual thalamus to the occipital cortex, a fact that will matter when we come to theories of dreaming.

Exam trap

Slow-wave (NREM) sleep begins when neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic area become active. The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the circadian clock (it decides when sleep is likely, not how it is switched on). The sublaterodorsal nucleus is a REM-on region, and the ventrolateral periaqueductal grey is a REM-off region; both concern REM, not slow-wave sleep.

5.8 Why we sleep, what deprivation does and when sleep goes wrongMedium yield · 4 Qs

Sleep is costly. An animal asleep is not eating, mating or watching for predators. Yet every mammal and bird that has been studied sleeps, and even fruit flies and roundworms have sleep-like states. Something so universal and so risky must do something important. But what?

Theories of why we sleep

Pinel groups the answers into two kinds. Recuperation theories (also called restorative theories), associated with the British psychiatrist Ian Oswald among others, hold that being awake disrupts the body's internal balance in some way and that sleep is needed to restore it: to replenish energy, repair tissue, clear waste products and restore the brain. There is real evidence for restoration. Growth hormone is released mainly during slow-wave sleep. Slow-wave sleep increases after unusually tiring days and after sleep loss. And in 2013 Lulu Xie, Maiken Nedergaard and colleagues showed in mice that the spaces between brain cells widen during sleep, allowing cerebrospinal fluid to flush out waste products, including the protein beta-amyloid that accumulates in Alzheimer's disease, a cleaning system called the glymphatic system.

Adaptation theories (Pinel's term; also called adaptive, evolutionary or circadian theories), associated with Wilse Webb and Ray Meddis in the 1970s, hold that sleep is less a response to the wear of waking than a behaviour that evolution timed to keep animals still and safe during the part of the day when activity would be useless or dangerous, and to conserve energy. They explain well why different species sleep such different amounts. Large grazing animals, which must eat for most of the day and cannot hide from predators, sleep only a few hours; animals that are safe in burrows or at the top of the food chain, and that can meet their needs quickly, sleep much longer. They also explain why sleep is so tightly tied to the circadian clock.

A third family of theories stresses memory consolidation. As far back as 1924, John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach found that people forgot less of what they had learned if they slept after learning than if they stayed awake (Chapter 7). Modern research suggests that slow-wave sleep helps to consolidate factual, declarative memories, partly by "replaying" the day's patterns of hippocampal activity, while REM sleep helps to consolidate procedural skills and emotional memories; Avi Karni and colleagues (1994) found that improvement on a visual skill overnight disappeared when participants were deprived of REM sleep. Giulio Tononi and Chiara Cirelli have added that sleep may prune back the synaptic strengthening of the day, keeping the brain efficient. These theories are not rivals so much as parts of one answer: sleep probably does several jobs, and its timing has been fitted to each species' way of life.

Sleep deprivation

Key study: Randy Gardner's 264 hours (1964)

In the winter of 1963 to 1964, Randy Gardner, a 17-year-old high-school student in San Diego, set out to break the world record for staying awake as a school science project, kept awake by two friends. William Dement heard about it and joined them. Gardner stayed awake for 264 hours, eleven days. He grew irritable, his speech became slurred, his memory and concentration lapsed, and he had brief perceptual distortions, but he never became psychotic; near the end he held a press conference and conducted himself well. Afterwards he slept for about 14 hours and then returned to his normal schedule. He did not need to recover all the lost sleep, a typical finding.

Laboratory studies give a more systematic picture. Even moderate sleep loss produces three consistent effects: increased sleepiness, more negative mood and, above all, poor performance on tasks of sustained attention, the dull vigilance tasks of Section 5.4. Sleep-deprived people suffer microsleeps, episodes of a few seconds in which the eyelids droop, the EEG shows sleep and the person stops responding, even while sitting or standing; at the wheel of a car on a highway, a microsleep can be fatal. Chronic partial deprivation is also harmful: Hans Van Dongen and colleagues (2003) found that people restricted to six hours a night for two weeks performed as badly on attention tests as people kept awake for one or two whole nights, yet they rated themselves as only slightly sleepy. Total deprivation for weeks is lethal in rats (Allan Rechtschaffen's studies in the 1980s), and a rare inherited human prion disease that destroys the thalamus and abolishes sleep, fatal familial insomnia, is invariably fatal. Interpreting human deprivation studies requires caution, however, because sleep loss is so often confounded with stress.

REM deprivation and REM rebound

In 1960 Dement woke volunteers every time they entered REM sleep, night after night, and woke a control group equally often during NREM sleep. Two things happened. First, the REM-deprived volunteers tried to enter REM more and more often each night, so that they had to be woken ever more frequently. Second, when they were finally allowed to sleep undisturbed, they spent much more time than usual in REM sleep. This compensatory increase is called REM rebound. Dement at first thought REM deprivation also caused anxiety and irritability, but later studies did not confirm serious psychological harm; people taking antidepressants that suppress REM sleep for months show no obvious ill effects. REM rebound is clinically familiar too: alcohol and many sleeping pills suppress REM, and when they are stopped, REM rebounds with intense, often disturbing dreams, which can tempt the person to go back to the drug.

REM sleep behaviour disorder, first described by Carlos Schenck, Mark Mahowald and colleagues in 1986, is the mirror image of cataplexy: the atonia that should accompany REM sleep fails, so the sleeper acts out the dream, sometimes injuring a bed partner. And the contrast between sleep terrors and nightmares is a classic exam point: the first comes from deep NREM sleep early in the night with little recall, the second from REM sleep late in the night with vivid recall.

Exam trap

Somnambulism means sitting, walking or performing complex behaviour while asleep. Extreme fear, agitation and screaming while asleep describes sleep terrors; acting out dreams (including nightmares) describes REM sleep behaviour disorder; uncomfortable sensations in the legs causing movement and lost sleep describe restless legs syndrome. Sleepwalking and sleep terrors arise from N3 (slow-wave) sleep, not REM: a person in REM sleep is paralysed and cannot walk. Sleep talking (somniloquy) can occur in any stage.

Clinical link

The first-line treatment for chronic insomnia is not a sleeping pill but cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which outperforms medication in the long run. Its techniques are examined by name. Stimulus control (Richard Bootzin, 1972) re-associates the bed with sleep: go to bed only when sleepy, use the bed only for sleep, get up if not asleep within about 20 minutes, and rise at the same time every day. Sleep restriction (Arthur Spielman) limits the time in bed to the time actually slept, building sleep pressure, and then extends it gradually. Paradoxical intention, a technique from Viktor Frankl's logotherapy, asks the patient to lie in bed in the dark and try as hard as possible to stay awake; this removes the performance anxiety of "trying to sleep", which is what keeps many insomniacs awake. Dereflection, Frankl's other technique, is different: it redirects attention away from oneself and the symptom toward other meaningful goals. A man told to try to stay awake is receiving paradoxical intention.

5.9 DreamsMedium yield · 8 Qs

Every culture has wondered about dreams. In the ancient world they were messages from gods or ancestors; in the Indian epics they foretell victory and disaster; in twentieth-century Vienna they became the key to the unconscious. Sleep research has answered some old questions and sharpened others. Before we look at the theories, let us be clear about what dreams are actually like.

What dreams are like

A dream is a sequence of images, feelings and thoughts experienced during sleep, usually as a story in which the dreamer takes part. Vivid, emotional, narrative dreams are most common in REM sleep, but mental activity also occurs in NREM sleep, mostly as shorter, more thought-like fragments; late in the night, NREM dreams can be as story-like as REM dreams. Everyone dreams every night, including people who insist they never do: "non-dreamers" have normal amounts of REM sleep and report dreams when woken from it.

Early laboratory work tested common beliefs about dreams. Do dreams flash by in an instant? No: in 1957 Dement and Kleitman woke people either 5 or 15 minutes after a REM period began and asked which it had been, judging only from the length of their dream; they were correct in 92 of 111 cases, so dreams unfold in roughly real time. Can outside stimuli enter dreams? Yes: when Dement and Edward Wolpert (1958) sprayed water on sleepers in REM sleep and woke them a few seconds later, the water had been worked into the dream in 14 of 33 cases (a leaking roof, a sudden downpour on a stage). What do people dream about? Calvin Hall, who collected tens of thousands of dream reports, and Robert Van de Castle (1966), who devised a coding system for them, found that most dreams are surprisingly ordinary: familiar settings and people, everyday activities and the dreamer's current concerns. Negative emotions such as anxiety and fear are more common than positive ones, and being chased, falling and failing an exam are among the commonest themes worldwide. Fragments of the previous day's events, which Freud called the day residue, appear often.

Why should a brain produce such experiences at all? The theories fall into a few families.

Freud: dreams as disguised wishes

Freud's The Interpretation of Dreams appeared in November 1899, but the publisher dated it 1900 to mark the new century, and 1900 is the date to remember. Freud called the interpretation of dreams "the royal road to a knowledge of the unconscious activities of the mind". His central claim was that every dream is a wish fulfilment: the disguised fulfilment of a repressed wish, often sexual or aggressive and often dating from childhood. During sleep the ego's guard is lowered, and these wishes press toward expression. If they appeared undisguised, the dreamer would be so disturbed that he would wake, so an internal censor disguises them. In this sense, Freud said, dreams are the guardians of sleep.

Freud therefore distinguished two levels of every dream. The manifest content is the dream as the dreamer remembers and reports it: the story, the people, the places. The latent content is its hidden meaning, the unconscious wishes and thoughts that gave rise to it. The mental process that turns latent content into manifest content is the dream work, and it uses four main mechanisms:

  • Condensation: several latent ideas are compressed into a single image. A person in a dream who has your teacher's face, your father's voice and your uncle's walk condenses feelings about all three.
  • Displacement: the emotional significance of an important but threatening object is shifted onto a trivial, harmless one. Anger at a parent may appear as anger at a stray dog.
  • Symbolisation (Freud's "considerations of representability"): abstract thoughts are turned into concrete, mostly visual images and symbols, so that a wish to be free of an obligation might appear as a flight from a locked room.
  • Secondary revision (secondary elaboration): on waking and in recall, the mind tidies the confused material into a more coherent, logical-seeming story, hiding the latent meaning further.

To interpret a dream, the analyst asks the patient to free associate to each element of the manifest content, retracing the dream work backwards toward the latent thoughts. The theory has been enormously influential in culture and in psychotherapy, but its scientific standing is weak. There is no convincing evidence that dreams systematically disguise wishes; many dreams (the repeated nightmares of trauma survivors, for example) are hard to see as wishes at all, as Freud himself came to accept in 1920; and the brain science on which he built the theory is now obsolete. What has survived is the broader idea that dreams draw on our emotional concerns.

Exam trap

The Interpretation of Dreams: 1900 (printed in November 1899 but dated 1900). Manifest content is what is remembered; latent content is the hidden meaning. The four mechanisms of the dream work are condensation, displacement, symbolisation (representation) and secondary revision; "sublimation" and "projection" are defence mechanisms, not dream-work mechanisms, and "day residue" is the raw material, not a mechanism. "Royal road to the unconscious" refers to dreams, not to free association or slips of the tongue.

Jung: dreams as messages from the psyche

Carl Gustav Jung (1875 to 1961), once Freud's closest colleague, broke with him in 1913 partly over dreams. For Jung a dream is not a disguise but a natural, spontaneous self-portrait of the psyche, speaking in the language of symbols. Its chief function is compensatory: it balances a one-sided conscious attitude, so that a man who is outwardly all confidence may dream of being lost and helpless. Dreams can also be prospective, anticipating future development. Here Jung's disagreement with Freud over the unconscious matters. Jung held that the unconscious exists on two levels: a personal (individual) unconscious of the person's own forgotten and repressed experiences, and beneath it the collective unconscious, the inherited layer of the psyche shared by all humanity, which he accounted for in evolutionary terms, through the similar brain structure inherited by all human races. Some dreams draw on the collective unconscious and present universal images, the archetypes (such as the persona, the social mask; the shadow, the disowned dark side; the anima, the feminine side of a man; the animus, the masculine side of a woman; and the wise old man), which Jung found repeated in myths and religions across cultures. Early in his career, at the Burghölzli clinic in Zurich, Jung had also developed the word association test, in which hesitations and odd replies to stimulus words reveal unconscious emotional complexes. Instead of free association, Jung used amplification, enriching a dream symbol by comparing it with parallel images from myth, folklore and religion. (Jung's theory of personality is taught in the Personality book.)

Activation-synthesis: dreams from the brainstem

The discovery of REM sleep made a purely physiological theory possible. In 1977 the Harvard psychiatrists J. Allan Hobson (1933 to 2021) and Robert McCarley proposed the activation-synthesis hypothesis. During REM sleep, circuits in the pons fire spontaneously and send waves of activation (the PGO waves of Section 5.7) up to the forebrain, stimulating visual, motor and emotional areas more or less at random. That is the activation. The cortex, which is built to make sense of input, does its best with this chaotic barrage and weaves it into a story, drawing on the dreamer's memories and concerns. That is the synthesis. Think of how you see faces and animals in drifting clouds: the clouds are random, but your brain imposes a meaning on them.

The hypothesis explains several features of dreams neatly. Dreams are intensely visual because the visual system is activated. They are bizarre, with sudden scene shifts, because the input is random. We dream of running yet being unable to move because motor areas are activated while the body is paralysed. And dreams are quickly forgotten because the brain chemicals that support memory formation (noradrenaline and serotonin) are almost absent in REM sleep. On this view dreams are not disguised messages; any meaning is added by the synthesising brain, although that meaning can still reflect the dreamer's personality. The hypothesis has been criticised: the neuropsychologist Mark Solms showed that patients with certain forebrain lesions stop dreaming although their REM sleep is intact, so dreaming depends on the forebrain, not just the pons, and dreaming also occurs outside REM. Hobson himself later revised the theory, and in 2009 proposed that dreaming is a kind of "protoconsciousness", a virtual-reality rehearsal that helps develop and maintain waking consciousness.

Information-processing, memory and threat-simulation theories

Information-processing theories see dreaming as a by-product or part of the brain's overnight handling of the day's experiences. In one early, provocative version, Francis Crick (1916 to 2004) and Graeme Mitchison (1983) proposed "reverse learning": REM sleep weakens unwanted, parasitic connections formed during the day, so that, in their phrase, we dream in order to forget. More influential today are memory-consolidation views, which link dreams to the role of sleep in strengthening and integrating memories (Section 5.8): new learning is replayed and connected with older memories, and dream content reflects fragments of this process. When Robert Stickgold and colleagues (2000) had people play the computer game Tetris for hours, many saw falling blocks as they drifted off to sleep, including amnesic patients who could not remember having played. Rosalind Cartwright, studying people going through divorce, argued that dreams also help regulate mood, working through emotional problems overnight. The neurocognitive or continuity view of William Domhoff holds that dreams express our waking concerns and conceptions, using the same cognitive systems that we use when awake; dreams develop gradually in childhood as these systems mature.

The Finnish psychologist Antti Revonsuo took an evolutionary approach in his threat-simulation theory (2000). Dreams, he argued, evolved as a virtual training ground in which our ancestors rehearsed responses to threats (being chased, attacked, injured or rejected) safely during sleep, making them better at handling such threats when awake. In support, threatening events are greatly over-represented in dreams relative to waking life, and children who have lived through real danger dream of threats more often. Critics note that many dreams contain no threat, and that the rehearsal would be useless if it is forgotten by morning. Table 5.8 brings the theories of dreaming together.

Lucid dreams

Sometimes a dreamer realises, within the dream, that he is dreaming, and may even take control of what happens next, choosing to fly or to turn and face a pursuer. This is a lucid dream, a term coined by the Dutch psychiatrist Frederik van Eeden in 1913. For a long time scientists doubted that such dreams were real rather than reports made up after waking. The doubt was settled by an ingenious method. Keith Hearne in Britain in the 1970s, and Stephen LaBerge at Stanford in the early 1980s, asked lucid dreamers to make a pre-arranged pattern of eye movements (left, right, left, right) as soon as they became lucid. Because the eye muscles are not paralysed in REM sleep, the signals appeared clearly on the EOG, in the middle of unmistakable REM sleep. Many people have at least one lucid dream in their lives, and a few have them regularly; lucid dreaming techniques have been tried as a treatment for recurring nightmares.

Hypnagogic and hypnopompic imagery

The borders of sleep have experiences of their own. Hypnagogic imagery occurs while falling asleep (in N1): flashes of light, geometric patterns, faces, landscapes, snatches of speech or music, or the sensation of falling that may end in a hypnic jerk. The Tetris images described above are hypnagogic. Hypnopompic imagery is similar imagery experienced while waking up, sometimes so vivid that a person sees a figure standing by the bed for a moment after opening the eyes. (The terms are credited to the French scholar Alfred Maury and the British writer Frederic Myers respectively.) Such images are common and normal, and they are distinguished from the hallucinations of psychosis by occurring only at the edges of sleep. When they combine with sleep paralysis, the experience can be terrifying, typically a sense of an evil presence pressing on the chest; many cultures interpret it as an attack by a spirit or ghost. Frequent, intense episodes, together with daytime sleep attacks, should raise the question of narcolepsy.

5.10 HypnosisLower yield · 4 Qs

Few topics in psychology are surrounded by so much showmanship. On stage, the hypnotist swings a watch, the volunteers' eyes close, and moments later grown adults are clucking like hens. Is this a special state of consciousness, or are the volunteers simply playing along? Psychologists have argued about that question for two centuries, and the argument is a good model of how science sorts real effects from wishful thinking.

Hypnosis is best defined as a social interaction in which one person, the hypnotist, offers suggestions for changes in perception, feeling, memory or behaviour, and the other person, the participant, experiences those changes as real and as happening by themselves, without deliberate effort. It usually begins with an induction: the participant is asked to focus attention (on the hypnotist's voice or a point on the wall), to relax and to let go of critical evaluation. The essential ingredient, the crux of the whole phenomenon, is suggestibility: a heightened readiness to accept and respond to suggestions. A suggestion is a communication that invites an automatic, non-volitional response, such as "your arm is becoming lighter and is rising by itself". Notice what is not required: neither sleep nor a special swinging watch, and not even a formal induction, since suggestible people respond to many suggestions without one.

Exam trap

The main crux of hypnosis is suggestibility. The "analytical mind" and "logical mind" are what hypnosis is said to quieten, not what it rests on. Hypnosis is not sleep: the EEG of a hypnotised person is a waking EEG, and people under hypnosis can hear, respond and, in general, refuse.

From animal magnetism to the Nancy school: a short history

The story begins with the Viennese physician Franz Anton Mesmer (1734 to 1815). From the mid-1770s, Mesmer claimed that health depended on the free flow of an invisible universal fluid through the body, which he called animal magnetism, and that a physician could redirect it to cure disease. In Paris from 1778 he treated fashionable patients in groups around a tub (the baquet) filled with "magnetised" water and iron rods; many fell into convulsive "crises" and declared themselves cured. In 1784 King Louis XVI appointed a royal commission, which included the American scientist Benjamin Franklin and the chemist Antoine Lavoisier. In some of the first blind experiments in history, they showed that people reacted dramatically to objects they merely believed had been magnetised, and not at all to objects secretly magnetised. The commission concluded that the effects were real but were caused by imagination, not by any fluid. Mesmer was discredited, but "mesmerism" lived on. In the same year one of his followers, the Marquis de Puységur, found that some patients entered not a convulsive crisis but a calm, sleep-like trance in which they spoke and obeyed instructions and afterwards remembered nothing; he called it artificial somnambulism.

The name we use came from the Scottish surgeon James Braid (1795 to 1860), practising in Manchester. After watching a travelling mesmerist's demonstration in 1841, Braid concluded that there was no magnetic fluid; the trance was a physiological and psychological effect of prolonged, fixed attention, which he produced by having patients stare at a bright object held above their eyes. He called the state "neuro-hypnotism" (nervous sleep), from Hypnos, the Greek god of sleep, and the shortened form hypnotism (hypnosis) stuck; his book Neurypnology appeared in 1843. Braid later realised that the state was not sleep at all and tried to rename it "monoideism", concentration on a single idea, but it was too late to change the name.

Indian context

Some of the most remarkable early uses of mesmerism took place in Bengal. James Esdaile (1808 to 1859), a Scottish surgeon with the East India Company at Hooghly, began in 1845 to operate on patients in a mesmeric trance, before chemical anaesthesia had reached India. Over the next few years he reported hundreds of operations, including amputations and the removal of large tumours, performed with little apparent pain and with a lower death rate than usual; the government gave him a small mesmeric hospital in Calcutta, and he described his work in Mesmerism in India (1846). Once ether and chloroform became available, mesmeric surgery quickly disappeared, but Esdaile's reports remain an early documented case of hypnotic analgesia.

In the second half of the nineteenth century, hypnosis became the centre of a famous French controversy. At the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris, the great neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot (1825 to 1893) studied hypnosis in his patients with hysteria and concluded that hypnotisability was itself a sign of a diseased nervous system, a pathological state that passed through fixed stages and was found mainly in hysterics. His prestige made hypnosis respectable for scientific study. Against him stood the Nancy school: the country doctor Ambroise-Auguste Liébeault (1823 to 1904), who had treated poor patients with hypnosis for years, and the professor of medicine Hippolyte Bernheim (1840 to 1919), who joined him. They argued that hypnosis is a normal phenomenon, that most healthy people can be hypnotised, and that it works entirely through suggestion. The Nancy view won the argument, and it is essentially the modern view.

The young Sigmund Freud learned from both sides: he studied with Charcot in Paris in 1885 to 1886 and visited Bernheim at Nancy in 1889. With the Viennese physician Josef Breuer (1842 to 1925), he used hypnosis to help patients with hysteria recall and emotionally discharge forgotten traumatic experiences, the "cathartic method" first developed with Breuer's patient Anna O. Breuer and Freud reported their ideas on the psychical mechanism of hysterical phenomena in 1893 and in Studies on Hysteria in 1895. Freud soon abandoned hypnosis, because many patients could not be hypnotised and the improvements often did not last, and replaced it with free association, in which the patient, relaxing on a couch, reports whatever comes to mind without censoring it; this became the technique on which psychoanalysis was built. A generation later, the American learning theorist Clark Hull's Hypnosis and Suggestibility (1933) brought hypnosis into the experimental laboratory.

Exam trap

Liébeault and Bernheim: the Nancy school, hypnosis as normal suggestion. Freud and Breuer: the mechanism of hysteria (1893) and Studies on Hysteria (1895); Freud, not Bernheim, is the father of psychoanalysis, and free association replaced hypnosis.

Who can be hypnotised? Hypnotic susceptibility

People differ greatly in how readily they respond to hypnotic suggestions, a trait called hypnotic susceptibility (or hypnotisability). It is measured by standardised scales in which a standard induction is followed by a graded series of suggestions, from easy to hard, and the person's score is the number passed. The best known are the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scales, devised by André Weitzenhoffer and Ernest Ropiequet Hilgard (1904 to 2001) at Stanford University (Forms A and B in 1959, Form C in 1962), each with 12 items. An easy item is that an outstretched arm becomes heavy and drops; harder items include hallucinating a buzzing fly, posthypnotic amnesia and failing to see an object that is in plain view. The Harvard Group Scale (Ronald Shor and Emily Orne, 1962) adapts the test for groups.

Scores form a roughly bell-shaped distribution: perhaps 10 to 15 per cent of people are highly hypnotisable, a similar proportion respond to almost nothing, and most fall in between. Susceptibility is remarkably stable; in one study it correlated about 0.7 with the same person's score 25 years later. It is not related to gullibility, weakness of will or most personality traits, but it is related to absorption, the tendency to become deeply immersed in imaginative experiences such as a novel, a film or a daydream (Auke Tellegen and Gilbert Atkinson, 1974).

Jean-Roch Laurence and Campbell Perry (1983) suggested to hypnotised people that they had been woken by loud noises on a particular night the previous week; about half later "remembered" the noises, and some insisted on them even after being told of the suggestion. For this reason, many courts treat hypnotically refreshed testimony with great caution or refuse it altogether, and "recovered memories" of abuse produced under hypnosis cannot be trusted without independent evidence. Hypnotic age regression does not make adults think like children; it makes them act as they believe children act.

Theories of hypnosis: a special state or a social role?

The theoretical debate is between those who think hypnosis involves a special division of consciousness and those who think it is ordinary social and cognitive behaviour.

Ernest Hilgard's neodissociation theory, set out in his book Divided Consciousness (1977), revived an idea of the French psychiatrist Pierre Janet. Hilgard proposed that the mind contains several subsystems of control, normally coordinated by a central executive. Hypnotic suggestion splits consciousness into separate streams: one part of the mind carries out the suggestion and has experiences that are walled off from the other, which remains unaware of them. The evidence that made the theory famous came by accident. During a classroom demonstration of hypnotic deafness, a blind student showed no response to loud sounds. A member of the class asked whether "some part" of him might be hearing, so Hilgard quietly told the student that if some part of him could hear, his index finger should rise. It rose. Hilgard called this concealed, aware part of the mind the hidden observer. In later experiments using the cold pressor test, in which a hand is held in ice water, hypnotised participants given analgesia suggestions reported very little pain aloud, while their hidden observer, reporting through automatic writing or key presses, reported pain almost as high as in the normal waking state. The pain was registered, Hilgard concluded, but kept out of the main stream of awareness by an amnesia-like barrier.

The social-cognitive theory of hypnosis (also called sociocognitive or role theory) denies that any special state is needed. Its roots lie in Theodore Sarbin's role theory of the 1950s: hypnotised people are enacting the role of "hypnotised subject" as their culture understands it, not faking but absorbed in the role, as an actor can be absorbed in a part. Theodore Xenophon Barber (1927 to 2005) showed in the 1960s that ordinary participants given "task-motivating" instructions (to try their hardest to imagine and experience what is suggested) responded to suggestions almost as well as hypnotised participants. Nicholas Spanos (1942 to 1994) argued that hypnotic responses are goal-directed actions shaped by expectations and by how the situation is interpreted; in a direct challenge to Hilgard, he showed that the hidden observer reported more pain or less pain depending on what participants had been told the hidden observer would experience, suggesting that the hidden observer was itself a product of suggestion. Irving Kirsch added that the expectation of responding is itself a powerful cause of responding. Martin Orne, for his part, used the "real-simulator" design, comparing hypnotised participants with people instructed to fake hypnosis, and found subtle differences, such as "trance logic", the untroubled acceptance of logical contradictions (seeing a hallucinated person and the real person at the same time).

Today the two camps have moved closer. Almost everyone agrees that hypnosis is not sleep, that it is not a magical surrender of will, and that expectation and suggestion drive it. But brain imaging shows that, in highly hypnotisable people, suggestion produces real changes in brain activity: Stephen Kosslyn and colleagues (2000) found that the suggestion to see colour in a grey pattern activated colour-processing regions, and Amir Raz, Michael Posner and colleagues (2002) found that a posthypnotic suggestion that words were meaningless symbols greatly reduced the Stroop effect, an effect that, as you saw in Section 5.4, people cannot normally switch off. Clinical link

Hypnosis has respectable clinical uses, mostly as an addition to other treatment: for chronic and procedural pain, irritable bowel syndrome, smoking and weight control, and anxiety; adding hypnotic techniques to cognitive behavioural therapy has been found to improve outcomes. 5.11 Meditation, sensory deprivation and psychoactive drugsLower yield · 3 Qs

Hypnosis showed that a few words from another person can change experience. The last altered states in this chapter are produced in other ways: by deliberately training one's own attention, by taking stimulation away, and by putting chemicals into the brain.

Meditation

Meditation is a family of practices that train attention and awareness, usually with the aim of calming the mind and bringing mental processes under greater voluntary control. The many techniques of the world's traditions fall into two broad kinds, which differ in what they do with attention.

In concentrative meditation (now often called focused-attention meditation), attention is fixed on a single object: the breath, a repeated word or sound (a mantra), a candle flame, an image or a prayer. Whenever the mind wanders, as it constantly will, the meditator notices this and gently brings attention back. The best-known modern form is transcendental meditation (TM), introduced by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi (1918 to 2008) in India in the mid-1950s and spread worldwide from 1958, especially after the Beatles stayed at his ashram in Rishikesh in 1968. In TM the meditator sits comfortably and silently repeats a personal mantra for about 20 minutes, twice a day. In mindfulness meditation (also called opening-up or open-monitoring meditation), attention is not narrowed but widened: the meditator observes whatever arises, thoughts, feelings, bodily sensations and sounds, with calm, non-judging awareness, noticing each and letting it pass without holding on to it or pushing it away. Its roots are in Buddhist vipassana ("insight") practice. Concentrative meditation is like narrowing the zoom lens of Section 5.3 to a single point; mindfulness is like opening it wide while keeping it steady.

What does meditation do? In 1970 Robert Keith Wallace reported that during TM, oxygen consumption, heart rate and breathing rate fell, skin resistance rose (a sign of relaxation) and alpha waves increased. The Harvard cardiologist Herbert Benson (1935 to 2022) then showed that the same changes, which he called the relaxation response (1975), could be produced by any similar technique (sitting quietly, relaxing the muscles and repeating a neutral word) and were not unique to TM. A sceptical review by David Holmes (1984) went further, concluding that meditation reduced physiological arousal no more than simply resting with the eyes closed. Later and better-controlled work has been kinder. Jon Kabat-Zinn (born 1944) developed mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) in 1979 for patients with chronic pain and stress, and Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale combined mindfulness with cognitive therapy in mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT, 2002), which substantially reduces relapse in people who have had three or more episodes of depression. Imaging studies have reported structural and functional brain changes in long-term meditators, for example greater cortical thickness in attention-related regions (Sara Lazar and colleagues, 2005).

Meditation research also teaches an important lesson about evidence. Suppose researchers survey 10,000 randomly selected adults in ten countries and find that people who meditate regularly report less stress than people who rarely or never meditate. Can we conclude that meditation lowers stress? Not necessarily. Random selection makes the sample representative, and a large sample makes the estimate precise, but neither makes the study an experiment. Without random assignment of people to meditate or not, the finding is correlational: perhaps calmer people find it easier to keep up a meditation habit, or perhaps a third factor, such as a healthier lifestyle or more free time, produces both. Only an experiment can show that meditation causes the reduction (the logic is taught in the Research Methodology book).

Indian context

Meditation is among India's oldest contributions to psychology. In Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, the eight limbs of yoga (ashtanga) move from ethical restraints and observances (yama, niyama), posture (asana) and breath control (pranayama) to withdrawal of the senses from their objects (pratyahara), concentration (dharana), meditation (dhyana) and absorption (samadhi). Dharana and dhyana are close to what psychologists call concentrative meditation, while Buddhist vipassana is the source of mindfulness practice. Sufism describes its process of psycho-spiritual transformation as a comparable journey, from our mundane surface awareness to the depths and heights of self-consciousness, using practices such as dhikr, the rhythmic repetition of divine names. (Indian psychology is taught in Chapter 2.)

Sensory deprivation

Meditation reduces the variety of what we attend to. What happens if the world's input is taken away almost entirely? In the early 1950s, in Donald Hebb's laboratory at McGill University in Montreal, William Bexton, Woodburn Heron and Thomas Scott (1954) tried to find out.

Key study: the McGill sensory deprivation experiments (1954)

Male students were paid 20 dollars a day, generous money at the time, to do nothing at all. Each lay on a comfortable bed in a lit cubicle for as long as he could bear, wearing translucent goggles that let in diffuse light but no patterns, cotton gloves and long cardboard cuffs that limited touch, with his head on a U-shaped foam pillow and the steady hum of an air conditioner masking other sounds. He came out only to eat and use the toilet. Although the money was good, most volunteers quit within two or three days. They found it hard to think clearly or concentrate, did worse on simple cognitive tests, became irritable and restless, and many experienced hallucinations, beginning with dots and geometric patterns and developing into whole scenes; Heron later described one volunteer who saw a procession of squirrels with sacks over their shoulders marching across a snowfield. They also became more easily persuaded by recorded talks about the supernatural.

The McGill studies, sometimes described as research into the "pathology of boredom", showed that the waking brain needs a steady flow of varied stimulation to function normally, an idea that fed into arousal theories of motivation (Chapter 10). Later researchers softened the picture. The neuroscientist John Lilly developed the flotation tank, in which a person floats in skin-temperature salt water in darkness and silence, and the psychologist Peter Suedfeld showed that short sessions of sensory deprivation, which he called the restricted environmental stimulation technique (REST), are often experienced as deeply relaxing and can help people stop smoking. The clinical relevance is real: hallucinations are common in people with severe visual loss (the Charles Bonnet syndrome), and the monotony of an intensive care unit contributes to confusion in some patients.

Psychoactive drugs

The most common way people deliberately alter consciousness is chemical: the morning coffee, the evening drink, the cigarette, the cup of bhang at Holi. A psychoactive drug is a chemical substance that changes perception, mood, thought or consciousness by acting on the nervous system, mostly at synapses: some mimic a neurotransmitter, some block its receptors, and some change how much of it is released or how quickly it is removed. (The synaptic mechanisms are taught in the Physiological Psychology book.)

Three terms describe what happens with repeated use. Tolerance is the decreasing effect of the same dose with repeated use, so that larger doses are needed to get the original effect; tolerance to one drug often brings tolerance to similar drugs (cross-tolerance, as between alcohol and benzodiazepines). Dependence is a state in which the person needs the drug to function normally. Physical dependence means that the body has adapted to the drug's presence, so that stopping it produces withdrawal; psychological dependence is a compulsive craving to use the drug for its emotional effects. Withdrawal is the set of unpleasant symptoms that follows stopping a drug on which one is physically dependent, and the symptoms are usually the opposite of the drug's effects: anxiety and tremor after alcohol, fatigue and depression after stimulants, and pain, diarrhoea and gooseflesh after opiates. Depressants slow the activity of the nervous system. Alcohol, the most widely used, acts largely by enhancing the inhibitory transmitter GABA; at low doses it disinhibits (which is why it can look like a stimulant), at higher doses it impairs judgement, coordination and memory, and at very high doses it can be fatal. Claude Steele and Robert Josephs (1990) described its effect on thought as alcohol myopia: alcohol narrows attention to the most salient cues of the moment, so that a drunk person responds to immediate provocations and forgets distant consequences. The barbiturates (such as phenobarbital and amobarbital) were once widely prescribed as sedatives and sleeping pills, but the margin between an effective and a fatal dose is narrow, and they have largely been replaced by the benzodiazepines, a class that includes diazepam (sold as Valium), alprazolam (Xanax), flunitrazepam (Rohypnol) and triazolam (Halcion). Benzodiazepines are used to reduce anxiety (as anxiolytics), to sedate and to induce sleep (as hypnotics); they are much safer than barbiturates on their own but still cause tolerance and dependence, and they are dangerous when combined with alcohol.

Stimulants increase nervous system activity. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors (Section 5.6); nicotine acts on acetylcholine receptors; amphetamines increase the release of dopamine and noradrenaline; cocaine blocks their reuptake, prolonging their action, and is followed by a "crash" of fatigue and low mood. MDMA (ecstasy) is a stimulant with mild hallucinogenic effects that releases large amounts of serotonin. Opiates, derived from the opium poppy (morphine, codeine and heroin) and their synthetic relatives (opioids such as fentanyl), relieve pain and produce euphoria because they mimic the brain's own opioids, the endorphins and enkephalins, at opiate receptors; they produce strong physical dependence, and overdose kills by stopping breathing. Hallucinogens (psychedelics) distort perception and thought. The best known, LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), was first synthesised by the Swiss chemist Albert Hofmann (1906 to 2008), who discovered its extraordinary effects by accident in 1943; psilocybin (from certain mushrooms) and mescaline (from the peyote cactus) have similar effects, largely through serotonin receptors. Cannabis (marijuana, and in India ganja, charas and bhang) is usually placed in a class of its own because its effects are mixed: its active ingredient, THC (tetrahydrocannabinol), acts on the brain's cannabinoid receptors to produce relaxation and mild euphoria, heightened sensory experience, a slowed sense of time, increased appetite and impaired short-term memory and coordination; at high doses it can produce hallucinations, and heavy use in adolescence raises the risk of psychosis in vulnerable people.

Exam trap

Valium, Xanax, Rohypnol and Halcion are all benzodiazepines. They are also sedatives and hypnotics in function, which makes those options tempting, but the question asks for the drug class they share, and "sedative" and "hypnotic" describe uses that barbiturates and other drugs share too. They are not barbiturates; examples of barbiturates are phenobarbital, amobarbital (sodium amytal) and secobarbital. Alcohol is a depressant, not a stimulant, despite its early disinhibiting effect; MDMA is usually classed as a stimulant with hallucinogenic properties.

Clinical link

Tolerance, withdrawal and craving are the building blocks of substance use disorders, which are among the commonest problems in Indian psychiatric practice (alcohol above all, then cannabis, opioids and tobacco); the diagnostic criteria and treatments are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book. Two facts from this chapter recur in the clinic: benzodiazepines and alcohol suppress REM sleep, so stopping them brings vivid dreams and broken sleep that tempt relapse, and long-term sleeping-pill use can itself cause insomnia, a reason that CBT-I is preferred.

The case of Armond's dancing eyes, revisited

Think back to the night in Chicago when the pens of Eugene Aserinsky's old machine began to jerk while his son slept. You can now say exactly what he was seeing. About 70 to 90 minutes after falling asleep, Armond had passed down through N1, N2 and the delta waves of N3, climbed back up, and entered his first period of REM sleep: a desynchronised, waking-like EEG, bursts of rapid eye movements, the large muscles of his body held in atonia by circuits in his brainstem, and, very probably, a dream. Through the rest of the night the pattern returned every 90 minutes or so, four to six times, the REM periods growing longer toward morning. At his age, about a fifth to a quarter of his sleep was REM; as a newborn it had been half.

The discovery that Aserinsky first suspected was a machine fault also changed how psychologists think about consciousness. Sleep is not the absence of mind but a sequence of states, switched on by the brain's own circuits and timed by a clock in the hypothalamus. Waking consciousness, too, turned out to be far smaller and more selective than it feels: a narrow stream chosen by attention from a flood of input, while much of the mind's work goes on unseen. Hypnosis, meditation, sensory deprivation and drugs show how far that stream can be bent. Aserinsky's real contribution, in the end, was to wake people up and ask them what they had been experiencing, which is the question this whole chapter has been asking.

Chapter summary

5.1 Attention: what it is, how it behaves and what captures it. William James (1890) defined attention as the mind taking possession of one of several possible objects, with focalisation and concentration as its essence. Attention may be involuntary, voluntary or habitual, and Sohlberg and Mateer's clinical model distinguishes focused, sustained, selective, alternating and divided attention. Its characteristics are selectivity, shifting, a limited span, fluctuation and limited division. The span of attention, studied by Jevons (1871) with beans and later with the tachistoscope, is about six to eight unrelated items (about four for errorless subitising), which is why number plates use short groups. Fluctuation is shown by the Masson disc. Attention is determined by external factors (intensity, size, contrast, change, movement, novelty, repetition) and internal factors (motives, interests, mental set, attitudes).

5.2 Selective listening: filters, attenuators and late selection. Cherry (1953) named the cocktail party problem and used dichotic listening with shadowing; listeners noticed physical but not semantic changes in the unattended ear, although Moray (1959) found that about a third heard their own names. Broadbent's filter model (1958), the first information-processing theory, placed an all-or-none filter early. Treisman's attenuation model (1964) turned unattended messages down rather than off, with dictionary units of varying thresholds, and best explains the cocktail party phenomenon. Deutsch and Deutsch (1963) and Norman (1968) placed selection late. Johnston and Heinz (1978) made selection flexible but costly, and Lavie's perceptual load theory showed that high load produces early selection and low load late selection.

5.3 Visual attention: spotlights, binding and blindness to the obvious. Covert visual attention behaves like a spotlight or a zoom lens (Eriksen and St James, 1986). Posner's cueing task (1980) separated endogenous (voluntary, central cues) from exogenous (automatic, peripheral cues) attention and revealed inhibition of return. Treisman and Gelade's feature integration theory (1980) proposed a parallel preattentive stage for features and a serial focused-attention stage that binds them, explaining pop-out, linear conjunction search slopes and illusory conjunctions. Inattentional blindness (Mack and Rock; Simons and Chabris's gorilla, 1999), change blindness (Rensink and colleagues; Simons and Levin) and the attentional blink (Raymond and colleagues, 1992) show how much goes unseen without attention.

5.4 Divided attention, automaticity, the Stroop effect and vigilance. Kahneman's capacity model (1973) treats attention as a single pool of effort allocated by policy; Wickens's multiple resource theory explains why similar tasks interfere more than different ones. Phone use impairs driving even when hands-free. Posner and Snyder (1975) and Shiffrin and Schneider (1977) distinguished fast, parallel, effortless, inflexible automatic processes from slow, serial, effortful, flexible controlled ones. The Stroop effect (1935) shows that automatic word reading interferes with colour naming, a failure of selective attention and a measure of inhibition. Vigilance, or sustained attention, declines over time (Mackworth's clock, 1948), as in radar and air traffic control work. ADHD, neglect and delirium are clinical disorders of attention.

5.5 Consciousness: definitions, levels and the brain. Consciousness is awareness of ourselves and our environment, with a level (measured by the Glasgow Coma Scale) and a content. James (1890) described a personal, changing, continuous, object-directed and selective stream of consciousness, whose most studied feature is daydreaming. Freud's topographic model divides the mind into conscious, preconscious (recallable at will) and unconscious (repressed) levels; modern psychology adds the nonconscious and the cognitive unconscious, supported by blindsight, fast threat processing and implicit attitudes. The reticular activating system maintains arousal; Chalmers's hard problem asks why processing is experienced at all. Altered states of consciousness (Tart, Ludwig) include sleep, hypnosis, meditation and drug states; the Mandukya Upanishad describes waking, dreaming, deep sleep and turiya.

5.6 Biological rhythms: the body's clocks. Circadian rhythms of about 24 hours free-run at about 24.2 hours without time cues and are entrained by zeitgebers, chiefly light. The suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus is the master clock, receiving light through the retinohypothalamic tract and controlling melatonin release from the pineal gland. Jet lag is worse flying east (phase advance), and shift rotations work better forward. Borbély's two-process model combines homeostatic sleep pressure with the circadian signal. Ultradian rhythms (such as the 90-minute sleep cycle) are shorter than a day; infradian rhythms (such as the menstrual cycle) are longer.

5.7 The stages of sleep. Polysomnography records EEG, EOG and EMG. Beta marks alert waking, alpha relaxed waking with eyes closed, theta N1, spindles and K-complexes N2, and delta N3 (slow-wave sleep; stages 3 and 4 in the older system). REM sleep, discovered by Aserinsky and Kleitman (1953) and called paradoxical by Jouvet, combines a desynchronised EEG, rapid eye movements, atonia, autonomic variability, genital arousal and vivid dreams. Cycles of about 90 minutes repeat four to six times a night, with N3 early and REM late. REM forms half of a newborn's sleep and about a fifth of an adult's (Roffwarg and colleagues, 1966). The VLPO switches sleep on; orexin stabilises waking; the pons generates REM.

5.8 Why we sleep, what deprivation does and when sleep goes wrong. Recuperation theories stress restoration and waste clearance, adaptive theories stress safety and energy conservation, and other theories stress memory consolidation. Sleep deprivation (Randy Gardner's 264 hours) mainly impairs mood and sustained attention and produces microsleeps; REM deprivation produces REM rebound. Sleep disorders include insomnia, narcolepsy with cataplexy, sleep apnoea, NREM parasomnias (sleepwalking and sleep terrors, from N3), REM parasomnias (nightmares and REM sleep behaviour disorder) and restless legs syndrome. CBT-I uses stimulus control, sleep restriction and paradoxical intention.

5.9 Dreams. Dreams are most vivid in REM sleep, unfold in roughly real time, incorporate outside stimuli and usually reflect everyday concerns. Freud (1900) saw dreams as disguised wish fulfilments in which the dream work (condensation, displacement, symbolisation, secondary revision) turns latent into manifest content; Jung saw them as compensatory symbolic messages that can carry archetypes. Hobson and McCarley's activation-synthesis hypothesis (1977) explains dreams as the cortex's synthesis of random brainstem activation; information-processing, memory-consolidation and continuity theories link dreams to the handling of experience; Revonsuo's threat-simulation theory (2000) gives them an evolutionary function. Lucid dreams were verified with eye signals; hypnagogic imagery occurs falling asleep and hypnopompic imagery waking up.

5.10 Hypnosis. Hypnosis is a social interaction based on suggestibility. Its history runs from Mesmer's animal magnetism and the 1784 commission through Braid, who coined hypnotism, Charcot's view of it as pathology, the Nancy school's view of it as normal suggestion, and Freud and Breuer's cathartic method. Susceptibility, measured by the Stanford scales, is stable and linked to absorption. Hypnosis can relieve pain and produce posthypnotic suggestions and reversible amnesia, but it cannot give superhuman strength or reliably recover memories. Hilgard's neodissociation theory and hidden observer contrast with the social-cognitive theories of Sarbin, Barber and Spanos.

5.11 Meditation, sensory deprivation and psychoactive drugs. Concentrative meditation (including TM) focuses attention on one object; mindfulness meditation opens it to whatever arises. Meditation produces Benson's relaxation response, and MBSR and MBCT have clinical value, though correlational surveys cannot prove that meditation causes benefits. The McGill sensory deprivation studies (1954) produced impaired thinking and hallucinations, while brief REST can relax. Psychoactive drugs (depressants including alcohol and benzodiazepines, stimulants, opiates, hallucinogens and cannabis) alter consciousness through synaptic actions and produce tolerance, dependence and withdrawal.

From the past papers

  1. The Stroop effect demonstrates interference in: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Long-term memory retrieval (b) Working memory (c) Selective attention (d) Procedural memory

    Answer: (c) Selective attention. Automatic word reading cannot be filtered out while naming the ink colour, so the task shows a failure of selective attention. The memory options are distractors; no stored memory is being retrieved or rehearsed.

  2. Which psychological process is the Stroop effect most closely associated with? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Memory retrieval (b) Attention (c) Perception (d) Language development

    Answer: (b) Attention. The colours and words are perceived perfectly well; the difficulty lies in selecting the relevant dimension and inhibiting the automatic one, which is attention. Reading skill matters, but the effect is not about how language develops.

  3. In cognitive psychology, the "Stroop effect" primarily demonstrates which cognitive phenomenon? (RML 2025)

    (a) The interference that occurs in reaction time due to conflicting information processing (b) Improved recall when semantically related information is presented (c) The increased accuracy of perception under divided attention tasks (d) The predictive power of schema theory on episodic memory

    Answer: (a). Conflicting word and colour information slows reaction time. Option (b) describes semantic priming or organisation effects in memory, (c) is false (dividing attention reduces accuracy) and (d) belongs to memory research.

  4. "Stroop effect" is an account of: (RML 2024)

    (a) Stimulus dominance (b) Semantic dominance (c) Instructional dominance (d) Conceptual dominance

    Answer: (a) Stimulus dominance. One dimension of the stimulus, the printed word, dominates the other, its ink colour, in controlling the response. "Semantic dominance" tempts because the word is meaningful, but the keyed answer describes one stimulus attribute overpowering another; the other two are not terms used for this effect.

  5. Air traffic controllers and radar readers provide us with a good example of which of the following phenomena? (RML 2024)

    (a) Selective attention (b) Sustained attention (c) Divided attention (d) Perceptual attention

    Answer: (b) Sustained attention. Watching for rare, unpredictable signals over long periods is vigilance, studied with the Mackworth clock. Selective attention picks one input among several; divided attention handles two tasks at once.

  6. Why do the number plates of motorbikes and cars carry only four-digit numbers coupled with some letters? (RML 2024)

    (a) Selected attention (b) Span of attention (c) Divided attention (d) Sustained attention

    Answer: (b) Span of attention. Only a limited number of items (about six to eight, fewer for accuracy) can be apprehended at a glance, so plates use short groups that an observer can take in quickly.

  7. Who developed the filter-attenuation theory? (RML 2024)

    (a) Broadbent (b) Treisman (c) Johnston (d) Heinz

    Answer: (b) Treisman. Treisman (1964) modified Broadbent's all-or-none filter into an attenuator. Broadbent proposed the original filter (1958); Johnston and Heinz (1978) proposed the multimode theory.

  8. You are at a party and someone calls out your name. Instantly, in the middle of a crowd, you can hear it. This phenomenon is called: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Bottleneck effect (b) Cocktail phenomenon (c) Divided attention (d) Sustained attention

    Answer: (b) Cocktail party phenomenon. Named after Cherry (1953) and shown in the lab by Moray (1959); Treisman's low-threshold dictionary units explain it. "Bottleneck" refers to the narrow stage in attention models, not to this effect.

  9. What term describes the phenomenon where individuals fail to notice significant changes in their environment, even when those changes are obvious? (RML 2023)

    (a) Change blindness (b) Inattentional blindness (c) Perceptual constancy (d) Selective attention

    Answer: (a) Change blindness. The stress on changes points to change blindness (Rensink and colleagues; Simons and Levin's door study). Inattentional blindness is missing an unexpected object, perceptual constancy is the stable perception of objects, and selective attention is the general process.

  10. Low-prejudiced people engage in: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Automatic information processing resulting in involuntary stereotyping (b) Controlled information processing resulting in refuting stereotype (c) Controlled information processing resulting in involuntary stereotyping (d) Automatic information processing resulting in voluntary stereotyping

    Answer: (b). In Devine's (1989) model, stereotypes are activated automatically in everyone, but low-prejudiced people use controlled, effortful processing to override them. Controlled processing cannot produce "involuntary" stereotyping, and automatic processing is not "voluntary".

  11. The term "stream of consciousness", referring to the flow of thoughts in the human mind, was coined by which psychologist? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Carl Jung (b) Wilhelm Wundt (c) William James (d) B.F. Skinner

    Answer: (c) William James, in Principles of Psychology (1890). Wundt analysed consciousness into elements, Jung described the collective unconscious and Skinner rejected the study of consciousness altogether.

  12. According to Freud, the ___ mind includes accessible memories, which can be recalled at will. (RML 2020)

    (a) Conscious (b) Preconscious (c) Unconscious (d) Psychic determinism

    Answer: (b) Preconscious. The conscious holds only what is in awareness now; the unconscious cannot be recalled at will; psychic determinism is a principle, not a level of mind.

  13. The Mandukya Upanishad describes four states of consciousness (avasthas). The "fourth", pure consciousness beyond waking, dream and deep sleep, is called: (Amity recall)

    (a) Jagrat (b) Swapna (c) Sushupti (d) Turiya

    Answer: (d) Turiya. Jagrat is waking, swapna (svapna) dreaming and sushupti dreamless deep sleep; turiya, "the fourth", is the witnessing consciousness present in all three.

  14. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) is crucial for: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Memory consolidation (b) Circadian rhythm regulation (c) Language processing (d) Pain perception

    Answer: (b) Circadian rhythm regulation. The SCN is the master clock, entrained by light through the retinohypothalamic tract. Memory consolidation depends on the hippocampus and language on Broca's and Wernicke's areas.

  15. In a typical major sleep period, how many cycles of NREM-REM are there? (RML 2024)

    (a) 2 to 4 (b) 4 to 6 (c) 6 to 10 (d) 10 to 12

    Answer: (b) 4 to 6. Cycles last about 90 minutes, so seven to eight hours hold four to six. Two to four is too few for a full night; six or more would need cycles far shorter than 90 minutes.

  16. During a normal night's sleep, how many times do we pass through the different stages of sleep? (RML 2022)

    (a) 8 to 11 (b) 5 to 8 (c) 3 to 5 (d) 4 to 7

    Answer: (d) 4 to 7. The standard figure is four to six cycles; of the options given, 4 to 7 is the range that contains it. Eight to eleven is far too many, and 3 to 5 and 5 to 8 each miss part of the usual range.

  17. Which of the following characterises slow-wave sleep? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Stage 1 to 4 and REM sleep (b) Stage 3 and 4 (c) NREM and REM sleep (d) Stage 1 to 4

    Answer: (b) Stages 3 and 4. In the older Rechtschaffen and Kales system, stages 3 and 4 (dominated by delta waves) were together called slow-wave sleep; since 2007 they are merged as N3. Stages 1 and 2 are light sleep and REM has a fast, desynchronised EEG.

  18. Which one of the following characterises somnambulism? (RML 2022)

    (a) Sitting, walking or performing complex behaviour while asleep (b) Extreme fear, agitation, screaming while asleep (c) Acting out nightmares (d) Uncomfortable sensations in the legs causing movement and loss of sleep

    Answer: (a). Somnambulism is sleepwalking, an NREM parasomnia from N3. Option (b) is sleep terrors, (c) is REM sleep behaviour disorder and (d) is restless legs syndrome.

  19. The main crux of hypnosis is: (RML 2020)

    (a) Analytical mind (b) Logical mind (c) Suggestibility (d) None

    Answer: (c) Suggestibility. Hypnosis works through heightened responsiveness to suggestion, measured by scales such as the Stanford. The analytical and logical faculties are what hypnotic induction quietens, not its basis.

  20. Researchers studying meditation contacted over 10,000 randomly selected adults in 10 nations. Those who meditated regularly were less likely to suffer from stress than adults who meditated less regularly or not at all. Can we conclude that meditation lowers stress? Why? (RML 2021)

    (a) Yes, because the sample size was large (b) Yes, because the adults were randomly selected (c) Yes, because the adults were randomly assigned (d) Not necessarily, because this wasn't an experiment

    Answer: (d). The study is correlational: calmer people may meditate more, or a third factor may cause both. A large, randomly selected sample improves generalisability, not causal inference, and nobody was randomly assigned, so (c) is simply untrue.

  21. Valium, Xanax, Rohypnol and Halcion are: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Benzodiazepines (b) Barbiturates (c) Sedatives (d) Hypnotics

    Answer: (a) Benzodiazepines (diazepam, alprazolam, flunitrazepam and triazolam). They do act as sedatives and hypnotics, but the class they share is benzodiazepines; barbiturates are an older, more dangerous class such as phenobarbital.

  22. A man with sleep-onset insomnia is told to lie in bed with the lights off and try as hard as he can to stay awake. This technique is: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Paradoxical intention (b) Stimulus control (c) Sleep restriction (d) Dereflection

    Answer: (a) Paradoxical intention, which removes the performance anxiety of trying to sleep. Stimulus control re-links the bed with sleep, sleep restriction limits time in bed to build sleep pressure, and dereflection (also Frankl's) turns attention away from the symptom altogether.
