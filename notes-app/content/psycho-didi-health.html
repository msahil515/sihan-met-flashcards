Health Psychology

Epidemiology and disease measures

Epidemiology is the study of the frequency, distribution and causes of infectious and non-infectious disease in a population. Its core measures are:

TermMeaning
MorbidityThe number of cases of a disease that exist at some given point in time.
IncidenceThe number of new cases at some given point of time.
PrevalenceThe total number of existing cases at some given point of time.
MortalityThe number of deaths due to particular causes.
Don't confuse. Incidence counts only new cases; prevalence counts all existing cases (new + old). Morbidity is about illness; mortality is about death.

Levels of prevention

LevelFocus
Primordial preventionInhibit the emergence of risk factors in the form of environmental, economic, social, and behavioral conditions and cultural patterns of living, etc.
Primary preventionAwareness programs, psychoeducation, vaccination, wearing a helmet.
Secondary preventionEarly detection and treatment; reversal of problems/damage.
Tertiary preventionOnly management, since reversal is not possible; stopping further progression of problems.
Key. Order by when they act: Primordial (before risk factors even appear) → Primary (before disease) → Secondary (early disease, still reversible) → Tertiary (established disease, manage and limit progression).

Health-behaviour models

The Health Belief Model (HBM)

Given by Rosenstock. It explains health behaviour through six constructs:

  • Perceived susceptibility — a person's subjective perception of the risk of acquiring an illness or disease.
  • Perceived severity — a person's feelings on the seriousness of contracting an illness or disease.
  • Perceived benefits — a person's perception of the effectiveness of various actions available to reduce the threat of illness or disease.
  • Perceived barriers — a person's feelings on the obstacles to performing a recommended health action.
  • Cue to action — the stimulus needed to trigger the decision-making process to accept a recommended health action.
  • Self-efficacy — the level of a person's confidence in his or her ability to successfully perform a behaviour.

Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB)

Given by Ajzen; earlier called the Theory of Reasoned Action. According to this theory, a health behaviour is the direct result of a behavioural intention. Behavioural intentions are themselves made up of three components:

  • Attitudes toward the specific action — beliefs about the outcomes of the behaviour (e.g. "if I change my diet, I will lose weight, improve my health, and be more attractive") and evaluations of those outcomes (e.g. "being healthy and looking good are desirable").
  • Subjective norms regarding the action — normative beliefs (e.g. "my family and friends think I should change my diet") and motivation to comply (e.g. "I want to do what they want me to do").
  • Perceived behavioural control — e.g. "I will be able to change my diet".

Transtheoretical Model of behaviour change (TTM)

Given by James Prochaska. It posits that health behaviour change involves progress through six stages of change:

  1. Precontemplation
  2. Contemplation
  3. Preparation
  4. Action
  5. Maintenance
  6. Termination

Biopsychosocial model of health

Health and illness are understood as the product of interacting biological, psychological, and social factors, rather than biology alone.

Model → author.
  • Health Belief Model → Rosenstock
  • Theory of Planned Behaviour (formerly Theory of Reasoned Action) → Ajzen
  • Transtheoretical Model (TTM) → James Prochaska
Don't confuse. HBM's self-efficacy vs TPB's perceived behavioural control — both are about confidence to perform the behaviour but belong to different models. TPB's older name is the Theory of Reasoned Action (which lacked perceived behavioural control).

Ethics & Laws

APA Ethical Principles and Code of Conduct

The American Psychological Association's (APA) Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct consists of an Introduction, a Preamble, five General Principles (A–E), and specific Ethical Standards. The Ethics Code applies only to psychologists' activities that are part of their scientific, educational, or professional roles as psychologists.

Preamble and goal

In the Preamble, psychologists are committed to increasing scientific and professional knowledge of behavior, respect and protect civil and human rights, and strive to help the public in developing informed judgments and choices concerning human behavior.

Goal of the Ethics Code: the welfare and protection of the individuals and groups with whom psychologists work, and the education of members, students, and the public regarding the ethical standards of the discipline.

General Principles (A–E)

Key. The General Principles are aspirational in nature; they cannot form the basis for sanctions. The Ethical Standards (below) are the enforceable rules.
PrincipleContent
A: Beneficence and Non-maleficencePsychologists strive to benefit those with whom they work and take care to do no harm.
B: Fidelity and ResponsibilityPsychologists establish relationships of trust; they are aware of their professional and scientific responsibilities to society and to the specific communities in which they work.
C: IntegrityPsychologists seek to promote accuracy, honesty, and truthfulness in the science, teaching, and practice of psychology; they strive to keep their promises and to avoid unwise or unclear commitments.
D: JusticePsychologists recognize that fairness and justice entitle all persons to access to and benefit from the contributions of psychology, and to equal quality in the processes, procedures, and services conducted by psychologists.
E: Respect for People's Rights and DignityPsychologists respect the dignity and worth of all people, and the rights of individuals to privacy, confidentiality, and self-determination.

Ethical Standards (Sections 1–10)

SectionStandard
1. Resolving Ethical IssuesAddress ethical conflicts, report serious violations, and avoid retaliation against whistleblowers.
2. CompetenceWork within areas of expertise, seek ongoing training, and refer cases beyond one's competence.
3. Human RelationsAvoid discrimination, exploitation, and conflicts of interest; obtain informed consent and minimize harm.
4. Privacy and ConfidentialityMaintain confidentiality, inform clients of its limits, and securely handle records.
5. Advertising and Public StatementsEnsure honesty in self-promotion, avoid misleading claims, and do not use client testimonials.
6. Record Keeping and FeesKeep accurate records, maintain transparency in billing, and handle financial matters ethically.
7. Education and TrainingProvide fair, accurate instruction and ensure students meet competency standards.
8. Research and PublicationObtain informed consent, protect participants, avoid plagiarism, and credit contributions properly.
9. AssessmentUse valid tests, ensure fairness in administration and interpretation, and explain results clearly.
10. TherapyObtain informed consent, clarify roles in multi-client settings, avoid dual relationships, and ethically terminate treatment.
Why it matters. Informed consent and confidentiality recur across multiple standards (Human Relations, Privacy & Confidentiality, Research, Assessment, Therapy). They are not single rules but threads that run through the whole code.

Mental Health Laws in India

Colonial laws

  • 1851 — The Lunatic Removal Act
  • 1858 — The Lunacy (Supreme Courts) Act
  • 1858 — The Lunacy (District Courts) Act of 1858

The Mental Health Act, 1987

Implemented in 1993. It is organized into 10 sections:

SectionSubject
IWordings
IIEstablishment process
IIIEstablishment and maintenance
IVAdmission and Detention
VInspection, discharge, leaves
VIProperty of Mentally Ill
VIIMaintenance of mentally ill
VIIIProtection of human rights
IXPenalty and Procedure
XMiscellaneous

Advantages: better than the Lunacy Act; OPD for outpatient cases; prohibition on research without consent; separate spaces for children and adults.

Disadvantages: treatment like criminals; isolation from society; exclusion of dementia and mental retardation; government men don't need a license; suicide considered an offence; excessive focus on hospitalization; no provision for rehabilitation.

Other key statutes and milestones

  • 1982 — NMHP (National Mental Health Programme) launched: treatment of disorder within the community using PHC (Primary Health Centres), providing training.
  • 1985 — Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act.
  • 1992 — RCI Act (Rehabilitation Council of India Act).
  • 1995 — Persons with Disabilities Act: mental illness was considered a disability for the first time.
  • 2013 — New Mental Health Bill: no solitary confinement; community living; confidentiality; right to contact family; legal aid.
  • 2016 — Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act (RPWD).
1995 Persons with Disabilities Act — recognized disabilities
  • Physical: cerebral palsy, leprosy, dwarfism, muscular dystrophy, acid attack
  • Visual impairment
  • Hearing impairment
  • Speech and language
  • Intellectual disability (ID): autism, specific learning disabilities
2016 Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act (RPWD) — added disabilities and benefits

Additional recognized conditions:

  • Neurological: multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease
  • Blood diseases: thalassemia, sickle cell anaemia
  • Mental illness (vague)
  • Multiple disabilities

Benefits: 5% reservation in education; 4% reservation in jobs; punishment for violation; special courts.

Mental Healthcare Act, 2017

Government support. Key characteristics:

  • A person can't be held for more than 90 days.
  • Appeal to Magistrate for discharge.
  • Monthly checks.
  • Licensing and supervision.
  • Legal aid.

Advantages:

  • Decriminalizes suicide.
  • Everybody has the right to mental health care.
  • Central and State Mental Health Authority to advise government.
  • Client's role in admission, treatment and discharge.
  • Prohibits ECT (in specified circumstances).
  • Establish hospitals nearby.
  • Insurance.
  • Financial punishment (₹50k–₹500k) and jail.
  • No sterilization.

Disadvantages:

  • Not made by mental health professionals.
  • Does not talk about awareness and prevention.
  • Western-standard based.
  • Expenditure allocation not clear.
Key. The advance directive is a signature feature of the 2017 Act: a person can state in advance how they wish to be treated (or not treated) for mental illness and nominate a representative.

Mental Healthcare (Amendment) Bill, 2022

  • Decriminalizes suicide.
  • Strengthens community mental health.
  • Ensures patient rights and care quality.
  • Mandates insurance for mental illnesses.
  • Reduces stigma through awareness.
Don't confuse the three big acts. Mental Health Act 1987 (implemented 1993, 10 sections, hospitalization-heavy, suicide an offence) → Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (decriminalizes suicide, advance directive, right to care, 90-day cap) → Mental Healthcare (Amendment) Bill 2022 (reinforces decriminalized suicide, community care, mandatory insurance).
Watch the years. Lunatic Removal Act 1851; Lunacy Acts 1858; NMHP 1982; NDPS Act 1985; MHA 1987 (implemented 1993); RCI Act 1992; Persons with Disabilities Act 1995; New Mental Health Bill 2013; RPWD Act 2016; Mental Healthcare Act 2017; Amendment Bill 2022.
