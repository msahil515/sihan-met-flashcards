1. Biopsychology as a neuroscience

The one idea behind the whole field. Biopsychology is the scientific study of the biology of behaviour: how the nervous system produces what we think, feel and do. It is the branch of neuroscience (the larger study of the nervous system) that keeps behaviour at the centre. The recurring lesson is that mind and brain are not two things: every thought is a physical event in tissue.

The six divisions of biopsychology

The field is split by what you study and how. Learn each by its defining method, because the paper tests "which division does X?".

DivisionWhat it studiesTypical method

Trap

Psychophysiology vs physiological psychology. Both have "physiolog" in the name. Physiological psychology = invasive manipulation of the brain, usually in animals. Psychophysiology = non-invasive recording from the surface of healthy humans. And neuropsychology = the study of human brain-damaged patients.

Kinds of research, kinds of inference

  • Pure vs applied. Pure = curiosity-driven knowledge; applied = solving a practical problem. Most real work is a blend.
  • Experiments vs non-experiments. Only a true experiment (the researcher manipulates an independent variable and randomly assigns) can support a causal claim. Quasi-experiments and case studies cannot fully rule out confounds.
  • Converging operations. Because every single method is flawed, biopsychology trusts a conclusion only when several different methods point the same way. This is the field's signature methodological value.
  • Morgan's Canon / law of parsimony: prefer the simplest explanation that fits; do not attribute a higher mental process when a lower one will do.
High-yield

Human brain-damaged patients = neuropsychology; drugs = psychopharmacology; non-invasive measures in healthy humans = psychophysiology; imaging of cognition = cognitive neuroscience; animal brain manipulation = physiological psychology; across species = comparative. Causation needs a true experiment; trust the conclusion many methods agree on.

2. Evolution, genetics & experience

The problem this fixes. The old "nature versus nurture" question is a false dichotomy. Genes and experience are not rival causes adding up to 100%; they interact continuously. A trait is a genetic program read out in an environment. You cannot say a behaviour is "60% genetic" for an individual, only how much variation in a population is associated with genetic variation (heritability).

Evolution of the brain

  • Natural selection (Darwin): heritable traits that improve fitness (reproductive success) spread. Behaviour evolves too.
  • Larger brains are not automatically "better"; what matters is brain size relative to body size and the expansion of specific regions, especially the cerebral cortex in primates.
  • Convergent evolution = similar traits arise independently (analogous structures). Homologous structures share a common ancestor. Do not read similarity as shared ancestry.
  • Spandrels & exaptations: not every trait is a direct adaptation; some are by-products later co-opted for new uses.

Mendelian genetics & the molecular layer

  • Gene = a stretch of DNA on a chromosome. Humans have 23 pairs; the 23rd is the sex pair (XX female, XY male).
  • Dominant vs recessive alleles; homozygous vs heterozygous; genotype (the genes) vs phenotype (the expressed trait).
  • Dizygotic (fraternal) twins share ~50% of genes (like ordinary siblings); monozygotic (identical) twins share ~100%. Comparing them is the classic heritability tool.
  • Central dogma: DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation) → protein.

Epigenetics: experience changes which genes are read

Epigenetics is the mechanism that finally dissolves nature-versus-nurture. The DNA sequence does not change, but experience can switch genes on or off via chemical tags, especially DNA methylation (silences genes) and histone modification. These marks can persist and even be inherited, so early experience literally edits gene expression: that is how environment becomes biology. The classic demonstration is the McGowan / Meaney rat studies, where high maternal licking raised glucocorticoid-receptor expression in the hippocampus, producing calmer, less stress-reactive adults.

Ontogeny = individual development; phylogeny = species evolution. The mnemonic "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" (Haeckel) is largely discredited, but examiners still test which word means which. Also: heritability is a population statistic, not a personal percentage. The Bouchard Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart gave high heritability estimates (IQ ~0.5–0.7 in adults); MZ ≈ 100% shared genes, DZ ≈ 50%.

3. Anatomy of the nervous system

Before any function makes sense you need the map and the cells that build it: the cells, then the divisions, the protective wrappings, the directions, and finally the brain regions.

The neuron, part by part

  • Dendrites receive input; surface area is multiplied by spines.
  • Soma (cell body) integrates input; holds nucleus, ribosomes, mitochondria.
  • Axon hillock is the trigger zone; it decides whether threshold is reached.
  • Axon conducts the action potential; may be myelinated.
  • Axon terminals (buttons) release neurotransmitter at the synapse.

Neuron types by function

  • Sensory (afferent) — periphery → CNS. Often pseudounipolar.
  • Motor (efferent) — CNS → muscles/glands. Multipolar.
  • Interneurons — local processing inside the CNS; the most numerous.

Meninges, CSF and ventricles

The CNS is protected by three membranes, the meninges, from outside in: dura mater (tough outer), arachnoid mater (web-like middle), pia mater (delicate, hugs the brain). Between arachnoid and pia is the subarachnoid space, filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

  • CSF cushions the brain, removes waste, and lets it float (reducing effective weight). It is produced by the choroid plexus in the ventricles.
  • Ventricles: two lateral → third → cerebral aqueduct → fourth → central canal / subarachnoid space. Blockage causes hydrocephalus.

  • Left: language (Broca, Wernicke), logic, sequential processing, calculation.
  • Right: spatial, music, faces, holistic, emotional prosody.
  • Corpus callosum — the major commissure; severance produces the split-brain (Sperry, Gazzaniga).
High-yield

5. Neural conduction & the action potential

Definition

An action potential (AP) is the basic signal a neuron uses to talk: a brief (~1–2 ms) all-or-none electrical pulse that travels down the axon and, at the terminal, triggers neurotransmitter release. Stimulus intensity is coded by the frequency of APs, never their size.

The resting membrane potential (RMP)

An unstimulated neuron holds its inside at about −70 mV (range −60 to −90 depending on neuron type) relative to the outside. Three forces set this up:

ForceWhat it doesEffect
Concentration gradientK⁺ wants to leave (high inside), Na⁺ wants to enter (high outside)Sets up the chemistry
Electrical gradientInside is negative, attracts cationsOpposes K⁺ leaving
Selective permeability at restMembrane is ~50× more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺ (leak channels)K⁺ dominates → RMP sits near K⁺'s equilibrium

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase) pumps 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in, using 1 ATP. It is electrogenic (net export of +1 charge, contributing ~−4 mV) and maintains the gradients, but it does not directly set the RMP, which is the K⁺-leak phenomenon.

Mnemonic

"K is in, Na is out, Cl is out, Ca is way out." · "3 out, 2 in, 1 ATP" (the pump) · "K sets the rest" (RMP is a K⁺ phenomenon).

High-yield

All-or-none law: once threshold is reached the AP fires at constant amplitude. Intensity is coded by firing frequency, never spike size. "Na⁺ in, K⁺ out" is the whole story.

Refractory periods

  • Absolute refractory period (~1–2 ms) — Na⁺ channels are inactivated (a "ball-and-chain" inactivation gate blocks the pore, distinct from being merely closed); no stimulus, however strong, can fire another AP. This forces one-way propagation and sets the maximum firing rate.
  • Relative refractory period (~2–4 ms after) — K⁺ channels still open / membrane hyperpolarised; a stronger-than-usual stimulus can fire one. Caps maximum rate at ~1000 Hz.

Na⁺ channels have three states: closed (resting), open (during AP), inactivated (after AP). They reset only after repolarisation.

Propagation & conduction velocity

  • Continuous (unmyelinated): AP regenerates point-by-point; slow (0.5–2 m/s), e.g. C fibres (slow pain, temperature, postganglionic autonomic).
  • Saltatory (myelinated): the AP "jumps" between Nodes of Ranvier (where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated), which is faster and far more energy-efficient. Up to 120 m/s in large A-alpha fibres (motor, proprioception). "Saltatory" = Latin saltare, to leap.
  • Velocity rises with myelination (biggest factor), axon diameter, and temperature.
  • Demyelinating disease (multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré) destroys saltatory conduction → slowing/block → numbness, weakness, vision loss.
Trap

What sets RMP = K⁺ leak channels, NOT the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (the pump is the gradient-maintainer). Absolute refractory = Na⁺ inactivated (≠ closed). Intensity is coded by frequency, not amplitude. NT release is triggered by Ca²⁺ at the terminal. Fastest fibre = A-alpha; slowest = C.

6. Synaptic transmission & neurotransmitter systems

The synapse, step by step

  1. AP arrives at the terminal.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters (the trigger ion — without Ca²⁺ there is no release).
  3. Ca²⁺ triggers vesicle fusion with the presynaptic membrane (SNARE proteins; synaptotagmin = the Ca²⁺ sensor); NT is released into the cleft.
  4. NT crosses the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.
  5. An EPSP (depolarising: Na⁺ in or Ca²⁺ in) or IPSP (hyperpolarising: Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out) results. Spatial summation (different synapses at once) + temporal summation (one synapse firing rapidly) at the axon hillock decide whether a new AP fires.
  6. NT is cleared by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion.

Postsynaptic potentials are graded (vary in size, decay with distance), unlike the all-or-none AP. Receptors come in two styles: ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels, fast, e.g. nicotinic ACh, GABA-A, AMPA/NMDA, 5-HT3) and metabotropic (G-protein-coupled, slower, longer-lasting, e.g. muscarinic ACh, most dopamine receptors). Autoreceptors on the presynaptic terminal sense the cell's own NT for feedback.

The other systems

  • Norepinephrine — cell bodies in the locus coeruleus (pons); one small cluster projects diffusely across cortex, thalamus, hypothalamus, cerebellum, cord. Drives arousal, vigilance, attention to novelty; rises in alertness, falls in sleep, peaks in stress. Receptors α1, α2, β1, β2, β3. β-blockers that cross the blood-brain barrier (propranolol) reduce somatic anxiety.
  • Serotonin — raphe nuclei (midline brainstem); dorsal raphe → forebrain/midbrain, median raphe → limbic, caudal → cord. ≥14 subtypes: 5-HT1A (raphe autoreceptor; buspirone partial agonist), 5-HT2A (psychedelic site, LSD/psilocybin; atypicals antagonise), 5-HT3 (the only ionotropic 5-HT receptor; ondansetron antiemetic).   • GABA — GABA-A is an ionotropic Cl⁻ channel; benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol and neurosteroids all bind allosterically. GABA-B is metabotropic (Gi) → K⁺ efflux; baclofen agonist.
  • Neuropeptides (slow, G-protein, modulatory): endorphin/enkephalin/dynorphin (opioids — μ, δ, κ), substance P (pain), oxytocin/vasopressin (bonding, fluid balance), NPY/AgRP (hunger), orexin/hypocretin (wakefulness; loss = narcolepsy).

Additional signalling details: gap junctions (electrical synapses) give ultra-fast bidirectional transmission with no NT; nitric oxide and endocannabinoids are retrograde messengers (postsynaptic → presynaptic); Dale's principle ("one neuron, one transmitter") is now qualified by co-transmission; agonists mimic/boost while antagonists block; AChE, MAO and COMT are the degrading enzymes (MAOIs treat depression; COMT clears synaptic dopamine).

LTP in the hippocampus depends on NMDA, a coincidence detector needing glutamate AND postsynaptic depolarisation to displace its Mg²⁺ block; Ca²⁺ entry through NMDA triggers LTP, while AMPA carries the fast EPSP. Dopamine is built from tyrosine, serotonin from tryptophan (do not swap precursors). Benzo vs barbiturate: both are GABA-A positive allosteric modulators at different sites — benzos raise channel-opening frequency, barbiturates the duration (which is why barbiturates kill in overdose and benzos, alone, usually do not). Mesolimbic → NAc (reward); mesocortical → PFC (cognition); nigrostriatal arises from substantia nigra, not VTA.

7. Research methods of biopsychology

Every claim about brain and behaviour rests on a method, and every method answers a different question: lesion, recording, imaging, stimulation, and psychophysiology.

Lesion / ablation methods

  • Ablation / lesion: remove or destroy tissue and observe the deficit (the oldest approach). Aspiration (suction), radio-frequency / electrolytic lesions, knife cuts, and chemical excitotoxic lesions (kainic acid spares fibres of passage, killing only cell bodies).
  • Reversible lesions: cooling or local anaesthetic (lidocaine) temporarily knock out a region, then it recovers, a cleaner causal test.
  • Stereotaxic surgery: the stereotaxic atlas + instrument reaches a deep target by 3-D coordinates from the bregma skull landmark (where coronal & sagittal sutures meet). This is how electrodes and cannulae are placed.

8. The visual system

Vision is the model sensory system because its wiring is best understood. The job: turn patterns of light into a neural representation of the world.

The retina

  • Light passes through the retina's layers to reach the photoreceptors at the back, so the retina is "inside out".
  • Rods — peripheral, very light-sensitive, no colour, low acuity (night/scotopic; pigment = rhodopsin). Cones — concentrated at the fovea centralis, colour, high acuity (day/photopic). This is the duplexity theory. The Purkinje shift: in dim light sensitivity moves toward blue (reds darken first at dusk).
  • The optic disc (where the optic nerve leaves) has no photoreceptors = the blind spot.
  • Signal flow within the retina: photoreceptors → bipolar cells → retinal ganglion cells (whose axons form the optic nerve), with horizontal and amacrine cells doing lateral interaction. Ganglion cells have antagonistic centre-surround receptive fields (Kuffler). Lateral inhibition sharpens edges (Mach bands).

The retina-geniculate-striate pathway

Retina → optic nerve → optic chiasm (nasal fibres cross, so the left visual field of both eyes goes right) → optic tract → LGN of the thalamus → optic radiations → primary visual cortex (V1 / striate cortex) in the occipital lobe. The map is retinotopic. LGN layers split into magnocellular (motion/contrast/coarse) and parvocellular (colour/detail). Hubel & Wiesel found V1 cells are feature detectors (simple, complex, hypercomplex cells tuned to edges, orientation and movement). Blindsight: cortical-blind patients respond to stimuli they deny seeing, via subcortical routes.

Theories of colour vision (complementary, not rivals)

  • Trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz) — three cone types (S/M/L); colour = ratio of their activity. Operates at the receptor (retinal) level.
  • Opponent-process (Hering) — colour coded in opposing pairs: red-green, blue-yellow, black-white. Operates after the retina (ganglion cells, LGN). Explains negative afterimages and why we never see "reddish-green".
Trap

The two colour theories are both right at different stages: trichromatic at the cones, opponent-process downstream (afterimages = opponent-process evidence). Rods do not code colour. Dorsal = where/how (parietal); ventral = what (temporal) — "dorsal is up and points to where".

Audition

10. The sensorimotor system

The organising idea: a hierarchy. Movement is planned top-down and executed bottom-up. The chain runs: association cortex (intention) → secondary motor cortex (SMA + premotor, the plan) → primary motor cortex M1 (the command) → brainstem & spinal circuits → muscle, with the basal ganglia and cerebellum tuning it from the side.

Descending tracts

  • Pyramidal (corticospinal): origin M1 (Brodmann area 4, the giant Betz cells of layer V) + premotor; path internal capsule → cerebral peduncle → pons → medullary pyramids; decussates at the pyramids of the medulla (~85–90% → lateral corticospinal tract for fine distal voluntary movement; the other 10–15% form the anterior corticospinal tract and cross at the spinal segment).
  • Extrapyramidal: rubrospinal (red nucleus, flexor bias), vestibulospinal (postural extensors, balance), reticulospinal (locomotion, posture, gross movement), tectospinal (head/neck orienting).

11. Development of the nervous system

How a single fertilised cell becomes a brain with ~86 billion neurons wired into the right circuits: five phases, then two sculpting processes.

  1. Neural tube formation (neurulation): ectoderm folds into the neural tube (~week 3–4); its hollow centre becomes the ventricles, the anterior end the brain. Failure = spina bifida / anencephaly (folic acid protects). The neural crest cells migrate off the tube edges to form the PNS, adrenal medulla and other tissues.
  2. Proliferation: cells in the ventricular zone divide to produce neurons and glia.
  3. Migration: new neurons travel to their destinations, many climbing radial glia; the cortex is built inside-out (deep layers first).
  4. Differentiation & axon growth: neurons take on their type; the growth cone navigates by chemical cues (chemoaffinity, Sperry).
  5. Synaptogenesis: synapses form, in huge excess at first.

Then refinement:

  • Neural death (apoptosis) & synaptic pruning: ~50% of neurons die and unused synapses are eliminated. Neurotrophins (NGF, BDNF) are target-derived survival factors that keep useful connections alive: "use it or lose it".
  • Myelination: continues for years; the prefrontal cortex finishes myelinating in the early–mid 20s (why mature judgement comes late).
High-yield

Critical / sensitive periods. Some circuits must get the right experience within a time window or they wire abnormally for life. Hubel & Wiesel's monocular-deprivation kittens lost cortical responses to the closed eye (amblyopia) — but the same deprivation in an adult did not. Language and binocular vision have sensitive periods. Experience is part of construction, not a finishing touch.

Trap

Sequence: proliferation → migration → differentiation → synaptogenesis → cell death/pruning → myelination. The cortex develops inside-out. Pruning reduces synapses. Do not confuse apoptosis (programmed, tidy death) with necrosis (messy injury death with inflammation).

Degeneration & regeneration

  • Anterograde degeneration = the cut axon segment away from the cell body dies. Retrograde = the segment toward the cell body (and sometimes the cell) degenerates.
  • Regeneration is much better in the PNS than the CNS, because Schwann cells guide and support regrowth, whereas CNS oligodendrocytes / glial scarring inhibit it.

Plasticity & recovery of function

"Plasticity" = the brain changes its structure and connections with experience and after injury. Recovery mechanisms: cortical reorganisation, collateral sprouting, redundant circuits, and behaviourally driven relearning (e.g. constraint-induced therapy, forcing use of the affected limb). Diaschisis is dysfunction of an intact area that lost its input from a lesion elsewhere, recovering over time. Adult neurogenesis occurs mainly in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (subgranular zone) and the subventricular zone → olfactory bulb, but is limited. Related plasticity phenomena: LTD (long-term depression, the weakening partner to LTP), kindling (repeated subthreshold stimulation lowers seizure threshold), and excitotoxicity (glutamate/Ca²⁺ overload kills neurons after stroke).

13. Learning, memory & amnesia

The landmark case. Patient H.M. had both medial temporal lobes (including the hippocampus) removed in 1953 to stop seizures. He could no longer form new long-term explicit memories (severe anterograde amnesia) yet kept old memories, normal IQ, and could still learn new motor skills (mirror drawing) without remembering having practised. This proved (1) the hippocampus is needed to form new declarative memories but is not the storehouse, and (2) declarative and procedural memory are separate systems.

Long-term potentiation (LTP): the molecular cascade

LTP = a long-lasting strengthening of a synapse after high-frequency stimulation, the leading cellular model of learning, first shown in the hippocampus (Bliss & Lømo, 1973). The cascade:

  1. Baseline: glutamate is released onto the postsynaptic spine; AMPA receptors open (Na⁺ in, depolarising), but NMDA receptors, though glutamate-bound, are plugged by Mg²⁺ at resting potential, so nothing flows through them.
  2. Strong / paired stimulation: the postsynaptic membrane depolarises enough to electrostatically expel the Mg²⁺ plug.
  3. The NMDA channel is now open with glutamate and voltage present, so Ca²⁺ flows in.
  4. Ca²⁺ activates CaMKII, which phosphorylates AMPA receptors (more conductance) and inserts new AMPA receptors into the spine.
  5. Next glutamate release at the same spine → bigger AMPA response = the synapse is potentiated. With sustained activity, gene transcription (CREB) + protein synthesis (Arc, BDNF) makes it last hours to days = late-phase LTP.

This is the molecular face of Hebb's rule: "neurons that fire together wire together." Block NMDA (APV, ketamine) → no LTP → no spatial learning (Morris water maze). The opposite process is LTD. The engram (physical memory trace) was named by Richard Semon and hunted by Lashley.

Amnesias

  • Anterograde = cannot form new memories after the event (H.M.). Retrograde = lost memories from before the event. Ribot's law: recent memories are more vulnerable than remote ones.
  • Korsakoff's syndrome = thiamine (B1) deficiency, usually from chronic alcoholism; damages the mammillary bodies + dorsomedial thalamus. Hallmark: dense anterograde amnesia + confabulation. The acute precursor is Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia); together = Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.
Trap

H.M. = bilateral medial temporal/hippocampus → anterograde amnesia, intact procedural learning; the hippocampus forms declarative memory but is not the permanent store. Korsakoff = thiamine, mammillary bodies + thalamus, confabulation. LTP needs NMDA; AMPA carries the fast current.

  • Set-point theory: the body defends a fixed weight like a thermostat. Explains homeostasis but not why we overeat or why obesity rises. The HPA axis & stress endocrinology

The HPA axis appears under four hats: biopsych "draw the axis", abnormal psych "HPA in MDD/PTSD/Cushing's", stress/health psych "Selye + allostatic load", and assessment "dexamethasone suppression test".

  1. A stressor (physical, psychological, immune) is sensed; the amygdala flags threat, prefrontal/hippocampus appraises context.
  2. The paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus releases CRH (41-amino-acid peptide) + AVP into the hypophyseal portal blood.
  3. Anterior-pituitary corticotrophs release ACTH (cleaved from POMC, alongside β-endorphin + MSH) into systemic blood.
  4. The adrenal cortex, zona fasciculata releases cortisol (+ small DHEA).
  5. Cortisol acts everywhere (gluconeogenesis, immunosuppression, sodium retention) and feeds back to shut off CRH + ACTH.

Adrenal-cortex layers (outside in): Glomerulosa (aldosterone), Fasciculata (cortisol), Reticularis (androgens) — "GFR, salt-sugar-sex". The medulla makes adrenaline/NE via the fast SAM axis (seconds), whereas the HPA is the slower sustained arm (minutes).

Cortisol actions & rhythm

  • Metabolic: ↑ gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, lipolysis, proteolysis (catabolic; raises blood glucose). Immune: anti-inflammatory, ↓ lymphocytes, neutrophilia. CV: maintains vascular tone, permissive for catecholamines. CNS: alertness; chronic excess → hippocampal atrophy, memory deficits, mood change. GI: ↑ gastric acid (ulcers). Bone: ↑ resorption → osteoporosis.
  • Diurnal rhythm: peak ~30 min after waking (the cortisol awakening response), trough ~midnight, tied to the SCN. Disrupted in shift work, depression, insomnia, jet lag.

The hippocampus is the brain's main inhibitor of the HPA axis via GR. Sustained high cortisol → dendritic atrophy in CA3, suppressed neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus, eventual volume loss → less feedback → more cortisol → more damage (the Sapolsky loop; ↓ BDNF underpins the neurotrophic hypothesis of depression). The amygdala by contrast tends to hypertrophy/hyperreact.

HPA in disorders

  • MDD: hyperactive HPA (one of the most replicated findings), elevated baseline cortisol, blunted/flattened rhythm, enlarged adrenals, impaired GR feedback. Dexamethasone suppression test (DST): 1 mg dexamethasone at night → in health, morning cortisol is suppressed; in ~50% of melancholic/psychotic depression there is non-suppression (sensitivity ~40–50%, specificity ~80–90%; the Carroll DST — suggestive, not diagnostic). SSRIs + CBT normalise the axis over weeks.
  • PTSD (paradox): often low basal cortisol with enhanced negative feedback (super-suppression on low-dose DST) and high CSF CRH — hypersensitive GRs. Contrast with MDD (hypercortisol + failed feedback).
  • Cushing's disease = pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma; Cushing's syndrome = any cause of hypercortisolism (often iatrogenic steroids). Central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, thin skin; depression in 50–80%. Addison's = primary adrenal insufficiency (↓ cortisol + aldosterone): fatigue, weight loss, hyperpigmentation (high ACTH → MSH), apathy/depression.
  • Also: HPA hyperactivity in first-episode schizophrenia and anorexia nervosa; childhood maltreatment → durable HPA dysregulation via GR-promoter methylation (Meaney).

Stress theory

  • Cannon (1915) coined "fight or flight" (the acute SAM response) and "homeostasis". Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS): alarm → resistance → exhaustion; he coined "stress" (the response) and "stressor" (the trigger). McEwen updated this with allostasis (stability through change), allostatic load (wear-and-tear of chronic adaptation) and allostatic overload (load becomes pathology). Lazarus & Folkman added cognitive appraisal (primary: is it threatening? secondary: can I cope?) and the problem-focused vs emotion-focused coping distinction.
  • Measuring the axis: salivary cortisol (diurnal rhythm/CAR), 24-h urinary free cortisol (Cushing's screen), midnight serum cortisol, the DST (low-dose 1 mg screen; high-dose 8 mg distinguishes pituitary from ectopic ACTH), the CRH stimulation test, and the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST).
High-yield

PVN → CRH → (anterior pituitary) ACTH → (adrenal cortex) cortisol → negative feedback at hypothalamus + pituitary via MR (high-affinity, tonic) and GR (low-affinity, stress). Chronic stress shrinks the hippocampus → feedback fails → cortisol stays high. MDD = hyper-cortisol + failed suppression; PTSD = hypo-cortisol + super-suppression. "Stress" = Selye, "fight or flight"/"homeostasis" = Cannon, "allostatic load" = McEwen, "appraisal" = Lazarus & Folkman.

The four stages (read off the EEG)

  • NREM 1 (~5%) — theta waves (4–8 Hz), very light, easily woken; hypnagogic images, hypnic jerks.
  • NREM 2 (~45–55%, most of the night) — theta + sleep spindles (12–14 Hz bursts) + K-complexes; spindles come from the thalamus and gate sensory input. The sign of being "truly asleep".
  • NREM 3 (~15–20%) — delta waves (0.5–4 Hz, high amplitude), deep slow-wave sleep, hard to rouse (sleep inertia). Growth hormone released; immune repair. Parasomnias (sleepwalking, sleep terrors, enuresis) arise here, common in children. Dominates the first half of the night.
  • REM (~20–25%) — mixed-frequency, low-amplitude EEG resembling waking ("paradoxical"), rapid eye movements, muscle atonia, vivid narrative dreams, autonomic instability, genital tumescence. REM episodes lengthen across the night (first ~10 min, last 45+ min) and dominate the second half. NOT delta-dominant.

The cycle = ~90 minutes, 4–6 cycles per 8 hours; if sleep is cut short you lose REM disproportionately. Across the lifespan: newborns sleep 16–18 h with ~50% REM and enter sleep via REM (adults via NREM 1); REM% and total sleep fall with age, and the elderly have less N3, more awakenings, and a phase advance.

Neural control — the flip-flop switch

  • Sleep-promoting: the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) of the anterior hypothalamus releases GABA + galanin to inhibit every wake-promoter. Adenosine builds with wakefulness to drive sleep pressure (caffeine blocks adenosine receptors).
  • Wake-promoting (ARAS): pontine cholinergic (PPT/LDT → thalamus), locus coeruleus (NE), dorsal raphe (5-HT), tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine — why old H1 antihistamines sedate), all supervised by lateral-hypothalamus orexin/hypocretin neurons that bias the switch toward "wake".
  • Flip-flop: VLPO and the wake nuclei mutually inhibit each other, making the system bistable (sharp transitions, unstable intermediate states). Lose orexin → the switch flips erratically → daytime sleep attacks + fragmented night sleep = narcolepsy.
  • REM-ON/REM-OFF: REM is driven by cholinergic REM-on cells (PPT/LDT) while aminergic REM-off cells (NE locus coeruleus, 5-HT raphe) fall silent. PGO waves herald REM onset.
  • REM atonia: descending glycine + GABA inhibition from the sublaterodorsal nucleus (subLDT) → medial medulla (magnocellular/gigantocellular nucleus) → spinal motor neurons paralyses skeletal muscle (sparing diaphragm + eye muscles). Lesion this (Jouvet's cats) → "REM without atonia". The human analogue is REM sleep behaviour disorder, which strongly predicts Parkinson's / Lewy body dementia within 10–15 years.
  • Circadian: the master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) (~24.2 h free-running period), entrained ("set") by light via the retinohypothalamic tract (from intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, a zeitgeber); the SCN drives the pineal to release melatonin in darkness. Borbély's two-process model: homeostatic sleep debt (Process S) × circadian rhythm (Process C).

Sleep, memory & deprivation

  • Declarative memory consolidates in NREM (slow-wave; hippocampal replay during delta); procedural in REM + stage-2 spindles; emotional in REM. After REM deprivation there is REM rebound. Sleep deprivation paradoxically lifts depression short-term (↑ BDNF, ↓ REM intrusion).

17. Reward, drugs of abuse & addiction

The mesolimbic reward circuit, end-to-end

  1. The VTA (ventral tegmental area, midbrain) is the origin of mesolimbic dopamine neurons.
  2. They project up the medial forebrain bundle (MFB) — the bundle Olds & Milner (1954) stimulated to produce self-stimulation.
  3. The main target is the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in the ventral striatum.
  4. From NAc, output goes to the ventral pallidum, then back via mediodorsal thalamus → prefrontal cortex, which sends glutamatergic feedback; the amygdala & hippocampus add affective + contextual signal.

Dopamine signals reward prediction error (Schultz), not "pleasure": it fires when an outcome is better than expected, is silent when expected, dips when worse. "Wanting" (incentive salience, mesolimbic DA) is dissociable from "liking" (the hedonic hotspot, opioid + cannabinoid signalling in NAc shell + ventral pallidum). Addiction is mostly wanting without liking — the addict craves long after the drug stops being fun (Robinson & Berridge's incentive-sensitisation theory). Behavioural assays: intracranial self-stimulation (MFB/VTA give the highest lever-press rates), conditioned place preference, and drug self-administration.

Core concepts

  • Tolerance = needing more drug for the same effect (dose-response shifts right; receptor downregulation). Withdrawal = aversive symptoms when the drug stops, often the mirror image of its effects (opponent process: NAc DA below baseline, amygdala stress system activated). Together = physical dependence.
  • Sensitisation = the reverse of tolerance: increased response with repetition (the incentive/"wanting" component).
  • Conditioned (Siegel) tolerance: tolerance is partly learned and cued by the setting, so a usual dose in a novel environment can become an overdose. ΔFosB is a transcription factor accumulating with chronic use, a molecular switch for addiction.

18. Lateralisation, language & the split brain

Most functions are bilateral, but some are lateralised. In ~90% of people (including most left-handers) language sits in the left hemisphere; the right is biased toward spatial processing, faces, music and emotional prosody.

The split-brain studies (Sperry & Gazzaniga)

Cutting the corpus callosum (commissurotomy, to stop severe epilepsy) leaves two hemispheres that cannot talk to each other. Because each visual field projects to the opposite hemisphere, an object flashed to the left visual field → right hemisphere cannot be named (language is on the left), yet the left hand (right hemisphere) can pick it out by touch. The left hemisphere ("the interpreter," Gazzaniga) confabulates explanations. Sperry won the Nobel (1981). Dichotic listening (Kimura): different sounds to each ear; the right-ear advantage for words reflects left-hemisphere dominance. The Wada test (sodium amobarbital into one carotid) clinically establishes the language-dominant hemisphere before surgery.

19. Emotion, stress & health

The neural circuitry of emotion

  • Papez circuit (the early emotion loop): hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → anterior thalamus → cingulate → entorhinal → back to hippocampus. MacLean extended this into the limbic system.
  • Amygdala = the hub of fear and threat detection. Its lateral nucleus is the input gate (sensory input from thalamus + cortex), the basal/accessory basal nuclei integrate cortical context, and the central nucleus (CeA) is the output gate → hypothalamus (autonomic), PAG (freezing, analgesia), brainstem (startle), basal forebrain (vigilance).
  • LeDoux's two roads to fear: a fast crude low road (thalamus → lateral amygdala) and a slower precise high road (thalamus → cortex → lateral amygdala). The lateral amygdala stores the CS-US association via LTP at its glutamate synapses.
  • Klüver-Bucy syndrome (bilateral amygdala / anterior-temporal lesion, e.g. after HSV encephalitis or lobectomy): hyperorality (mouthing objects), hypersexuality, placidity/tameness, visual agnosia, hypermetamorphosis.

20. Biopsychology of psychiatric disorders

Learn each disorder by its leading biological hypothesis plus the drug that follows from it. The diathesis-stress model frames disorders as biological vulnerability × environmental stress.

Schizophrenia

  • Dopamine hypothesis (original): too much DA. Evidence: D2-blocking drugs work; amphetamine (raises DA) mimics a paranoid psychosis.
  • Updated: mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia → positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions); mesocortical hypodopaminergia → negative/cognitive symptoms (avolition, flat affect).
  • Glutamate / NMDA-hypofunction hypothesis: PCP / ketamine produce the full spectrum (positive + negative + cognitive), whereas DA-only drugs produce only positive symptoms.
  • Structural: ventricular enlargement, reduced cortical grey matter (PFC + temporal), reduced hippocampal volume.
  • Drugs: typicals (haloperidol, chlorpromazine) = pure D2 blockade → EPS / tardive dyskinesia. Atypicals (clozapine, risperidone, olanzapine) = D2 + 5-HT2A blockade → fewer EPS, more metabolic effects. Clozapine = the only one shown to help treatment-resistant cases and reduce suicide; needs WBC monitoring (agranulocytosis).

Depression

  • Monoamine hypothesis: deficit of 5-HT & NE (TCAs raise both, SSRIs raise 5-HT, MAOIs raise everything). Limitation: monoamines rise in hours but mood lifts in weeks.
  • Neuroplasticity / BDNF hypothesis: loss of hippocampal volume + low BDNF; effective antidepressants raise BDNF and promote neurogenesis over weeks (fits the time course).
  • HPA hypothesis: hypercortisolism + DST non-suppression in melancholic depression (see §15).
  • Drugs: SSRI (first line), SNRI (venlafaxine, duloxetine), atypicals (bupropion = NDRI; mirtazapine = α2 + 5-HT2/3 antagonist), TCA, MAOI (tyramine diet). Ketamine/esketamine (NMDA antagonist) gives a rapid effect, reframing the field as glutamate-dependent. Somatic options: ECT, rTMS, DBS, VNS.

Bipolar, anxiety & addiction

  • Bipolar: strong polygenic loading. Lithium = first-line mood stabiliser (mechanism via inositol monophosphatase + GSK-3β inhibition; also reduces suicide in bipolar specifically). Antidepressant monotherapy can flip a patient into mania.
  • Anxiety: amygdala-centred fear circuit with weaker prefrontal regulation. GABA-A modulation (benzos) gives fast relief; 5-HT (SSRI/SNRI) is first-line maintenance for panic, GAD, OCD, PTSD, social anxiety. OCD = cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop overactivity (caudate hyperactivity on PET, normalising with high-dose SSRI / CBT).
  • Addiction: hijack of mesolimbic DA (see §17) with tolerance + sensitisation + opponent-process withdrawal; long-term cue-induced craving driven by amygdala + PFC + hippocampus.
Trap

Schizophrenia positive symptoms = excess mesolimbic dopamine (treated by D2 antagonists; they can worsen the mesocortical-hypo negative symptoms) + NMDA hypofunction; depression = too little 5-HT/NE (plus BDNF + HPA). Typicals → EPS/tardive dyskinesia; clozapine → agranulocytosis. Lithium = bipolar, not depression alone; lithium and clozapine each reduce suicide in their disorder.

23. Landmark studies, walked through

Stems love the who-did-what swap. Learn the procedure, not just the headline.

  • Hubel & Wiesel (1959+) — recorded single V1 cells in cats/monkeys: feature detectors (simple = orientation + position; complex = orientation regardless of position + motion; hypercomplex = adds length), ocular-dominance + orientation columns, and a visual critical period (monocular-deprivation amblyopia). Nobel 1981. Trap: "feature detector" ≠ "grandmother cell".
  • Olds & Milner (1954) — rats lever-pressed thousands of times/hour for electrical self-stimulation of the septal area / medial forebrain bundle = intracranial self-stimulation reward (ICSS), later mapped to mesolimbic DA (VTA → NAc). (Not memory — that is the other Milner, Brenda.)
  • Scoville & (Brenda) Milner (1957) — patient H.M.: bilateral medial-temporal removal → dense anterograde amnesia, intact IQ + working memory + procedural learning (mirror-drawing, Milner's demonstration). The most-cited memory case.
  • Sperry & Gazzaniga (1960s) — split-brain: right hemisphere can't name an LVF object but the left hand can select it; left hemisphere = "the interpreter". Sperry Nobel 1981.
  • Lashley (1929) — maze-lesion search for the engram yielded mass action (deficit ∝ amount of cortex destroyed) + equipotentiality. Hebb (1949, his student) countered with cell assemblies ("fire together, wire together"); LTP (Bliss & Lømo 1973) later vindicated him.
  • Penfield (1930s–50s) — awake cortical stimulation mapped the motor (precentral) + sensory (postcentral) homunculi; the disproportion reflects innervation density, not body size.
  • Phineas Gage (1848) — tamping iron through the orbitofrontal / ventromedial PFC → personality change, disinhibition, intact basic cognition (Damasio's somatic-marker reconstruction).
  • K.C. (Tulving) & Clive Wearing — episodic loss with spared semantic (K.C.); encephalitis amnesic with ~7-second span but spared music/procedural (Clive Wearing).
  • O'Keefe (1971) & the Morris water maze — hippocampal place cells = the cognitive map; the Mosers found entorhinal grid cells (shared 2014 Nobel). The water maze is the standard hippocampal spatial-memory test.
  • Kandel — Aplysia gill-withdrawal: habituation (less transmitter) vs sensitisation (more, via serotonin → cAMP); long-term memory needs protein synthesis + CREB. Nobel 2000.
  • McGaugh & LeDoux — basolateral amygdala + stress hormones strengthen emotional-memory consolidation; LeDoux's two roads (low: thalamus→amygdala; high: thalamus→cortex→amygdala).
  • Cannon-Bard, Papez & MacLean — Cannon-Bard thalamic theory (simultaneous body + emotion; "sham rage" in decorticate animals); Papez circuit; MacLean's limbic-system concept.
  • Bell-Magendie, Broca, Brown-Séquard — dorsal roots sensory, ventral roots motor (Bell-Magendie); Broca = left-frontal production (patient "Tan"), Wernicke = left-temporal comprehension; Brown-Séquard = ipsilateral touch/motor loss + contralateral pain/temp loss.
  • Bouchard — Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart → high heritability estimates; heritability is a population statistic.

24. Extended concept bank

The remaining named terms an MCQ can pull, grouped by home topic. Each is the kind of one-line fact a distractor is built from.

Neuroanatomy & cells

  • Nissl substance = rough ER in the soma (Nissl stain reveals cell bodies). Neuron doctrine = Cajal's principle that the neuron is the discrete unit (beat Golgi's reticular theory). Gyri/sulci/fissures = ridges/grooves/deep grooves. Commissures: corpus callosum (largest), anterior commissure, massa intermedia. Tract/nerve = axon bundle in CNS/PNS; nucleus/ganglion = cell-body cluster in CNS/PNS. White = myelinated axons, grey = cell bodies. Reticular formation = brainstem arousal/tone network (houses the ARAS). Area postrema = medullary vomiting centre (a circumventricular organ lacking a BBB). Blood-brain barrier = tight endothelial junctions + astrocyte end-feet.

Conduction & synapses

  • Nernst / equilibrium potential = the voltage at which an ion's electrical + chemical gradients balance (K⁺ ≈ −90 mV). Neuromodulator = transmitter that tunes many neurons diffusely. Gap junction = electrical synapse. Retrograde messengers = NO + endocannabinoids.
