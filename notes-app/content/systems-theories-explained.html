Q1What do Herman Ebbinghaus, Francis Galton and Sigmund Freud share in common?

  • aEach of them worked independently and were pioneers in the field of psychology
  • bEach of them founded a formal psychology laboratory
  • cEach of them developed novel observational methods to study how the mind works
  • d(a) and (c)
✓ Answer: (d) (a) and (c)
Ebbinghaus, Galton and Freud, late nineteenth-century pioneers
The concept

Why (d)Two separate claims, (a) and (c), are each fully true of all three men, and stopping at one undersells what they share.

The eraAll three belong to the late nineteenth-century emergence of psychology as a science, when the study of mind broke away from pure philosophy and physiology and began to use real methods.

Independent pioneers (a)Hermann Ebbinghaus (Germany, memory research), Francis Galton (England, differential psychology) and Sigmund Freud (Austria, psychoanalysis) worked in different countries and traditions and never collaborated. Yet each launched a brand-new line of inquiry on his own initiative.

Novel methods (c)None merely speculated. Ebbinghaus created the nonsense-syllable technique, with himself as sole subject, to chart the forgetting curve, bringing Wilhelm Wundt's experimental rigour to memory; Galton measured individual differences (questionnaires, twin studies, mental tests, correlation), seeding psychometrics and behaviour genetics; Freud used free association and dream interpretation to probe the unconscious, opening the dynamic/clinical tradition parallel to the academic labs.

Exam tipWhen two single options are each independently true and a 'both' choice exists, the 'both' choice usually wins, so never stop at the first correct-looking answer.

Panwar keyHerman Ebbinghaus, Francis Galton and Sigmund Freud, all 3 of them worked independently and were pioneers in the field of psychology. And they developed novel observational methods to study how the mind works.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) Each of them worked independently and were pioneers in the field of psychologyTrue of all three (independent, pioneering originality), but only half of what they share: it ignores (c)'s equally true point about new observational methods. True but incomplete, so (d) is the complete answer.
  • (b) Each of them founded a formal psychology laboratoryFounding a formal lab belongs to Wilhelm Wundt (first lab, Leipzig in 1879), the standard distractor. None of the three did: Hermann Ebbinghaus self-experimented, Galton ran an anthropometric measurement station, Sigmund Freud worked clinically.
  • (c) Each of them developed novel observational methods to study how the mind worksFully true of all three (nonsense syllables, correlation, free association), but it covers only their methodological side and leaves out (a)'s independent pioneering, so the combined (d) is more complete.

Q2In which year the first mental hospital in the USA was opened?

  • a1693
  • b1753
  • c1793
  • d1547
✓ Answer: (b) 1753
Pennsylvania Hospital, Philadelphia, backed by Benjamin Franklin (1753)
The concept

Why 1753In 1753 the Pennsylvania Hospital in Philadelphia, the first hospital in the United States, opened its doors and admitted patients with mental illness from the very start.

Before 1753In the colonial era there was no organised system of care: people with mental illness were confined at home, jailed or left to wander. No public institution received them, and madness was explained in moral and supernatural rather than medical terms.

What came after1753 opens the institutional and moral-treatment story, the move from neglect toward treatment, that later runs through Philippe Pinel 'unchaining' patients in France, William Tuke's York Retreat in England and Dorothea Dix's mid-nineteenth-century reform crusade across America.

General, not asylumChampioned by Benjamin Franklin and physician Thomas Bond, it cared for the sick poor of all kinds, and several of its earliest patients were mentally ill. So organised care began inside a general hospital; purpose-built facilities such as the Williamsburg asylum (1773), often called the first American hospital solely for the mentally ill, came later.

Exam tipFirst U.S. hospital equals 1753, Philadelphia, with mental patients among its first admissions: a general hospital, not the later dedicated asylums.

Panwar keyThe first hospital in the U.S. opened its doors in 1753 in Philadelphia. While it treated a variety of patients, six of its first patients suffered from mental illness.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) 1693Far too early: the American colonies had no public hospital of any kind then, and care of the mentally ill was entirely informal (home confinement or jail). A near-miss late-seventeenth-century decoy.
  • (c) 1793A real Philadelphia date, but it marks the catastrophic yellow-fever epidemic, not a hospital founding: right city, wrong event. The Pennsylvania Hospital had already been open forty years by then.
  • (d) 1547The year London's Bethlem Hospital (the infamous 'Bedlam') was granted to the City and became a dedicated asylum, in England, not the USA. Famous, but an English date: wrong by country.

Q3Who established the first laboratory for experimental study of psychology?

  • aGalton
  • bWeber
  • cWundt
  • dJung
✓ Answer: (c) Wundt
Wilhelm Wundt, Leipzig laboratory (1879)
The concept

Why WundtWilhelm Wundt opened the first laboratory devoted to the experimental study of psychology, conventionally treated as the founding moment of psychology as an independent science.

Before 1879The mind belonged to two parent disciplines: philosophy, reasoning from the armchair, and physiology, studying the body's sensory and nervous machinery (Weber, Fechner, Helmholtz). No separate discipline examined mental processes through controlled experiment.

The Leipzig labWundt fused the two at the University of Leipzig in 1879, Germany, measuring conscious experience under controlled conditions: timing reactions to stimuli (mental chronometry) and training observers to report sensations by introspection.

His legacyWith students (Edward Titchener, Hall, Cattell and others) who carried the method across Europe and America, he is the father of experimental psychology; his school is structuralism, later systematised by Titchener. He is the hinge between pre-scientific philosophy of mind and the later schools (functionalism, behaviourism, Gestalt, psychoanalysis, the cognitive era), each defined by agreeing with or revolting against his programme.

Exam tipLock the trio: Wundt, 1879, Leipzig. For who founded the first psychology lab or made psychology experimental, answer Wundt, not the physiologists or differential-psychology figures around him.

Panwar keyWilhelm Wundt opened the first laboratory of psychology in 1879, in Leipzig, Germany.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) GaltonFrancis Galton, same era: mental testing, individual differences, an 'anthropometric laboratory' (a tempting word-match). But that was a measurement station gathering public data, not an experimental lab for consciousness, and came after Leipzig.
  • (b) WeberErnst Weber, a physiologist: his sensory thresholds (Weber's law, the just-noticeable difference) fed experimental psychology. However, he studied sensation as physiology and founded no psychology lab; he is a precursor to Wilhelm Wundt.
  • (d) JungCarl Jung, a psychoanalyst who founded analytical psychology in the early twentieth century. Born 1875, only four years before the Leipzig lab, he worked in the clinical/depth tradition and founded no experimental laboratory.

Q4American Psychological Association (APA) was founded in:

  • a1892
  • b1893
  • c1896
  • d1897
✓ Answer: (a) 1892
G. Stanley Hall, Clark University (July 1892)
The concept

Why 1892The American Psychological Association was founded in July 1892 at Clark University, Worcester, Massachusetts, when a small group of psychologists met to create a national society advancing psychology as a science and profession.

First presidentG. Stanley Hall was its driving force and first president. He studied in Wundt's Leipzig tradition, opened one of the first U.S. psychology labs (Johns Hopkins), founded the first American psychology journal and trained many early psychologists.

The 1890sAmerican psychology came of age and detached from philosophy and physiology. The science born in Wundt's Leipzig lab in 1879 had crossed the Atlantic; William James had published Principles of Psychology (1890); structuralism and emerging functionalism competed; labs multiplied on American campuses.

Why it matteredPractitioners needed a shared organisation for standards, communication and annual meetings. The APA filled that role, marking psychology's professional, institutional phase, and is now the largest professional psychology body in the world.

Exam tipLock in APA, 1892, Clark University, Hall as first president. Watch the year: do not blur the founding with later annual meetings, the first formal convention or later expansions.

Panwar keyThe American Psychological Association was formed in July 1892 at Clark University, Worcester, Massachusetts, United States.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (b) 1893A deliberate off-by-one decoy next to the true year, catching anyone who recalls 'early 1890s' but not the exact digit. The APA was founded in 1892; the one-year gap is the whole trap.
  • (c) 1896The year Lightner Witmer opened the first psychological clinic (University of Pennsylvania), launching clinical psychology. A real 'first', but a different one from the APA founding.
  • (d) 1897Five years after the 1892 founding, a later year offered to muddy the timeline. There is no major APA-founding event in 1897; it only catches those unsure whether it was the early or late 1890s.

Q5What is true about Structuralism?

  • aAim was to create a "periodic table" of the "elements of sensations"
  • bAim was to understand the nature of consciousness
  • cAim was to study the behaviour and not mind
  • dall the above
✓ Answer: (a) Aim was to create a "periodic table" of the "elements of sensations"
Wundt and Titchener's structuralism
The concept

Why (a)The signature ambition of structuralism was a mental equivalent of chemistry's periodic table, cataloguing the basic elements of sensation.

The schoolFounded by Wilhelm Wundt in his 1879 Leipzig laboratory and systematised by his English-born American student Edward Titchener, it was the first formal school after psychology became a science.

The chemistry modelAs complex substances reduce to a finite set of chemical elements, complex conscious experience should reduce to irreducible mental elements: sensations, images and affective feelings. Disciplined observers isolated them by trained introspection, reporting the raw contents of awareness (quality, intensity, duration) under controlled conditions. The aim was analytic and descriptive: dissect consciousness into atoms and arrange them in an orderly table.

Its fallHistorically this was psychology's first attempt to look like a hard science. It was challenged by functionalism (what the mind is for, not what it is made of), then largely swept aside by behaviourism, which rejected the study of consciousness; its failure (introspective reports proved unreliable and irreproducible between laboratories) helped trigger both.

Exam tipStructuralists did care about consciousness generally, but the question rewards the unique goal: reducing consciousness to elemental sensations. So (a) beats the vaguer (b), and the behaviourist-flavoured (c) is simply wrong.

Panwar keyStructuralism was the first approach to understanding psychology after the field was born. The aim was to understand and be able to well describe the structures of which the human mind is formed. It also aimed at creating a table of elements of sensations like the periodic table in chemistry.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (b) Aim was to understand the nature of consciousnessLoosely true, a 'safe' choice, but too broad: it describes the general territory, not structuralism's distinctive aim, the 'periodic table of sensations' in (a). Nor can it fold into 'all the above', since (c) is false.
  • (c) Aim was to study the behaviour and not mindThat is behaviourism (John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner), which rejected introspection and consciousness: the opposite of structuralism, which studied the mind by introspection. Studying behaviour alone is false here, and that falsity also sinks (d).
  • (d) all the aboveOnly correct if every bundled statement is true, and (c) (behaviour not mind) flatly contradicts structuralism. One false member kills an 'all of the above' choice.

Q6According to John Dewey, ____ is the connection between sensory stimuli and motor responses.

  • aEfferent arc
  • bReflex arc
  • cAssociation arc
  • dDynamic arc
✓ Answer: (b) Reflex arc
John Dewey, The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology (1896)
The concept

Why reflex arcIn his 1896 paper The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology, John Dewey took the reflex arc, the supposed connection between sensory stimulus and motor response, as his explicit subject and target of criticism.

What it isIn physiology, a stimulus excites a sensory (afferent) pathway that connects through the nervous system to a motor (efferent) pathway, producing a response; it is usually drawn as a one-way stimulus-then-response chain.

Historical placeThe paper is a founding statement of American functionalism (the Chicago school) and an early shot against stimulus-response (S-R) psychology, which would harden into behaviourism under John B. Watson. S-R treated stimulus and response as separate basic building blocks stitched together.

Dewey's critiqueThat carving is artificial: sensation and movement are phases of one continuous, coordinated act of adaptation, and the response loops back to reshape how the next stimulus is perceived (the child reaching for the candle flame is one organised act). Physiology implies a unity of coordinated action, which the old psychology wrongly split. This stress on the whole adaptive act, function over fixed elements, makes the paper functionalist and a bridge toward organism-centred and Gestalt-flavoured thinking.

Exam tipThe term Dewey defined and then criticised is the reflex arc; his contribution was rejecting its fragmentation in favour of a unified, functional act.

Panwar keyJohn Dewey's Education theory based on his concept of functional psychology, focused on action and application. It went against the traditional concept of stimulus-response. Dewey through his theory argued that the neural circuitry of the reflex arc was being misinterpreted by behaviourist psychology which was using stimulus and response as basic units. Physiology implied a unity of coordinated action, but psychology was treating 'stimulus' and 'response' as separable.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) Efferent arcOnly the outgoing motor limb (efferent: carrying signals away from the centre toward the muscles), so it sounds plausible. John Dewey discussed the whole afferent-to-efferent loop; picking the half-term misses it.
  • (c) Association arcNot a term John Dewey used; there is no such structure in his reflex-arc paper. It borrows the respectable word 'association' (from associationist psychology) to fake a technical concept: a pure fabrication distractor.
  • (d) Dynamic arcNot a recognised Dewey concept either. It appeals because John Dewey argued the act is dynamic and continuous, not static, so the examiner turns his spirit into a plausible false label; he named the reflex arc.

Q7According to Titchener, Introspection is a highly specialised form of ______ in which the psychologist attempts to study his own consciousness scientifically.

  • aSelf perception
  • bSelf thinking
  • cAttention
  • dSelf observation
✓ Answer: (d) Self observation
Edward Titchener, structuralist introspection
The concept

Why self observationEdward Titchener framed introspection as a highly specialised form of self-observation in which the psychologist studies his own consciousness scientifically.

BackgroundWundt's English-born student, he carried German laboratory psychology to America and founded structuralism at Cornell in the 1890s. Its aim, inherited from Wundt's 1879 Leipzig programme, was to reduce mental life to elemental sensations, images and affective states.

How it worksReaching those elements needs a disciplined inward look, not watching behaviour from outside: a comparatively neutral, systematic act of observing the self, unlike casual everyday reflection. Trained observers ('reagents') reported an experience's quality, intensity, duration and clearness, avoiding the stimulus error: naming the object ('an apple') instead of the bare sensation ('a round red patch with these brightness and hue qualities').

Its weaknessHistorically this method was structuralism's fatal vulnerability: it proved unreliable and irreproducible, with different labs reporting different 'elements'. That let behaviourism (John B. Watson, 1913) dismiss it as unscientific and shift psychology to observable behaviour.

Exam tipTitchener's introspection is self-observation made rigorous, a controlled report of conscious contents rather than ordinary thinking, perceiving or merely attending. So the answer is self observation, not attention, self-perception or self-thinking.

Panwar keyAccording to Titchener, Introspection is a highly specialised form of self observation in which the psychologist attempts to study his own consciousness scientifically. Self-observation is the relatively neutral approach to study the self and was used by Edward Tichener.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) Self perceptionThe term from Daryl Bem's 1960s self-perception theory (we infer our attitudes by observing our behaviour), a social-psychology concept from another era. Edward Titchener's method directly reported the contents of consciousness instead.
  • (b) Self thinkingNot a recognised term in structuralism's account of introspection, just a loose echo of an inward act. Edward Titchener set his method apart from ordinary thinking, which is itself a conscious content to be observed.
  • (c) AttentionA real, central topic in early experimental psychology (focusing consciousness on a stimulus), but it is an object of study the observer reports on, not the act of self-observation Edward Titchener called introspection.

Q8The aim of Functionalism was to study ______ as it functions in adapting the ______ to its environment.

  • aOrganism, Mind
  • bMind, Organism
  • cThinking, Organism
  • dAttention, Individual
✓ Answer: (b) Mind, Organism
William James, Dewey and Angell's functionalism
The concept

Why (b)The classic aim of functionalism was to study the mind as it functions in adapting the organism to its environment.

The contrastStructuralism (Wundt's Leipzig lab, Titchener at Cornell), the first force, asked what the mind is made of, breaking consciousness into elemental sensations by introspection. Functionalism, the distinctively American second force, led in spirit by William James at Harvard and developed by John Dewey and James Angell at the University of Chicago around the turn of the twentieth century, asked what it is for.

Darwin's influenceCharles Darwin's evolution drove the shift: if every biological feature aids survival, mental processes (perceiving, remembering, feeling, willing) must serve an adaptive purpose too. The guiding question became practical and biological: how do mental processes help a living creature meet the demands of its surroundings and survive?

Mind serves organismSo the mind is what is studied and the organism is what it serves. This imported Darwinian thinking into psychology and later seeded evolutionary psychology and applied psychology (education, industry, mental testing).

Exam tipThe order of the blanks is the test: functionalism studies the mind functioning to adapt the organism to its environment, not the reverse.

Panwar keyFunctionalism was an early approach after the idea of Cartesian duality was popular. The aim was to study the mind as it functions in adapting the organism to its environment.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) Organism, MindThe right two words in reversed order, the trap for careless readers who skip checking the slots. Making the organism adapt the mind inverts functionalism's logic and its Darwinian, survival-serving role for mental processes.
  • (c) Thinking, OrganismToo narrow: thinking is one obvious mental activity, but functionalism's declared object was the mind broadly, the whole sweep of mental processes serving adaptation. Substituting one faculty is also historically inaccurate.
  • (d) Attention, IndividualBoth real terms, but attention is just one specific mental process, not functionalism's overarching subject, and 'individual' does not match the biological framing of an organism adapting to its environment. Both blanks mismatch.

Q9______ of the American Psychological Association represents Health Psychology.

  • aDivision 16
  • bDivision 38
  • cDivision 47
  • dDivision 98
✓ Answer: (b) Division 38
APA Division 38, Society for Health Psychology
The concept

Why 38Division 38 of the APA is designated for Health Psychology, now formally called the Society for Health Psychology.

How the APA worksThe American Psychological Association (founded 1892, the institutional milestone in psychology becoming a profession) is split into numbered divisions, each a distinct specialty or interest area. The numbers roughly track chartering order, not importance, so they are essentially arbitrary labels.

What it coversThe psychological side of physical health and illness: how behaviour, stress, beliefs and emotions affect the onset and course of disease, how to promote healthy behaviour, and how psychologists work in healthcare to prevent, treat and manage illness and disability.

BackgroundHealth psychology emerged in the 1970s alongside behavioural medicine. It rests on George Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977): health and illness arise from biological, psychological and social factors together, a direct challenge to the older purely biomedical model it helped overturn. The APA recognises clinical health psychology as a professional specialty, with Division 38 as its organisational home.

Exam tipA pure memory item: Division 38 equals Health Psychology. Anchor 38 firmly and do not let it slide into the neighbouring numbered divisions.

Panwar keyDivision 38 of the American Psychological Association represents Health Psychology. Under APA (American Psychological Association) clinical health psychology is a professionally recognized specialty that investigates and implements clinical services across diverse populations and settings to promote health and well-being and to prevent, treat and manage illness and disability.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) Division 16The Division of School Psychology, serving children and education systems. A real, well-known division, hence a strong decoy, but it has nothing to do with physical health and illness.
  • (c) Division 47The Society for Sport, Exercise and Performance Psychology. Exercise and fitness feel close to 'health', but this distinct division focuses on athletic performance, not the biopsychosocial study of illness (Division 38).
  • (d) Division 98A pure fabrication: no APA division numbered that high represents health psychology. The large, round-feeling number catches anyone without firm recall who gambles high; the real designation is Division 38.

Q10Which of the following historical perspectives gave rise to the evolutionary perspective?

  • abehaviourism
  • bStructuralism
  • cPsychoanalysis
  • dFunctionalism
✓ Answer: (d) Functionalism
William James and the Chicago school's functionalism
The concept

Why functionalismThe evolutionary perspective grew directly out of functionalism's central question: what mental processes are for.

FunctionalismFounded in spirit by William James and developed by the Chicago school (John Dewey, Angell), it arose in late-nineteenth-century America against structuralism. Where structuralism dissected the contents of consciousness, functionalism, shaped by Charles Darwin's evolution, asked how consciousness helps an organism adapt and survive, putting adaptation, purpose and survival value at the heart of explanation.

Evolutionary psychologyA modern perspective that crystallised in the late twentieth century (Tooby, Cosmides, Buss), it explains behaviours and mental traits as products of natural selection: how a tendency improved survival and reproduction in ancestral environments. The bridge is function and adaptive value, which it reframes as inherited, gene-based adaptations.

The other schoolsStructuralism (Wilhelm Wundt, Edward Titchener) catalogued what consciousness contains; psychoanalysis (Freud) used unconscious drives and childhood conflict; behaviourism (John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner) used stimulus-response links learned from the environment in a single lifetime. None put inherited adaptive function at the centre.

Exam tipFunctionalism, with its Darwinian adaptive emphasis, seeded the modern evolutionary perspective.

Panwar keyFunctionalism paved the way for the understanding and interpreting human evolution and behaviour from a perspective that focused on the functionality aspect of thought and behaviour, the choices and the fears that people make and have.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) behaviourismBehaviourism (John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner) studied conditioned responses and banned inner states and adaptive 'purpose'. Biological-sounding and scientific, but its environment-driven learning opposes the evolutionary stress on inherited, naturally selected traits.
  • (b) StructuralismStructuralism (Wilhelm Wundt, Edward Titchener) catalogued elemental sensations and ignored purpose, function and survival value: the opposite of adaptive thinking. It provoked functionalism as a reaction rather than seeding the evolutionary view.
  • (c) PsychoanalysisPsychoanalysis (Freud) explored unconscious drives, childhood conflict and instinctual energy; 'instinct' makes it feel biological. But it explains behaviour by intrapsychic conflict and developmental history, not naturally selected adaptive function.

Q11Who is the most associated with the techniques of introspection?

  • aWundt
  • bJames
  • cWatson
  • dWertheimer
✓ Answer: (a) Wundt
Wilhelm Wundt, Leipzig laboratory (1879)
The concept

Why WundtWilhelm Wundt made introspection the signature method of the first scientific psychology, so he is the figure most identified with it.

What it meantNot casual self-reflection but a trained, controlled, almost experimental procedure (experimental self-observation). Observers got a precise, repeatable stimulus and reported the immediate contents of consciousness (quality, intensity, duration) under standardised lab conditions, with trials repeated to check reliability.

BackgroundWundt founded the first psychology lab at the University of Leipzig in 1879, Germany, psychology's birth as a science, and built structuralism around this method, breaking experience into elements on analogy with chemistry. His student Edward Titchener carried it to America and refined it (the stimulus error), but Wundt is its originator.

The other foundersWilliam James (functionalism) was sceptical of rigid introspection; John Watson (behaviourism, 1913) rejected it as unscientific, demanding observable behaviour; Max Wertheimer (Gestalt) studied whole perceptual forms. Indeed, introspection's unreliability between labs gave behaviourism its opening.

Exam tipWundt, introspection, the Leipzig 1879 lab and structuralism form one cluster: when introspection is named, default to Wundt.

Panwar keyWilhelm Wundt, the founder of modern psychology, a proponent of the structuralist perspective, used the technique of introspection to understand and interpret the human mind. He started the first psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (b) JamesWilliam James, same generation, used and discussed introspection, but founded functionalism and criticised Wilhelm Wundt and Edward Titchener's rigid, element-hunting version, preferring a naturalistic study of the 'stream of consciousness'.
  • (c) WatsonJohn Watson is linked to introspection for the opposite reason: his 1913 behaviourist manifesto rejected it as unscientific and unverifiable, demanding observable behaviour. Choosing him reverses the relationship; he is its great opponent.
  • (d) WertheimerMax Wertheimer founded Gestalt psychology, which studied whole perceptual organisations ('the whole is greater than the sum of its parts') and reacted against the atomistic, element-by-element approach introspection served.

Q12Which among the following branches of Psychology specifically studies behaviour in relation to genetic inheritance?

  • aEvolutionary Psychology
  • bDevelopment Psychology
  • cHealth Psychology
  • dExperimental Psychology
✓ Answer: (a) Evolutionary Psychology
Darwin-based evolutionary psychology (Cosmides, Tooby, Buss)
The concept

Why this oneEvolutionary psychology is the branch that specifically explains behaviour in relation to genetic inheritance and the action of natural selection.

RootsIt applies Charles Darwin's nineteenth-century theory of evolution to the mind, treating psychological traits as products of the selection pressures our ancestors faced. Conceptually it descends from functionalism (William James, Chicago school), which first asked what mental processes are for; it grounded that in genetics, crystallising in the late twentieth century through Leda Cosmides, John Tooby and David Buss.

Core ideaTendencies that improved survival and reproduction passed down through the genes, so present-day behaviour carries inherited adaptations from the ancestral 'environment of evolutionary adaptedness'. Fusing psychology with biology, it asks why an emotion, mate preference or fear exists by reconstructing the adaptive problem it solved: ancestral environment, then naturally selected genes, then an inherited tendency.

The othersDevelopmental psychology studies change across the lifespan, health psychology studies behaviour and illness, and experimental psychology names a method, not a content area.

Exam tipWhen a question ties behaviour to genes, heredity or natural selection, the answer is evolutionary psychology: heredity and adaptation are its defining lens.

Panwar keyEvolutionary psychology is a scientific discipline that approaches human behaviour through a lens that incorporates the effects of evolution. It combines the science of psychology with the study of biology. It specifically studies behaviour in relation to genetic inheritance.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (b) Development PsychologyDevelopmental psychology studies change across the lifespan, infancy to old age, and touches genes via nature-nurture. But it weighs nature and nurture over time; 'in relation to genetic inheritance' points to evolutionary psychology.
  • (c) Health PsychologyHealth psychology studies how stress, behaviour and beliefs affect physical health and illness, using the biopsychosocial model. 'Biology' makes it feel gene-related, but it is about health behaviour and illness, not inherited traits.
  • (d) Experimental PsychologyExperimental psychology is defined by its method (controlled laboratory experiments), usable on memory, perception, learning and more, not by any subject matter. It sounds rigorous, but choosing it confuses a methodology with a content focus.

Q13Which among the following Schools of Thought used Introspection as a method of analysis?

  • aFunctionalism
  • bbehaviourism
  • cStructuralism
  • dPsychoanalysis
✓ Answer: (c) Structuralism
Wundt and Titchener's structuralism
The concept

Why structuralismStructuralism is the school of thought built on introspection as its method of analysis.

The schoolFounded by Wilhelm Wundt in his 1879 Leipzig laboratory, when psychology became an independent science, and systematised by his English-born student Edward Titchener at Cornell, it was the first formal school. Its chemistry-borrowed aim was to reduce mental life to basic elements (sensations, images, affective feelings), like a periodic table of the mind.

Why introspectionYou cannot reach those elements by watching behaviour from outside; you must look inward in a disciplined way. Introspection gave a trained, systematic report of one's raw conscious contents under controlled conditions, describing bare qualities (intensity, duration, quality) rather than the object that caused them, so avoiding the stimulus error.

The other schoolsBehaviourism (John B. Watson, around 1913) rejected introspection as unscientific; functionalism (James, John Dewey) used broader observation and applied work; psychoanalysis (Freud) used free association and dream analysis to reach the unconscious. Indeed, introspection's unreliability between labs discredited structuralism and opened the door to behaviourism.

Exam tipIntrospection is the structuralist tool: when a question pairs a school with introspection, choose structuralism.

Panwar keyIt has often been claimed that Wilhelm Wundt, the father of experimental psychology and founder of structuralism, was the first to adopt introspection to experimental psychology. The focus of structuralism was on reducing mental processes down into their most basic elements. The structuralists used techniques such as introspection to analyse the inner processes of the human mind.

Why the other options are wrong
  • (a) FunctionalismFunctionalism (James, John Dewey), same era, also studied consciousness, but asked what it is for in adaptation and used varied methods (naturalistic observation, mental testing, applied work) rather than strict laboratory introspection.
  • (b) behaviourismBehaviourism (John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner) is famous for rejecting introspection: Watson called it unscientific and studied observable behaviour alone. Choosing it reverses the relationship; it is introspection's chief opponent.
  • (d) PsychoanalysisPsychoanalysis (Freud) used inward-looking clinical techniques, free association and dream analysis, to uncover the unconscious: a depth approach quite unlike controlled laboratory introspection on conscious sensory elements.

Q14Who among the following is credited with the book titled Principles of Psychology?

  • aWilhelm Wundt
  • bWilliam James
  • cB.F. Skinner
  • dNone of the above
✓ Answer: (b) William James
William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890)
The concept

Why JamesWilliam James wrote the landmark two-volume The Principles of Psychology, published in 1890.

BackgroundPsychology was born in 1879 in Wundt's Leipzig lab and crossed the Atlantic within a decade; James, at Harvard, became its leading American voice. Trained first as a physician before turning to physiology, psychology and philosophy, he set up an early demonstration lab in the United States and inspired functionalism (what the mind is for), a Darwinian counterweight to the structuralism of Wilhelm Wundt and Titchener.

The bookA synthesis of more than a decade of his thinking, it became the foundational American textbook of the discipline, shaping generations; it was so vast that he later issued a condensed version, nicknamed 'Jimmy'.

Q18Which one of the following branches of philosophy of science deals with the question of existence?

  • aPost-positivism
  • bStructuralism
  • cOntology
  • dScientific realism
✓ Answer: (c) Ontology
Ontology, the study of being
The concept

Why ontologyAmong the branches of the philosophy of science, ontology is the one that deals with the question of existence.

What it isThe branch of metaphysics concerned with what exists, the nature of being, and the kinds and structures of entities that make up reality. From the Greek ontos (being) and logos (study), it asks what sorts of things exist, how they are categorised and what it means to be real.

Its sister branchEpistemology (from episteme, knowledge) asks instead how we can know reality and what counts as valid knowledge. Exam tipPost-positivism and scientific realism are positions within the philosophy of science (largely epistemological or metaphysical stances), and structuralism is a school of thought. Existence and being equal ontology; knowing equals epistemology.

Panwar keyOntology, as a branch of philosophy, is the science of what is, of the kinds and structures of objects. In simple terms, ontology seeks the classification and explanation of entities. Ontology concerns claims about the nature of being and existence.

Why the other options are wrong

  • (b) StructuralismIn psychology, Wilhelm Wundt and Edward Titchener's school analysing the structure of consciousness; in linguistics/anthropology, a separate movement. Either way a school or method, not a branch about being: a familiar-word trap.
  • (d) Scientific realismThe position that successful scientific theories truly describe a mind-independent world, unobservables included. It touches existence, a clever trap, but it is a stance on whether theories track reality, not the branch studying being.

Q27In a psychoanalytic practice, where the patient relaxes on a couch and freely tells whatever comes into his/her mind is called

  • aDreaming
  • bNarration
  • cFree association
  • dStory telling
✓ Answer: (c) Free association
Freud, classical psychoanalytic technique
The concept

Why (c)Free association is Freud's defining technique: the patient reclines and reports whatever enters the mind, uncensored.

The goalPsychoanalysis aims to surface repressed material from the unconscious. Direct questioning fails because the patient's defences guard the painful content; free association sidesteps them by having the patient voice every thought, image and feeling as it arises, however trivial, embarrassing, illogical or irrelevant.

Why it worksIt rests on psychic determinism: mental life is never random, so a chain of seemingly stray associations is steered by unconscious conflicts, and following it leads back to the repressed root.

HistoricallyFreud developed free association to replace hypnosis, the cathartic-hypnotic method he took from Charcot in Paris and his colleague Josef Breuer in Vienna; hypnosis was unreliable and made patients parrot the analyst's suggestions, while free association let patients 'speak for themselves'. Panwar keyFree association is the expression of the content of consciousness without censorship as an aid in gaining access to unconscious processes.   • (b) NarrationNot a defined psychoanalytic technique, just an everyday word for telling an account. Free association is precisely unstructured and uncensored, the opposite of a coherent narrative.
  • (d) Story tellingNot the technical term either: a story is organized, sequenced and edited, whereas free association demands a deliberately disorganized, unedited flow. That absence of structure is what lets repressed links surface.

  • cBasic anxiety
  • dNeurotic anxiety

The concept

Why (c)Karen Horney used exactly the phrase basic anxiety for the pervasive loneliness and helplessness she placed at the foundation of neurosis.

  • bSuperordinate
  • cSubordinate
  • dCongruence

Q38The defence mechanism involving an intellectual reason to justify a certain action connected only in the mind of the person after the behaviour is completed is:

  • aDenial
  • bRepression
  • cRationalisation
  • dProjection
✓ Answer: (c) Rationalisation
Named by Ernest Jones (1908); catalogued by Anna Freud (1936)
The concept

Why (c)Rationalisation is the only listed defence that supplies an after-the-fact intellectual justification: a reason the person builds in their own mind only once the behaviour is already complete.

Begin with defencesA defence mechanism is an unconscious strategy the ego uses to reduce the anxiety that erupts when a forbidden id-impulse, or an ugly truth about oneself, threatens to reach awareness. It belongs to classical psychoanalysis, the first force of depth psychology, founded by Sigmund Freud in fin-de-siecle Vienna in the 1890s and 1900s.

HistoryFreud sketched the defences, but his daughter Anna Freud systematised them in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936), pivoting the movement from Freud's drive-focus toward the ego psychology school (later Heinz Hartmann). Rationalisation was named by Freud's English follower Ernest Jones in 1908; historically, later ego psychologists refined this defence family, sharpening the line between self-deceiving justification and honest reasoning.

How it worksIt manufactures a plausible, logical, socially acceptable explanation that masks the real, often unconscious, motive: a student who failed says 'the questions were unfair' rather than admit not studying; a person who overpays calls it 'an investment'. The emotional verdict comes first and reasoning is recruited to defend it, like a lawyer arguing a case already decided. The key is sequence: the action happens for hidden reasons, then the mind invents an acceptable cause the person genuinely believes, protecting self-esteem.

Exam tipBuilding a coherent argument makes it a relatively mature, secondary defence that demands a fair degree of intelligence, unlike cruder, primitive defences such as denial or projection that simply block or expel reality. It gives self-serving reasons after the behaviour is completed, swapping the true motive for a respectable one.

Panwar keyDenial is denying the reality altogether, repression is involved in almost all defense mechanisms, projection is projecting the negative aspect of self onto others. Rationalisation is a secondary and much more mature defence mechanism and requires a decent amount of intelligence by explaining the reasons to justify one's actions.

Why the other options are wrong

  • (b) RepressionThe master defence Sigmund Freud called the cornerstone of psychoanalysis: pushing threatening thoughts, memories or impulses out of consciousness and keeping them there. It underlies almost every other defence, but buries material rather than building an excuse.
  • (d) ProjectionAttributing one's own unacceptable impulses or feelings to others ('I'm not angry, you are'), on the same Freudian defence roster. It hides a true motive too, but externalises a feeling rather than justifying one's own finished action.

Q39An investment of psychic energy in an object or person is

  • aDisplacement
  • bCathexis
  • cInstinct
  • dRepression
✓ Answer: (b) Cathexis
Sigmund Freud, economic metapsychology
The concept

Why (b)Cathexis is Sigmund Freud's precise term for the investment of psychic energy (libido) into an object, person or mental image.

Where it belongsFreud's metapsychology, developed in Vienna across the 1890s to 1920s, had three angles: topographic (conscious, preconscious, unconscious), structural (id, ego, superego) and economic, which treats the mind as a system distributing a quantity of energy. Cathexis belongs to this third, economic view, the energy-accounting side of classical psychoanalysis.

How it worksBegin with the economic premise: the psyche runs on a finite pool of mental energy that can attach to mental representations. When the id desires something, it binds energy to that thing's image; the stronger the cathexis, the more the person is drawn to it, so it measures how much an object 'matters'. When the actual object is unavailable, energy goes into a mental representation of it; picture a grieving person whose libido stays bound to the lost loved one (Mourning and Melancholia, 1917), or the infant who first cathects the mother's breast.

Word and oppositeFreud's German word was Besetzung ('occupation' or 'filling'), rendered by his English translator James Strachey as the Greek-derived 'cathexis' (from kathexis, a holding or retaining). The opposite, anticathexis (counter-cathexis), is the ego's expenditure of energy to dam up or restrain an id impulse from reaching consciousness, the energetic basis of repression; the more energy spent holding an impulse down, the less is free for living.

Exam tipThis drive-economic, almost hydraulic vocabulary was later de-emphasised by the neo-Freudians and object-relations theorists, who put real relationships in place of abstract energy, yet it remains foundational. Cathexis is the investment of psychic/libidinal energy in an object or person, the basic currency of Freud's energy theory of mind.

Panwar keyAccording to Freud, when the object of desire is not available, individuals tend to invest psychic energy in creating a mental image of that object. This is termed as cathexis.

  • bAlbert Ellis
  • cAlbert Bandura
  • dB.F. Skinner

The concept

Why (c)Albert Bandura coined and developed self-efficacy: a person's belief in their own capacity to organize and execute the actions needed to manage a situation or accomplish a specific task. What it isEpistemology is the branch of philosophy that asks where knowledge comes from and how we justify it. Empiricism answers that the mind begins essentially empty and is filled only by what we see, hear, touch, taste and smell. It privileges observation, empirical evidence and substantiation over truths supposedly built into the mind.

Key figuresJohn Locke pictured the mind as a tabula rasa, a blank slate written on by lived experience; David Hume and George Berkeley argued even abstract ideas are faded copies or recombinations of earlier sensory impressions. The great rival, rationalism (René Descartes, Leibniz, Spinoza), credits reason and innate ideas; a third stance, skepticism, doubts certainty altogether.

In psychologyIn Systems & Theories of psychology, empiricism is a deep taproot: when psychology broke from philosophy in 1879 under Wilhelm Wundt, it inherited the demand that mind be studied through observable data, not armchair speculation. The concept

Why (b)The Theory of Opposite Wishes is the concept by which Bose wove Hindu thought into psychoanalysis.

Who Bose wasGirindra Shekhar Bose founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society (1922) and corresponded with Sigmund Freud for roughly two decades.
