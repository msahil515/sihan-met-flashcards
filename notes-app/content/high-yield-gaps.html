1. Indian intelligence & developmental tests

Lead fact: Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence (C. M. Bhatia, 1955) is the classic Indian non-verbal/performance IQ test, standardised on Indian children, useful for the illiterate and the hearing-impaired. Its 5 subtests are the single most-asked Indian-test item: Koh's Block Design, Alexander's Pass-Along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory (for digits), Picture Construction.

Hooks: "Malin did the two Indian standards (MISIC = WISC, and the Indian VSMS)." "Kamat = Binet" (both K/B verbal age-scales). Seguin = form board, best-of-3-trials. Goodenough = Draw-A-Man.

2. Which statistical test do I choose?

Lead logic: three questions decide it, in order: (1) What is the level of measurement of the outcome? nominal/categorical → chi-square family; ordinal → nonparametric; interval/ratio & normal → parametric. (2) How many groups/conditions? 1, 2, or 3+. (3) Are the groups independent or paired (repeated/matched)? Those three answers point to exactly one test.

Master decision table

1 group vs a known valueOne-sample t-testWilcoxon signed-rank (vs median)Chi-square goodness-of-fit; binomial
2 independent groupsIndependent (unpaired) t-testMann-Whitney UChi-square test of independence; Fisher's exact (small n)
2 paired / repeated measuresPaired t-testWilcoxon signed-rankMcNemar's test
3+ independent groupsOne-way ANOVA (then post-hoc, e.g. Tukey)Kruskal-Wallis HChi-square (r × c table)
3+ paired / repeated measuresRepeated-measures ANOVAFriedman testCochran's Q
Relationship between 2 continuous variablesPearson r; linear regressionSpearman's rho; Kendall's tau(see correlation types, cluster 4)
Predict outcome from predictorsMultiple regression (continuous DV)—Logistic regression (binary DV)

Worked examples (the way PYQs phrase them)

  • "Compare mean anxiety scores of a therapy group vs a control group (scores normal)." → 2 independent groups, interval → independent t-test.
  • "Same patients' depression scores before and after treatment." → 2 paired, interval → paired t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank if ordinal/skewed).
  • "Mean reaction time across four drug-dose groups." → 3+ independent, interval → one-way ANOVA.
  • "Median pain rating (ordinal 0–10) across three hospitals." → 3+ independent, ordinal → Kruskal-Wallis.
  • "Association between gender (M/F) and passed/failed." → both nominal → chi-square test of independence.
  • "Rank-correlate class rank with exam rank." → two ordinal → Spearman's rho.
Trap: "Repeated / matched / before-after / same subjects" → paired test (paired t, Wilcoxon, RM-ANOVA, Friedman, McNemar). "Two different groups of people" → independent (independent t, Mann-Whitney, one-way ANOVA, chi-square). The examiner hides this in one word: same vs different participants. Also: ANOVA with 2 groups = t-test (F = t²).

3. Nonparametric tests (selection & when to use)

Lead fact: use a nonparametric test when (a) data are ordinal/ranked, or (b) the DV is interval but assumptions of the parametric test fail (non-normal distribution, small n, heterogeneity of variance, or many outliers). They work on ranks/signs, not means, so they need no normality assumption and are less affected by outliers, at the cost of some power.

Parametric ↔ nonparametric pairing table

DesignParametric testNonparametric counterpartBased on
2 independent groupsIndependent t-testMann-Whitney U (= Wilcoxon rank-sum)Ranks across both groups
2 paired / repeatedPaired t-testWilcoxon signed-rankSigned ranks of difference scores
2 paired, direction only—Sign testOnly the sign (+/−) of each difference (ignores magnitude)
3+ independent groupsOne-way ANOVAKruskal-Wallis HRanks across all groups (extension of Mann-Whitney)
3+ paired / repeatedRepeated-measures ANOVAFriedman testRanks within each subject across conditions
Association, 2 continuousPearson rSpearman's rho / Kendall's tauRanks
Categorical frequencies(no mean)Chi-square (goodness-of-fit / independence)Observed vs expected counts
Trap (the classic swap): Mann-Whitney U = 2 INDEPENDENT groups. Wilcoxon SIGNED-RANK = 2 PAIRED groups. Both are "Wilcoxon-ish," which is the trap: Mann-Whitney is also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (independent), distinct from the Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired). Kruskal-Wallis is the 3+ version of Mann-Whitney; Friedman is the 3+ version of Wilcoxon signed-rank (i.e. the nonparametric RM-ANOVA). Sign test vs Wilcoxon signed-rank: the sign test uses only direction; signed-rank also uses the magnitude of differences (more powerful).
Hook: "Independent = Mann-Whitney U" and "Wilcoxon signed = paired (signs of differences need a pair)." Kruskal-Wallis & Friedman both extend to 3+; Kruskal = independent (like ANOVA), Friedman = repeated.

4. Special correlation types

Lead fact: the coefficient is chosen by the types of the two variables. Two continuous & linear → Pearson r. Two ranks → Spearman/Kendall. One continuous + one true dichotomy → point-biserial. Two true dichotomies → phi (φ). That phi/point-biserial line is the single best-answer trap.
CoefficientVariable typesUse when
Pearson rTwo continuous (interval/ratio), linearThe default product-moment correlation; both variables normally distributed and linearly related.
Spearman's rho (ρ)Two ordinal (ranked)Monotonic relationship; ranked data or non-normal continuous data. Pearson r computed on the ranks.
Kendall's tau (τ)Two ordinal (ranked)Like Spearman but based on concordant/discordant pairs; preferred for small samples / many tied ranks.
Point-biserial (rpb)One continuous + one true (genuine) dichotomye.g. exam score (continuous) × pass/fail or male/female (a real either/or). It is mathematically Pearson r applied to a 0/1 coding. Used in item analysis (item right/wrong × total score).
Biserial (rb)One continuous + one artificial dichotomy (an underlying continuum forced into two)e.g. score × "high vs low" where the split is arbitrary on an underlying continuous trait. Estimates what r would be if the dichotomy were continuous.
Phi (φ)Two true dichotomies (2 × 2)e.g. smoker/non-smoker × disease/no-disease. Equivalent to Pearson r on two 0/1 variables; related to chi-square (φ = √(χ²/N)).
Tetrachoric (rtet)Two artificial dichotomies (both underlying continua)The 2×2 analogue of biserial: both variables are really continuous but recorded as yes/no.
Partial correlationTwo continuous, controlling for a thirdRelationship between X and Y with the effect of Z statistically removed/held constant.
Trap matrix (memorise this): continuous × TRUE dichotomy → point-biserial; continuous × artificial dichotomy → biserial; TRUE × TRUE dichotomy → phi; artificial × artificial → tetrachoric. The split is "biserial-family = at least one ARTIFICIAL dichotomy (underlying continuum); point-biserial/phi = TRUE/genuine dichotomy." Partial correlation = the "controlling for / holding constant a third variable" stem.
Hook: "Point-biserial has a real point of division (true dichotomy)." "Biserial = the dichotomy is built (artificial) from a continuum." "Phi = Four cells, both true (2×2)." "Tetra = four cells, both fake (artificial)."

5. Indian mental-health law & regulatory bodies

Lead fact: the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (in force 2018) repealed the Mental Health Act 1987, is rights-based, effectively decriminalised suicide (presumed severe stress, not punished under IPC 309), and introduced the Advance Directive and Nominated Representative. The RCI Act 1992 is what licenses Clinical Psychologists (you must be RCI-registered with a CRR number to practise).

RCI Act, 1992 (Rehabilitation Council of India)

  • Statutory body that regulates training and registration of rehabilitation professionals, including Clinical Psychologists.
  • Maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR); practising without registration is an offence. A clinical psychologist needs RCI registration (a CRR number) to practise.
  • Prescribes minimum standards of education (e.g. recognises the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology).

Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (MHCA) — key provisions

  • Repealed the Mental Health Act 1987; rights-based framework aligned with the UN CRPD.
  • Decriminalised suicide: a person who attempts suicide is presumed to be under severe stress and shall not be prosecuted (overrides IPC 309 in effect).
  • Advance Directive (AD): a person may state in advance how they wish to be (or not be) treated for mental illness, and who their representative is.
  • Nominated Representative (NR): a person nominated to take decisions on the patient's behalf.
  • Mental Health Review Boards (MHRB): review admissions, advance directives, and protect rights (replaced the old "magistrate" route).
  • Established the Central & State Mental Health Authorities; right to access mental healthcare, confidentiality, community living, and to be free from cruel/degrading treatment.
  • Restricts ECT (no unmodified ECT; not for minors except with board approval) and regulates seclusion/restraint.
  • Defines capacity to make mental-healthcare decisions; supported decision-making model.

RPwD Act, 2016 (Rights of Persons with Disabilities)

  • Repealed the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995; implements the UN CRPD.
  • Expanded recognised disabilities from 7 to 21 conditions, including mental illness, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, specific learning disabilities, and multiple disabilities.
  • Benchmark disability = 40% or more of a specified disability (the threshold for reservations/benefits).
  • Reservation in higher education and government jobs; accessibility mandates; guardianship provisions.
Trap (MHCA vs RPwD): MHCA 2017 = treatment & rights of people with mental ILLNESS (advance directive, nominated representative, decriminalised suicide, review boards). RPwD 2016 = rights/benefits of people with DISABILITY (21 conditions, 40% benchmark, reservations). Both repealed older 1987/1995 laws and both follow the CRPD, that's the overlap the examiner exploits. RCI Act 1992 = who is allowed to PRACTISE (registration/CRR). If a stem mentions "40% / benchmark / reservation / 21 conditions" → RPwD. "Advance directive / nominated representative / decriminalised suicide" → MHCA. "CRR number / register to practise" → RCI.

6. Neuropsychological test batteries

Lead fact: the PGI Memory Scale (Pershad & Wig, 1977; PGIMER Chandigarh) is the standard Indian memory battery with 10 subtests (e.g. remote & recent memory, mental balance, attention/concentration, delayed & immediate recall, retention of similar & dissimilar pairs, visual retention, recognition). It is the most-asked Indian neuropsych instrument.

7. Amnesia types & memory disorders

Lead fact: the contrast is defined relative to the injury. Anterograde = cannot form NEW memories AFTER the event (forward). Retrograde = loss of OLD memories from BEFORE the event (backward). H.M. is the classic anterograde case (bilateral medial temporal lobe / hippocampus removed for epilepsy).
Anterograde amnesiaRetrograde amnesia
Lost memoriesNew ones can't be formed after the eventOld ones from before the event are gone
Time directionForward from the injuryBackward from the injury
Classic case / causeH.M.; Korsakoff's; hippocampal damageHead trauma; often follows Ribot's gradient
  • Ribot's Law: in retrograde amnesia, recent memories are lost first and more severely; older/remote memories are most resistant (temporal gradient). "Last in, first out."
  • Korsakoff's syndrome: chronic thiamine (B1) deficiency, usually from alcoholism; profound anterograde amnesia (plus some retrograde) with confabulation. Follows Wernicke's encephalopathy (Wernicke-Korsakoff). Mammillary bodies / thalamus.
  • Transient Global Amnesia (TGA): sudden, temporary, self-limiting episode of dense anterograde (and some retrograde) amnesia lasting hours, with repetitive questioning; resolves fully.
  • Dissociative (psychogenic) amnesia: memory loss from psychological trauma, not organic injury; typically loss of autobiographical/personal info (often selective for the trauma); a dissociative disorder. Contrast with the organic amnesias above.
  • Source amnesia: you remember the information but not where/when you learned it (lost the source/context). Linked to frontal-lobe function.
  • H.M. (Henry Molaison): bilateral medial temporal lobectomy → severe anterograde amnesia but intact procedural learning (could learn mirror-drawing without remembering the practice) → proved declarative vs procedural memory are separate systems.
Trap: "Can't make NEW memories" = anterograde (H.M., Korsakoff). "Forgot the PAST" = retrograde (Ribot's gradient: recent gone first). Korsakoff = thiamine + alcohol + confabulation. Dissociative amnesia is PSYCHOGENIC (trauma, loss of personal identity/autobiography), not from brain damage, that's the organic-vs-functional split the examiner tests.

8. Item Response Theory (IRT) / Rasch

Lead fact: IRT models the probability of a correct response as a function of the person's ability (θ) and the item's parameters. The number of item parameters names the model: 1PL (Rasch) = difficulty only; 2PL = difficulty + discrimination; 3PL = difficulty + discrimination + guessing.
ModelParameters estimatedKey idea
1PL / Raschb = difficulty onlyAll items assumed equally discriminating; only difficulty varies. (Strictly, Rasch fixes discrimination = 1.)
2PLb = difficulty, a = discriminationItems differ in how sharply they separate high- from low-ability people.
3PLb, a, and c = guessing (lower asymptote)Adds a guessing/pseudo-chance parameter, e.g. ~0.25 on a 4-option MCQ.
  • Item Characteristic Curve (ICC): S-shaped (logistic) curve plotting P(correct) against ability θ. Difficulty (b) = the θ at which P = 0.5 (curve shifts right for harder items). Discrimination (a) = the steepness of the curve at that point. Guessing (c) = the height of the lower tail (where the curve flattens above 0).
  • IRT vs Classical Test Theory (CTT): CTT statistics (item difficulty p, discrimination, reliability) are sample-dependent; IRT item parameters are sample-invariant (theoretically stable across groups) and ability is estimated on the same scale as item difficulty. IRT underpins computer-adaptive testing.
Trap: "Rasch = 1-parameter (difficulty only)." Count the parameters: 1PL→b, 2PL→b+a, 3PL→b+a+c (c = guessing). On the ICC, difficulty = horizontal position (θ at P=0.5), discrimination = slope. IRT's selling point over CTT = invariance / sample-independence of item parameters.

9. Item analysis formulae (difficulty & discrimination)

Lead fact: Item difficulty index (p) = proportion who answered the item correctly. Counter-intuitively, a higher p means an EASIER item (more people got it right). The ideal range is roughly p = 0.3–0.7, centred near 0.5 for maximum discrimination.

Item difficulty index (p)

p = (number of examinees who answered correctly) / (total number of examinees)

  • Range 0 to 1. High p = easy item; low p = hard item.
  • p near 0.5 maximises differentiation; acceptable band ≈ 0.30–0.70. p > 0.9 (too easy) or < 0.2 (too hard) items are usually dropped.

Item discrimination index (D)

D = (proportion correct in HIGH group) − (proportion correct in LOW group) = pupper − plower

  • Split by total score into upper and lower groups (commonly the top 27% vs bottom 27%).
  • Range −1 to +1. A good item is correctly answered by more high-scorers than low-scorers (positive D).
D valueInterpretation
D ≥ 0.40Very good / excellent discriminator
0.30–0.39Good (reasonably discriminating)
0.20–0.29Marginal (needs revision)
< 0.20Poor (revise or discard)
Negative DFaulty item, low-scorers outperform high-scorers (often a mis-keyed or ambiguous item); always discard/recheck key.

Distractor analysis: examine each wrong option. A good distractor is chosen by more low-scorers than high-scorers. A distractor chosen by nobody is non-functional (not plausible) and should be replaced; a distractor that attracts more high-scorers than low-scorers signals a flawed item.

Trap (the most-missed point): a HIGH difficulty index (p) means an EASY item, the word "difficulty" is backwards from intuition. D ≥ 0.4 = good; negative D = throw it out. The point-biserial correlation (cluster 4) is the other standard discrimination statistic (item right/wrong × total score).

10. Sampling methods (identify from the scenario)

Lead fact: the master split is probability sampling (every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection → allows generalisation & estimation of sampling error) vs non-probability sampling (selection by convenience/judgement → cannot estimate sampling error, higher bias). Spotting "random / known chance" vs "researcher chose / whoever was available" decides the half.

Probability (random) methods

MethodHow it worksScenario cue (the tell)
Simple randomEvery unit has equal chance; lottery / random-number table"Names drawn from a hat," "random number generator"
SystematicPick every k-th unit after a random start (k = N/n)"Every 10th patient," "every k-th name on the list"
StratifiedDivide population into strata (subgroups), sample randomly within each"Divided by gender/grade/income, then sampled from each" — homogeneous strata, heterogeneous between
ClusterDivide into clusters (e.g. schools, wards), randomly select whole clusters, study everyone in them"Selected 5 schools at random and tested all students" — naturally occurring groups
MultistageSampling in successive stages (e.g. state → district → village → household)"Sampled in several stages / nested levels"

Non-probability methods

MethodHow it worksScenario cue (the tell)
Convenience (accidental)Whoever is easiest/available"Students in the researcher's own class," "people at the mall"
Purposive (judgemental)Researcher deliberately picks units fitting a criterion"Selected experts / typical cases on purpose / chosen for their expertise"
QuotaFill preset quotas per subgroup, but units chosen non-randomly"Fill 50 men and 50 women" but NOT random within — the non-probability twin of stratified

Trap pairs: Stratified (probability) vs Quota (non-probability): both use subgroups, but stratified samples randomly within strata, quota does not. Stratified vs Cluster: stratified samples within every subgroup (subgroups are homogeneous, you want representation of each); cluster picks whole groups at random and studies all of them (clusters mini-mirror the population). Systematic = "every k-th." Snowball = referral chains (hard-to-reach). Purposive = expert/criterion judgement.
Hook: "Every k-th = systematic (tic-tic-tic, regular)." "Stratified = randomly within strata; Quota = quotas but no randomness." "Cluster = grab whole groups." "Snowball rolls from referral to referral."
