1. Indian intelligence & developmental tests

Lead fact: Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence (C. M. Bhatia, 1955) is the classic Indian non-verbal/performance IQ test, standardised on Indian children, useful for the illiterate and the hearing-impaired. Its 5 subtests are the single most-asked Indian-test item: Koh's Block Design, Alexander's Pass-Along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory (for digits), Picture Construction.

The decisive distinction an examiner tests here is what the score is called and what it measures. There are three different quotients in this cluster and they are not interchangeable: a true intelligence test yields an IQ (via Mental Age, MA), an adaptive/social measure yields a Social Quotient (SQ), and a developmental screen yields a Developmental Quotient (DQ). Always read the stem for the output word ("IQ" vs "social age" vs "developmental quotient") and the data source (does the child perform a task, or does a parent/informant report?). That single read usually disambiguates the whole question.

2. Which statistical test do I choose?

Lead logic: three questions decide it, in order: (1) What is the level of measurement of the outcome (DV)? nominal/categorical → chi-square family; ordinal → nonparametric; interval/ratio & normal → parametric. (2) How many groups/conditions? 1, 2, or 3+. (3) Are the groups independent or paired (repeated/matched)? Those three answers point to exactly one test. A fourth question, "relationship vs difference?", splits off the correlation/regression family from the group-comparison family.

Every "which test" PYQ is really asking you to run a small decision tree. The examiner buries the answer in the wording: the scale of the outcome (a score, a rank, or a category), the number of groups or conditions, and whether the same people are measured more than once (paired/repeated/matched) or different people make up each group (independent). Get those three facts out of the stem and the test is forced.

Step 1: level of measurement of the outcome

  • Nominal/categorical (counts in categories: passed/failed, M/F, blood group): chi-square family, McNemar, Cochran's Q, Fisher's exact, logistic regression.
  • Ordinal (ranks, Likert single items, 0 to 10 pain ratings treated as order only): nonparametric tests on ranks (Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman, Spearman, Kendall).
  • Interval/ratio AND assumptions met (a genuine numeric score that is roughly normal with equal variances): the parametric tests (t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson, regression). If the score is interval but assumptions fail, you drop down to the matching nonparametric test (see cluster 3).

The F = t² relationship

Key identity: a one-way ANOVA on exactly 2 groups gives the same p-value as an independent t-test, because F = t² (with 1 df in the numerator). ANOVA is the generalisation of the t-test to 3+ groups; the t-test is the special 2-group case. So "compare two groups, normal data" can be answered by either, and an F of, say, 9.0 from a 2-group design corresponds to t = 3.0.

Why ANOVA, not many t-tests (and post-hoc tests)

With 3+ groups you do not run multiple t-tests, because each test carries a 5% Type I error and running many inflates the familywise error rate (the chance of at least one false positive climbs well above 5%). ANOVA's omnibus F asks one question: "is there any difference among the means?" If F is significant, F only tells you that a difference exists, not which pairs differ. To locate the pairs you run a post-hoc test, which controls the familywise error:

One-tailed vs two-tailed

A two-tailed test asks "is there a difference in either direction?" (H1: μ1 ≠ μ2) and splits alpha across both tails (2.5% each at α = .05). A one-tailed (directional) test asks "is it specifically greater (or less)?" (H1: μ1 > μ2) and puts the whole 5% in one tail, making it easier to reach significance in that predicted direction but unable to detect an effect the other way. Use one-tailed only when a directional hypothesis is justified before seeing the data; default and safest answer in exams is two-tailed.

3. Nonparametric tests (selection & when to use)

Lead fact: use a nonparametric test when (a) data are ordinal/ranked or nominal, or (b) the DV is interval but assumptions of the parametric test fail (non-normal distribution, small n, heterogeneity of variance, or many outliers). They work on ranks, signs, or frequencies, not means, so they make no assumption about the shape of the population distribution ("distribution-free"), and they are robust to outliers, at the cost of some statistical power.

Why and when to go nonparametric

Parametric tests (t, ANOVA, Pearson) assume an interval/ratio DV that is normally distributed with roughly equal variances and independent observations. A nonparametric test is the correct choice whenever those props are knocked away:

  • Ordinal data: ranks or single Likert items carry order but not equal intervals, so a "mean" is not strictly meaningful; rank-based tests are appropriate.
  • Nominal data: counts in categories have no order at all → chi-square family.
  • Non-normal distribution: strong skew or a known non-normal shape that the Central Limit Theorem cannot rescue.
  • Small samples: with small n you cannot verify normality and the CLT does not help, so a distribution-free test is safer.
  • Outliers / heavy tails: ranks compress extreme values, so a few outliers cannot drag the result the way they drag a mean.
  • Heterogeneity of variance: when groups have very unequal spread.

How they work and the power cost

Most nonparametric tests replace the raw scores with ranks (or with the bare sign of a difference) and then ask whether the ranks are distributed differently than chance would predict. Because they throw away information about the exact distances between scores, they are typically less powerful than their parametric twin when the parametric assumptions actually hold (the Mann-Whitney has roughly 95% of the t-test's power, i.e. a small efficiency loss). But when assumptions are violated, the nonparametric test can be more powerful and is the valid one. Trade-off summary: less power, fewer assumptions, more robustness.

The chi-square family

Chi-square (χ²) compares observed frequencies to expected frequencies. Two main forms: goodness-of-fit (one categorical variable vs a theoretical distribution, e.g. "are births equal across days of the week?") and test of independence/association (two categorical variables in an r × c table, e.g. "is smoking associated with disease?"). Assumptions: independent observations, and expected count ≥ 5 in (almost) every cell; if not, use Fisher's exact test (small 2×2) or Yates' continuity correction for a 2×2. The 2×2 phi coefficient links directly to χ²: φ = √(χ² / N). Strictly, chi-square tests on frequencies are nonparametric, though they are not rank-based.

The runs test

The runs test (Wald-Wolfowitz) checks whether a binary sequence is random by counting "runs" (unbroken streaks of the same symbol, e.g. HHH TT HH = 3 runs). Too few runs suggests clustering/trend; too many suggests over-alternation. It is the test a stem points to when it asks whether a series of outcomes occurred "in random order."

Worked examples

  • "Compare median stress (ordinal) of two unrelated wards." → 2 independent, ordinal → Mann-Whitney U.
  • "Same patients ranked pain before and after, want sign and size of change." → 2 paired, ordinal → Wilcoxon signed-rank.
  • "Only whether each patient got better or worse (not how much)." → direction only → sign test.
  • "Compare ranked satisfaction across four clinics." → 3+ independent, ordinal → Kruskal-Wallis.
  • "Same judges rank three products." → 3+ paired, ordinal → Friedman.
  • "Is a coin's sequence of heads/tails random?" → runs test.

4. Special correlation types

Lead fact: the coefficient is chosen by the types of the two variables. Two continuous & linear → Pearson r. Two ranks → Spearman/Kendall. One continuous + one true dichotomy → point-biserial. Two true dichotomies → phi (φ). One continuous + one artificial dichotomy → biserial; two artificial dichotomies → tetrachoric. That true-vs-artificial line is the single best-answer trap.

The underlying logic: true vs artificial dichotomy

A correlation coefficient is just an index of how two variables co-vary; the "special" coefficients are all ways of handling variables that are not both clean continuous measures. The decisive question for the dichotomous cases is whether the two-category split is true (genuine) or artificial:

  • True / genuine dichotomy: the variable is naturally two-valued, with no continuum underneath. Examples: alive/dead, male/female, pregnant/not, smoker/non-smoker by definition.
  • Artificial dichotomy: there is a real underlying continuum that has been chopped into two categories. Examples: pass/fail (from a continuous score split at a cut-off), "high vs low anxiety," "tall vs short." The biserial and tetrachoric coefficients exist to estimate what the correlation would have been had we not thrown away the continuum, so they tend to be a bit larger than the matched point-biserial/phi.

Interpreting r, and the link to R²

For any of these, the sign gives direction (positive = move together, negative = inversely) and the absolute value (0 to 1) gives strength. Squaring it gives the coefficient of determination, r², the proportion of variance shared between the two variables. So r = 0.60 means r² = 0.36, i.e. 36% of the variance in one variable is accounted for by the other (this is the bivariate version of the R² in cluster B12). Note that a moderate-looking r explains a much smaller share of variance once squared (r = .30 → only 9%).

Point-biserial in item analysis, and attenuation

  • Item discrimination: the point-biserial between an item (scored 0/1, right/wrong) and total test score tells you how well that item separates high from low scorers. A low or negative rpb flags a bad item.
  • Range restriction: if the sample's spread on a variable is narrowed (e.g. only high scorers were selected), the observed correlation is attenuated (shrunk) toward zero. Classic case: validity coefficients look low when computed only on already-selected employees/students.
  • Attenuation due to unreliability: measurement error in either variable also pulls the observed correlation below the true correlation; the "correction for attenuation" formula estimates what r would be with perfectly reliable measures.

Worked examples

  • "Correlate IQ score with gender (M/F)." → continuous × true dichotomy → point-biserial.
  • "Correlate test score with a median-split 'high vs low motivation'." → continuous × artificial dichotomy → biserial.
  • "Relate smoking (yes/no) to lung disease (yes/no), both genuine." → true × true → phi (and φ = √(χ²/N)).
  • "Two yes/no items each splitting an underlying trait." → artificial × artificial → tetrachoric.
  • "Relation of study hours to marks, removing the effect of IQ from both." → partial correlation.

5. Indian mental-health law & regulatory bodies

Lead fact: the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (passed 2017, came into force July 2018) repealed the Mental Health Act 1987. It is rights-based and aligned to the UN CRPD, effectively decriminalised attempted suicide (Section 115: a person attempting suicide is presumed to have severe stress and shall not be tried/punished, overriding IPC 309 in practice), and introduced the Advance Directive and Nominated Representative. Keep three statutes apart: MHCA 2017 = treatment & rights of the mentally ill; RPwD 2016 = rights/benefits of persons with disability; RCI Act 1992 = who is licensed to practise (a clinical psychologist must be RCI-registered with a CRR number).

Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (MHCA): key provisions

  • Repealed the Mental Health Act 1987; shifts from a custodial model to a rights-based, patient-centred framework consistent with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), which India ratified in 2007.
  • Definition of "mental illness": a substantial disorder of thinking, mood, perception, orientation or memory that grossly impairs judgement, behaviour, capacity to recognise reality or ability to meet the ordinary demands of life; includes mental conditions associated with the abuse of alcohol and drugs, but excludes mental retardation/intellectual disability (i.e. arrested or incomplete development of mind, which is covered instead under RPwD).
  • Decriminalised suicide (Sec 115): any person who attempts suicide is presumed, unless proven otherwise, to be under severe stress and shall not be prosecuted or punished; the government has a duty to provide care, treatment and rehabilitation. This effectively neutralises IPC 309.
  • Right to access mental healthcare run or funded by government, plus rights to community living, dignity, confidentiality, legal aid, information, and protection from cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment.
  • Advance Directive (AD): a person may state in advance how they wish (or do not wish) to be treated for mental illness, and may appoint their representative. It operates when the person ceases to have capacity.
  • Nominated Representative (NR): a person nominated to support and take decisions on behalf of the patient; if none is named, the Act specifies an order of default (e.g. relative, caregiver) and the NR is appointed accordingly.
  • Mental Health Review Boards (MHRBs): quasi-judicial bodies that review advance directives, registrations of admissions, complaints, and protect rights. They replaced the old magistrate/judicial-route reception orders of the 1987 Act.
  • Central Mental Health Authority (CMHA) and State Mental Health Authorities (SMHAs): register and supervise mental-health establishments and maintain registers of professionals.
  • ECT & restraint rules: unmodified ECT is banned (ECT only with muscle relaxants and anaesthesia); ECT is not permitted for minors except with the MHRB's prior permission (and informed consent of the guardian). Sterilisation as a treatment for mental illness is prohibited; chained restraint is prohibited; seclusion and physical restraint are tightly regulated and only when necessary to prevent imminent harm.
  • Capacity & supported decision-making: the Act presumes capacity to make mental-healthcare decisions and adopts a supported (not substituted) decision-making model; capacity is defined functionally (ability to understand information, appreciate consequences and communicate a decision).

RPwD Act, 2016 (Rights of Persons with Disabilities)

  • Repealed the Persons with Disabilities (PwD) Act 1995; enacted to give effect to the UN CRPD.
  • Expanded the recognised disabilities from 7 to 21 conditions, including mental illness, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, specific learning disabilities, cerebral palsy, blindness/low vision, deaf/hard of hearing, locomotor disability, muscular dystrophy, chronic neurological conditions, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, haemophilia, thalassaemia, sickle-cell disease, acid-attack victims, and multiple disabilities.
  • Benchmark disability = 40% or more of a specified disability (as certified); this is the threshold that unlocks reservations and benefits.
  • Reservation: 4% in government jobs and 5% in higher-education institutions for persons with benchmark disabilities; plus accessibility mandates, guardianship provisions and penalties for discrimination.

RCI Act, 1992 (Rehabilitation Council of India)

  • Statutory body that regulates the training and registration of rehabilitation professionals, including Clinical Psychologists.
  • Maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR); a clinical psychologist needs RCI registration (a CRR number) to practise legally, and practising without registration is a punishable offence.
  • Prescribes and recognises minimum standards of education, including the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology as the qualifying degree for registration as a clinical psychologist.

  • NTA (National Testing Agency): the autonomous body that conducts national entrance examinations; relevant as the conducting/regulatory body for entrance tests, distinct from RCI which governs the right to practise.

Lead fact: the PGI Memory Scale (Pershad & Wig, 1977; PGIMER Chandigarh) is the standard Indian memory battery with 10 subtests. It is the most-asked Indian neuropsych instrument. 7. Amnesia types & memory disorders

Lead fact: the whole topic is anchored to one reference point, the moment of injury. Anterograde = cannot form NEW memories AFTER the event (the clock runs forward, the past is intact). Retrograde = loss of OLD memories from BEFORE the event (the clock runs backward into the past). H.M. is the classic anterograde case (bilateral medial temporal lobe / hippocampus removed for epilepsy). Most real organic amnesias are mixed but anterograde-dominant; pure retrograde amnesia is rare and raises suspicion of a functional (psychogenic) cause.

The underlying logic the examiner tests: the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe are needed to consolidate new declarative memories, but once a memory is fully consolidated it is stored in distributed neocortex. So damaging that consolidation machinery wipes out the ability to lay down new traces (anterograde) and the not-yet-consolidated recent past (a graded retrograde loss), while old, well-consolidated memories survive. This single mechanism explains both Ribot's gradient and why H.M. kept his childhood but not his afternoon.

Ribot's Law and the temporal gradient

  • Ribot's Law (Théodule Ribot, 1881): in retrograde amnesia, recent memories are lost first and most severely; older, remote memories are most resistant. This is the temporal (Ribot) gradient, summarised as "last in, first out." The reason: remote memories have had more time to consolidate into neocortex and no longer depend on the damaged hippocampus.
  • A flat (non-graded) retrograde loss, where even childhood is gone but new learning is fine, points away from organic amnesia and toward a dissociative/functional picture.

Korsakoff's syndrome and the Wernicke-Korsakoff complex

  • Cause: chronic thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, classically from alcoholism (also severe malnutrition, hyperemesis, bariatric surgery). Thiamine is a cofactor for glucose metabolism, so deprived diencephalic neurons die.
  • Lesion sites: the mammillary bodies and the dorsomedial (mediodorsal) nucleus of the thalamus; this is the prototype diencephalic amnesia.
  • Clinical picture: dense anterograde amnesia (plus a graded retrograde component) with prominent confabulation (fabricated answers filling memory gaps, without intent to lie) and poor insight.
  • Wernicke's encephalopathy is the acute, reversible phase that precedes/accompanies it. Its classic triad: confusion (global encephalopathy) + ophthalmoplegia / nystagmus (eye-movement palsy) + ataxia (gait). Treat emergently with thiamine before glucose (giving glucose first can precipitate or worsen Wernicke's). Untreated Wernicke's can progress to the chronic, largely irreversible Korsakoff psychosis: hence Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.

The other named amnesias

  • Transient Global Amnesia (TGA): sudden, temporary, self-limiting episode of dense anterograde (and some retrograde) amnesia lasting a few hours, with characteristic repetitive questioning ("where am I? what are we doing?"). Personal identity is preserved and there is no focal neurology; it resolves fully within 24 hours and rarely recurs.
  • Dissociative (psychogenic) amnesia: memory loss from psychological trauma, not organic injury; typically a loss of autobiographical / personal identity information, often selective for the traumatic event, and reversible. A DSM-5 dissociative disorder; dissociative fugue (sudden travel with identity loss) is a specifier. This is the key organic-vs-functional split: functional amnesia spares new learning but loses the personal past, often flat rather than graded.
  • Source amnesia: the information is retained but its source/context is lost (you know the fact but not where or when you learned it). Tied to frontal-lobe function and prominent in ageing and frontal damage; underlies "cryptomnesia" (unintentional plagiarism).
  • Infantile (childhood) amnesia: the normal inability of adults to recall events from roughly the first 3 to 4 years of life, attributed to immature hippocampal/prefrontal consolidation and lack of an autobiographical "self" framework.

The landmark cases

  • H.M. (Henry Molaison): underwent bilateral medial temporal lobectomy (hippocampus + nearby structures) by William Scoville for intractable epilepsy. Result: severe anterograde amnesia and graded retrograde loss, but intact procedural learning (improved at the mirror-drawing task across days while denying ever having done it). Studied for decades by Brenda Milner; the proof that declarative and procedural (non-declarative) memory are separate systems, and that the medial temporal lobe is for declarative memory.
  • Clive Wearing: herpes simplex encephalitis destroyed his hippocampi, leaving perhaps the most extreme combined anterograde + retrograde amnesia (a memory span of seconds, repeated "first awakening"), yet he retained procedural skill, still playing and conducting music.
  • K.C. (Kent Cochrane): motorcycle head injury; a striking dissociation in which episodic memory (personal re-experienced events) was lost but semantic memory (facts, general knowledge) was preserved, key evidence for Tulving's episodic/semantic distinction.

8. Item Response Theory (IRT) / Rasch

Lead fact: IRT models the probability of a correct response as a function of the person's latent ability (θ, "theta") and the item's parameters. The number of item parameters names the model: 1PL (Rasch) = difficulty only; 2PL = difficulty + discrimination; 3PL = difficulty + discrimination + guessing; 4PL adds an upper asymptote (carelessness/slip). Ability and item difficulty are placed on the same scale, so a person and an item can be directly compared.

The underlying logic: latent trait and the logistic link

IRT (also called latent trait theory) assumes a single unobservable trait θ underlies the responses; θ is conventionally scaled with mean 0 and SD 1, roughly from −3 (very low ability) to +3 (very high ability). The probability of a correct answer rises monotonically with θ following an S-shaped logistic function. For the 2PL model the probability is:

P(θ) = c + (1 − c) / (1 + e^(−a(θ − b)))

where b = difficulty, a = discrimination, c = guessing (lower asymptote). Set c = 0 and you have the 2PL; further fix a = 1 (constant across items) and you have the 1PL / Rasch model, where the only thing that varies between items is difficulty.

Reading the Item Characteristic Curve (ICC)

  • The ICC is the S-shaped (logistic) curve plotting P(correct) on the y-axis against ability θ on the x-axis. Each item has its own ICC.
  • Difficulty (b) = horizontal shift. It is the value of θ at which P = 0.5 (for the 2PL; for the 3PL it is the inflection point, where P = (1+c)/2). A harder item shifts the whole curve to the right (you need more θ to reach 50% chance); an easy item sits to the left.
  • Discrimination (a) = slope/steepness of the curve at its inflection point. A steep curve sharply separates people just below vs just above b; a flat curve barely distinguishes ability levels (poor discrimination).
  • Guessing (c) = lower asymptote, the height of the left tail where the curve flattens above zero. It is the floor probability for very low θ.
  • Carelessness (d) = upper asymptote (4PL only), the ceiling below 1.0 on the right tail.

Item and Test Information Functions

IRT reframes precision through information rather than a single reliability coefficient. The Item Information Function (IIF) shows how much an item reduces measurement error at each level of θ. An item gives maximum information near θ = its own difficulty b, and a higher discrimination (a) yields a taller, narrower information peak. The Test Information Function (TIF) is simply the sum of the item information functions, so a test built from items spread across difficulties measures well over a wide ability range. Information relates inversely to the Standard Error of measurement of θ:

SE(θ) = 1 / √(Information(θ))

This is IRT's key advantage: precision is conditional on θ (the test can be precise for mid-range examinees but vague at the extremes), unlike CTT's single SEM applied to every score.

Core assumptions

  • Unidimensionality: one dominant latent trait accounts for the responses.
  • Local (conditional) independence: once θ is held constant, responses to different items are statistically independent; an examinee's answer to item 2 is not influenced by item 1 beyond their shared dependence on θ. (Violated by "testlets", e.g. several questions hanging off one reading passage.)
  • Correct model fit / monotonicity: P(correct) rises monotonically with θ, and the chosen model (1PL/2PL/3PL) actually fits the data.

Why it matters: CAT and equating

  • Computer-Adaptive Testing (CAT): because items and persons share one θ scale, the computer can pick the next item whose difficulty b matches the examinee's current θ estimate (maximising information), so a precise score is reached with fewer items. Examinees effectively sit different item sets yet receive comparable scores.
  • Test equating / linking: invariant item parameters let different test forms be placed on a common scale (via anchor/common items), so scores from different forms or years are interchangeable. This underpins modern large-scale testing and item banking.

9. Item analysis formulae (difficulty & discrimination)

Lead fact: Item difficulty index (p) = proportion who answered the item correctly. Counter-intuitively, a higher p means an EASIER item (more people got it right). The ideal range is roughly p = 0.30 to 0.70, centred near 0.5 for maximum discrimination. Item discrimination (D) = pupper − plower using the top vs bottom 27%; negative D means discard the item.

Item difficulty index (p)

p = (number who answered correctly) / (total number of examinees)

  • Range 0 to 1. High p = easy item; low p = hard item. The label "difficulty" runs backwards from intuition (it is really an "easiness" index).
  • p near 0.5 maximises differentiation because variance of a proportion, p(1−p), is greatest at 0.5, so the item splits the group most evenly. Acceptable band is about 0.30 to 0.70; items with p > 0.90 (almost everyone right, too easy) or p < 0.20 (almost everyone wrong, too hard) carry little discriminating power and are usually dropped.
  • The optimal p shifts upward to allow for guessing: the target is set midway between 1.0 and the chance level. For a 4-option MCQ (chance = 0.25), the ideal p is about (1.00 + 0.25)/2 = 0.625; for true/false (chance = 0.50) it is about 0.75.
  • Average test difficulty is usually aimed near 0.5; a spread of item difficulties is kept so the test discriminates across the whole ability range.

Guessing-corrected difficulty (when many examinees guess):

pcorrected = (R − W/(k − 1)) / N   where R = right, W = wrong, k = number of options, N = total.

Item discrimination index (D)

D = (proportion correct in HIGH group) − (proportion correct in LOW group) = pU − pL

  • Rank everyone by total test score, then compare the upper 27% against the lower 27% (Kelley's rule: 27% best balances having extreme groups against having enough cases). 25% to 33% are also used.
  • Range −1 to +1. A good item is answered correctly by more high-scorers than low-scorers (positive D). D = +1 means every upper-group examinee passed and every lower-group examinee failed (perfect discrimination).
  • D depends on p: an item with p ≈ 0.5 has the room to reach high D, while very easy (p→1) or very hard (p→0) items are mathematically capped at low D because both groups score alike. This is why mid-difficulty items are favoured.

Point-biserial correlation (the other discrimination statistic)

The point-biserial (rpb) correlates a dichotomous item (right = 1, wrong = 0) with the continuous total score. Unlike D (which uses only the extreme 27% groups), rpb uses all examinees, so it is the more statistically stable discrimination index.

rpb = ((M1 − M0) / st) × √(p·q)

where M1 = mean total score of those who got the item right, M0 = mean of those who got it wrong, st = SD of total scores, p = proportion right, q = 1 − p. A positive rpb (commonly ≥ 0.20 to 0.30 desired) means the item agrees with the test as a whole; a negative rpb flags the same problem as a negative D.

Trap (D vs rpb vs biserial): D = upper 27% minus lower 27% (a simple difference). Point-biserial = correlation of a genuinely dichotomous item with total score (uses everyone). Biserial correlation assumes the dichotomy reflects an underlying continuous, normally distributed trait artificially cut in two; biserial is always a bit larger than point-biserial.

Distractor / option analysis

  • Examine each wrong option (distractor) by ability group. A functional distractor is chosen by more low-scorers than high-scorers (it plausibly pulls the weak).
  • A distractor chosen by nobody (or almost nobody) is non-functional: not plausible, contributes nothing, and should be revised or replaced.
  • A distractor that attracts more high-scorers than low-scorers signals a flawed item, often a mis-keyed item or a partially-correct distractor; this is exactly the pattern behind a negative D.
  • If too many high-scorers pick a distractor and few pick the keyed answer, suspect the answer key is wrong (miskeyed item).

How item analysis feeds reliability and validity

Discrimination is the bridge from item analysis to reliability: items that each correlate well with the total score raise inter-item consistency, so retaining high-D / high-rpb items directly increases Cronbach's alpha / KR-20. Dropping near-zero and negative discriminators lengthens the consistent core of the test and tightens reliability. Selecting items that span the content domain also protects content validity, so item analysis is a quality-control step before a test is finalised.

Worked PYQ-style examples

  • Stem: "60 of 200 examinees answered item 7 correctly; the difficulty index is ___ and the item is ___." Answer: p = 60/200 = 0.30, a relatively hard but acceptable item (at the lower edge of the 0.30 to 0.70 band).
  • Stem: "In the upper group 80% passed an item and in the lower group 90% passed it. The discrimination index is ___." Answer: D = 0.80 − 0.90 = −0.10 (negative), so the item is faulty and should be discarded / the key checked; low-scorers outperformed high-scorers.
  • Stem: "A distractor is chosen by more students in the high-scoring group than the low-scoring group. This indicates ___." Answer: a flawed/ambiguous (possibly miskeyed) item, the distractor is functioning against the key.
Trap (the most-missed point): a HIGH difficulty index (p) means an EASY item; the word "difficulty" is backwards from intuition. D ≥ 0.40 = good; negative D = throw it out. Ideal p is near 0.5 but is nudged upward to offset guessing (about 0.625 for 4-option MCQs).
Trap (difficulty vs discrimination ceiling): very easy (p→1) and very hard (p→0) items cannot discriminate well, their D is mathematically squeezed toward 0, so p and D are linked, not independent.
Hook: "p is popularity (high p = easy); D is the difference (upper minus lower, top/bottom 27%)." "Negative D = nuke the item." "A distractor should fish for the weak: low > high is healthy, high > low is a red flag."

10. Sampling methods (identify from the scenario)

Lead fact: the master split is probability sampling (every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection, the selection is random so the sample is unbiased in expectation, generalisation is justified and sampling error can be estimated) vs non-probability sampling (selection by convenience, judgement or quota, so the probability of selection is unknown, sampling error cannot be estimated, and selection bias is higher). The first decision the stem forces is simply: was the selection random / known chance, or did the researcher choose / take whoever was available? That settles the half before you name the exact technique.

Why it matters: inferential statistics (confidence intervals, p-values, standard errors) all assume the sample was drawn by a random mechanism from a defined sampling frame. With non-probability methods you can still describe the data, but the inference back to the population is informal. Singh (Tests, Measurements & Research Methods) frames probability sampling as the basis for "representativeness by design," whereas non-probability sampling buys feasibility at the cost of unquantifiable bias.

Sampling error vs sampling bias

These are pitted against each other in MCQs. Sampling error is the natural, random discrepancy between a sample statistic and the true population parameter that arises simply because you studied a sample, not the whole population; it is random, shrinks as n grows, and can be quantified (it is the standard error). Sampling bias is a systematic, directional distortion from a faulty selection method (non-random selection, an incomplete frame, non-response); a bigger sample does NOT fix it, and it cannot be estimated from the data alone.

Sample-size logic (the basics tested)

  • Required n rises with greater population variability (larger SD), a narrower confidence interval / smaller margin of error, and a higher confidence level (e.g. 99% needs more than 95%).
  • Precision improves with the square root of n: to halve the standard error you must quadruple the sample (SE = SD / √n). Diminishing returns set in quickly.
  • For a proportion, variability is maximal at p = 0.5, so that value gives the most conservative (largest) sample-size estimate when prevalence is unknown.
  • Cluster designs lose efficiency: the design effect means a cluster sample needs more units than an SRS for the same precision, because units inside a cluster are correlated.
Trap pairs:
Stratified (probability) vs Quota (non-probability): both build subgroups, but stratified samples randomly within each stratum, quota fills its subgroup targets by convenience/judgement. Random-within = stratified; targets-but-no-randomness = quota.
Stratified vs Cluster: stratified samples within every subgroup (you want each subgroup represented; strata are homogeneous within, different between) and takes some from each. Cluster picks whole groups at random and studies all of them (each cluster is a mini-population, heterogeneous within, similar between). Stratified raises precision; cluster lowers cost.
Systematic vs SRS: systematic = "every k-th after a random start." Its one danger is the periodicity pitfall: if the list has a hidden cycle whose period matches k (e.g. a ward rota where every 7th bed is a corner bed, or a list ordered male/female so an even k draws only one sex), the sample becomes biased. SRS has no such cyclic risk.
Purposive vs Convenience: purposive is a deliberate criterion/expert choice; convenience is whoever is around with no criterion.
Snowball = referral chains for hard-to-reach groups. Consecutive = every eligible case in order until quota met (looks rigorous but is still non-probability).
Worked stems:
(1) "A researcher lists all 2000 employees, randomly selects a starting number, then surveys every 25th name." → Systematic (k = 2000/n, random start, every k-th). Not SRS (SRS has no fixed interval).
(2) "From 40 colleges in the city, 6 colleges are chosen at random and every student in those 6 is surveyed." → Cluster (single-stage). The tell is whole groups chosen and everyone inside studied. If it then said "and 30 students randomly picked per college," it becomes two-stage cluster.
(3) "To ensure equal numbers, an interviewer is told to question 25 urban and 25 rural adults, approaching whoever is available." → Quota (subgroup targets, non-random fill), not stratified (no randomness within).
(4) "Studying injecting drug users, the team recruits a few users who then introduce their peers." → Snowball.
Hook: "Every k-th = systematic (tic-tic-tic, regular)." "Stratified = random within strata; Quota = quotas but no randomness." "Cluster = grab whole groups and study all." "Snowball rolls referral to referral." "Error is random and shrinks with n; bias is systematic and a big n won't save you."

11. Defense mechanisms: levels & disorder links

Lead fact: name the defense from a vignette AND know its maturity level. Highest-frequency single-mark psychoanalytic item across the readable PYQ corpus (projection 22, displacement 15, sublimation 12, rationalization 11). The framework is Anna Freud (The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence, 1936), built on Sigmund Freud's idea that the ego deploys unconscious operations to keep unacceptable id impulses and the anxiety they generate out of awareness; George Vaillant later rank-ordered them into a maturity hierarchy (psychotic < immature < neurotic < mature) validated longitudinally in the Grant Study. Gabbard's Psychodynamic Psychiatry is the standard clinical source for the disorder links.

The mechanism: why the ego defends

A defense is an unconscious, automatic ego process that distorts internal or external reality to reduce anxiety arising from a conflict between drive (id), conscience (superego), and reality. Defenses are not pathological in themselves; everyone uses them. What matters clinically is which level a person habitually relies on. Mature defenses optimise both gratification and the demands of reality; psychotic defenses sacrifice reality entirely. Vaillant's key insight is that the same conflict can be handled at any level, and the level used predicts adjustment and diagnosis.

12. Emotion theories

Lead fact: match the theory to its sequence of arousal, cognition and felt emotion; the order IS the question. The recurring split is whether bodily arousal comes first (James-Lange), arousal and emotion are simultaneous (Cannon-Bard), arousal plus a cognitive label are both needed (Schachter-Singer), or cognitive appraisal comes first (Lazarus), versus Zajonc's claim that affect can precede cognition entirely. Standard sources: Carlson / Pinel for the neuro substrate, any general-psychology text (Atkinson & Hilgard) for the theories.

The core debate: which comes first?

An emotion has three components: a physiological arousal (heart races, palms sweat), a cognitive element (appraisal or label), and the subjective feeling. The classic theories disagree only about the ORDER and CAUSAL direction of these three. Common-sense says "I see the bear, I feel afraid, so I run/tremble." James and Lange inverted this: the bodily reaction comes first and the feeling is our perception of it. Every later theory is a reaction to that inversion.

Schachter-Singer: the 1962 adrenaline (epinephrine) study

Schachter and Singer injected participants with epinephrine (adrenaline) under the cover story of testing a vitamin ("Suproxin"). Some were correctly informed of the arousal symptoms, some misinformed/uninformed. Each then waited with a confederate who acted either euphoric or angry. Participants who had no explanation for their arousal "caught" the confederate's emotion, reporting euphoria or anger to match the context, whereas those told to expect the symptoms did not. Conclusion: the same physiological arousal is labelled as different emotions depending on the cognitive interpretation of the situation. Hence "two factors": arousal + label.

The Lazarus to Zajonc debate (affect vs cognition primacy)

This is a favourite pairing. Lazarus argued cognition (appraisal) is a precondition of emotion, even if it is fast or unconscious: you must minimally evaluate the stimulus as relevant before you feel. He distinguished primary appraisal ("is this relevant/threatening to my goals?") from secondary appraisal ("can I cope with it?"). Zajonc countered that affect can arise without prior cognition, citing the mere-exposure effect (we come to prefer stimuli we have merely been exposed to, even subliminally, with no recognition memory) and arguing affect and cognition are separate, partially independent systems. His slogan: "preferences need no inferences." The exam answer: Lazarus = cognitive/appraisal primacy; Zajonc = affective primacy.

Adjacent ideas the examiner pairs in

  • Facial-feedback hypothesis: facial muscle activity itself feeds back to intensify/initiate the matching emotion (Tomkins; tested by the pen-in-teeth "smile" study). A modern descendant of James-Lange's "body first" logic.
  • Plutchik's wheel of emotions: 8 primary emotions in 4 opposing pairs: joy to sadness, trust to disgust, fear to anger, surprise to anticipation. Adjacent primaries combine into dyads (e.g. joy + trust = love); each varies in intensity (a cone). Evolutionary/adaptive framing.
  • Ekman's basic emotions: cross-culturally recognised universal facial expressions; the core six are happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust (contempt later proposed). Evidence from preliterate cultures supports universality.
  • Papez circuit / limbic substrate: James Papez's proposed emotion circuit, hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → anterior thalamus → cingulate → back to hippocampus. The amygdala (fear conditioning, LeDoux's "low road" thalamo-amygdala fast route) and hypothalamus are the key modern emotional structures; the Kluver-Bucy syndrome follows bilateral amygdala/temporal damage.

  • "On seeing a snake, arousal and the feeling of fear arise at the same instant, routed by the thalamus" → Cannon-Bard.
  • "A person feels generic arousal and, noticing others are angry, concludes she too is angry" → Schachter-Singer (arousal + contextual label).
  • "Whether a forest sound triggers fear depends on first judging it as a threat to one's safety" → Lazarus (appraisal first; primary appraisal of threat).
  • "A person prefers a shape shown subliminally without recognising it" → Zajonc / mere exposure (affect without cognition).

13. ICD-11 vs ICD-10 / DSM-5: what changed

Lead fact: ICD-11 was adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2019 and came into effect on 1 January 2022 (WHO). Its headline changes: several new categories (Gaming Disorder, Complex PTSD, Prolonged Grief Disorder, Body Integrity Dysphoria, Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder), a fully dimensional personality-disorder model (one PD diagnosis rated for severity plus trait domains), and the removal of gender incongruence from the mental-disorders chapter. Two authorities to keep straight: ICD = WHO; DSM = APA (American Psychiatric Association).

The logic an examiner tests here is twofold. First, which system a feature belongs to: ICD-11 (the WHO global standard, used for Indian clinical coding) versus DSM-5 / DSM-5-TR (the APA's North-American manual). Second, which edition: ICD-11 versus the older ICD-10. Many "new diagnosis" stems are really asking "is this an ICD-11 addition or a DSM-5 addition?" because the two systems converged on some categories but diverged on others. Read every stem for the body that publishes it and the edition number.

14. Projective tests: scoring depth (Rorschach & TAT)

Lead fact: go past "it's a projective test." Rorschach and TAT recur heavily and the scoring system is the trap. Rorschach = 10 inkblots (5 achromatic, 2 black-red, 3 fully chromatic); Exner's Comprehensive System is the standard quantitative scoring. Standard sources: Anastasi & Urbina, Groth-Marnat (Handbook of Psychological Assessment), Singh (Tests, Measurements & Research Methods).

The projective hypothesis (the underlying logic)

The projective hypothesis (Frank, 1939) holds that when a person is given an ambiguous, unstructured stimulus and asked to impose meaning on it, they project their own needs, conflicts, defenses and ways of organising experience onto it, revealing personality structure that they could not or would not report directly. This makes projectives idiographic (rich individual picture, disguised purpose, qualitative) in contrast with objective self-report inventories (MMPI, 16PF, NEO-PI) which are nomothetic (standardised items, transparent purpose, normed scores). The two camps differ on stimulus ambiguity, scoring objectivity, and how easily the testee can fake.

Rorschach: structure and Exner scoring

Hermann Rorschach (Psychodiagnostik, 1921), 10 symmetrical inkblot cards: cards I, IV, V, VI, VII are achromatic (black-grey), cards II and III are black with red, and cards VIII, IX, X are fully chromatic (pastel/colour). 15. Reinforcement schedules (operant)

Lead fact: identify the schedule and its response pattern and extinction resistance. Operant content is dense in the corpus. Variable Ratio (VR) gives the highest, steadiest responding and is the MOST resistant to extinction (gambling is the classic VR example). Standard source: any learning text (Domjan, or general-psych chapters on Skinner / operant conditioning).

The logic: ratio vs interval, fixed vs variable

A schedule of reinforcement specifies WHICH responses get reinforced. Two binary dimensions cross to give the four partial schedules:

  • Ratio vs Interval: ratio schedules reinforce after a number of responses (so working faster earns more reinforcers → high response rates); interval schedules reinforce the first response after a period of time (working faster does NOT speed the reinforcer → lower, steadier rates).
  • Fixed vs Variable: fixed schedules have a predictable requirement (so a pause appears right after each reinforcer, the post-reinforcement pause / scallop); variable schedules are unpredictable, so the organism keeps responding steadily and is hard to extinguish.

Continuous reinforcement (CRF) reinforces every response: fastest to learn but fastest to extinguish. Partial (intermittent) schedules produce slower learning but far greater persistence, the partial-reinforcement extinction effect (PREE): behaviour reinforced intermittently resists extinction because the organism cannot quickly tell that reinforcement has stopped.

ScheduleRuleCumulative-record shape / response patternReal-world example
Fixed Ratio (FR)Reinforce after a set number of responsesHigh rate with a post-reinforcement pause after each reinforcer (break-and-run)Piece-work pay (paid per N items)
Variable Ratio (VR)After an unpredictable (average) numberHighest, steadiest rate; no pause; MOST extinction-resistantGambling / slot machines
Fixed Interval (FI)First response after a set time elapsesFI scallop: little responding early, accelerating near the interval's endWeekly/monthly paycheck; studying just before a scheduled exam

Extinction-resistance ranking

VR / VI > FR / FI > continuous (CRF). Variable beats fixed (unpredictability sustains responding); within that, the gambling-type VR is the prototype of dogged, extinction-resistant behaviour. Restated: ratio > interval for response RATE (number-contingent drives speed); variable > fixed for STEADINESS and persistence (unpredictability removes pauses and slows extinction).

Adjacent operant concepts the examiner pairs in

  • Shaping: reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour (method of successive approximation).
  • Chaining: linking a sequence of behaviours where each response is the cue for the next; built by forward or (often more effective) backward chaining.
  • Premack principle: a high-probability behaviour reinforces a low-probability one ("eat your vegetables, then you may play"); "Grandma's rule." The response-deprivation hypothesis is its refinement.
  • Primary vs secondary (conditioned) reinforcers: primary satisfy biological needs (food, water); secondary acquire value by pairing (money, tokens, praise). A generalized reinforcer (money/tokens) is exchangeable for many primaries.
  • Reinforcement vs punishment (the 2x2): positive reinforcement = add a pleasant stimulus to increase behaviour; negative reinforcement = remove an aversive stimulus to increase behaviour (escape/avoidance) (NOT punishment); positive punishment = add an aversive stimulus to decrease behaviour; negative punishment / omission = remove a pleasant stimulus (time-out, response cost) to decrease behaviour.
  • Token economy: applies secondary/generalized reinforcers (tokens) on a defined schedule, later exchanged for backup reinforcers; used in wards/classrooms.

Worked stems

  • "A slot machine pays out after an unpredictable number of pulls; the gambler plays persistently and is very hard to stop" → Variable Ratio (highest rate, most extinction-resistant).
  • "A factory worker is paid for every 20 garments sewn and pauses briefly after each payment" → Fixed Ratio (note the post-reinforcement pause).
  • "Employees work little early in the month and intensify effort just before the monthly salary date" → Fixed Interval (the scallop).
  • "Students study steadily because quizzes can occur on any unannounced day" → Variable Interval.
  • "A child is allowed video games only after finishing homework" → Premack principle.
  • "A rat's lever-pressing is built up by reinforcing closer and closer approximations" → shaping.
  • "Turning off an annoying buzzer by fastening the seatbelt increases seatbelt use" → negative reinforcement (removing an aversive stimulus), NOT punishment.
Trap (ratio vs interval): Ratio = based on NUMBER of responses → higher response rates (the organism can speed up the reinforcer). Interval = based on TIME elapsed → lower, steadier rates (speeding up does not help). If "after N responses" → ratio; if "first response after T time" → interval.
Trap (fixed vs variable): Fixed = predictable → a post-reinforcement pause (FR) or scallop (FI) appears. Variable = unpredictable → steady responding and greatest extinction resistance. So FI shows the scallop, VR shows no pause; never call the scallop a VR pattern.
Trap (negative reinforcement vs punishment): negative reinforcement INCREASES behaviour by removing an aversive stimulus (escape/avoidance). Punishment DECREASES behaviour. "Negative" only means a stimulus is subtracted, not that the effect is bad. Negative punishment (omission/time-out/response cost) removes a pleasant stimulus to decrease behaviour: opposite of negative reinforcement.
Hook: "Ratio = Rate (number drives speed); Variable = Vigorous & unstoppable (steady, extinction-proof)." Examples: "gambling = VR, paycheck = FI, pop quiz = VI, piece-work = FR." Extinction ladder: "Variable beats Fixed; intermittent beats continuous" → VR/VI > FR/FI > CRF.

16. Reliability & validity: every type

Lead fact: name the type from its definition. Reliability = consistency/repeatability; validity = measuring what you intend. A test can be reliable but invalid (consistently wrong), but it cannot be valid without being reliable: reliability caps validity (validity ≤ √reliability). Reliability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for validity.

Reliability: the logic

Classical test theory says an observed score = true score + error. Reliability is the proportion of score variance that is true (systematic) rather than random error. Each reliability method targets a different source of error: time, form, internal heterogeneity, or rater. Conventionally ≥ 0.70 is acceptable for research/group decisions, ≥ 0.80 to 0.90+ for individual high-stakes decisions.

rwhole = (2 × rhalf) / (1 + rhalf)   (the doubling case)

General: rnew = (n × r) / (1 + (n − 1)r), where n = factor by which the test length changes.

Kuder-Richardson 20:

KR-20 = (k/(k−1)) × (1 − Σpiqi / s²), where k = number of items, piqi = variance of each dichotomous item, s² = total score variance.

Standard Error of Measurement (SEM)

SEM converts reliability into the error band around an individual score: how much an observed score would wobble on repeated testing.

SEM = SD × √(1 − rxx), where SD = test score SD and rxx = reliability.

  • Higher reliability → smaller SEM (less measurement error). If reliability = 1.0, SEM = 0; if reliability = 0, SEM = the full SD.
  • A 95% confidence interval for the true score is roughly observed score ± 1.96 × SEM (about ±2 SEM).

The reliability ↔ validity relationship

  • Random error lowers both reliability and the ceiling on validity, so validity coefficient ≤ √(reliability of test) (more strictly, ≤ √(rtest × rcriterion): "attenuation").
  • Reliable but invalid is possible: a bathroom scale 2 kg out reads consistently (reliable) but never your true weight (invalid). Valid but unreliable is impossible; if it measures the right thing it must do so consistently.
  • So: reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

B2. Theories of suicide (Durkheim & the psychological rivals)

Lead fact: Émile Durkheim (1897, Le Suicide) explained suicide sociologically, by two social forces: integration (strength of social bonds / belonging) and regulation (degree of social control over needs and norms). Too little or too much of each gives four types: egoistic, altruistic, anomic, fatalistic. His method, treating the suicide rate as a social fact explained by social variables (religion, marriage, war), founded sociological method itself.

Indian epidemiology context

India carries a large share of global suicides. Suicide is a leading cause of death in young adults (roughly 15 to 39 years), with a recognised South-Indian belt of higher rates and prominent methods including hanging, pesticide/poisoning, and self-immolation. Marital and economic stress, and student/agrarian distress, feature heavily. Note Sec 309 IPC (attempt) is effectively decriminalised in practice via the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, which presumes severe stress in an attempt survivor. These social-structural drivers map neatly onto Durkheim's anomic (economic disruption) and egoistic (isolation) types, which is exactly the link examiners probe.

  • "A soldier throws himself on a grenade to save his unit." → over-integration into the group → altruistic.
  • "A long-term prisoner with no prospect of release." → over-regulation, blocked future → fatalistic.
  • "A socially isolated, unmarried, irreligious man." → under-integration → egoistic.

Anomic = acute breakdown of NORMS / expectations, typically after a sudden change (regulation). Egoistic is about who you are connected to; anomic is about whether your life still has rules.
Trap (anomic vs fatalistic): opposite ends of the SAME (regulation) axis. Anomic = norm-LESS-ness, too FEW rules (anarchy of desire). Fatalistic = too MANY rules, crushing over-control (no exit). Anomic is the most-tested type; fatalistic is the rarest. B3. Psychiatric rating scales (CGI, PANSS & the PANSS read)

Lead fact: PANSS (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale; Kay, Fiszbein & Opler, 1987) has 30 items scored 1 to 7, in three subscales: Positive (7 items), Negative (7 items), General Psychopathology (16 items); total range 30 to 210 (each item's minimum is 1, so the floor is 30, not 0). CGI (Clinical Global Impression) is the quick clinician-rated global scale: CGI-S = severity and CGI-I = improvement, each on a 1 to 7 scale.

The organising idea: a rating scale is defined by what it measures (which disorder/domain), how many items and on what anchor range, and who rates it (clinician-administered vs patient self-report). Examiners build "which scale?" stems by giving you item counts, score ranges, or the symptom domain, so memorise those three coordinates for each scale. Note that PANSS items start at 1 (1 = absent), which is why every subscale total has a non-zero floor.

Clinician-rated vs self-report (a frequent distractor)

A common trap is to swap a clinician scale for its self-report cousin. The depression pair is the classic: HAM-D / MADRS are clinician-administered, whereas BDI-II and PHQ-9 are patient self-report. Likewise, schizophrenia symptom scales (PANSS, BPRS, SAPS/SANS) are clinician-rated via interview, because patients with active psychosis cannot reliably self-rate. If a stem stresses "patient fills it in himself," it is a self-report instrument (BDI, PHQ-9), not HAM-D or PANSS.

Reading a PANSS profile

PANSS gives three separate numbers. A profile is "positive-predominant" when the Positive subscale > Negative subscale, and "negative-predominant" the other way. Because PANSS measures only schizophrenia-spectrum symptoms, it carries no mood (depression) and no manic criteria; you cannot diagnose a mood disorder from PANSS alone. That is exactly the logic the Q30 stem exploits.

Your Q30 worked through: "High positive, moderate negative on PANSS, very low HAM-D → interpretation?" → Schizophrenia, predominantly positive subtype. Why: low HAM-D rules OUT depression, so it can't be schizoaffective with depression; PANSS has no manic criteria, so mania with psychotic features is out; positive > negative on PANSS = positive-predominant schizophrenia. Exam rule: low HAM-D = depression excluded; positive > negative = positive-type schizophrenia.

Worked PYQ-style examples

  • Stem: "A 30-item, 1 to 7 scale with positive, negative and general subscales." → PANSS.

  • Stem: "Which depression scale is patient-rated rather than clinician-administered?" → BDI-II (or PHQ-9). HAM-D and MADRS are clinician-rated.
  • Stem: "Andreasen's paired scales for positive and negative symptoms are ___ and ___." → SAPS and SANS.
  • Stem: "The 18-item ancestor of the PANSS is the ___." → BPRS.
Trap: PANSS is a schizophrenia scale (positive / negative / general); it does not diagnose mood. To call something "schizoaffective with depression" you need a depression measure (HAM-D) to be elevated, and a very low HAM-D kills it. Don't read "moderate negative symptoms" as depression: negative symptoms (avolition, blunting, alogia) are not the same as depressed mood.
Trap (CGI-S vs CGI-I): S = how sick now (1 normal to 7 most ill); I = how much changed from baseline (1 very much improved to 7 very much worse, 4 = no change). Keep the two anchors apart.
Trap (clinician vs self-report): HAM-D, MADRS, PANSS, BPRS, YMRS are clinician-rated. BDI-II and PHQ-9 are self-report. A psychotic or manic patient is rated by the clinician, not by self-report.
Trap (item counts): PANSS 30, BPRS 18, HAM-D 17 (or 21), MADRS 10, YMRS 11, HAM-A 14, BDI 21. Mixing these counts is the commonest "which scale" error.

B4. Substance-use screening scales (AUDIT & cousins)

Lead fact: AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test; WHO) = 10 items, score 0 to 40, cut-off ≥8 = hazardous/harmful drinking. It is the WHO's alcohol screener; the very short CAGE (4 questions, cut-off ≥2) is the bedside one. The key conceptual axis the examiner tests: screening (detect a problem) vs diagnostic (severity of a disorder) vs withdrawal-severity (guide detox) tools.

These instruments differ along three lines: substance covered (alcohol only, drugs only, or all substances), length / scoring / cut-off, and above all purpose. Screening tools (AUDIT, CAGE, MAST, DAST, ASSIST) ask "is there a problem to investigate?"; a withdrawal-severity tool (CIWA-Ar) does something completely different, it quantifies how severe an already-occurring withdrawal is to titrate treatment. Mixing a screening tool with the withdrawal tool is the single most common trap, so anchor on purpose first.

B5. Verbal learning & memory tests (RAVLT dimensions)

Lead fact: the RAVLT (Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test) reads out a 15-word list (List A) over 5 trials, then a 15-word interference list (List B, one trial), then immediate post-interference recall of List A, a ~20 to 30 min delayed recall, and finally recognition. The "dimensions" it yields: immediate memory span, total learning across trials (learning curve), proactive & retroactive interference, delayed recall/retention, and recognition.

Full administration sequence

  • Trials 1 to 5 (List A): the same 15-word list is read aloud and free-recalled five times. This builds the learning curve; normal learners gain words across trials (a typical Trial 1 is about 6 to 8 words rising to about 12 to 14 by Trial 5, age-dependent).
  • List B (interference trial): a new 15-word list is read once and recalled. List B itself serves as a distractor and a fresh "Trial 1" baseline.
  • Trial 6 (immediate recall of List A, post-interference): recall List A again without re-reading it, right after List B.
  • Trial 7 (delayed recall): after a filled delay of about 20 to 30 minutes, free-recall List A again with no re-reading.
  • Recognition: a list (commonly read aloud or as a word sheet) mixes the 15 List A words with distractors (including List B words and semantically/phonemically related foils); the person identifies the original List A words. Yields hits, false positives, and a discrimination/recognition index.

Proactive vs retroactive interference (read it off the trials)

  • Proactive interference (PI): OLD learning hurts NEW. On RAVLT, strong List A learning lowers List B recall (compare Trial B to Trial 1 of List A). Direction: old → new (forwards).
  • Retroactive interference (RI): NEW learning hurts OLD. On RAVLT, hearing List B lowers the immediate recall of List A at Trial 6 (compare Trial 6 to Trial 5). Direction: new → old (backwards).

Storage vs retrieval interpretation (the classic item)

  • Recognition intact, free recall poor → retrieval deficit. The information was stored but the person cannot self-cue it; recognition "rescues" it by providing the cue. Typical of subcortical / frontal-executive profiles (e.g. subcortical dementias, Parkinson, depression-related "pseudodementia").
  • Both recall AND recognition poor → encoding/storage (consolidation) deficit. The trace was never laid down, so even a cue cannot recover it. Typical of medial-temporal / hippocampal amnesia (e.g. Alzheimer-type, Korsakoff).
  • Rapid forgetting (Trial 6 high but Trial 7 collapses) with poor recognition also points to a consolidation/storage problem.
  • Many false positives / intrusions on recognition suggest poor monitoring / source memory (frontal) or confabulation tendencies.

  • Stem: "On the RAVLT a patient recalls only 4 words on delayed recall but identifies 14/15 on recognition. This indicates ___." Answer: a retrieval deficit (storage intact, cueing helps), suggesting a subcortical/frontal profile.
  • Stem: "List B recall is markedly lower than Trial 1 of List A. This reflects ___." Answer: proactive interference (prior List A learning intrudes on the new list).
  • Stem: "Which verbal memory test is built from words in semantic categories so it indexes use of clustering strategy?" Answer: the CVLT (16 words, 4 categories), not the RAVLT.
Trap (which test): RAVLT is auditory-verbal, 15 unrelated words. The CVLT uses 16 words in semantic categories (so it reads clustering strategy); the WMS is a broad multi-subtest battery (and the one that covers visual memory), not a single list.
Trap (PI vs RI): Proactive = prior learning hurts the new list (Trial B drop); Retroactive = the new list reaches back to hurt the old one (Trial 6 drop). Direction of the arrow is the discriminator.

Hook: "RAVLT = Rey, Auditory, 15 words, 5 trials; CVLT = 16 words, categories, clustering." "Recognition saves a retrieval deficit; nothing saves a storage deficit." "Pro = prior hurts new; Retro = recent hurts old."

B6. Levodopa & the dopamine story (Parkinson's)

Lead fact: Levodopa (L-DOPA) is the immediate metabolic precursor of dopamine and, unlike dopamine itself, it crosses the blood-brain barrier. It is the single most effective drug for Parkinson's disease, in which the nigrostriatal dopamine pathway (substantia nigra pars compacta projecting to the striatum) has degenerated. L-DOPA is always given with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor (carbidopa or benserazide) so that more L-DOPA reaches the brain and peripheral side effects (nausea, hypotension) fall.

The synthesis pathway (why L-DOPA, not dopamine)

Dopamine is made in two steps from the amino acid tyrosine. The key teaching point is that the final product, dopamine, is too polar to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), whereas its precursor L-DOPA is carried across by an amino-acid transporter. So you deliver the precursor and let the brain finish the job.

Tyrosine → (tyrosine hydroxylase, rate-limiting) → L-DOPA → (DOPA decarboxylase / AADC) → Dopamine

  • Tyrosine hydroxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme of the whole catecholamine pathway (dopamine then continues to noradrenaline then adrenaline elsewhere).
  • DOPA decarboxylase (aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase, AADC) converts L-DOPA to dopamine. It exists both in the brain and in the periphery, which is exactly the problem carbidopa solves.

Why add carbidopa / benserazide

  • Given alone, most oral L-DOPA is decarboxylated to dopamine in the periphery before it ever reaches the brain, wasting it and causing nausea, vomiting and hypotension (peripheral dopamine acts on the gut and the area postrema chemoreceptor trigger zone).
  • Carbidopa (and benserazide) are peripheral DOPA-decarboxylase inhibitors that do not cross the BBB. They block conversion only outside the brain, so more intact L-DOPA crosses, the effective dose drops (by roughly 75%), and peripheral side effects fall, while central conversion to dopamine is untouched.
  • This is the logic behind the combination products Sinemet (levodopa + carbidopa) and Madopar (levodopa + benserazide).

Antipsychotics: the mirror image of Parkinson's

Parkinson's is too little nigrostriatal dopamine, and you treat it by adding dopamine (L-DOPA). Antipsychotics do the opposite: they block D2 receptors. Blocking the nigrostriatal pathway therefore reproduces a Parkinson-like state, drug-induced parkinsonism and other extrapyramidal side effects (EPS): acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, and (long-term) tardive dyskinesia. Blocking the tuberoinfundibular pathway raises prolactin. This symmetry is the favourite exam pairing: a Parkinson's patient on L-DOPA can develop psychosis (too much dopamine), and a schizophrenia patient on a D2 blocker can develop parkinsonism (too little), and treating one risks the other.

Long-term L-DOPA problems

  • Dyskinesias (involuntary choreiform movements at peak dose), as the responsive striatal window narrows over years.
  • Motor fluctuations: "wearing-off" (benefit fades before the next dose) and the abrupt, unpredictable "on-off" phenomenon.
  • Dopaminergic / psychiatric effects: hallucinations and psychosis, impulse-control disorders, plus nausea and orthostatic hypotension.

(4) Stem: "An antipsychotic causes a tremor and rigidity; which pathway is blocked?" Answer: the nigrostriatal pathway (D2 blockade producing EPS / drug-induced parkinsonism).

B7. Glasgow Coma Scale

Lead fact: the GCS (Teasdale & Jennett, Lancet 1974, University of Glasgow) scores consciousness on three independent responses: Eye opening /4, Verbal /5, Motor /6, summed to a total of 3 (worst) to 15 (best). Severity bands: ≤8 = severe (coma), 9 to 12 = moderate, 13 to 15 = mild. The minimum possible is 3, not 0 (1+1+1), because every component scores at least 1.

The point of the scale is a quick, reproducible, communicable number for level of consciousness that any clinician can repeat at the bedside to track deterioration or improvement. Always record the breakdown (for example E3 V4 M5 = 12), not just the sum, because the components carry different prognostic weight, and the motor response is the most powerful single predictor of outcome.

Limitations and special scoring

  • Intubation: an intubated patient cannot speak, so verbal is scored "T" (VT) and the total is reported as, e.g., 10T rather than guessed.

B9. Special hallucination types (functional & friends)

Lead fact: a functional hallucination = a real external stimulus in the SAME modality triggers and coexists with the hallucination (hears a voice only while the tap runs, and hears both the tap and the voice). Contrast the reflex hallucination, where a stimulus in one modality triggers a hallucination in a different modality. Standard source: Fish's Clinical Psychopathology (Casey & Kelly); also Sims, Symptoms in the Mind.

The core definitions (Fish)

A hallucination is a perception without an object, experienced in external/objective space with the full force and clarity of a true perception, and (in the strict sense) not under voluntary control. It is distinguished from an illusion (a misperception of a real external object), from a pseudohallucination (a vivid image in inner subjective space, lacking the substantiality of true perception, with insight retained), and from ordinary mental imagery (faint, in inner space, voluntary, under one's control). B11. Affect attunement & DIR/Floortime

Lead fact: Affect attunement = Daniel Stern's term (The Interpersonal World of the Infant, 1985) for the caregiver's cross-modal matching of the infant's inner emotional state, sharing the feeling, not just imitating the behaviour. Floortime is the intervention arm of the DIR model (Developmental, Individual-differences, Relationship-based; Greenspan & Wieder), used for autism: the adult gets down on the floor, follows the child's lead, and builds the functional emotional developmental capacities through back-and-forth circles of communication.

Stern: the senses of self and how attunement works

Stern reframed infancy around a developing sense of self that emerges in layers: the emergent self (0 to 2 months), the core self (2 to 6 months), the subjective self (7 to 9 months, when shared inner states first become possible), and the verbal self (from ~15 months). Affect attunement belongs to the subjective-self period: once the infant can sense that inner states are shareable, the caregiver demonstrates this by matching the vitality of the affect.

The matching is amodal / cross-modal: the parent picks out the intensity, timing, and shape of the baby's feeling and re-expresses it in a different modality. The baby gives an excited arm-wave; the mother answers with a rising "yeaaah!" vocal swell that matches the burst's contour but not its form. What is shared is the vitality affect (the dynamic, kinetic quality of feeling: "surging", "fading", "bursting"), not the categorical emotion. This is how the infant learns that inner experience can be communed with another mind.

  • Attunement ≠ imitation: imitation copies the form of the act (baby waves, you wave). Attunement matches the feeling behind it, in another channel, which is why it tells the baby "I share your state" rather than "I can copy you".
  • Selective attunement shapes the child: which affects a parent does (and does not) attune to steers which inner states become part of the shared, knowable self.

DIR / Floortime (Greenspan & Wieder)

  • D = Developmental: a ladder of functional emotional developmental capacities (regulation & shared attention → engagement → two-way intentional communication → complex problem-solving → symbolic/emotional ideas → logical/abstract thinking).
  • I = Individual differences: each child's unique sensory processing, regulation, and motor-planning profile (what soothes, what overwhelms).
  • R = Relationship-based: learning is driven through affect within a relationship.
  • Floortime = the technique: get on the floor, follow the child's natural interest and lead, then open and close "circles of communication" (child initiates → adult responds → child responds back = one closed circle), gently expanding affect and complexity to climb the developmental ladder. It is a child-led, relationship-based, developmental intervention for autism.

B12. R² in multiple regression (coefficient of determination)

Lead fact: R² (the coefficient of determination) = the proportion of variance in the outcome (DV) that is explained by the predictor(s). It ranges 0 to 1 (often read as a %). In multiple regression, R² = the square of the multiple correlation R. R² = 0.36 means the model explains 36% of the variance in the DV; the remaining 64% is unexplained (error/other causes).

What R² is, mechanically

Regression splits the total variability in the outcome into a part the model accounts for and a part it does not: Total SS = Regression (explained) SS + Residual (error) SS. R² is simply the explained share of that total: R² = SSexplained / SStotal = 1 − (SSresidual / SStotal). So R² = 0 means the predictors explain nothing (the best guess is just the mean of Y), and R² = 1 means the predictors perfectly reproduce Y. R, the multiple correlation, is the correlation between the observed Y values and the predicted Y values from the whole set of predictors; squaring it gives R².

Because R² is a square, it is always positive and is always smaller than |r| for r values between 0 and 1 (e.g. r = .50 → R² = .25 = 25%). A correlation that "sounds" moderate often explains a modest slice of variance.

Adjusted R² and why it can drop

Plain R² has a flaw: it never decreases when you add a predictor, even a useless one, because any predictor can soak up a sliver of variance by chance. So R² over-rewards big models and overstates fit. Adjusted R² penalises the model for the number of predictors relative to sample size; it goes up only if a new predictor improves the model more than chance would predict, and it can fall (or even go slightly negative) when a predictor adds nothing useful. Adjusted R² is therefore the honest figure to report and compare when models have different numbers of predictors, and it is always ≤ R².

Neither b nor β is the same thing as R². The coefficients describe each predictor's individual contribution (slope); R² describes the whole model's overall explanatory power. In a simple (one-predictor) regression, the standardized β equals the Pearson r between X and Y, and its square equals R².

What R² does NOT tell you

  • Not causation: explained variance is still just association; a high R² does not prove X causes Y.
  • Not validity of the model: a high R² is meaningless if regression assumptions are violated (non-linearity, non-normal or heteroscedastic residuals, influential outliers, multicollinearity). Always check the residuals, not just R².
  • Not the slope or the size of effect per unit: that is b. R² is a proportion, not a rate of change.
  • Not which predictor matters: that needs the individual coefficients and their significance tests, plus semipartial correlations.
  • Not out-of-sample accuracy: R² describes fit to this sample and tends to shrink on new data (which is part of why adjusted R² and cross-validation exist).

  • "Multiple R = 0.60; what proportion of variance is explained?" → R² = 0.60² = 0.36 = 36%.
  • "Adding a 4th predictor, R² rose from .40 to .41 but adjusted R² fell." → the new predictor adds essentially nothing; drop it.
  • "Which phrase = R²?" → "percentage / proportion of variance accounted for / explained" = R² (not the correlation, not the slope).
  • "Want to compare which predictor is strongest." → use the standardized β weights, not R².

Trap: Adjusted R² being lower than R² is normal and expected, not an error; it corrects for the number of predictors. If adjusted R² is much lower than R², you likely have too many weak predictors for your sample size.
Trap: a high R² does not mean a good or valid model, and never implies causation. Check assumptions/residuals first; explained variance ≠ cause.

Hook: "R² = variance explained. Square the (multiple) correlation, read it as a percent. Adjusted R² is the honest one (can drop); b/β are the slopes, not the variance."

B13. Tests notified in the Gazette of India

Lead fact: for certification of disability (under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016, via Gazette of India notifications), India specifies which standardised tests are acceptable to assess intellectual disability (ID) and specific learning disability (SLD). The classic Gazette-notified intelligence/IQ tests for ID are the Indian-standardised batteries: Binet-Kamat, MISIC, Bhatia's Battery, Seguin Form Board, Gesell Developmental Schedule, DST (Bharat Raj), paired with VSMS for adaptive behaviour.

The logic the examiner wants is the certification framework, not just a list of test names. Under the RPwD Act 2016, disability is certified by a medical board; the percentage of disability is fixed by Gazette "Guidelines for assessment". For intellectual disability the law follows the standard diagnostic logic of ID: you must demonstrate both (a) significantly sub-average intelligence (IQ) and (b) a deficit in adaptive behaviour, with onset during the developmental period (before age 18). One alone is insufficient: a low IQ without adaptive deficit, or an adaptive deficit without low IQ, does not certify ID. That is why the notified kit always pairs an IQ test with an adaptive measure (VSMS).

B14. Levels of prevention (primary / secondary / tertiary)

Lead fact: the classic Leavell & Clark framework (echoed by Caplan's community psychiatry) splits prevention by timing relative to disease onset. Primary = before the disorder starts (reduce incidence: health promotion + specific protection). Secondary = early detection & prompt treatment of disease that exists but is early/asymptomatic (reduce prevalence & duration: screening, early intervention). Tertiary = limit disability, complications & relapse in established disease (rehabilitation). A fourth idea, quaternary prevention, protects patients from unnecessary medicalisation / over-treatment.

The logic runs along the natural history of disease. Leavell & Clark map prevention onto the prepathogenesis and pathogenesis phases: before the agent-host-environment interaction produces disease you act on susceptibility (primary); once early pathological change has begun but is sub-clinical you act at early pathogenesis (secondary); once disease is advanced you act on residual disability (tertiary). Caplan (1964) imported the same three-level scheme into community mental health, defining primary prevention as lowering the rate of new cases of mental disorder in a population, secondary as reducing the duration of cases through early treatment, and tertiary as reducing the impairment that results from disorder. Park's PSM is the standard Indian source and keeps the Leavell & Clark labels.

B15. Cortical gyri & the auditory trap (Heschl's gyrus)

Lead fact: the primary auditory cortex is Heschl's gyrus (the transverse temporal gyrus), Brodmann areas 41 & 42, sitting on the upper surface of the superior temporal gyrus. It is the first cortical relay for sound. In a typical option list (Q29) it is the only auditory area and the only primary sensory area; the other gyri offered are association cortex or belong to a different modality, so they are distractors.

Why this matters: examiners build the trap by mixing a primary sensory area with nearby association gyri that handle language or reading, plus a visual gyrus, then asking "which is the primary auditory cortex?" If you know that "primary auditory = Heschl = BA 41/42 on the superior temporal gyrus," and that everything else listed is association or visual, the answer is forced.

Tonotopic organisation

Heschl's gyrus is tonotopically organised: it carries a spatial map of frequency, the cochlea's place code preserved all the way to cortex. Conventionally high frequencies map medially/posteromedially and low frequencies laterally/anterolaterally. This frequency map is the auditory analogue of retinotopy in V1 and somatotopy (the homunculus) in S1.

B16. Cluster A personality disorders (schizoid vs schizotypal vs paranoid)

Lead fact: Cluster A (paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal) is the "odd / eccentric" group. The split your Q31 hangs on: schizoid = the content loner who genuinely doesn't want relationships and has flat, restricted emotion, but NO odd beliefs. Schizotypal = the eccentric loner with social anxiety PLUS cognitive-perceptual oddities (magical thinking, ideas of reference, odd speech and perception), i.e. attenuated schizophrenia-spectrum traits. Paranoid = pervasive distrust and suspiciousness. Only schizotypal sits on the schizophrenia spectrum.

The discriminator the examiner is really testing is the presence or absence of "odd" cognition/perception and whether the person wants relationships at all. All three Cluster A disorders share social isolation, so isolation alone tells you nothing. What separates them is the reason for the isolation and the content of the inner life:

  • Schizoid isolates because they are genuinely indifferent to people; the inner life is emotionally flat, not bizarre.
  • Schizotypal isolates because they are anxious and eccentric; the inner life is full of odd, magical, quasi-psychotic content (but not frank, sustained delusions/hallucinations).
  • Paranoid isolates (or keeps people at arm's length) because they distrust others' motives.

ICD vs DSM placement of schizotypal (high-yield)

This is a classic trap. In DSM-5, Schizotypal Personality Disorder is listed in Cluster A of the personality disorders and is also cross-listed within the schizophrenia spectrum chapter ("Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders"), reflecting its genetic link to schizophrenia. In ICD-10/ICD-11, it is not classed as a personality disorder at all: it appears as "Schizotypal Disorder" within the schizophrenia / psychotic-spectrum grouping (ICD-10 code F21), explicitly alongside schizophrenia rather than with the personality disorders. So "schizotypal" is a personality disorder in DSM but a schizophrenia-spectrum disorder in ICD.
