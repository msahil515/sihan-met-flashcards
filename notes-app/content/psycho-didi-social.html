2 · Attribution & Attributional Biases

Attribution refers to the way in which people assign responsibility for certain outcomes. Typically attribution falls into two categories:

  • Dispositional (individual) — assumes that the cause of a behaviour or outcome is internal.
  • Situational — assigns the cause to the environment or external conditions.
Watch the biases. Several attributional biases are easily confused. The fundamental attribution error (Lee Ross, 1977) is about judging others, while the actor–observer bias and self-serving bias concern how we explain our own actions. Learn the direction (internal vs external) each one pulls.

Types of attributional bias

  • Flexible attribution — a general label for the shifting, motivated way people assign causes.
  • Fundamental attribution error — when judging the behaviour of others, people are more likely to overestimate the role of dispositional attributes and to underestimate the role of the situation.
  • Self-serving bias — seeing the cause of actions as internal when the outcomes are positive and external when the results are negative.
  • Actor–observer bias — we tend to attribute our own actions to the situation.
  • Optimism bias — we like to believe that bad things happen to other people, but not to ourselves.
  • Just-world belief (M. J. Lerner) — a belief that the world is essentially fair and just (leads to victim blaming).
  • Self-fulfilling prophecy — because person A expects person B to achieve or fail, person B is likely to do just that.
    • Positive: Rosenthal / Pygmalion effect
    • Negative: Golem effect

4 · Cognitive Dissonance

Cognitive dissonance → Leon Festinger.

Cognitive dissonance refers to the tension that results from holding conflicting beliefs, attitudes, opinions, or values, or when our actions do not coincide with these cognitions.

Key — minimum justification effect. Dissonance is experienced when external rewards are not adequate to justify behaviour. Therefore:
  • Large reward = small attitude change
  • Small reward = large attitude change

Types of cognitive dissonance

  • Free choice — when you liked both options.
  • Forced choice — when you dislike both options.

Methods of reducing dissonance

  • Changing one's attitude
  • Changing one's behaviour
  • Trivialization

6 · Social Influence: Compliance, Conformity, Obedience

There are three main types of social influence:

  • Compliance — behaviour change based on a request.
  • Conformity — behaviour change based on social pressure.
  • Obedience — behaviour change based on instruction from an authority.
Don't confuse the three. Compliance follows a request, conformity follows social pressure (peers/group), and obedience follows an authority's instruction. The source of the influence is the key distinction.

  • Distance between teacher and learner
  • Expertise of the experimenter
  • Signs of authority of the experimenter
