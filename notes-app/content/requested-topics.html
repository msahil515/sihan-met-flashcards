1. The diathesis-stress model

Why do two people who go through the same divorce, the same job loss or the same childhood poverty end up so differently, one with a major depressive episode and the other with a bad few months? And why do identical twins, who share all their genes, so often differ on whether they develop schizophrenia? The diathesis-stress model is psychology's oldest and most widely used answer to both questions. It is less a single theory than a template for explaining how a disorder comes about, and almost every modern theory of depression, anxiety and psychosis is built on it.

Definition

A diathesis is a predisposition or vulnerability to a disorder that a person carries before the disorder appears. The word comes from the Greek diathesis, meaning "disposition" or "arrangement" (from diatithenai, to arrange or dispose), and was used in older medicine for a constitutional tendency to a disease (for example the "gouty diathesis"). The diathesis-stress model states that a disorder develops when a diathesis is combined with sufficient stress (an adverse environmental event or condition), and that neither the diathesis alone nor the stress alone is usually sufficient.

1.1 The core idea, built from scratch

Think of three ingredients:

  • The diathesis: something relatively enduring that the person brings to the situation. It can be biological (genes, neurodevelopmental damage, a reactive stress hormone system), psychological (a negative thinking style, dysfunctional schemas, a personality trait such as neuroticism) or social and developmental (early loss, insecure attachment). It is usually latent: you cannot see it in daily behaviour until something activates it.
  • The stress: an environmental challenge, usually after the diathesis is in place. Classic examples are negative life events (bereavement, failure, migration), chronic strain (poverty, caregiving, a hostile family) and biological insults (cannabis use, infection, sleep loss).
  • The interaction: the outcome depends on the combination. A high-diathesis person may break down after a modest stressor; a low-diathesis person may need an extreme stressor, or may never break down at all.

The statistical word for this is interaction: the effect of stress on outcome is not the same for everyone, it depends on (is "moderated by") the level of diathesis. In plain words, stress hurts the vulnerable much more than the non-vulnerable. This is why the model is often written as vulnerability x stress rather than vulnerability + stress.

Exam trap

The diathesis is the predisposition, never the stressor. If an option says "diathesis refers to the precipitating life event", it is wrong. The stressor is the precipitant (trigger); the diathesis is the predisposing factor. In the familiar "4 P" case formulation, diathesis maps onto predisposing factors and stress onto precipitating (and sometimes perpetuating) factors.

1.2 History: from Meehl to Zubin and Spring

Paul E. Meehl (1962): schizotaxia, schizotypy, schizophrenia

The modern model is usually traced to Paul E. Meehl (1962), whose presidential address to the American Psychological Association was published in the American Psychologist as "Schizotaxia, schizotypy, schizophrenia". Meehl proposed a chain of three levels:

  1. Schizogene: Meehl hypothesised a single dominant gene (the "schizogene") as the inherited starting point. This specific single-gene claim has not been supported; schizophrenia is now understood as highly polygenic.
  2. Schizotaxia: the gene produces a subtle neural integrative defect, a fault in how the nervous system integrates information (Meehl spoke of a defect he called "hypokrisia" at the level of neuronal functioning). Schizotaxia is purely the inherited neurological vulnerability.
  3. Schizotypy: almost any schizotaxic person, growing up in any ordinary social learning environment, develops a characteristic personality organisation called schizotypy: cognitive slippage (mild thought disorder), anhedonia, interpersonal aversiveness and ambivalence. Schizotypy is learned on top of schizotaxia.
  4. Schizophrenia: only a minority of schizotypes decompensate into clinical schizophrenia, and this happens when they meet adverse environmental conditions (Meehl, writing in 1962, gave the much-criticised example of a "schizophrenogenic" mother). Most schizotypes remain compensated for life.

In his later reformulation (Meehl, 1990) he gave rough figures, often quoted as about 10 percent of the population being schizotypal in his sense and about 10 percent of those developing clinical schizophrenia. Treat these as Meehl's speculative estimates, not epidemiological facts.

Exam trap

Schizotaxia = inherited neural integrative defect (biology). Schizotypy = the personality organisation that develops from it through social learning (psychology). Schizotypal personality disorder = a DSM diagnostic category (DSM-III, 1980, onward), partly inspired by the schizotypy concept and by the Danish adoption studies of relatives of people with schizophrenia, but not the same thing as Meehl's schizotypy. Also note: in ICD-10 schizotypal disorder sits in the schizophrenia chapter (F21), whereas DSM places schizotypal personality disorder among personality disorders and also lists it in the schizophrenia spectrum chapter.

David Rosenthal (1970): popularising the term

The exact phrase "diathesis-stress" is often credited to David Rosenthal (1970), in his book Genetic Theory and Abnormal Behavior, which used it to summarise the genetic and environmental evidence on schizophrenia, including the Danish adoption studies he ran with Seymour Kety and others. Who used the phrase first is uncertain; sources differ, so the safe statement is that Rosenthal popularised it.

Joseph Zubin and Bonnie Spring (1977): the vulnerability-stress model

Joseph Zubin and Bonnie Spring (1977), in "Vulnerability: a new view of schizophrenia" (Journal of Abnormal Psychology), restated the idea in a form that is still the standard textbook diagram. Their key claims:

  • Vulnerability is the enduring trait; schizophrenia itself is not permanent. What a person carries for life is the vulnerability, not the illness.
  • Episodes are time-limited. An episode occurs when stress pushes the person above their personal vulnerability threshold; when the stress subsides the person tends to return to their premorbid level. This was a deliberate challenge to the Kraepelinian assumption of inevitable deterioration.
  • Inborn vs acquired vulnerability. Inborn vulnerability lies in the genes and internal environment (neurophysiology). Acquired vulnerability comes from traumas, specific diseases, perinatal complications, family experiences, adolescent peer interactions and other life events.
  • Moderators. Whether stress tips someone over also depends on their social network, ecological niche and premorbid personality.

Graphically, Zubin and Spring plotted vulnerability against challenging events: a curved threshold line divides a "well" zone from an "episode" zone. A highly vulnerable person crosses the line with little stress; a person with low vulnerability needs a great deal.

Memory hook

Zubin and Spring = 1977, the same year as George Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977) and one year before Kety. Remember "seven-seven: vulnerability and biopsychosocial were born together". But they are different ideas: Engel's model is a general philosophy of medicine (biology, psychology and social context all matter for every illness); the diathesis-stress model is a specific causal claim that a pre-existing vulnerability interacts with later stress.

1.3 Refinements: what form does the interaction take?

Scott Monroe and Anne Simons (1991)

Scott M. Monroe and Anne D. Simons (1991), "Diathesis-stress theories in the context of life stress research: implications for the depressive disorders" (Psychological Bulletin), is the classic critical review. Their main points:

  • Most theories had been vague about how diathesis and stress combine. Researchers needed to specify the form of the relationship rather than just say "both matter".
  • Stress must be measured carefully. Self-report checklists confound the stressor with the person's reaction to it. They favoured interview-based, contextual ratings of life events (in the tradition of George Brown and Tirril Harris's Life Events and Difficulties Schedule) and the distinction between independent events (outside the person's control, such as an earthquake) and dependent events (partly produced by the person, such as a breakup after repeated arguments).
  • Diathesis and stress are not always independent. The diathesis may shape which stressors the person meets, and stress may change the diathesis over time (a dynamic rather than fixed diathesis).

Forms of the model (Ingram and Luxton, 2005)

Rick Ingram and David Luxton (2005), in a widely cited chapter on vulnerability-stress models, organised the possible forms. The terms below are the ones exam setters lift:

FormWhat it claims
Dichotomous diathesisThe diathesis is either present or absent (as in Meehl's single gene). Without it, no amount of stress produces the disorder.
Quasi-continuous diathesisThe diathesis varies in degree (as with polygenic risk or a graded cognitive style). The more diathesis, the less stress needed.
Ipsative (additive) modelDiathesis and stress are inversely related: they add up, and disorder appears once the sum crosses a threshold. A very strong diathesis may need almost no stress, and an extreme stressor may produce disorder even in someone with little diathesis. Zubin and Spring's diagram is of this kind.

The exact labels vary between textbooks, so in an MCQ go by the description rather than the label alone.

Stress generation (Constance Hammen, 1991)

Constance Hammen (1991), studying women with depression, showed that depressed people experience more dependent stressful events (especially interpersonal conflicts) than controls, but not more independent ones. In other words, people with a depressive diathesis partly generate their own stress. This breaks the simple assumption that stress just "happens to" the vulnerable person and is a key reason Monroe and Simons called for a dynamic model.

Kindling (Robert Post, 1992)

Robert M. Post (1992), in the American Journal of Psychiatry, borrowed the term "kindling" from epilepsy research. In mood disorders the first episodes are usually triggered by major life stress, but later episodes need less and less stress, and eventually may occur almost spontaneously. The diathesis itself is being changed (sensitised) by earlier episodes. Monroe and Kate Harkness (2005) later distinguished two readings: stress sensitisation (smaller stressors become enough) and stress autonomy (episodes occur without stress). Kindling is a related idea, not a version of the original diathesis-stress model: it describes a diathesis that grows over time.

1.4 Examples of diatheses

Cognitive diatheses in depression

  • Aaron T. Beck (cognitive theory, 1967 onward): early experience lays down dysfunctional schemas (for example "I am worthless unless I am loved"). They stay latent until a congruent stressor (a rejection) activates them, producing the negative cognitive triad (self, world, future). Beck (1983) added the personality diatheses sociotropy (dependence on relationships) and autonomy (dependence on achievement and independence), with the congruency hypothesis: sociotropic people are vulnerable to interpersonal loss, autonomous people to failure.
  • Hopelessness theory, Lyn Abramson, Gerald Metalsky and Lauren Alloy (1989), Psychological Review: the diathesis is a negative inferential style, a tendency to explain negative events with stable and global causes, to infer negative consequences, and to infer negative things about oneself. When such a person meets negative events, they are likely to become hopeless, and hopelessness is the proximal sufficient cause of a subtype, "hopelessness depression". This is an explicit diathesis-stress theory, and it revised the earlier learned helplessness reformulation (Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale, 1978).

A genetic example: Caspi et al. (2003) and what happened next

Avshalom Caspi, Terrie Moffitt and colleagues (2003), in Science, used the Dunedin (New Zealand) birth cohort and reported that people carrying one or two short alleles of the serotonin transporter promoter polymorphism (5-HTTLPR) were more likely to become depressed after stressful life events than people with two long alleles. It became the textbook example of a gene x environment (G x E) interaction, that is, a molecular diathesis-stress effect.

The story did not hold up well. Neil Risch and colleagues (2009), a meta-analysis in JAMA, found no evidence that 5-HTTLPR moderated the stress-depression link (stress itself strongly predicted depression). A 2011 meta-analysis by Karg and colleagues, using broader inclusion, did report support, so the literature was contested for a while. Then Richard Border and colleagues (2019), in the American Journal of Psychiatry, tested historical depression candidate genes, including 5-HTTLPR and its interaction with stress, in very large samples and found no support. The lesson for exams: Caspi et al. is the classic G x E example, but it is now regarded as a failed replication story, and polygenic approaches have replaced single candidate genes.

Schizophrenia examples

  • Copenhagen high-risk study: begun in 1962 by Sarnoff Mednick and Fini Schulsinger. Children of mothers with schizophrenia (high genetic risk) and matched controls were followed from childhood. Among the high-risk children, those who later developed schizophrenia were more likely to have had obstetric complications and disruptive early rearing (for example separation from the mother), a diathesis-stress pattern where acquired insults act on a genetic predisposition.
  • Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia, led by Pekka Tienari: adopted-away children of mothers with schizophrenia spectrum disorders were compared with adoptees of mothers without them, and the adoptive families' functioning was rated independently. High genetic risk raised the rate of schizophrenia spectrum disorders mainly when the adoptive family was rated as disturbed (dysfunctional communication); in healthy adoptive families the high-risk adoptees did about as well as controls (Tienari et al., 2004, British Journal of Psychiatry). This is one of the cleanest human demonstrations of genotype x rearing environment interaction.

A cousin: Barlow's triple vulnerability theory

David H. Barlow (2000, 2002) explained anxiety and related emotional disorders through three vulnerabilities: a generalised biological vulnerability (heritable negative affect, neuroticism), a generalised psychological vulnerability (an early sense that events are uncontrollable and unpredictable) and a specific psychological vulnerability (learning that particular objects or bodily sensations are dangerous, which explains why one person develops panic disorder and another social anxiety). Stress activates these vulnerabilities. It is a diathesis-stress model with the diathesis split into three layers.

1.5 Differential susceptibility: the challenge to diathesis-stress

The diathesis-stress model assumes that "vulnerable" people only ever do worse: in a good environment they are at best equal to everyone else. Jay Belsky (1997) proposed, on evolutionary grounds, that some children are simply more plastic (more responsive to rearing) than others. Because parents cannot know which rearing strategy will pay off in an unpredictable future, natural selection would favour having some offspring who are highly shaped by the environment and others who are not. Belsky and Michael Pluess (2009), in "Beyond diathesis stress: differential susceptibility to environmental influences" (Psychological Bulletin), reviewed evidence that the same traits once called vulnerabilities (difficult or negatively emotional infant temperament, certain dopamine and serotonin gene variants, high physiological reactivity) make people do worse in adverse environments but better in supportive ones. The slogan is "for better and for worse", in contrast to diathesis-stress, which is only "for worse".

  • Biological sensitivity to context: W. Thomas Boyce and Bruce J. Ellis (2005), Development and Psychopathology, argued that high stress reactivity (autonomic and adrenocortical) reflects heightened sensitivity to context, both harmful and protective. They proposed a U-shaped relation: highly reactive children are over-represented in both very stressful and very protective early environments.
  • Orchid vs dandelion children: a metaphor drawn from the Swedish expression maskrosbarn ("dandelion child", one who thrives anywhere). Dandelion children do reasonably well in almost any environment; orchid children wilt under neglect but flourish spectacularly with good care. Boyce popularised it in his book The Orchid and the Dandelion (2019).
  • Vantage sensitivity: Pluess and Belsky (2013), Psychological Bulletin, described the positive half on its own: some individuals benefit more than others from positive experiences and interventions (for example psychotherapy or school programmes). Vantage resistance is the failure to benefit. Diathesis-stress describes the negative pole, vantage sensitivity the positive pole, and differential susceptibility combines both.

Seeing the difference: the crossover interaction

Plot environment quality on the x axis (adverse on the left, supportive on the right) and outcome (well-being, higher is better) on the y axis. Draw one line for the "vulnerable" or "susceptible" group and one for the comparison group.

Panel A (diathesis-stress): the lines fan out toward the adverse end and converge at the supportive end. In a good environment the vulnerable do as well as everyone else; the diathesis only shows under stress. This is an ordinal (non-crossing) interaction.

Panel B (differential susceptibility): the susceptible line is steeper and crosses the other line. Susceptible people do worst in bad environments and best in good ones. This is a disordinal (crossover) interaction. To claim differential susceptibility, researchers must show the crossover point lies within the realistic range of environments, and that the "better" side is real, not just noise (Glenn Roisman and colleagues, 2012, proposed formal statistical tests for this).

1.6 Clinical implications

  • Prevention: if the diathesis can be identified early (family history, high-risk mental states, a negative cognitive style), stress exposure can be reduced or coping strengthened before a first episode. Clinical high-risk (prodromal) services for psychosis rest on this logic.
  • Relapse prevention: in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, medication is understood as lowering biological vulnerability, while psychosocial work reduces stress. Relapse signature plans teach patients and families to spot early warning signs after a stressor.
  • Stress management and coping: problem-solving training, sleep regularity, avoiding cannabis and other drugs, and building social support all aim at the stress side or at the moderators that Zubin and Spring listed.
  • Cognitive therapy: Beck's therapy tries to modify the diathesis itself (the schemas) so that future stressors do less damage; mindfulness-based cognitive therapy targets the kindling-like reactivation of depressive thinking in recurrent depression.
  • Family expressed emotion (EE): George W. Brown, J. L. T. Birley and John Wing (1972), building on Brown's earlier work in the 1950s and 1960s, showed that people with schizophrenia discharged to families high in criticism, hostility or emotional over-involvement relapsed more often. Christine Vaughn and Julian Leff (1976) replicated this using the Camberwell Family Interview and found the risk was greatest when high-EE contact was long (more than about 35 hours a week face to face) and medication was not taken. High EE is an environmental stressor acting on a biological vulnerability. Family psychoeducation and behavioural family therapy (for example Ian Falloon's work) lower EE and reduce relapse. Indian relevance: EE studies in north India (Chandigarh, by N. N. Wig, Leff and colleagues in the 1980s, linked to the WHO Determinants of Outcome study) found that high EE was less common in Indian families than in London samples, one proposed reason for the better course of schizophrenia observed in developing-country WHO centres.

Exam trap

(1) Diathesis = predisposition, not trigger. (2) Schizotaxia (neural defect) vs schizotypy (personality organisation) vs schizotypal personality disorder (DSM category). (3) Zubin and Spring is 1977, not 1962 (Meehl) or 1970 (Rosenthal). (4) In Zubin and Spring, the vulnerability is permanent, the episode is time-limited. (5) Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977) is not the diathesis-stress model. (6) Differential susceptibility is "for better and for worse"; diathesis-stress is "for worse only". (7) Stress generation (Hammen) means dependent events increase, not independent ones. (8) Kindling is Post (1992), borrowed from epilepsy research.

2. Bleuler's four A's and the history of the schizophrenia concept

"Schizophrenia" is a little over a century old as a word, and the concept has been reshaped three or four times since. Entrance examiners love this history because it is full of near-identical names, years one apart and symptom lists that sound alike. The way to keep it straight is to see each figure as answering a different question: Kraepelin asked "what is the course?", Bleuler asked "what is the underlying psychological disturbance?", and Schneider asked "which symptoms can be reliably recognised and used for diagnosis?"

2.1 Before Kraepelin: the pieces

  • Bénédict Augustin Morel (France) used the term démence précoce ("early dementia") for young people who showed early mental deterioration. The usual dates given are 1852 (Études cliniques) and 1860 (Traité des maladies mentales); sources differ on which is the "first" use. Morel worked within his theory of hereditary degeneration.
  • Ewald Hecker (1871) described hebephrenia, an illness beginning in adolescence with silly, incongruous behaviour and rapid deterioration (the name had been suggested earlier by his teacher Karl Ludwig Kahlbaum).
  • Karl Ludwig Kahlbaum (1874) described catatonia ("tension insanity"), with motor symptoms such as stupor, posturing and waxy flexibility.
  • Paranoid states (with delusions as the main feature) were described by several authors as separate conditions.

2.2 Emil Kraepelin: dementia praecox

Emil Kraepelin, working in Heidelberg and later Munich, revised his textbook (Psychiatrie) repeatedly. He used the Latin term dementia praecox in the 4th edition (1893), at first for a narrower group among the "degenerative" processes. In the 6th edition (1899) he consolidated hebephrenia, catatonia and paranoid dementia into one disease, dementia praecox, on the grounds that, despite different symptoms, they shared:

  • early onset (praecox = early, typically adolescence or young adulthood), and
  • a deteriorating course ending in a characteristic weakening of mind (the "dementia").

In the same edition he set this against manic-depressive insanity, which ran an episodic course with good recovery between episodes. This Kraepelinian dichotomy (dementia praecox vs manic-depressive insanity, separated by course and outcome) still underlies the split between schizophrenia spectrum and mood disorder chapters in DSM and ICD. Kraepelin's method was longitudinal: he grouped patients by how the illness unfolded over years, recorded on his famous index cards (Zählkarten), rather than by any single symptom. Later editions (the 8th, 1909 to 1915) added further subforms such as simple dementia praecox.

Exam trap

Kraepelin did not coin the French "démence précoce" (that is Morel). He did not coin "schizophrenia" (that is Bleuler). What Kraepelin did was unify hebephrenia, catatonia and paranoid forms under "dementia praecox" (consolidated in the 6th edition, 1899) and separate it from manic-depressive insanity. If a question asks who first described catatonia, the answer is Kahlbaum (1874); hebephrenia, Hecker (1871).

2.3 Eugen Bleuler: from dementia praecox to "the group of schizophrenias"

Eugen Bleuler (1857 to 1939) was director of the Burghölzli, the psychiatric hospital of the University of Zurich, from 1898; Carl Gustav Jung worked there under him. Bleuler lived alongside his patients and was influenced by the associationist psychology of the day and by Freud's ideas, which he applied to the content of psychotic symptoms.

  • He introduced the word "schizophrenia" in a lecture in 1908 (to the German Psychiatric Association, Berlin).
  • His great monograph, Dementia Praecox oder Gruppe der Schizophrenien ("Dementia praecox, or the group of schizophrenias"), appeared in 1911 as part of Gustav Aschaffenburg's handbook of psychiatry.
  • It was translated into English only in 1950, by Joseph Zinkin.

Why rename it?

"Schizophrenia" comes from Greek schizein (to split) and phren (mind). Bleuler meant the splitting (loosening) of the psychic functions from one another: thought, emotion and will no longer work together as a coherent whole, so a patient might describe a tragedy while smiling. He did not mean a "split personality" in the sense of multiple identities (that is dissociative identity disorder, a completely different condition). He also rejected Kraepelin's name because his own patients showed that the illness was:

  • not always a dementia: many did not deteriorate to a final weakened state, and some recovered, and
  • not always praecox: onset could be later in life.

The phrase "group of schizophrenias" shows that he already saw it as a heterogeneous group of conditions, not a single disease.

2.4 The four A's, one by one

Bleuler did not use the phrase "four A's" himself; it is a teaching mnemonic added later by textbook writers to summarise his fundamental symptoms. Each is explained below with a clinical picture.

1. Associations (loosening of associations)

For Bleuler this was the central disturbance. In normal thinking, ideas follow one another guided by a goal (the "determining tendency"). In schizophrenia the threads that link ideas are broken, so thoughts follow chance, sound or private links. Example: asked "How are you sleeping?", a patient replies, "Sleep is a bed of roses, roses are red, the Red Army marches at night, I keep watch." Modern terms that descend from this include derailment, tangentiality, incoherence and, in its mildest form, Meehl's "cognitive slippage". Bleuler also included thought blocking (sudden stopping of the train of thought).

2. Affect (affective disturbance)

Emotional expression becomes flattened (blunted) or incongruous (inappropriate) to the content of thought. Example: a young man reports, without visible emotion, that his mother died last week, or laughs while describing being persecuted. Bleuler stressed that the inner emotional life might be less impoverished than it looks; what is lost is the integration between feeling and the rest of the mind.

3. Ambivalence

Bleuler coined this word too. It means the simultaneous presence of contradictory feelings, wishes or thoughts toward the same object, both held at full strength without resolution. He described three forms:

  • affective ambivalence: loving and hating the same person at the same moment;
  • ambitendency (volitional ambivalence): wanting and not wanting to do something at once, so the patient starts to shake hands, withdraws, extends the hand again;
  • intellectual ambivalence: thinking "I am a man" and "I am not a man" at the same time.

Ordinary mixed feelings are not what he meant: in schizophrenia the opposites coexist without the normal pressure to reconcile them.

4. Autism

Bleuler coined "autism" (Greek autos, self), around 1910 to 1911 (sources differ on the very first use), for the withdrawal from external reality into an inner world of fantasy, with "autistic thinking" governed by wishes and private logic rather than by reality. Example: a patient sits for hours absorbed in an inner drama, treating reality as less important than the inner world. The word was later borrowed by Leo Kanner (1943), "Autistic disturbances of affective contact", and Hans Asperger (1944), "autistic psychopathy", for a developmental condition of childhood. That borrowing is why the same word now names a completely different disorder.

Memory hook

Associations, Affect, Ambivalence, Autism. None of the four is a hallucination or a delusion. Bleuler thought the dramatic "positive" symptoms were the surface; the real illness was the loosening underneath.

2.5 Two different axes: fundamental vs accessory, primary vs secondary

This is the single most confused point in Bleuler questions, so build it slowly. Bleuler classified symptoms in two separate ways.

Axis 1: fundamental (basic) vs accessory symptoms (a diagnostic axis)

  • Fundamental symptoms are present, at least to some degree, in every case and at every stage, and are characteristic of schizophrenia. They are therefore what the diagnosis rests on. They include the four A's, and Bleuler also listed further fundamental disturbances (of attention, will, the "person" or sense of self, and schizophrenic "dementia" in his special sense).
  • Accessory symptoms may or may not be present, come and go, and also occur in other illnesses. They include hallucinations, delusions, catatonic symptoms, certain disturbances of memory, speech and writing, and acute syndromes. They are often what brings the patient to hospital, yet for Bleuler they were not essential to the diagnosis.

Axis 2: primary vs secondary symptoms (a theoretical, causal axis)

  • Primary symptoms are the direct product of the underlying disease process, which Bleuler presumed to be organic. The chief primary symptom in his account was the loosening of associations (along with some others he thought organic, such as certain disturbances of consciousness and physical signs).
  • Secondary symptoms are the person's psychological reactions to the primary disturbance and to their life situation, shaped by complexes and wishes. Much of the clinical picture, including autism, much of the affective change, hallucinations and delusions, was secondary in this sense.

Because these are different axes, a symptom can be fundamental but secondary: autism is present in every case (fundamental) yet Bleuler regarded it as a psychological consequence (secondary). Hallucinations are accessory and secondary. Loosening of associations is fundamental and primary.

2.6 Bleuler's influence: a broad concept and the US-UK gap

Because Bleuler's fundamental symptoms were subtle and not tied to deterioration, his concept was broad: mild, latent and "simple" cases with no hallucinations or delusions could qualify. This broad view became dominant in the United States (helped by psychoanalytic psychiatry and by Harry Stack Sullivan and others), whereas British and European psychiatry stayed closer to Kraepelin and Schneider.

  • US-UK Diagnostic Project (John E. Cooper, Robert Kendell, Barry Gurland and colleagues, 1972, Psychiatric Diagnosis in New York and London): New York hospital psychiatrists diagnosed schizophrenia far more often than London psychiatrists, including many patients London would have called manic-depressive or personality disordered. When the project team rated the same patients with a standardised interview (the Present State Examination), most of the difference disappeared. The gap was in diagnostic habits, not in the patients.
  • International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (IPSS), WHO (1973): nine centres, including Agra in India. Seven centres shared a broadly similar core concept; Washington and Moscow used much broader concepts. The IPSS and its successor, the WHO Determinants of Outcome study (with Chandigarh as an Indian centre), also suggested a better course in developing-country centres.

These findings pushed psychiatry toward operational criteria (Feighner criteria 1972, Research Diagnostic Criteria 1978, DSM-III 1980) that narrowed the American concept and leaned on Schneider's symptoms.

2.7 Kurt Schneider: first-rank symptoms

Kurt Schneider (Heidelberg) took a pragmatic route. Whatever the underlying process, which symptoms are both easy to recognise and highly characteristic? He set these out in Klinische Psychopathologie (first edition 1950, developed from earlier work in the late 1930s), translated into English by M. W. Hamilton in 1959. In the absence of organic brain disease, the presence of a first-rank symptom (FRS) was to be taken as strongly suggestive of schizophrenia. Christopher S. Mellor (1970) later gave the standard operational definitions. The commonly cited list of 11:

  1. Audible thoughts (Gedankenlautwerden, also "thought echo" or écho de la pensée): hearing one's own thoughts spoken aloud.
  2. Voices arguing or discussing the patient in the third person.
  3. Voices commenting on the patient's actions ("now she is picking up the cup").
  4. Somatic passivity: bodily sensations experienced as imposed by an outside agency.
  5. Thought withdrawal: thoughts are taken out of the mind by an outside force.
  6. Thought insertion: thoughts that are not one's own are put into the mind.
  7. Thought broadcasting (diffusion): one's thoughts escape and are known to others.
  8. Made feelings: emotions imposed from outside.
  9. Made impulses (drives): urges imposed from outside.
  10. Made volitional acts: actions controlled by an outside agency.
  11. Delusional perception: a normal perception is given a sudden, delusional, self-referent meaning (for example, "the traffic light turned red, so I knew I had been chosen to save the world"). This is a two-stage process: normal percept, then unconnected delusional meaning.

Items 4 and 8 to 10 together are often grouped as passivity experiences (delusions of control); items 5 to 7 as thought alienation; items 1 to 3 as third-person auditory hallucinations.

Second-rank symptoms were other features that could support the diagnosis but were less specific: other kinds of hallucination, sudden delusional ideas (delusional notions), perplexity, depressive or euphoric mood changes and emotional blunting. These occur in many disorders.

High yield

FRS are not pathognomonic. Later studies found them in a proportion of patients with mania and other psychoses, and a substantial minority of people with schizophrenia never show any. ICD-10 gave FRS heavy weight. DSM-IV allowed a single Criterion A symptom to suffice if delusions were bizarre or hallucinations were a running commentary or two voices conversing. DSM-5 (2013) dropped this special weighting (and the classic subtypes), so these symptoms now count like any other Criterion A symptom.

2.8 After Schneider: positive and negative symptoms, Crow, Langfeldt

  • Gabriel Langfeldt (Norway, 1939) separated "true" process schizophrenia (insidious, poor prognosis) from schizophreniform states (acute onset, clear precipitant, good prognosis). The term survives as DSM's schizophreniform disorder, though now defined by duration (1 to 6 months), not prognosis.
  • Timothy Crow (1980) proposed two syndromes. Type I: positive symptoms, acute, good response to neuroleptics, presumed dopamine (D2) excess, reversible, no intellectual impairment. Type II: negative symptoms, chronic, poor drug response, structural brain change (ventricular enlargement), intellectual impairment, poorer outcome.
  • Nancy Andreasen built rating scales for negative (SANS, 1982) and positive (SAPS, 1984) symptoms and, with Olsen (1982), positive, negative and mixed subtypes. The negative symptom concept looks back to Bleuler's fundamental symptoms, and the positive concept to Schneider's FRS. The terms "positive" and "negative" symptoms themselves go back to the neurologist John Hughlings Jackson in the 19th century.

2.10 Manfred Bleuler and long-term outcome

Eugen Bleuler's son Manfred Bleuler, also director of the Burghölzli, personally followed a cohort of patients for more than two decades (the usually quoted figure is 208 patients), published in German in 1972 and in English in 1978. Do not confuse father and son: Eugen coined schizophrenia and autism; Manfred did the long-term outcome study.

3. The theory of planned behaviour

The theory of planned behaviour (TPB) is the most widely used model in social and health psychology for predicting what people will actually do. To understand why it exists you have to start with an embarrassing problem that social psychology faced in the middle of the twentieth century: attitudes, the field's favourite concept, did not seem to predict behaviour.

3.1 The attitude-behaviour problem

Gordon Allport (1935) had called attitude "the most distinctive and indispensable concept" in social psychology, on the assumption that if you know how a person feels about something you can predict how they will act toward it. Two pieces of evidence shook that assumption.

Richard LaPiere (1934), "Attitudes vs. actions". Between 1930 and 1932 LaPiere travelled across the United States with a young Chinese couple at a time of strong anti-Chinese prejudice. They visited hotels, auto camps and restaurants (the commonly cited total is 251 establishments) and were refused service only once. About six months later LaPiere mailed a questionnaire to the same establishments asking "Will you accept members of the Chinese race as guests?" Of those who replied, roughly 92% said no. The stated attitude (rejection) and the actual behaviour (acceptance) were almost opposite. The study has methodological problems (the person answering the letter was probably not the person at the front desk, the couple were well dressed and accompanied by a white professor, and a letter about "the Chinese race" is a far more abstract target than a particular polite couple), but it became the classic demonstration of the gap.

Allan Wicker (1969). Wicker reviewed several dozen studies that had measured both attitudes and overt behaviour and concluded that the relationship was weak: correlations rarely exceeded about .30 and the typical value was around .15. He even suggested that the attitude concept might be abandoned. A correlation of .15 means attitudes explain only about 2% of the variance in behaviour.

3.2 The rescue: the principle of compatibility

Icek Ajzen and Martin Fishbein (1977, Psychological Bulletin) argued that attitudes do predict behaviour, but only when both are measured at the same level of specificity. They originally called this the principle of correspondence; Ajzen later relabelled it compatibility. Any behaviour has four elements, remembered as TACT: Target (toward what), Action (what is done), Context (where), and Time (when).

LaPiere measured a general attitude ("the Chinese race") and tried to predict a highly specific act (serving this couple, in this hotel, on this evening). A general attitude toward religion will not predict whether you attend a particular temple next Sunday, but it will predict a broad aggregate of religious behaviours across many occasions. Conversely, a specific attitude ("my attending the temple this Sunday") predicts that specific act well. When attitude and behaviour measures are compatible, correlations rise substantially.

Definition

Principle of compatibility (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1977): an attitude predicts a behaviour well only when both are defined at the same level of generality on Target, Action, Context and Time. General attitudes predict aggregated behaviour; specific attitudes predict specific acts.

3.3 The Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA)

Martin Fishbein and Icek Ajzen set out the Theory of Reasoned Action in Belief, Attitude, Intention, and Behavior: An Introduction to Theory and Research (1975), building on Fishbein's earlier expectancy-value work on attitudes in the 1960s. Ajzen and Fishbein then presented it for applied readers in Understanding Attitudes and Predicting Social Behavior (1980). The word "reasoned" does not mean people are perfectly logical; it means behaviour follows reasonably from the information and beliefs a person holds.

The TRA has a simple chain:

  1. Behavioural intention is the immediate antecedent of behaviour. Intention is the person's readiness or plan to perform the act ("I intend to exercise three times this week").
  2. Intention is determined by two things: the attitude toward the behaviour and the subjective norm.
  3. Attitude toward the behaviour is the person's overall positive or negative evaluation of performing the behaviour. It is built by an expectancy-value rule: for each salient outcome, multiply the strength of the belief that the behaviour leads to that outcome (behavioural belief, b) by the evaluation of that outcome (e), and sum: AB = Σ biei. Example: "Exercising will reduce my weight" (very likely, +3) × "reducing my weight is" (very good, +3) = +9; add up across outcomes like "exercising will tire me" and "exercising will take time from study".
  4. Subjective norm is the perceived social pressure to perform or not perform the behaviour. It is built from normative beliefs (what I think specific important others, called referents, want me to do: parents, spouse, doctor) multiplied by motivation to comply with each referent, then summed: SN = Σ njmj.
  5. The relative weight of attitude and subjective norm varies across behaviours and people. For some acts (choosing a career in a joint family) the norm dominates; for others (choosing a flavour of ice cream) attitude dominates.

Everything else (personality, demographics, general attitudes toward objects) is treated as an external variable: it influences behaviour only indirectly by shaping these beliefs.

The limit of the TRA: volitional control. The TRA works for behaviours fully under volitional control, where intending is enough to act. Many important behaviours are not like that. You may fully intend to quit smoking, lose weight or attend therapy but lack the skill, money, time, transport or cooperation of others. For such behaviours intention alone predicts poorly. This is the gap the TPB was built to fill.

3.4 The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB)

Icek Ajzen extended the TRA in a book chapter, "From intentions to actions: A theory of planned behavior", in Julius Kuhl and Jürgen Beckmann (Eds.), Action Control: From Cognition to Behavior (Springer, 1985). The fully developed and most cited statement is Ajzen (1991), "The theory of planned behavior", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 50, 179 to 211. Note that the TPB is Ajzen's theory alone; Fishbein was co-author of the TRA, not of the TPB papers.

The single addition is perceived behavioural control (PBC): the person's perception of how easy or difficult it will be to perform the behaviour. Like the other constructs it rests on beliefs. Control beliefs (c) are beliefs about the presence of factors that can help or hinder (time, money, skill, a nearby gym, a supportive partner), and each is weighted by its perceived power (p) to facilitate or inhibit the behaviour: PBC = Σ ckpk.

The two routes of PBC. PBC works in two ways, and examiners love this point:

  • Indirect route, via intention. People form stronger intentions to do things they believe they can do. If I think quitting is impossible for me, I will hardly intend to quit.
  • Direct route, to behaviour. PBC also predicts behaviour directly, but only to the extent that PBC is an accurate reflection of actual behavioural control. Here PBC acts as a proxy for actual control (real skills, resources, opportunities). Two people with equally strong intentions to exercise will differ in success if one actually has a gym and free time; PBC captures part of that difference. When perceptions are unrealistic (little information, a new behaviour, changed circumstances) the direct path weakens.

Ajzen (1991) also suggested that PBC moderates the intention-behaviour link (intentions translate into behaviour more strongly when control is high), though in practice most studies test only additive paths.

Link to self-efficacy. Ajzen explicitly noted that PBC is most compatible with Albert Bandura's (1977) concept of self-efficacy, "beliefs in one's capability to organise and execute the courses of action required" (from Bandura's 1977 Psychological Review paper, "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change"). Later research showed PBC items split into two factors: self-efficacy / perceived capacity (ease or difficulty, confidence) and perceived controllability / autonomy (whether it is up to me). Self-efficacy items usually predict intention better.

High yield

TPB = TRA + perceived behavioural control. Three belief bases feed three constructs: behavioural beliefs → attitude toward the behaviour; normative beliefs → subjective norm; control beliefs → PBC. These three → intention → behaviour, plus a direct PBC → behaviour path that works only insofar as PBC mirrors actual control.

3.5 Later extensions

Descriptive vs injunctive norms. The subjective norm in the TRA and TPB is essentially injunctive: what others think I ought to do. Robert Cialdini and colleagues (Cialdini, Reno and Kallgren, 1990, the "focus theory of normative conduct", tested with littering studies) distinguished this from the descriptive norm: what others actually do. Seeing a littered car park makes people litter more (descriptive), even if they know littering is disapproved (injunctive). TPB researchers later added descriptive norms and found they add predictive power.

The Reasoned Action Approach (RAA). Fishbein and Ajzen (2010), Predicting and Changing Behavior: The Reasoned Action Approach, unified the TRA and TPB. The RAA keeps attitude, perceived norm and PBC, but splits each into two subcomponents: attitude into instrumental (useful/useless) and experiential (pleasant/unpleasant); perceived norm into injunctive and descriptive; PBC into capacity and autonomy. Fishbein died in 2009, before the book appeared.

3.6 How good is the evidence?

Two meta-analyses are quoted in almost every textbook. Treat the exact numbers as the commonly cited figures:

  • Christopher Armitage and Mark Conner (2001), British Journal of Social Psychology, about 185 independent studies: the TPB explained about 39% of the variance in intention and about 27% of the variance in behaviour. PBC added significantly to the prediction of intention over attitude and subjective norm. Subjective norm was the weakest predictor. Prediction of self-reported behaviour was better than prediction of objectively measured behaviour.
  • Paschal Sheeran (2002), European Review of Social Psychology, a meta-analysis of meta-analyses: the average intention-behaviour correlation was about .53, meaning intentions explain about 28% of the variance in behaviour. This leaves a large intention-behaviour gap: Sheeran showed most of the gap comes from "inclined abstainers", people who intend to act but do not.

3.7 Applications in health psychology

The TPB has been applied to condom use, smoking initiation and cessation, exercise, diet, alcohol use, breast and cervical screening, medication adherence and help-seeking. In India it is a natural fit for public health behaviours such as handwashing with soap, vaccination uptake (including COVID-19 and childhood immunisation), toilet use under sanitation campaigns, tobacco and gutka cessation, and mental health help-seeking. A clinical psychologist might use it to understand why a patient with depression does not attend follow-up: is it attitude ("therapy will not help"), norm ("my family thinks seeing a psychiatrist is shameful"), or PBC ("the hospital is two buses away and I cannot take leave")? Each points to a different intervention.

How a TPB questionnaire is built (Ajzen's recommended procedure):

  1. Define the behaviour in TACT terms ("washing hands with soap before every meal at home over the next two weeks").
  2. Elicitation study. In a small sample from the target population, ask open-ended questions: What are the advantages/disadvantages of doing this? Who would approve/disapprove? What would make it easier/harder? The most frequently mentioned answers become the modal salient beliefs.
  3. Main questionnaire. Build items for each belief and each direct construct, typically on 7-point scales. Direct attitude uses semantic differential pairs ("For me, washing hands before meals is: bad 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 good; unpleasant ... pleasant; harmful ... beneficial"). Belief strength items are often scored from 1 to 7 or from -3 to +3; evaluations and motivation to comply are usually bipolar.
  4. Measure behaviour later (prospective design), ideally objectively.
  5. Analyse with hierarchical regression or structural equation modelling.

3.8 Criticisms

  • "Time to retire". Falko Sniehotta, Justin Presseau and Vera Araújo-Soares (2014), in Health Psychology Review, argued that the TPB has limited predictive validity, that its constructs often do not change behaviour when targeted in interventions, and that it is too static. Ajzen published a reply defending it.
  • Rationality bias. The model assumes deliberate, conscious processing. It neglects emotion, impulse, and automatic processes.
  • Past behaviour and habit. Past behaviour often predicts future behaviour better than TPB variables and adds variance beyond them, which suggests habit (automatic responding cued by context) is missing from the model.
  • Self-report and common method variance inflate correlations; objective behaviour is predicted less well.
  • Intention-behaviour gap. Even strong intentions often fail.
  • Sufficiency assumption. Ajzen allowed extra predictors in principle, and many have been added (moral norm, anticipated regret, self-identity), which weakens the claim that three constructs are enough.

Bridging the gap: implementation intentions. Peter Gollwitzer (1999, American Psychologist) distinguished goal intentions ("I intend to do X") from implementation intentions, "if-then" plans that link a situation to a response: "If it is 7 am on a weekday, then I will walk for 20 minutes around the park." Such plans delegate control to situational cues and help close the intention-behaviour gap. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's (2006) meta-analysis is often cited for a medium-to-large effect.

4. Types of brain scanning and imaging tests

Clinical psychology entrance papers ask about brain imaging in two ways: "which technique measures what" and "who invented it, when". To answer both you need a small set of organising ideas first.

4.1 The organising ideas

Structural vs functional imaging. Structural (anatomical) methods show what the brain looks like: size and shape of regions, tumours, strokes, atrophy, enlarged ventricles. CT, structural MRI and DTI are structural. Functional methods show what the brain is doing: electrical activity, blood flow, oxygenation, glucose metabolism, receptor binding. EEG, MEG, fMRI, PET, SPECT and fNIRS are functional. The question to ask is: does the image change when the person changes what they are thinking? If yes, it is functional.

Spatial vs temporal resolution. Spatial resolution is how precisely you can say where something is (millimetres). Temporal resolution is how precisely you can say when it happened (milliseconds to minutes). Methods that measure electrical or magnetic signals directly (EEG, MEG) are fast (milliseconds) but poor at localisation. Methods that measure blood flow or metabolism (fMRI, PET, SPECT, fNIRS) are slow, because blood responds over seconds, but fMRI and PET localise better. This is the spatial-temporal trade-off, and it is the most examined idea in this topic.

Invasive vs non-invasive. Invasive methods break the skin or enter the body: injecting a radioactive tracer (PET, SPECT), injecting contrast through an arterial catheter (angiography), or placing electrodes on or in the brain (intracranial recording). EEG, MEG, MRI, fMRI and fNIRS are non-invasive.

Ionising radiation. X-rays and gamma rays carry enough energy to knock electrons off atoms and can damage DNA, so exposure is limited, especially in children and pregnancy. CT and angiography use X-rays; PET and SPECT use gamma-emitting radioactive tracers. MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves, and EEG, MEG and fNIRS use no ionising radiation at all.

4.3 Computed tomography (CT / CAT)

FeatureCT
Full nameComputed tomography; older name computerised axial tomography (CAT scan)
Inventor / key people and yearAllan Cormack developed the mathematics in papers of 1963 and 1964; Godfrey Hounsfield built the scanner at EMI, and the first clinical brain scan was performed on 1 October 1971 at Atkinson Morley's Hospital, London (with radiologist James Ambrose). Hounsfield and Cormack shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine
What it measures, physical principleAn X-ray tube rotates around the head; detectors measure how much the beam is attenuated along many angles; a computer reconstructs cross-sectional slices from these projections. Brightness reflects tissue density (X-ray attenuation)
Structural or functionalStructural (perfusion CT adds limited functional information)
Spatial resolutionGood, roughly 0.5 to 1 mm in modern scanners; poorer soft-tissue contrast than MRI
Temporal resolutionNot used for brain activity; a head scan takes seconds
Radiation / invasiveIonising radiation (X-rays); non-invasive unless iodinated contrast is injected
Clinical and research usesFirst-line in acute stroke (to exclude haemorrhage before thrombolysis), head injury, acute haemorrhage, skull fractures, calcification, hydrocephalus; useful when MRI is contraindicated
LimitsRadiation; poor for posterior fossa (bone artefact), small lesions, early ischaemic stroke and white matter disease; poorer grey/white matter contrast than MRI

Hounsfield units (HU) put attenuation on a fixed scale: water = 0, air = -1000, dense bone roughly +1000 (values for bone vary, roughly +400 to above +1000). Fat is negative (around -100), CSF near 0, brain around +20 to +40. Acute blood is hyperdense (bright, roughly +50 to +80 HU, values approximate) because clotted blood is concentrated; over days to weeks it becomes isodense and then hypodense. An acute ischaemic infarct may be invisible on CT in the first hours, then appears hypodense (dark). This is why CT in stroke is used mainly to rule out bleeding.

Memory hook

On CT, "white is dense": bone and fresh blood bright, air and CSF dark. CT = X-rays; "CAT" is just the old name for the same scan.

4.4 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

FeatureMRI
Full nameMagnetic resonance imaging (originally nuclear magnetic resonance imaging; "nuclear" was dropped to avoid confusion with radioactivity)
Inventor / key people and yearNMR physics: Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell (1946). Raymond Damadian reported in 1971 (Science) that tumour tissue has different relaxation times and claimed priority for the idea of MR scanning. Paul Lauterbur published the first MR image using magnetic field gradients in 1973 (Nature); Peter Mansfield developed the mathematics for fast imaging and echo-planar imaging (1977). Lauterbur and Mansfield shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine; Damadian's exclusion was publicly contested
What it measures, physical principleHydrogen nuclei (protons, mostly in water and fat) align in a strong magnetic field (typically 1.5 or 3 tesla). A radiofrequency pulse tips them; as they relax back they emit a radio signal. Different tissues relax at different rates (T1, T2), and gradients encode position
Structural or functionalStructural (fMRI, DTI and MRS are special MRI techniques)
Spatial resolutionVery good, about 1 mm routinely, sub-millimetre at high field
Temporal resolutionNot applicable for structural scans (a sequence takes minutes)
Radiation / invasiveNo ionising radiation; non-invasive (gadolinium contrast optional)
Clinical and research usesTumours, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy foci (e.g. hippocampal sclerosis), posterior fossa, dementia (hippocampal atrophy), early stroke (diffusion-weighted imaging), volumetric research in psychiatry
LimitsContraindicated with pacemakers (unless MR-conditional), cochlear implants, ferromagnetic aneurysm clips, metallic foreign bodies (e.g. in the eye); noisy, claustrophobic, slow, expensive; patient must stay still

T1 vs T2. On T1-weighted images CSF is dark, fat is bright and white matter is brighter than grey matter: T1 looks like the anatomy textbook and is used for anatomy and volumetric measurement. On T2-weighted images CSF is bright and most pathology (oedema, inflammation, demyelination, tumour) is bright because it contains extra water: T2 is used to find lesions. FLAIR (fluid-attenuated inversion recovery) is a T2-type image with the CSF signal suppressed, so CSF is dark while lesions stay bright; this makes periventricular multiple sclerosis plaques easy to see. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) shows an acute ischaemic infarct within minutes, far earlier than CT.

Memory hook

"T2 = H2O bright": water (CSF) is white on T2. "World War 2": water white on T2.

4.5 Functional MRI (fMRI)

FeaturefMRI
Full nameFunctional magnetic resonance imaging, usually BOLD fMRI
Inventor / key people and yearSeiji Ogawa described blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) contrast in 1990 (rat studies, PNAS). John Belliveau published a human functional MRI study using injected contrast in 1991; the first human BOLD studies appeared in 1992 (groups including Kenneth Kwong, Ogawa and Peter Bandettini)
What it measures, physical principleDeoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic and distorts the local magnetic field, lowering the MR signal; oxyhaemoglobin is diamagnetic and does not. When neurons become active, blood flow increases more than oxygen use, so the local concentration of deoxyhaemoglobin falls and the signal rises
Structural or functionalFunctional
Spatial resolutionGood, about 1 to 3 mm
Temporal resolutionModerate, seconds; the haemodynamic response peaks about 4 to 6 s after neural activity and lasts 10 to 20 s
Radiation / invasiveNo radiation, no injection; non-invasive
Clinical and research usesCognitive neuroscience; pre-surgical mapping of language and motor areas; resting-state connectivity in psychiatric disorders
LimitsIndirect measure (blood, not neurons); slow; sensitive to head motion; noisy scanner; same contraindications as MRI; results are relative (task vs baseline), not absolute

Why "indirect"? fMRI does not record neurons firing. It records a vascular response that follows neural activity. Nikos Logothetis and colleagues (2001) found the BOLD signal tracks local field potentials (synaptic input and local processing) better than spiking output. The haemodynamic response function may show a small initial dip, a peak at about 4 to 6 s, and a post-stimulus undershoot.

Designs. In a block design the participant alternates long blocks (e.g. 20 to 30 s) of task and rest; it gives strong signal. In an event-related design brief single trials are presented and the response to each is modelled; this allows randomised trials and sorting by behaviour (e.g. remembered vs forgotten items). Resting-state fMRI measures spontaneous slow fluctuations while the person lies quietly: Bharat Biswal (1995) showed that left and right motor cortices fluctuate together at rest. Marcus Raichle and colleagues (2001, PNAS, "A default mode of brain function") named the default mode network (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate/precuneus, inferior parietal lobule), which is more active at rest and during self-referential thought and deactivates during external tasks. DMN abnormalities are reported in depression, schizophrenia, autism and Alzheimer's disease.

Exam trap

fMRI measures blood oxygenation (the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated haemoglobin), not neuronal firing, glucose use or electrical activity. And it is deoxyhaemoglobin that is paramagnetic.

4.6 Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)

FeatureDTI
Full nameDiffusion tensor imaging (a form of diffusion MRI)
Inventor / key people and yearPeter Basser, James Mattiello and Denis Le Bihan, 1994; Le Bihan had introduced diffusion MRI in the mid 1980s
What it measures, physical principleWater molecules diffuse more easily along axons than across them, because myelin and membranes restrict movement. DTI measures the direction and degree of this diffusion in each voxel
Structural or functionalStructural (white matter microstructure and connectivity)
Spatial resolutionAbout 2 mm
Temporal resolutionNot applicable
Radiation / invasiveNone; non-invasive
Clinical and research usesWhite matter integrity in traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, schizophrenia, ageing; surgical planning to avoid major tracts
LimitsCrossing fibres confuse the tensor model; tractography can produce false tracts; cannot show direction of signal flow

Fractional anisotropy (FA) ranges from 0 (diffusion equal in all directions, isotropic, as in CSF) to 1 (diffusion in a single direction). High FA indicates organised, intact white matter (e.g. corpus callosum); reduced FA is reported in many disorders. Tractography follows the principal diffusion direction from voxel to voxel to reconstruct fibre pathways such as the arcuate fasciculus.

4.7 Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)

MRS uses the same scanner but, instead of an image, produces a spectrum showing the concentration of chemicals in a chosen region (a "voxel"). Common peaks: N-acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of neuronal integrity that falls with neuronal loss; choline (membrane turnover, raised in tumours); creatine (energy metabolism, used as a reference); myo-inositol (glial marker); lactate (anaerobic metabolism); and glutamate/glutamine (often reported together as Glx) and GABA with special sequences. It is non-invasive, has no radiation, and is used in psychiatry research (e.g. glutamate in schizophrenia, GABA in depression). Its limits are low spatial resolution (voxels of a few cubic centimetres) and low signal.

4.8 Positron emission tomography (PET)

FeaturePET
Full namePositron emission tomography
Inventor / key people and yearEarlier positron imaging by Gordon Brownell and William Sweet (1950s, Boston). The modern tomographic PET scanner was developed at Washington University, St Louis, by Michael Phelps, Edward Hoffman and Michel Ter-Pogossian, 1973 to 1975. 18F-FDG was synthesised at Brookhaven by Tatsuo Ido and Alfred (Al) Wolf with colleagues, about 1976 to 1978 (sources differ on the exact year); the first human FDG brain images were made at the University of Pennsylvania (Abass Alavi, Martin Reivich) in 1976. FDG built on Louis Sokoloff's deoxyglucose method (published 1977)
What it measures, physical principleA tracer labelled with a positron-emitting isotope is injected. Each positron travels a millimetre or so, meets an electron and annihilates, producing two 511 keV gamma photons travelling in opposite directions. Detectors in a ring register both photons simultaneously (coincidence detection) and the computer locates the source along that line
Structural or functionalFunctional (metabolism, blood flow, receptor binding, protein deposits)
Spatial resolutionModerate, about 4 to 5 mm clinically (better in research scanners)
Temporal resolutionPoor, tens of seconds (water) to many minutes (FDG uptake takes about 30 to 45 minutes)
Radiation / invasiveIonising radiation; invasive (intravenous injection of a radioactive tracer)
Clinical and research usesDementia (temporoparietal hypometabolism in Alzheimer's disease), epilepsy focus (interictal hypometabolism), tumours; receptor studies, e.g. 11C-raclopride for dopamine D2/D3 receptors and dopamine release, and amyloid PET (Pittsburgh compound B, reported in 2004) and tau PET
LimitsRadiation limits repeat scans; short-lived isotopes (15O about 2 min, 11C about 20 min, 18F about 110 min) often need an on-site cyclotron; expensive; poor temporal resolution

FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose) is a glucose analogue: it is taken up by active cells and phosphorylated but cannot be metabolised further, so it becomes trapped, and its concentration reflects regional glucose metabolism. With 15O-labelled water, PET measures regional cerebral blood flow and was the main tool for cognitive activation studies in the 1980s and early 1990s before fMRI.

4.9 Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)

FeatureSPECT
Full nameSingle photon emission computed tomography (also SPET)
Inventor / key people and yearDavid Kuhl and Roy Edwards, University of Pennsylvania, early 1960s (emission tomography reported around 1963)
What it measures, physical principleA tracer emitting single gamma photons is injected; a gamma camera rotating around the head records them, and tomographic slices are reconstructed. No positrons, no annihilation, no coincidence detection
Structural or functionalFunctional (mainly regional cerebral blood flow; also dopamine transporter)
Spatial resolutionLower than PET, roughly 8 to 10 mm
Temporal resolutionPoor (minutes); but because HMPAO is fixed in the brain within minutes of injection, it gives a "snapshot" of flow at the moment of injection, useful for injecting during a seizure
Radiation / invasiveIonising radiation; invasive (injection)
Clinical and research usesRegional cerebral blood flow with 99mTc-HMPAO or 99mTc-ECD; ictal SPECT in epilepsy; dementia differential; DaTscan (123I-ioflupane) images the dopamine transporter to distinguish Parkinsonian syndromes and dementia with Lewy bodies from essential tremor or Alzheimer's disease

SPECT is cheaper and more widely available than PET because its isotopes (technetium-99m, half-life about 6 hours, from a generator) last longer and do not need an on-site cyclotron. That is why it is common in hospitals that cannot afford PET.

Exam trap

PET = positrons, which annihilate into two photons (511 keV each). SPECT = single photons. PET has better resolution; SPECT is cheaper. Both use radioactive tracers.

4.10 Electroencephalography (EEG) and event-related potentials (ERPs)

FeatureEEG
Full nameElectroencephalography
Inventor / key people and yearRichard Caton recorded electrical activity from animal brains in 1875. Hans Berger, Jena, made the first human recording in 1924 and published in 1929 ("Über das Elektrenkephalogramm des Menschen"); alpha is still called the "Berger rhythm". The 10-20 electrode system was introduced by Herbert Jasper in 1958
What it measures, physical principleScalp electrodes record voltage differences produced by the summed postsynaptic potentials of large populations of synchronously active, parallel-oriented cortical pyramidal neurons
Structural or functionalFunctional
Spatial resolutionPoor (centimetres); the skull smears the signal and the "inverse problem" means many source configurations fit the same scalp pattern
Temporal resolutionExcellent, milliseconds
Radiation / invasiveNone; non-invasive; cheap and portable
Clinical and research usesEpilepsy diagnosis, sleep staging (polysomnography), encephalopathy and delirium (diffuse slowing), brain death assessment, monitoring anaesthesia; ERP research on attention, memory and language
LimitsPoor localisation; mainly sees superficial cortex; artefacts from eye movements and muscle

Rhythms (boundaries vary slightly between sources):

  • Delta, about 0.5 to 4 Hz: deep (slow-wave) sleep, infants; in an awake adult suggests pathology.
  • Theta, about 4 to 8 Hz: drowsiness, light sleep, children; frontal midline theta during concentration.
  • Alpha, about 8 to 13 Hz: relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed, maximal over occipital regions; blocked by eye opening (alpha blocking).
  • Beta, about 13 to 30 Hz: alert, active thinking; increased by benzodiazepines.
  • Gamma, above about 30 Hz: feature binding, perception.

In the 10-20 system electrodes are placed at 10% or 20% of the distance between skull landmarks (nasion, inion, preauricular points); letters denote region (Fp, F, C, T, P, O), odd numbers the left hemisphere, even numbers the right, and "z" the midline (Fz, Cz, Pz).

ERPs are small voltage changes time-locked to a stimulus, extracted by averaging many trials. Key components:

  • Contingent negative variation (CNV): W. Grey Walter and colleagues (1964), a slow negative shift between a warning signal and an imperative stimulus, reflecting expectancy and preparation.
  • P300 (P3): Samuel Sutton, Margery Braren, Joseph Zubin and E. Roy John (1965), a positive wave about 300 ms after a rare, task-relevant stimulus in an oddball paradigm; reflects context updating; reduced amplitude in schizophrenia.
  • Mismatch negativity (MMN): Risto Näätänen, Anthony Gaillard and Sirkka Mäntysalo (1978), an automatic response to a deviant sound in a repetitive sequence, even without attention; reduced in schizophrenia.
  • N400: Marta Kutas and Steven Hillyard (1980), a negativity about 400 ms after a semantically unexpected word ("He spread the warm bread with socks").

4.11 Magnetoencephalography (MEG)

FeatureMEG
Full nameMagnetoencephalography
Inventor / key people and yearDavid Cohen, 1968 (first recording of the alpha rhythm's magnetic field, with an induction coil); in 1972 Cohen recorded with a SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device, developed by James Zimmerman and colleagues)
What it measures, physical principleThe same neuronal currents that produce EEG also produce tiny magnetic fields (femtotesla range, billions of times weaker than the Earth's field). SQUID sensors cooled in liquid helium detect them inside a magnetically shielded room

4.13 Not imaging: TMS, the lesion method and intracranial recording

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) was introduced by Anthony Barker and colleagues in Sheffield in 1985. A coil held over the scalp produces a brief, strong magnetic pulse that induces an electrical current in the cortex underneath, depolarising neurons. Single pulses over motor cortex cause a muscle twitch (motor evoked potential); repetitive TMS (rTMS) can raise or lower cortical excitability for a while. It stimulates the brain; it does not image it. Its research value is causal: a temporary "virtual lesion" tests whether a region is necessary for a task. Clinically, rTMS over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is approved for treatment-resistant depression (US FDA clearance 2008), and it is used in Indian centres including NIMHANS (details of individual services not verified here).

The lesion method infers function from what is lost after brain damage (Paul Broca's patient "Tan", 1861; Phineas Gage, 1848; H.M., 1953). It is causal but depends on accidents of nature, and lesions rarely respect functional boundaries. Intracranial recording (electrocorticography from grids on the cortex, or depth electrodes, in epilepsy surgery patients) gives both excellent temporal and excellent spatial resolution but is highly invasive and done only for clinical reasons.

Exam trap

TMS uses a magnet, like MRI and MEG, but it is a stimulation technique, not an imaging technique. If asked which option is "not a neuroimaging method", TMS (or ECT, or tDCS) is the answer.

4.15 Imaging in psychiatry

  • Schizophrenia: enlarged lateral ventricles. Eve Johnstone, Timothy Crow, Christopher Frith and colleagues (1976, The Lancet) showed on CT that chronic schizophrenia patients had larger lateral ventricles than controls. This was the first CT demonstration and revived interest in brain structure in schizophrenia (pneumoencephalography studies from 1927 had hinted at it). Later MRI work added reduced hippocampal, temporal and grey matter volume.
  • Hypofrontality. Reduced frontal activity in schizophrenia was first described by David Ingvar and Göran Franzén (1974) using xenon-133 regional cerebral blood flow, before PET; it was later reported in PET studies (e.g. Monte Buchsbaum and colleagues, early 1980s) and in fMRI during working memory tasks, though findings are inconsistent.
  • Dopamine. PET with raclopride showed increased amphetamine-induced dopamine release in schizophrenia (mid-1990s studies) and is used to estimate antipsychotic D2 occupancy (therapeutic window about 65% to 80%, commonly cited).
  • OCD. PET studies (Lewis Baxter and colleagues, 1987) reported hypermetabolism in the orbitofrontal cortex and caudate nucleus, which decreased after successful treatment with medication or behaviour therapy; this supports the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit model.
  • Depression. Reduced activity in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and altered subgenual cingulate activity (target of deep brain stimulation by Helen Mayberg, 2005).
  • Dementia. MRI hippocampal atrophy and FDG-PET temporoparietal hypometabolism in Alzheimer's disease; amyloid PET to detect plaques.

4.16 Exam traps

Exam trap

CT uses X-rays, not magnets. CAT = computerised axial tomography = CT (same scan).

MRI uses no ionising radiation (magnetic field plus radio waves). Its danger is metal, not radiation.

fMRI measures blood oxygenation, not neuronal firing directly. PET-FDG measures glucose metabolism.

PET uses positrons (two 511 keV photons); SPECT uses single photons.

EEG: best temporal, poor spatial. fMRI: better spatial, poor temporal.

MEG vs EEG: both milliseconds; MEG localises better and is less distorted by the skull, but sees mainly tangential sources and is far costlier.

TMS is not an imaging technique.

Angiography needs contrast dye. Egas Moniz: angiography 1927, Nobel 1949 for leucotomy.

Nobel pairings: CT = Hounsfield and Cormack (1979); MRI = Lauterbur and Mansfield (2003), not Damadian.

Berger: first human EEG 1924, published 1929. Do not confuse the two years.

T1: CSF dark. T2: CSF bright. FLAIR: T2 with CSF made dark.

4.17 Self-check

  1. Which technique has the best temporal resolution?
    (a) PET
    (b) fMRI
    (c) EEG
    (d) SPECT
    Answer: (c). EEG records electrical activity in milliseconds; the others depend on slow haemodynamic or metabolic changes.
  2. The BOLD signal in fMRI depends mainly on the magnetic properties of:
    (a) Oxyhaemoglobin
    (b) Deoxyhaemoglobin
    (c) Glucose
    (d) Gadolinium
    Answer: (b). Deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic; its local decrease raises the signal.
  3. The first clinical CT scan was performed by:
    (a) Paul Lauterbur, 1973
    (b) Godfrey Hounsfield, 1971
    (c) Egas Moniz, 1927
    (d) Michael Phelps, 1975
    Answer: (b). At Atkinson Morley's Hospital, London, in 1971.
  4. On a T2-weighted MRI, cerebrospinal fluid appears:
    (a) Dark
    (b) Bright
    (c) Invisible
    (d) Same as bone
    Answer: (b). Water is bright on T2; dark on T1 and on FLAIR.
  5. Which of the following involves no ionising radiation?
    (a) CT
    (b) PET
    (c) SPECT
    (d) MRI
    Answer: (d). MRI uses a magnetic field and radiofrequency pulses.
  6. Coincidence detection of two 511 keV photons is the basis of:
    (a) SPECT
    (b) PET
    (c) MEG
    (d) CT
    Answer: (b). Positron annihilation yields two photons travelling in opposite directions.
  7. Fractional anisotropy is a measure derived from:
    (a) MRS
    (b) DTI
    (c) fNIRS
    (d) ERP
    Answer: (b). FA indexes directional water diffusion, mostly reflecting white matter organisation.
  8. Which of these is NOT a neuroimaging technique?
    (a) MEG
    (b) fNIRS
    (c) TMS
    (d) SPECT
    Answer: (c). TMS (Barker, 1985) stimulates cortex rather than recording or imaging it.
  9. The P300 component was first described by:
    (a) Kutas and Hillyard, 1980
    (b) Sutton and colleagues, 1965
    (c) Näätänen and colleagues, 1978
    (d) Grey Walter and colleagues, 1964
    Answer: (b). Kutas and Hillyard described the N400, Näätänen the MMN, Grey Walter the CNV.
  10. Enlarged lateral ventricles in schizophrenia were first demonstrated on CT by:
    (a) Johnstone and colleagues, 1976
    (b) Ingvar and Franzén, 1974
    (c) Baxter and colleagues, 1987
    (d) Raichle and colleagues, 2001
    Answer: (a). Ingvar and Franzén described hypofrontality, Baxter the OCD PET findings, Raichle the default mode network.
