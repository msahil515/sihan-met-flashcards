1. Approaches to human cognition

Cognitive psychology is the study of the internal processes that stand between a stimulus arriving and a response coming out: attending, perceiving, recognising, remembering, understanding language, reasoning, deciding. The word that does the work in that definition is internal. Nobody has ever observed a memory trace or a decision rule directly. What is observed is that someone was shown a word list and later recalled eleven of the twenty items, or that they named a picture 80 milliseconds faster when it had been primed. Everything cognitive psychology claims about the machinery in between is an inference from such measurements, and the whole methodological argument of this chapter is about which kinds of inference are trustworthy.

That is why Eysenck and Keane open not with content but with four approaches. Each is a different way of getting evidence about an invisible process, each has a characteristic strength, and each has a characteristic blind spot. When two researchers disagree about, say, whether there are two visual systems or one, the disagreement is very often not about the data at all: it is about which approach's data should be allowed to settle the matter. Get the four straight now and the rest of the book stops looking like a list of controversies.

The historical starting point: behaviourism and why it broke

From roughly 1920 to 1950, North American psychology was dominated by behaviourism, whose founding claim (John B. Watson, 1913) was that psychology should confine itself to observable stimuli and observable responses, because anything in between is unobservable and therefore unscientific. This was a genuinely reasonable position at the time. Introspection, the main alternative, had produced decades of disputes that no experiment could settle: when Wundt's laboratory and the Würzburg school disagreed about whether thought could be imageless, there was no way to check.

Behaviourism broke on several problems at once. Edward Tolman's rats learned the layout of a maze while being rewarded for nothing (latent learning), which meant learning had happened with no reinforced response to point to; the rats had built something like a cognitive map. Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior argued that language could not be a chain of reinforced responses, because speakers routinely produce and understand sentences that they have never heard and that have therefore never been reinforced. And the arrival of the digital computer supplied something behaviourism had lacked: a way of talking about internal processing that was not mystical. A computer plainly has internal states, plainly transforms input into output through a sequence of operations, and is plainly a physical machine. If that is respectable for a computer, it can be respectable for a person.

High-yield

The cognitive revolution is usually dated to the mid-1950s and given three landmarks: Chomsky's generative grammar and his 1959 demolition of Skinner; Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist, the first program to prove theorems in the way a human might; and Broadbent's filter theory of attention (1958), the first flow-diagram model of a mental process. Miller's "magical number seven" paper (1956) belongs on the same list.

Approach 1: experimental cognitive psychology

This is the oldest of the four and still the default. Its method is to run behavioural experiments on healthy participants and infer the internal processing from the pattern of results, usually reaction times and error rates. The theoretical framework it grew up inside is the information-processing approach: the mind is treated as a series of stages, each taking an input, doing something to it, and passing the result on. Stimulus, then attention, then perception, then thought, then decision, then response.

That early version carried two strong assumptions worth naming, because most of the subsequent history of the field consists of weakening them. The first is seriality: one stage at a time, each finishing before the next begins. The second is that processing is bottom-up (also called stimulus-driven), meaning it is determined by the input rather than by what the perceiver already knows. Both turned out to be too strong. Processing is now generally accepted to be substantially parallel, with stages overlapping in time, and heavily top-down: expectations, context and prior knowledge shape perception rather than merely decorating it. Read a smudged handwritten word in a sentence and you will read it correctly because the sentence told you what to expect; that is top-down processing doing visible work.

The great strength of this approach is control. You can manipulate one variable, hold everything else fixed, and draw a causal conclusion. Its great weakness is that reaction times are a very indirect readout. Two quite different architectures can predict the same millisecond pattern, so behavioural data frequently underdetermine the theory. A related problem is paradigm specificity: a finding may hold for the particular laboratory task used and not generalise, which is a serious charge when the task is something like naming a letter in a briefly flashed array, an activity nobody performs outside a laboratory. Eysenck and Keane also flag lack of ecological validity and, more subtly, the fact that theories in this tradition are often stated vaguely enough that they are hard to falsify.

Approach 2: cognitive neuropsychology

Cognitive neuropsychology studies patients with brain damage in order to work out the structure of normal cognition. The logic is not "find out what the damaged area does"; it is subtler than that, and the difference matters. The reasoning runs: if brain damage can knock out ability A while leaving ability B intact, then A and B cannot depend on exactly the same machinery. Damage is being used as a natural experiment that cuts the system apart at joints an intact brain never reveals.

Four theoretical assumptions underlie it, and they are examinable as a set. Modularity: the cognitive system consists of relatively independent modules or processors, each doing one job, so that one can be damaged without the others going down. Anatomical modularity: each module is localised in a reasonably circumscribed brain region, which is what makes brain damage selective rather than global. Uniformity of functional architecture across people: the patient's cognitive system was organised like yours before the damage, otherwise nothing generalises. Subtractivity: brain damage impairs or removes processes but does not cause the system to develop new ones, so the patient is the normal system minus a part, not a different system.

Single and double dissociation

A single dissociation is one patient who is impaired on task A but normal on task B. It is weak evidence on its own, because task A might simply be harder, and a general loss of resources would produce the same picture. A double dissociation is two patients showing the opposite pattern: patient 1 impaired on A but not B, patient 2 impaired on B but not A. Task difficulty can no longer explain both, so the two tasks must draw on at least partly separate mechanisms. This is the single most powerful inferential tool in neuropsychology and it recurs throughout the book: amnesia (episodic memory impaired, procedural learning intact) against Huntington's or Parkinson's patients (the reverse); Broca's aphasia (production impaired, comprehension relatively spared) against Wernicke's (the reverse); prosopagnosia (faces impaired, objects spared) against visual object agnosia.

The limitations are equally important. Brain damage from stroke, tumour or trauma does not respect module boundaries, so patients almost always have several impairments and it is a matter of judgement which is the one of interest. Patients also compensate: by the time they are tested, months may have passed and they may be performing the task by an unusual route, which straightforwardly violates subtractivity. Numbers are small, often a single case, which makes generalisation delicate. And the whole enterprise presupposes modularity, which is at best partly true; a great deal of cognition looks distributed rather than modular, and for those parts the method has little purchase.

Approach 3: cognitive neuroscience

Cognitive neuroscience uses evidence from the brain, in healthy people, to constrain theories of cognition. It is best understood as a toolbox, and the single most useful thing to hold in your head is the trade-off between spatial and temporal resolution, because it explains why researchers use several techniques on the same question.

Single-unit recording pushes a microelectrode next to a single neuron and records its firing. Resolution is superb in both space (one cell) and time (milliseconds), but it is invasive and therefore almost entirely an animal method. EEG records electrical activity from scalp electrodes; averaging the EEG over many trials time-locked to a stimulus yields event-related potentials (ERPs), whose temporal resolution is of the order of a millisecond but whose spatial resolution is poor, because the inverse problem (working back from a scalp voltage pattern to the generators inside) has no unique solution. MEG records the magnetic fields produced by the same neural currents; magnetic fields are less distorted by skull and scalp than electrical ones, so MEG buys somewhat better spatial resolution while keeping the millisecond timing.

PET and fMRI work the other way round. fMRI measures the BOLD signal (blood oxygen level dependent): active neurons consume oxygen, local blood flow over-compensates, and the resulting change in the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated haemoglobin is detectable magnetically. Spatial resolution is good, around a millimetre, but the haemodynamic response is sluggish, peaking several seconds after the neural activity, so temporal resolution is measured in seconds. fMRI tells you where with confidence and when only crudely; ERP tells you when with confidence and where only crudely. Used together they complement each other, which is exactly why they are used together.

Two further techniques are worth naming. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) delivers a brief magnetic pulse through the skull that transiently disrupts processing in the cortex beneath, creating what is often called a "virtual lesion". This matters because it converts correlation into causation: fMRI can only show that a region is active during a task, whereas TMS can show that interfering with the region impairs the task. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) tracks the diffusion of water along white-matter tracts and so maps connections rather than regions, which suits the increasingly common view that cognition depends on networks rather than isolated areas.

Trap

The standard criticism of neuroimaging is not that it is inaccurate but that it is often merely correlational and merely locational. Showing that the fusiform gyrus is active during face processing does not by itself decide any psychological question. There is also the reverse-inference fallacy: observing amygdala activity and concluding the participant was afraid is invalid, because the amygdala is active in many conditions besides fear. Cognitive neuroscience earns its keep when a brain measure decides between two psychological theories that make identical behavioural predictions, and not when it simply adds a colourful picture to a result already established behaviourally.

Approach 4: computational cognitive science

The fourth approach builds computational models: programs whose behaviour is compared with human behaviour. The discipline this imposes is severe and is the main argument for the approach. A verbal theory can say "the word is activated by context" and get away with it; a program cannot, because activated by how much, from what, on what time course, and what happens when two contexts conflict must all be specified before it will run at all. Vagueness that survives in prose dies in code.

Two families dominate. Production-system architectures such as ACT-R (John Anderson) represent knowledge as IF-THEN rules operating on a declarative memory, and are strong on complex, strategic, multi-step tasks such as problem solving and skill acquisition. Connectionist networks represent knowledge as patterns of activation across simple units joined by weighted connections; nothing is stored at an address, and knowledge lives in the weights. Connectionist models learn by gradually adjusting those weights (typically by back-propagation), degrade gracefully when units are removed rather than failing outright, and generalise to new inputs automatically because similar inputs produce similar activation patterns. Those three properties are also properties of human cognition, which is the substantive argument in their favour.

A newer and increasingly important framework is the Bayesian or predictive-processing one, associated with Karl Friston's free-energy formulation and with Andy Clark's exposition of it. Here the brain is not a passive stimulus processor but a prediction machine: it continually generates top-down predictions about incoming input and only the prediction error, the mismatch, is passed forward for further processing. This turns the classic bottom-up picture upside down and provides a principled account of why top-down effects are everywhere rather than being occasional exceptions. Keep it in mind for the perception chapters, where it reappears constantly.

The limitations of computational modelling are real. A model that fits the data does not thereby prove the human system works that way, since other models may fit equally well; models typically have free parameters that can be tuned until they fit, which weakens the evidential force of the fit; and models are usually built for one narrow task rather than for cognition as a whole.

Comparing the four, and why nobody wins

Set the four side by side and their strengths are close to complementary. Experimental cognitive psychology gives tight causal control over behaviour but indirect evidence about mechanism. Cognitive neuropsychology gives strong dissociative evidence about the separability of components but depends on messy lesions and small samples. Cognitive neuroscience gives direct measures of the brain but has to resist the temptation to treat a location as an explanation. Computational modelling forces precision but at the price of narrowness and free parameters.

What follows methodologically is converging operations: a claim is credible when it is supported from more than one of these directions at once. The clearest case in this book is the two-visual-systems theory of Chapter 2. It was proposed on behavioural and anatomical grounds, then supported by a double dissociation between two patients (DF and the optic-ataxia cases), then localised by neuroimaging, then made causal by TMS to the relevant parietal region. No single one of those would have been decisive. Together they are hard to dismiss, and that is the pattern the good arguments in the rest of the book follow.

Trap

Do not treat the four approaches as a historical sequence in which each replaced the last. They are contemporaneous and all four are in active use. An exam answer that says cognitive neuroscience superseded experimental cognitive psychology has the relationship wrong: the experiments still supply the tasks, and without a decent behavioural paradigm there is nothing worth imaging.

Worked example

Take a concrete question and push it through all four approaches: is recognising a face done by the same machinery as recognising a chair?

Experimental cognitive psychology notes that faces suffer far more from inversion than objects do (the face-inversion effect) and that the parts of a face are hard to judge in isolation (the composite effect), suggesting faces are processed holistically while objects are processed more part by part. Strong control, but everything rests on inferring a mechanism from a reaction-time difference.

Cognitive neuropsychology supplies the double dissociation. Patients with prosopagnosia cannot recognise familiar faces, sometimes including their own in a mirror, while recognising objects normally; other patients with visual object agnosia show the reverse. Difficulty cannot explain both directions, so at least partly separate mechanisms exist.

Cognitive neuroscience localises it: the fusiform face area responds far more to faces than to objects, and ERP work finds the face-selective N170 component at around 170 ms, telling you the separation happens early rather than at a late decision stage. TMS over the relevant occipitotemporal site selectively impairs face discrimination, which upgrades the claim from correlation to causation.

Computational modelling asks what "holistic" would actually mean in a working system, and shows that a network trained to discriminate many individuals within one visually homogeneous category naturally develops representations sensitive to the whole configuration rather than to parts, which explains why faces would be special without having to stipulate a face module by fiat.

The answer that survives all four is not "there is a face module" but something more careful: face recognition depends on configural or holistic processing that object recognition uses much less, this shows up early, it can be lost selectively, and it may arise from expertise with a homogeneous category rather than from a genetically specified face box. Notice that each approach on its own would have supported a cruder conclusion. That is the argument for using all four.

2. Basic processes in visual perception

Vision feels effortless and immediate, and that feeling is the main obstacle to studying it. What actually arrives at the eye is a two-dimensional, constantly moving, ambiguous pattern of light intensities; what is experienced is a stable three-dimensional world of coloured objects at definite distances. Everything in between has to be constructed, and this chapter is about how the construction is done and where it can be caught out.

One framing point before the machinery. David Marr's influential analysis proposed that any information-processing system can be described at three levels: the computational level (what problem is being solved and why), the algorithmic level (what representations and procedures solve it), and the implementational level (how that is realised in neurons). Much apparent disagreement in perception research is disagreement across levels rather than within one, and Marr's point was that a complete account needs all three.

Vision and the brain: the route from retina to cortex

Light reaches the retina, where two classes of photoreceptor transduce it. Cones are concentrated in the fovea, need good light, and support colour vision and fine detail. Rods dominate the periphery, work in dim light, and are colour-blind. The functional consequence is that acuity and colour are excellent where you are looking and poor everywhere else, which is why the eye must move constantly; visual experience feels uniformly detailed only because you never catch the periphery being vague.

From the retina, ganglion cell axons form the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm the fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross, with the result that everything in the left visual field, from both eyes, ends up in the right hemisphere and vice versa. Most fibres then synapse in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus and project to primary visual cortex (V1), also called the striate cortex, at the occipital pole. This is the retino-geniculate-striate pathway and it carries the great majority of the traffic. A smaller retinotectal pathway runs via the superior colliculus, and it matters more than its size suggests, because it is the route that survives V1 damage and is the leading explanation of blindsight later in this chapter.

Two channels run in parallel within the main pathway, distinguishable from the retina onwards. The parvocellular (P) channel has small, colour-sensitive cells with fine spatial resolution and sluggish responses; it carries detail and colour. The magnocellular (M) channel has large, colour-blind cells with coarse spatial resolution and fast, transient responses; it carries motion and rapid change. Hold this distinction: it maps roughly (not perfectly) onto the two cortical streams that dominate the rest of the chapter.

High-yield

Hubel and Wiesel's single-unit recordings in cat and monkey V1 (Nobel Prize 1981) identified cells tuned not to spots of light but to oriented edges. Simple cells respond to a bar of a particular orientation at a particular retinal position. Complex cells respond to the same orientation anywhere within a larger region, and often prefer a direction of movement. The architecture is columnar: cells in a vertical column share an orientation preference, and a hypercolumn contains a full set of orientations plus both ocular-dominance columns for one patch of the visual field. The theoretical significance is that the very first cortical stage is already extracting features rather than transmitting a picture, and it does so with position invariance increasing as you ascend.

Beyond V1, processing becomes increasingly specialised: V4 is strongly implicated in colour and in shape, and V5/MT in motion. The evidence for functional specialisation is not merely correlational. Damage to V4 can produce achromatopsia, in which the world is seen in shades of grey while form and motion are intact; damage to V5/MT can produce akinetopsia, in which motion perception is lost while colour and form survive. The best-documented akinetopsia case, patient LM, described the world as a series of still snapshots, saw the coffee appear to freeze in the air while pouring, and found crossing roads dangerous because a car that was far away seemed suddenly to be close. Colour lost with motion spared, and motion lost with colour spared, is a double dissociation, and it is why functional specialisation is not seriously disputed.

Two visual systems: perception and action

The most important theoretical claim in this chapter is that vision serves two different jobs and does so through two anatomically distinct cortical streams. The formulation to know is Milner and Goodale's (1992, and revised repeatedly since).

The ventral stream runs from V1 down into the inferotemporal cortex and is the vision-for-perception system. It is concerned with identifying and recognising what an object is. It works in an allocentric frame, meaning object-relative or scene-relative coordinates, which is the right choice for recognition because an object's identity should not change when you move. It relies on stored knowledge, is relatively slow, and its products are available to consciousness.

The dorsal stream runs from V1 up into the posterior parietal cortex and is the vision-for-action system. It is concerned with locating objects and with the visual control of movements towards them. It works in an egocentric frame, in coordinates centred on the actor's hand or eye, because to grasp something you need to know where it is relative to you. It is fast, operates largely without conscious awareness, and its representations are short-lived, since information about where something was two seconds ago is useless for a movement made now.

The defining double dissociation

Patient DF suffered carbon monoxide poisoning producing bilateral damage to the lateral occipital complex in the ventral stream, leaving her with visual form agnosia. Asked to report the orientation of a slot, or to rotate a card to match it, she performed at chance. Asked to post the card through the same slot, she rotated her hand accurately as she reached. She could not see the orientation, yet she could act on it.

Optic ataxia patients, with damage to the posterior parietal cortex in the dorsal stream, show the reverse: they can describe an object's size, shape and orientation perfectly well but misreach for it and shape the hand badly as they grasp. Perception intact, action impaired.

Two patients, opposite patterns, same tasks. Task difficulty cannot explain both, so perception and action must depend on at least partly separate systems.

Converging support comes from healthy participants via visual illusions. In the Ebbinghaus (Titchener) illusion, a disc surrounded by large circles looks smaller than an identical disc surrounded by small circles. Ask people to judge the sizes and they are fooled. Ask them to reach out and pick the disc up, and the maximum grip aperture as the hand opens in flight is scaled to the disc's real size, or very nearly. Perception is fooled while action is not, which is what the two-systems account predicts.

Trap

The grasping-versus-illusion evidence is genuinely contested, and a good answer says so. Franz and others argued that the perceptual and grasping tasks were not properly matched: perceptual judgements were often made with both discs visible while grasping was directed at one, and grasping benefits from visual feedback from the hand that judgement does not get. When the tasks are equated more carefully, the dissociation shrinks and sometimes disappears. The safest position is that the anatomical and neuropsychological evidence for two streams is strong, while the claim that action is wholly immune to illusion is not. Milner and Goodale themselves revised the theory towards more interaction between the streams, which is why "two streams, extensively interacting" is a better summary than "two independent systems".

Colour vision

Colour is a construction, not a property of light. Light has wavelengths; colour is the visual system's response to them, and the mapping is many-to-one, which is why two physically different spectra can look identical.

Two classical theories were long treated as rivals and are now understood as descriptions of successive stages. Young-Helmholtz trichromatic theory holds that there are three cone types with peak sensitivities in the short (blue, around 420 nm), medium (green, around 530 nm) and long (red, around 560 nm) wavelengths, and that colour is coded by the ratio of activity across the three. This is straightforwardly correct at the receptor level and explains why three primaries suffice to match any colour, and why most colour deficiency involves one cone class being absent or shifted.

Hering's opponent-process theory holds that colour is coded by three opponent channels, red-green, blue-yellow and black-white, each signalling one member of the pair by increased activity and the other by decreased activity. This explains what trichromatic theory cannot: negative afterimages (stare at red, look away, see green, because the red-green channel adapts and rebounds), the fact that no colour is ever described as reddish-green or bluish-yellow, and the pattern of the common colour deficiencies, which come in red-green and blue-yellow varieties rather than in single-colour ones. Opponent cells with exactly this organisation were later recorded in the LGN. The resolution is that trichromatic coding happens at the cones and opponent coding at the ganglion cells and beyond.

The deeper problem is colour constancy: a banana looks yellow in daylight and under a tungsten bulb, although the wavelength composition reaching the eye differs enormously. Since the light reaching the eye is the product of the illuminant and the surface reflectance, and only the product is available, recovering the reflectance requires discounting the illuminant, which is formally an underdetermined problem. The visual system solves it approximately using several cues: chromatic adaptation (receptors adapt to the prevailing illumination), the relations between a surface and its surround rather than absolute values, familiarity with the object's typical colour, and specular highlights, which tend to carry the illuminant's own colour. Land's retinex theory is the best-known formal proposal, computing lightness separately in each of three wavebands by comparing across the whole scene. Constancy is good but not perfect, which is exactly what you would expect from a system using approximate cues; the viral disagreement about "the dress" was a case where the cues to the illuminant were unusually ambiguous and different observers' visual systems settled on different assumptions.

Depth perception

The retinal image is two-dimensional and depth must be inferred. The cues divide cleanly and are worth memorising by group.

Monocular (pictorial) cues are available to one eye and are the ones painters use. Linear perspective: parallel lines converge with distance. Texture gradient (Gibson's favourite): a uniformly textured surface projects finer, denser texture as it recedes. Interposition or occlusion: a partly hidden object is further away. Relative size: with two objects known to be the same size, the smaller image is more distant. Familiar size: knowing the real size lets image size specify distance. Aerial perspective: distant objects are hazier and bluer because of atmospheric scattering. Shading: patterns of light and shadow specify three-dimensional shape, under a default assumption that light comes from above. Height in the visual field: objects nearer the horizon are further away.

Motion-produced cues require movement. Motion parallax: when you move, near objects sweep across the retina faster than far ones and in the opposite direction relative to the fixation point. This is a powerful cue and available to one eye, which is why people with one eye judge depth far better while moving.

Binocular and oculomotor cues require two eyes or feedback from the eye muscles. Convergence: the eyes turn inwards for near objects, and the muscular signal indicates distance, though only usefully within about two metres. Accommodation: the lens changes shape to focus, another muscular signal with a similarly short range. Binocular disparity: the eyes are about 6.5 cm apart, so each receives a slightly different image, and the magnitude of the difference specifies depth. This is stereopsis and it is the most precise depth cue at close range.

High-yield

Julesz's random-dot stereograms settled an old question. Each eye is shown a field of random dots containing no recognisable form whatever; a central region is shifted horizontally in one eye's image. Viewed one eye at a time there is nothing to see. Viewed binocularly, the central region floats clearly in front of the background. Since there is no monocular form to recognise, stereopsis cannot depend on first identifying objects and then comparing them: disparity is computed early, on unstructured input, before recognition. This is a clean demonstration that a process happens earlier than intuition suggests.

Real perception uses cue combination, and the modern account of it is Bayesian: cues are weighted by their reliability in the current conditions and combined, which is why depth judgements degrade gracefully rather than collapsing when one cue is removed. Size constancy follows from the same machinery. Perceived size is a function of retinal size and perceived distance, so when distance is registered correctly an object retains its apparent size as it recedes. The Ponzo and Müller-Lyer illusions and the Ames room are all cases where the distance information is manipulated and size perception follows it obediently, which is evidence that the constancy mechanism is real rather than evidence that perception is unreliable.

Perception without awareness

The claim that stimuli can be processed without being consciously perceived has a poor reputation because of the subliminal-advertising folklore, so the evidence has to be assessed carefully; but the careful evidence is good.

Blindsight is the strongest case. Patients with damage to V1 report no conscious vision in the corresponding part of the field, yet when forced to guess they perform above chance at locating a stimulus, discriminating its orientation or direction of motion, and sometimes at detecting emotional expression. The best-known case, DB, studied by Weiskrantz, was consistently astonished to be told his guesses had been correct. The standard explanation is the retinotectal pathway via the superior colliculus, which bypasses V1 and can support crude discrimination without generating conscious experience. The standard sceptical alternative is that small islands of spared V1 tissue support degraded normal vision, and this is why later work concentrated on demonstrating qualitatively different performance rather than merely weaker performance in the blind field.

Subliminal perception in healthy people is demonstrated with masked priming: a prime word is presented for a few tens of milliseconds and immediately masked, so that participants cannot report it or even reliably detect its presence, yet it speeds responses to a semantically related target. The methodological issue that dominates this literature is how to establish that awareness was genuinely absent. An objective threshold (chance performance on a forced-choice detection task) is a stricter criterion than a subjective threshold (the participant says they saw nothing), and effects that survive the objective threshold are the ones worth citing. The honest summary is that unconscious processing is real, that it extends at least to simple semantic properties, and that its effects are small and short-lived, which is precisely why subliminal advertising does not work as advertised.

Worked example

Reach out and pick up the mug on your desk. Trace what the chapter says just happened.

Light from the mug falls on the retina; cones in the fovea handle the detail and colour of the part you are looking at, rods contribute nothing much in office lighting. Signals split into the P channel (the mug's colour and the fine detail of the handle) and the M channel (any movement, including the movement produced by your own hand). Fibres from the left half of each retina reach the right hemisphere. In V1, simple and complex cells extract the oriented edges of the rim and handle.

Now the two streams diverge. The ventral stream identifies it as your mug, using stored knowledge, in allocentric coordinates that do not change as you lean; this is the part you are aware of, and the part patient DF has lost. The dorsal stream computes where it is in relation to your hand, in egocentric coordinates that must be updated as you move; this is the part optic-ataxia patients have lost, and the part you are not aware of, which is why you cannot introspect the size your hand is opening to.

Depth comes from several cues at once: binocular disparity is precise at desk range, convergence adds a coarse distance signal, the mug occludes the papers behind it, and motion parallax updates continuously as you lean in. The mug's colour stays constant as your hand shadows it, because chromatic adaptation and surround comparison are discounting the change in illumination. Meanwhile your grip aperture is scaled to the mug's real size, not to its retinal size, which is size constancy operating on the dorsal side; if a colleague had surrounded the mug with large circles, your judgement of its size would have shifted while your grip would have shifted much less.

The whole thing takes about a fifth of a second and feels like nothing at all. That gap between the effort involved and the effort experienced is the reason the chapter exists.

3. Object and face recognition

Chapter 2 got as far as edges, colour and depth. That is nowhere near recognition. Recognising a chair means assigning a two-dimensional retinal pattern to a category despite the fact that the pattern changes completely with viewpoint, distance, lighting and partial occlusion, and despite the fact that no two chairs are alike. The problem is called invariance, and it is the reason object recognition was one of the hardest problems in artificial intelligence for fifty years while remaining something a three-year-old does without noticing.

Pattern recognition: why templates fail

The simplest possible theory is template matching: a stored template for each pattern, and recognition is finding the template that matches. It fails immediately. The letter A comes in countless fonts, sizes, orientations and handwritings, and a stored template will not match any of them exactly. You could store more templates, but the number required explodes, and the theory has no account of novel instances.

Feature theories improve on this. Instead of whole templates, store a list of features, and recognise by detecting the features and combining them: an A is two oblique lines meeting at the top plus a horizontal crossbar, however it is drawn. This is far more economical and inherits real support from Hubel and Wiesel's feature detectors in V1. Evidence includes visual search data, where finding a target defined by a feature the distractors lack is fast and roughly independent of the number of distractors. The weakness is that features alone do not fix an identity: the same features arranged differently make a different letter, so the relations between features must be represented as well. Feature theories also struggle with context effects, of which the word superiority effect is the standard example: a letter is identified more accurately when presented within a word than alone, which means word-level knowledge is feeding back down to letter-level identification, and a purely bottom-up feature account has no mechanism for that. Interactive-activation models (McClelland and Rumelhart) handle it naturally, because activation flows both up and down between feature, letter and word levels.

Perceptual organisation: the Gestalt contribution and its limits

Before an object can be recognised, the visual field must be carved into objects at all: which regions belong together, and which is figure and which is ground. The Gestalt psychologists (Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler) working in the 1910s to 1930s identified the principles by which this is done, and the principles have survived even though their theoretical explanation has not.

The main laws are proximity (elements close together group), similarity (elements alike in colour, size or shape group), good continuation (elements forming a smooth continuous line group, which is why two crossing lines are seen as two lines rather than four segments meeting), closure (a nearly complete figure is completed), common fate (elements moving together group, which is powerful enough to reveal a camouflaged animal the instant it moves), and common region and connectedness (elements inside a shared boundary, or joined, group). Above them the Gestaltists placed the law of Prägnanz: the perceptual field organises itself into the simplest, most stable structure the input allows.

Figure-ground segregation is treated separately. The figure has shape, is seen as in front, and the contour is assigned to it; the ground is shapeless and continues behind. The Rubin vase demonstrates that the assignment is a decision the visual system makes, and can reverse, on unchanging input.

Trap

The Gestalt laws are descriptively excellent and explanatorily thin. Three specific criticisms: they are stated qualitatively, so when proximity and similarity point in opposite directions nothing predicts which wins; the theoretical explanation the Gestaltists offered, electrical field forces in the brain isomorphic to the percept, was tested by Lashley and Sperry and disconfirmed; and the assumption that grouping is innate, bottom-up and prior to recognition is wrong, because grouping is demonstrably influenced by learning and by top-down knowledge. Modern work reframes Prägnanz in Bayesian terms as a prior favouring simpler interpretations, which restores quantitative force to an otherwise vague principle.

Approaches to object recognition

Marr's computational theory proposed a sequence of representations of increasing abstraction. The primal sketch is a two-dimensional description of intensity changes: edges, blobs, bars, terminations. The 2½-D sketch adds depth and orientation of surfaces, but from the viewer's vantage point, so it is still viewpoint-dependent. The 3-D model representation is viewpoint-independent and describes the object's three-dimensional structure in object-centred coordinates, built from generalised cones (shapes generated by sweeping a cross-section along an axis) organised hierarchically, so that a human is an axis with limb axes attached, each of which decomposes further. The virtue is that a description independent of viewpoint solves the invariance problem in principle. The vice is that the theory is far better on the early stages than on how the 3-D model is matched to memory, and it is largely bottom-up.

Biederman's recognition-by-components (RBC) theory is the best-known descendant. Objects are represented as arrangements of about 36 basic volumetric primitives called geons (cylinder, brick, wedge, cone and so on). Recognition proceeds by extracting edges, detecting non-accidental properties that are invariant across viewpoint (curvature, parallelism, co-termination, symmetry, collinearity), determining the object's components from these, and matching the resulting geon arrangement to stored structural descriptions. Two claims give the theory its power. First, geons are discriminable from nearly any viewpoint, which delivers viewpoint invariance. Second, the arrangement matters: a cylinder on top of a brick is a mug or a bucket depending on the relations, so RBC has the relational information feature theories lacked.

The supporting evidence is elegant. Biederman degraded line drawings by deleting parts of the contour either at the vertices (where geons are recovered) or at the midsegments (where they are not). Deleting the same amount of contour hurt recognition far more when it removed the vertices, which is what the theory predicts. Objects were also recognised almost as well from partial as from complete drawings when the geons were recoverable.

Trap

RBC has real limits and the examiner usually wants them. It is a theory of basic-level categorisation (that is a dog) and says very little about subordinate discrimination (that is a spaniel, that is my dog), because the geon description of two spaniels is identical. It ignores colour and texture, which are diagnostic for some categories such as fruit. It treats recognition as bottom-up and so has no account of context effects. And its central prediction of viewpoint invariance is contradicted by a large body of work, mostly from Tarr and colleagues, showing that recognition times increase systematically with the angular difference between the test view and a familiar view, exactly as if views were stored and mentally transformed.

That objection defines the field's main live debate: structural-description (viewpoint-invariant) theories against view-based (viewpoint-dependent) theories. The compromise most researchers now accept is that both are right about different tasks. Easy basic-level discriminations between structurally distinct objects behave in a broadly viewpoint-invariant way; hard within-category discriminations between structurally similar objects are strongly viewpoint-dependent. Since RBC's evidence came mostly from the first kind of task and Tarr's from the second, the two literatures were never really testing the same claim.

Neuropsychology adds a further division. Visual agnosia comes in two forms, distinguished by Lissauer and still used. In apperceptive agnosia the deficit is perceptual: the patient cannot copy a drawing or match shapes, so the percept itself never gets built. In associative agnosia perception is largely intact (the patient can copy a drawing, sometimes beautifully and slowly) but cannot say what it is, so the intact percept fails to contact stored knowledge. The classic gloss is that associative agnosia is a normal percept stripped of its meaning.

Face recognition

Faces are treated separately because there is good evidence they are processed differently from other objects. Three behavioural effects carry the argument.

The face-inversion effect: turning a face upside down impairs recognition far more than inverting other objects. The Thatcher illusion makes the point vividly: a face with eyes and mouth inverted within an upright face looks grotesque, but the same manipulation is barely detectable when the whole face is inverted. The composite effect: align the top half of one famous face with the bottom half of another and identifying either half becomes hard, because the two halves fuse into a new configuration; misalign the halves and the difficulty vanishes. The part-whole effect: a nose is recognised better within the whole face it came from than in isolation, whereas a house's door is not much better recognised within its house.

All three point the same way: face processing is holistic or configural, depending on the relations among features (and especially on the spacing between them) rather than on the features taken separately. Inversion disrupts configural processing while leaving featural processing more or less intact, which is why inversion hurts faces so much more.

High-yield

Bruce and Young (1986)'s model of face recognition is the one to be able to draw. Structural encoding produces descriptions of the face, both view-centred and expression-independent. From there, separate parallel routes handle different jobs: expression analysis, facial speech analysis (lip-reading), and directed visual processing (attending to selected features). The identity route runs in series: face recognition units (FRUs) signal familiarity and contain stored structural descriptions; person identity nodes (PINs) hold biographical information; and the name generation component holds the name. Because the stages are serial, the model predicts you can find a face familiar without recalling who they are, and recall who they are without recalling the name, but never the name without the identity, and diary studies of everyday face-recognition errors confirm exactly this asymmetry. It also predicts, correctly, that identity and expression can dissociate: some patients recognise faces but not expressions, others the reverse.

Prosopagnosia is the failure to recognise familiar faces despite intact vision and intact intelligence; in severe cases patients fail on their own face in a mirror and identify people by voice, gait or spectacles. Two things about it are heavily examined. First, many prosopagnosics show covert recognition: skin conductance responses are larger to familiar than unfamiliar faces, and they are faster to learn true name-face pairings than false ones, so recognition is happening without reaching awareness. In Bruce and Young terms, the FRUs are being activated but the output is not becoming conscious. Second, whether the deficit is truly face-specific is contested. Gauthier and colleagues showed that experts (bird experts, car experts, and people trained on novel objects called greebles) come to process their objects of expertise holistically and to activate the fusiform face area, supporting an expertise hypothesis: the region is specialised for fine within-category discrimination among visually homogeneous items, and faces are simply the category everyone is expert in. Against this, some prosopagnosics have normal within-category expertise for non-face objects, and developmental prosopagnosia can occur in people with otherwise normal vision and no brain injury, which suggests something more specific than general expertise.

The neural evidence is well known: the fusiform face area (FFA) in the fusiform gyrus responds far more strongly to faces than to other objects; the occipital face area (OFA) is implicated in earlier, more part-based processing; and the superior temporal sulcus (STS) handles the changeable aspects of faces, gaze direction and expression, which matches the split between identity and expression routes in the Bruce and Young model. The ERP marker is the N170, a negative deflection about 170 ms after a face appears, larger for faces than objects and, tellingly, delayed and enlarged by inversion.

Visual imagery

The last section connects perception to internal representation. The question is whether visual imagery uses the same machinery as visual perception, and whether images are picture-like at all.

Kosslyn's depictive position holds that images are quasi-pictorial, represented in a spatial medium with the properties of a picture. Two classic findings support it. Mental rotation (Shepard and Metzler, 1971): asked whether two three-dimensional block figures are the same shape, response time increases linearly with the angular difference between them, as if the object were being rotated continuously through intermediate positions. Image scanning (Kosslyn): asked to imagine a map and mentally travel from one landmark to another, the time taken is proportional to the real distance on the map, as if scanning across a spatial layout.

Pylyshyn's propositional position holds that the underlying representation is language-like and abstract, and that the apparent picture-like results come from tacit knowledge: participants know how long real rotation or real travel takes and unconsciously simulate that, producing the linear function without any spatial medium doing the work. This is a serious objection because it explains the data without the theoretical commitment, and some experiments do show the effects shrink when the task demands are disguised.

Neuroimaging has moved the argument on rather than settled it. Imagery activates many of the same areas as perception, including, in a number of studies, early visual cortex, with the topographic organisation showing the retinotopic signature you would expect if a spatial medium were involved, and imagining a larger object activating a larger area. Patients with visual-field losses show corresponding losses in the extent of their imagery. Against this, some patients with severe perceptual deficits retain intact imagery and vice versa, so the overlap is partial rather than total. The defensible summary is that imagery and perception share substantial neural machinery, especially the higher visual areas, that the involvement of early visual cortex varies with task detail, and that this is not the same as showing that images are pictures.

Worked example

You are at a party and someone across the room looks familiar. Walk the machinery.

Perceptual organisation comes first: their face has to be segmented from the background, and the Gestalt principles do that work (common region, connectedness, common fate as they turn their head). Structural encoding then builds a description of the face. Two things follow in parallel: the STS route reads the changeable aspects, that they are smiling and looking at you, while the identity route runs its serial sequence.

The face recognition unit fires: familiar. Note that this is exactly as far as you have got, and it is why the experience is "I know that face" and not yet anything else. The person identity node then delivers the biography: they were on your entrance-coaching batch. Only after that does name generation run, and this is where it typically fails, which is why you can stand there with the entire life history and no name. The reverse never happens, and Bruce and Young's serial architecture is why.

Now suppose the room is dark and you only catch them upside down as they bend to pick something up. Recognition collapses, far more than it would for an upside-down handbag, because inversion destroys the configural information that face processing depends on and leaves you with features alone. And if you had a lesion to the fusiform gyrus you would be in the position of a prosopagnosic: no conscious recognition at all, while your skin conductance quietly rose at the sight of them, because covert recognition would be running underneath.

Later, at home, you picture their face to try again. Whether that picture is literally a picture is the Kosslyn-Pylyshyn dispute; what is not disputed is that you are re-using a good deal of the same visual machinery to do it.

4. Perception, motion and action

The previous two chapters treated the perceiver as stationary and the world as something to be reconstructed from an impoverished retinal image. This chapter overturns both assumptions. Perceivers move, the world moves, and the argument here is that movement is not a complication to be corrected for but a large part of what makes perception possible.

Direct perception: Gibson's challenge

James J. Gibson's ecological approach is the most radical position in the book, and it is best understood as a direct attack on the constructivist assumption that perception requires inference. Gibson's claim is that the optic array, the structured pattern of light reaching a moving observer, contains far more information than theorists had supposed, enough that perception can be direct: information is picked up, not computed from impoverished data by unconscious inference.

The key concepts are worth stating precisely. The optic flow field is the pattern of movement of the optic array produced by the observer's own motion. When you move forward, the whole array expands outward from a single stationary point, the focus of expansion, which lies exactly in the direction of heading. Nothing needs to be inferred: the direction you are travelling is specified directly in the flow. Invariants are properties of the array that remain constant despite movement, such as the horizon ratio (the proportion of an object above the horizon is constant regardless of distance, so it specifies size directly) and texture-gradient relations, which specify surface slant. Affordances are the possibilities for action an object offers a particular actor: a surface affords walking on, a handle affords grasping. Gibson insisted affordances are perceived directly rather than being derived after the object is recognised, which is why his theory is fundamentally a theory of perception for action.

Gibson also introduced tau, later formalised by David Lee: the ratio of an object's retinal size to its rate of expansion specifies the time to contact directly, without any need to know the object's distance or its speed separately. This was Gibson's strongest case, because it shows a genuinely useful quantity being available in the array as a simple optical variable.

Trap

The standard criticisms of Gibson are three. First, he explained how useful information is available but had little to say about how the nervous system picks it up, so the theory is strong at Marr's computational level and nearly silent at the algorithmic and implementational levels. Second, direct perception has real trouble with visual illusions, with perception of stationary displays, and with the many demonstrations that knowledge and expectation influence what is seen. Third, tau on its own turns out to be insufficient: it assumes constant approach velocity, ignores acceleration, and empirically people use tau alongside binocular information and familiar size rather than instead of them. The productive way to hold Gibson is as a corrective rather than a replacement: he was right that the array is much richer than constructivists assumed and right to put action at the centre, and wrong that inference can be dispensed with.

Visually guided action

How do people actually steer, intercept and catch? The findings are unusually concrete.

For locomotion and steering, the flow field is used, but not exclusively. Studies in which optic flow is manipulated independently of the visible layout show people use flow when it is informative, and switch to egocentric direction (keeping the target at a fixed angle relative to the body) when the flow is degraded, as it is in a poorly textured environment. This is the same reliability-weighted cue-combination story as depth perception in Chapter 2.

For catching and intercepting, the elegant result is the outfielder problem. How does a fielder get to where a fly ball will land? The naive answer, that they compute the trajectory, is almost certainly wrong. The evidence supports optical acceleration cancellation: the fielder runs in whatever way keeps the ball's optical acceleration at zero, that is, keeps the ball rising through the visual field at a steady rate. Do that and you arrive where the ball does, without ever computing a landing point. The signature is that fielders run in curved rather than straight paths, which is what a continuous-control strategy predicts and what trajectory-computation does not.

This is an instance of a general principle worth carrying: much skilled action uses online control laws that couple perception to movement continuously, rather than internal models that compute a plan and then execute it. The former is cheaper, more robust, and matches the data in a range of tasks.

The planning-control model

Glover's planning-control model is a refinement of the two-visual-systems account from Chapter 2 and is best learned as a correction to it. Glover's point is that a reaching movement has two phases with different properties, and lumping them together caused the confusion in the illusion literature.

The planning system operates before the movement begins. It selects the target, decides how to grasp it, and sets the initial movement parameters. It draws on a wide range of information: the object's visual properties, its identity, its weight, the surrounding context, and the actor's intentions. It is relatively slow, and it is largely conscious. Its neural home is chiefly the inferior parietal lobule together with frontal areas.

The control system operates during the movement. It makes fine adjustments in flight, correcting for errors as the hand approaches. It uses a much narrower range of information, essentially the target's spatial characteristics and the position of the limb. It is fast, automatic and unconscious, and its neural home is chiefly the superior parietal lobule and the cerebellum.

The model makes a specific, testable prediction about illusions: because planning is context-sensitive and control is not, an illusion produced by context should affect the early part of a reach and then be corrected away as control takes over. That is broadly what is observed. It also reconciles the awkward finding that grasping is sometimes fooled by illusions after all: if the movement is short, or vision of the hand is denied, the control phase has no chance to correct the plan, and the illusion survives into the action. This is why Glover's model is a better answer to the Franz critique than simply restating Milner and Goodale.

High-yield

Learn the planning/control contrast as a table you can reproduce: planning = before movement, wide information including context and semantics, slow, conscious, inferior parietal; control = during movement, narrow spatial information only, fast, unconscious, superior parietal and cerebellum. Then attach the prediction: illusions bite the plan, not the correction, so their effect on grasping decays across the trajectory.

Perception of human motion

The visual system is startlingly good at reading other people's movement, and the demonstration of it is Johansson's point-light walker. Attach small lights to a person's joints, film them in darkness, and show the resulting display. A static frame looks like a meaningless scatter of dots. Set it in motion and observers instantly see a person walking, and can further judge the walker's sex, their mood, their identity if known, and the weight of a box they are lifting, all from a dozen moving points. This is biological motion perception and it is remarkably fast, often within 200 ms.

The neural substrate is well established: the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS) responds far more to biological than to non-biological motion, and TMS over pSTS impairs biological-motion discrimination while sparing coherent-motion discrimination, which is the causal evidence rather than the correlational kind. There are also dissociations from ordinary motion perception: patient LM, the akinetopsia case from Chapter 2, retained some biological-motion perception despite devastated general motion perception.

Two further ideas belong here. Mirror neurons, discovered by Rizzolatti and colleagues in macaque premotor cortex area F5, fire both when the monkey performs an action and when it observes the same action performed by another. The proposal that they underpin action understanding by simulation is influential and heavily contested; the honest summary is that human evidence for a mirror system is largely indirect, that the simulation account of action understanding faces the objection that we understand actions we cannot perform, and that the concept has been considerably overextended in the popular literature. Second, the perception of biological motion connects to the common coding idea that perception and action share representational format, which is a theme running from Gibson's affordances through to this chapter's central claim.

Change blindness and inattentional blindness

The chapter closes with a set of findings that overturn the intuition that we hold a detailed internal picture of the scene in front of us.

Change blindness is the failure to detect large changes to a visual scene when the change coincides with a visual disruption. The standard paradigms are the flicker paradigm (two versions of a scene alternate with a blank screen between them, and observers can take many seconds to spot a change as large as a missing aeroplane engine), mudsplashes (small irrelevant blobs appear at the moment of the change), and change across a saccade or a film cut. The most striking version is Simons and Levin's real-world study: an experimenter asking a pedestrian for directions was swapped for a different person while two confederates carried a door between them, and around half the pedestrians did not notice they were now talking to a different individual.

Inattentional blindness is the related failure to see a fully visible unexpected object when attention is engaged elsewhere. Simons and Chabris's invisible gorilla is the canonical demonstration: counting basketball passes, roughly half of observers fail to report a person in a gorilla suit who walks through the scene, stops, beats their chest, and walks off.

Trap

Do not over-read these results. The strong conclusion sometimes drawn, that we represent almost nothing of a scene, does not follow. Change blindness shows a failure of comparison as much as of representation: the change is missed because the pre-change representation was not compared with the post-change one, not necessarily because nothing was stored. There is good evidence for implicit registration of changes people cannot report, including eye movements towards the changed region before detection and above-chance forced-choice guessing. The defensible conclusion is that attention is necessary for conscious change detection, that representation outside the focus of attention is coarse and largely unavailable to report, and that our sense of a richly detailed visual world is largely a matter of being able to fetch detail on demand rather than of holding it all at once.

The practical significance is real and worth citing. Change blindness is a plausible contributor to the "looked but failed to see" category of road accidents, in which a driver looks directly at a motorcycle and pulls out anyway; it is a documented problem in eyewitness testimony; and it is exploited routinely by stage magicians and, less benignly, by continuity errors in film that nobody notices.

Worked example

You are cycling towards a junction and a car pulls out. Take the chapter's machinery through it.

As you ride, the optic array expands from a focus of expansion that sits exactly where you are heading; steer and the focus shifts, which is Gibson's direct specification of heading. In poorly textured surroundings, a bare road at dusk, the flow is weak and you fall back on egocentric direction, keeping the junction at a fixed angle to your body.

The car appears. Tau, the ratio of its retinal size to its rate of expansion, specifies time to contact without your ever computing its speed or distance, and this is what your braking is actually coupled to. Tau alone is not enough, though: it assumes constant velocity, and the car is accelerating, so you are also using binocular information and your knowledge of how big a car is. Your braking is online control, continuously adjusted, not a plan computed in advance.

You reach out to the brake lever. The planning system, inferior parietal, selected the lever and set the initial reach using everything you know about it; the control system, superior parietal and cerebellum, corrects the hand in flight using only spatial information. If the lever's apparent position had been distorted by context, the distortion would have shown in the early part of the reach and been corrected out by the end.

And the reason the driver pulled out in front of you is the last section of the chapter. They looked, and they did not see, because they were attending to the car coming from the other direction. A bicycle is small, unexpected in that lane, and outside the focus of attention, and none of that is compensated for by the vividness of their impression that they were looking at an empty road. Looked but failed to see is not a lapse of morality or even of effort. It is inattentional blindness, and it is what the visual system does by design.

5. Attention and performance

Attention is the topic on which cognitive psychology cut its teeth, and the reason is that it was the first mental process for which a genuine flow-diagram model was proposed and then tested to destruction. William James's definition still holds: attention is "the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought", and it "implies withdrawal from some things in order to deal effectively with others". Every theory in this chapter is an attempt to say where in the processing stream that withdrawal happens.

Two distinctions organise the chapter. Focused attention (selective attention) asks how one input is selected and what happens to the rest. Divided attention asks what limits our ability to do two things at once. Cutting across both is the distinction between bottom-up (exogenous, stimulus-driven) capture and top-down (endogenous, goal-driven) control.

Focused auditory attention: the bottleneck debate

Cherry (1953) named the cocktail party problem: how do we follow one conversation among many? His method was dichotic listening with shadowing: different messages to each ear, with instructions to repeat the attended one aloud. The results were sharp. From the unattended ear, participants noticed physical characteristics, whether the voice was male or female, whether it changed to a tone, but could report essentially nothing of the content, did not notice a switch from English to German, and did not notice speech played in reverse.

Broadbent's filter theory (1958) accounted for this. Information from all inputs enters a sensory buffer in parallel; a filter then selects one input on the basis of its physical characteristics (location, pitch, loudness) and passes it to a limited-capacity channel for semantic processing; everything else is blocked and decays. The filter is placed early, before meaning is extracted, which is why unattended content is not available.

The theory did not survive contact with three findings. First, Moray (1959) showed that roughly a third of participants detected their own name on the unattended channel, which is impossible if the filter blocks before meaning. Second, Gray and Wedderburn (1960) presented "Dear 7 Jane" to one ear and "9 Aunt 4" to the other and participants reported "Dear Aunt Jane", switching ears mid-message; selection therefore followed meaning, not location. Third, Treisman showed that when a shadowed message switched ears, participants often followed the meaning across to the other ear before catching themselves.

Treisman's attenuation theory (1964) is the standard fix. The unattended channel is not blocked but attenuated: it is passed on in weakened form. Recognition then depends on each word's threshold in a hierarchical analysis of the input, and thresholds vary. Personally important words such as your own name have permanently low thresholds; words primed by the current context have temporarily lowered thresholds. So an attenuated signal will occasionally still exceed a threshold, which is exactly the pattern observed: unattended material is usually lost, but not always, and what gets through is predictable from its importance.

Deutsch and Deutsch (1963) proposed the opposite extreme, late selection: all inputs are fully analysed for meaning and selection happens only at the point of response. This handles the breakthrough findings easily but predicts far more processing of unattended material than is generally observed, and it is expensive: it makes no sense to analyse everything fully and then discard nearly all of it.

High-yield

Lavie's perceptual load theory is the modern resolution of the early-versus-late dispute, and the point is that both camps were asking a badly formed question. Selection is early when perceptual load is high and late when perceptual load is low. Perceptual capacity is fixed and is allocated involuntarily; if the relevant task consumes it all, there is none left to spill onto distractors and they are not processed. If the task is easy, spare capacity is used up on distractors whether you want it or not. This explains why distractor interference is larger in easy tasks, which is counter-intuitive and is the finding to quote. Lavie adds a crucial second factor: cognitive load (demands on working memory) works the opposite way, because working memory is what maintains the priority settings that keep attention on target, so loading it makes distraction worse. High perceptual load protects you; high cognitive load exposes you.

Focused visual attention: spotlight, zoom lens, and what is selected

Three metaphors compete, and they are not mutually exclusive. The spotlight (Posner) treats attention as a beam of fixed size that moves around the visual field, enhancing processing within it. The zoom lens (Eriksen) adds that the beam's size is adjustable, with a trade-off: attend to a wide region and processing within it is less efficient. The split attention position, supported by evidence that attention can be allocated to two non-contiguous locations at once, undermines the assumption that the beam is a single contiguous region.

Posner's cueing paradigm is the workhorse. A cue indicates where a target is likely to appear, then the target appears at the cued (valid) or uncued (invalid) location. Responses are faster on valid trials and slower on invalid ones than on neutral trials, and Posner decomposed the underlying operation into three components, each with a distinct neural substrate: disengage attention from the current location (posterior parietal), shift or move it (superior colliculus), and engage at the new location (pulvinar). The value of this decomposition is that it predicted, correctly, a specific patient profile: unilateral parietal damage produces a marked deficit in disengaging from an ipsilesional stimulus, which is why the cost of invalid cues is huge when the target then appears on the contralesional side. Note also that cueing can be endogenous (a central arrow, voluntary, slower to take effect, and can be ignored) or exogenous (a peripheral flash, automatic, fast, and hard to ignore), and that exogenous cueing is followed after roughly 200-300 ms by inhibition of return, a bias against going back to a location already inspected, which is functionally useful for search.

A separate question is what attention selects: locations, or objects? Space-based evidence is the cueing data above. Object-based evidence comes from Duncan's work, in which two judgements about the same object are made more efficiently than two judgements about different objects, even when the objects occupy the same region of space; and from Egly's two-rectangle paradigm, in which shifting attention within an object is cheaper than shifting the same distance across an object boundary. The consensus is that attention can be allocated to both, and is allocated to objects once perceptual organisation has parsed the scene into them.

Disorders of visual attention

Two syndromes are examined constantly and are frequently confused.

Unilateral spatial neglect follows damage typically to the right inferior parietal lobule and temporoparietal junction. The patient fails to attend to, or act upon, stimuli on the contralesional (usually left) side: they eat from the right of the plate, shave the right of the face, and draw only the right half of a clock. Three features make it theoretically rich. It is a disorder of attention, not of vision: the visual fields can be intact, and the deficit can be object-centred, so a patient may neglect the left half of every object even when the objects lie to their right. There is evidence of implicit processing of the neglected side, most famously Marshall and Halligan's burning-house study, in which a patient claimed two houses were identical while consistently preferring the one that was not on fire, the flames being on the neglected side. And the syndrome is strongly lateralised, being far commoner and more severe after right-hemisphere damage, which is usually explained by the right hemisphere attending to both sides while the left attends mainly to the right.

Extinction is the milder relative: the patient detects a single stimulus on either side, but when two are presented simultaneously, only the ipsilesional one is reported. It reveals attention as a competitive process, since the contralesional stimulus is detectable until it has to compete.

Balint's syndrome, from bilateral parieto-occipital damage, produces simultanagnosia (only one object can be perceived at a time, so the patient sees the trees and never the wood), optic ataxia (from Chapter 2) and ocular apraxia. It is the clearest demonstration that binding a scene into multiple simultaneous objects is an attentional achievement.

Visual search and feature integration

Treisman's feature integration theory (FIT) is the most examined model in the chapter. It proposes two stages. In the pre-attentive stage, basic features (colour, orientation, size, motion) are processed rapidly, in parallel, across the whole field, and are encoded in separate feature maps. In the focused attention stage, attention is directed to one location and binds the features present there into a single object.

The predictions are precise. A feature search (find the red X among green Xs) can be done pre-attentively, so the target "pops out" and search time is flat with respect to the number of distractors. A conjunction search (find the red X among red Os and green Xs) requires binding, so attention must be moved from item to item and search time rises steeply with the number of distractors. Both predictions hold. The theory's most distinctive support, though, is the illusory conjunction: when attention is prevented from settling, by very brief exposure or by a demanding concurrent task, participants report combinations that were never presented, seeing a red X when a red O and a green X were shown. Features have been detected but bound wrongly, which is direct evidence that binding is a separate operation requiring attention.

Trap

FIT is too clean. Conjunction search is often much more efficient than the theory predicts, especially when the features are highly discriminable, and the flat-versus-steep dichotomy is really a continuum of search efficiency. Wolfe's Guided Search model is the standard revision and is what a strong answer cites: pre-attentive feature processing does not merely detect features, it produces an activation map combining bottom-up salience with top-down knowledge of the target's features, and attention is then guided to the highest-activation items first rather than being deployed at random. This preserves FIT's two stages while explaining why conjunction search is far faster than serial self-terminating search would allow.

Cross-modal effects

Attention is not organised separately for each sense. Attending to a location in one modality enhances processing at that location in others, and the systems interact in ways that reveal their relative weights. The ventriloquism effect shows vision dominating the localisation of sound, which makes sense because vision localises far more precisely. The McGurk effect shows vision altering what is heard: an auditory /ba/ dubbed onto a visual /ga/ is typically heard as /da/, a fusion belonging to neither input, and the effect persists even when you know exactly what is happening, which marks it as automatic rather than inferential. The modern account of both is again reliability-weighted integration: whichever modality is more reliable for the property in question dominates, which is why sound wins for timing judgements while vision wins for spatial ones.

Divided attention: the psychological refractory period and multitasking

The best-controlled evidence on dual-task limits comes from the psychological refractory period (PRP). Two stimuli requiring two responses are presented in quick succession; as the interval between them shortens, the response to the second is progressively delayed. The dominant explanation is a central bottleneck: perceptual encoding and response execution can proceed in parallel, but the central stage of response selection can handle only one task at a time, so the second task queues. The PRP is remarkably robust, survives extensive practice in most cases, and is the strongest single piece of evidence for a genuine structural limit rather than a mere shortage of effort.

Whether two tasks can be combined depends on three factors, each with clean supporting evidence. Task similarity: two tasks using the same modality, the same processing stage or the same response system interfere far more than dissimilar ones, which is the core insight of Baddeley's working-memory research and the reason a visual task and a verbal task combine so much more easily than two verbal ones. Practice: Spelke, Hirst and Neisser trained two students for many hours until they could read stories for comprehension while simultaneously taking dictation and eventually categorising the dictated words, which shows how far dual-task performance can be pushed. Task difficulty: harder tasks demand more resources and combine worse, though the relationship is not simply additive because two tasks may share specific rather than general resources.

The applied case worth having ready is driving. Mobile-phone conversation impairs driving substantially, and the finding that matters is that hands-free is not meaningfully safer than handheld, because the interference is central rather than manual. Conversation with a passenger interferes much less, because passengers modulate the conversation when the traffic gets difficult, which is a nice demonstration that the bottleneck is in shared central resources and that the social context can protect them.

Automatic processing

The traditional criteria for an automatic process are that it is fast, does not consume attentional capacity, is unavailable to consciousness, and is unavoidable, occurring whether or not it is wanted. Shiffrin and Schneider's classic work established that consistent mapping training (a stimulus is always a target, never a distractor) produces automatic detection with flat search functions, whereas varied mapping (the same item is sometimes a target and sometimes a distractor) leaves search controlled and effortful however long the practice.

The Stroop effect is the standard demonstration of unavoidability: naming the ink colour of a colour word is slowed when the word names a different colour, because reading is automatic and cannot be switched off. Note the direction of the asymmetry, which is the part usually missed: reading the word is not much slowed by an incongruent ink colour, because word-reading is the more automatic process and dominates.

The criteria are, however, too strict. Logan's instance theory offers a better account of how automaticity develops: with practice, each encounter with a stimulus lays down a separate memory trace, and performance shifts from applying a general algorithm to retrieving a specific stored instance. Because retrieval speed depends on the number of stored instances, the theory predicts the power law of practice, the observation that response time falls as a power function of the number of trials, with large early gains and diminishing later ones. This is the account to prefer, because it explains automatisation as a change in what is being done rather than merely as the same process getting faster.

Worked example

You are revising in a café and someone two tables away says your name. Push it through the chapter.

You were not attending to their conversation, and by Broadbent's filter theory you should have heard nothing of its content. You heard your name, which is precisely the finding that killed filter theory (Moray, 1959). Treisman's attenuation theory explains it: the unattended channel was weakened rather than blocked, and your own name has a permanently low recognition threshold, so an attenuated signal still crossed it.

Whether you notice depends on load, and here Lavie's two factors pull in opposite directions. If you were working through a hard diagram, perceptual load was high, capacity was consumed, and you would very likely have missed it. If you were half-heartedly re-reading a page you already know, spare capacity spilled onto the room and you would catch not just your name but most of their conversation. But if you were holding a long argument in working memory, cognitive load was high, and that makes distraction worse, not better, because working memory is what maintains your priority settings.

You look up and scan for the speaker. If you are looking for the one person in a red jacket, that is a feature search and they pop out regardless of how full the café is. If you are looking for a friend who is both wearing red and standing, that is a conjunction search, attention has to visit candidates one at a time, and how long it takes scales with the crowd, though Guided Search says you will visit the red items first rather than searching at random. Inhibition of return keeps you from re-checking tables you have already dismissed.

You go back to revising and try to keep half an ear on the conversation. You cannot, and the reason is the PRP bottleneck: response selection is strictly one at a time. And the reason the café's background music does not cost you anything like as much is task similarity: it does not compete for the verbal machinery your reading is using. Finally, notice that you cannot stop reading the words on the poster in your eyeline, however irrelevant they are. That is Stroop's lesson: skilled reading is automatic, and automatic means you do not get a say.
