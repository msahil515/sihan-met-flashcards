2 · Greek Theorists

The Mind–Body Argument

  • Monists: mind and body are a single entity.
  • Dualists: mind and body are separate entities.

Hippocrates — Humoral Theory

Hippocrates was a dualist. His humoral theory linked four bodily humors to four temperaments.

HumorTemperamentCharacter
Black bileMelancholicSad, depressed, poetic and artistic
Yellow bileCholericFiery, energetic, and passionate
BloodSanguineWarm-hearted, cheerful, optimistic, and confident
PhlegmPhlegmaticSlow, quiet, rational, and consistent

Socrates

  • Dualist.
  • Focused on morality.
  • Explained eudemonia: long-lasting contentment, peace and happiness comes from building virtues like honesty, generosity, kindness, altruism, etc.

Plato

  • Dualist.
  • Allegory of the cave and allegory of the chariot.
  • Tripartite theory of the soul: rational (mind), emotional (spirited), and appetites (pleasures).
  • Theory of forms: forms are universals and are the purest representation of things that actually exist.

Plato's two allegories

Allegory (source)The storyWhat it means
Cave (Republic, Book VII)Prisoners chained in a cave since birth see only shadows thrown on the wall by a fire behind them, and take the shadows to be reality. One prisoner is freed, sees the fire and the real objects, climbs out and finally sees the sun. When he goes back to tell the others, they mock him and resist.The senses show only shadows (appearances). True knowledge is of the Forms, reached by reason; the sun stands for the highest Form, the Good. Education means turning the mind from appearance to reality, and it is painful at first.
Chariot (Phaedrus)A charioteer drives two winged horses: one noble and obedient, the other unruly and pulling downward.A picture of the tripartite soul: charioteer = reason, noble horse = spirit (emotion, honour), unruly horse = appetite. A healthy soul is one where reason steers both horses.
Trap: cave = theory of forms and how we know (Republic); chariot = tripartite soul (Phaedrus). The chariot is often compared with Freud: in The Ego and the Id (1923) Freud likened the ego to a rider holding in check a stronger horse, the id.

Aristotle

  • Monist.
  • Gave the first theory of cognitive sciences; described sensation and perception — special sensible and common sensible.
  • Theory of associationism: law of contiguity, law of frequency, law of similarity, law of contrast.

Special vs common sensibles (Aristotle, De Anima)

In De Anima (On the Soul), Aristotle sorted the things we perceive by how many senses can pick them up.

TypeMeaningExamples
Special (proper) sensiblesQualities that only one sense can detect. Aristotle held that a sense is rarely, if ever, wrong about its own special object.Colour (sight only), sound (hearing), smell (smell), flavour (taste), hot/cold and hard/soft (touch)
Common sensiblesQualities that more than one sense can detect. Errors are more likely here.Movement, rest, number, shape (figure), size (magnitude): you can both see and feel that a coin is round
Trap: to explain how we combine the senses (e.g. knowing that the white thing is also sweet), Aristotle proposed a "common sense" (sensus communis): a faculty that unites the senses, not everyday "common sense". Do not mix it up with the common sensibles, which are the objects perceived, not the faculty.
Trap: Hippocrates, Socrates and Plato were dualists; Aristotle was a monist. The four laws of association (contiguity, frequency, similarity, contrast) belong to Aristotle.

3 · 17th-Century Philosophers

René Descartes

  • Famously stated "cogito, ergo sum" ("I think, therefore I am").
  • Innatism: the mind is born with ideas and knowledge.
  • Dualist.
  • Believed the pineal gland to be the seat of the soul.

John Locke

  • Empiricism: all knowledge comes from the senses.
  • Tabula Rasa: individuals are born as a blank slate and all knowledge is acquired from the external world.
  • Rejected innatism.

Immanuel Kant

  • Rejected the soul as the subject matter of psychology.
  • Types of knowledge:
    • A priori knowledge: innate knowledge — rationalism.
    • A posteriori knowledge: empiricism.

William Dilthey

  • Best known for the way he distinguished between the natural and human sciences.
  • Wrote Ideas for a Descriptive and Analytic Psychology (1894).

Wilhelm Dilthey (1833 to 1911, German philosopher; "William" is the English form of his name) argued that the study of human beings needs a different method from physics or chemistry.

Natural sciences (Naturwissenschaften)Human sciences (Geisteswissenschaften)
StudyPhysical nature, observed from the outsideMind, culture, history, art, grasped from the inside
AimExplain (Erklären): find causes and general lawsUnderstand (Verstehen): grasp the meaning of lived experience (Erlebnis)
ExampleWhy a stone falls: gravityWhy a person grieves or writes a poem: what the loss means to them

His famous line: "We explain nature, but we understand mental life." In the 1894 book he called for a descriptive psychology of the whole, lived person, instead of the explanatory psychology of his day that tried to build the mind up from a few hypothetical elements.

Trap: Dilthey = Verstehen (understanding), a root of hermeneutic (interpretive) and qualitative approaches. Despite this section's title, he is 19th century; Kant is 18th century; only Descartes and Locke are 17th century.
Who → what: René Descartes → innatism + "cogito, ergo sum" + pineal gland; Locke → empiricism + tabula rasa; Kant → a priori / a posteriori knowledge; Dilthey → natural vs. human sciences.

4 · Major Schools of Psychology

Each school is defined by its founder(s), method of study, and central focus. Several are tagged as a "force" in psychology.

School (year)Founder(s)Description
Structuralism (1896) Edward Bradford Titchener Aimed to discover the structural elements of the mind. Method: introspection. Emphasized basic conscious elements.
Functionalism (1896) William James Assumed that thinking, like the senses, developed because it was adaptive — it contributed to our ancestors' survival. The role of consciousness is adaptation.
Psychoanalysis (1900) Sigmund Freud Two levels of reality: conscious and unconscious. Unconscious instincts: eros (life instincts) and thanatos (death instincts). Parts of personality: id, ego, superego. 1st force in psychology.
Gestalt (1912) Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka, Wolfgang Köhler Method: phenomenology. Studied conscious experience and mental processes. Focus on holism.
Behaviourism (1913) John Watson; most famous behaviourist: B. F. Skinner Focus on behaviour only and discredited mental processes. 2nd force in psychology.
Existential (1940s) Rollo May Method: phenomenology. Focus on freedom, meaning, and death. Martin Heidegger's Dasein: Umwelt (nature), Mitwelt (people), Eigenwelt (oneself), Uberwelt (spirituality).
Humanistic (1954) Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow Method: phenomenology. Focused on the potential of individuals and highlighted each person's subjectivity, consciousness, free will and other special human qualities. 3rd force in psychology.
Cognitive (1956) Ulric Neisser Focused on cognition and mental process. Analogy of the brain as a computer.
Transpersonal (1960s) Abraham Maslow and Victor Frankl Concerned with the study of humanity's highest potential, and the recognition, understanding, and realization of unitive, spiritual, and transcendental states of human consciousness. 4th force in psychology.
Key — the four forces: 1st = Psychoanalysis, 2nd = Behaviourism, 3rd = Humanistic, 4th = Transpersonal.
Trap: Behaviourism was founded by John Watson; B. F. Skinner is named as the most famous behaviourist, not the founder.

Dasein and the four worlds (existential psychology)

Dasein (German, "being-there") is Martin Heidegger's word for human existence in Being and Time (1927). His point: a person is never a mind sealed off from things; we always exist as being-in-the-world. Existential therapists then split that "world" into dimensions we live in at the same time:

WorldLiteral meaningWhat it coversExample of a problem here
Umwelt"world around"Physical and biological world: body, nature, needs, illness, deathChronic pain, ageing
Mitwelt"with-world"Social world: relationships, family, cultureLoneliness, conflict with a partner
Eigenwelt"own world"Personal world: relationship with oneself, self-awareness, identityLow self-worth, not knowing what one wants
Uberwelt (Überwelt)"over-world"Spiritual world: values, beliefs, meaning, idealsFeeling that life has no purpose
Trap: the notes credit all four to Heidegger, but the split came later. Ludwig Binswanger (Swiss psychiatrist, founder of Daseinsanalysis) set out the three modes Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt, building on Heidegger; Rollo May brought them into American psychology (Existence, 1958); Emmy van Deurzen later added the fourth, Uberwelt. They are not stages: all four run together. Eigenwelt is the self, Mitwelt is other people (a common mix-up).

What the "forces" mean

A "force" is a major movement that offered a whole new picture of human nature, as a rival to the ones before it. The label comes from Abraham Maslow, who called humanistic psychology the "Third Force" (an alternative to the two forces that dominated American psychology in the 1950s, psychoanalysis and behaviourism). In 1968 he described a coming "Fourth" psychology: transpersonal.

ForceSchool (key names)Picture of the person
1stPsychoanalysis (Freud)Driven by unconscious instincts and childhood conflicts
2ndBehaviourism (Watson, Skinner)Shaped by the environment through conditioning
3rdHumanistic (Maslow, Rogers), usually grouped with existential psychologyFree, growth-seeking, able to self-actualise
4thTranspersonal (Maslow, Anthony Sutich, Stanislav Grof)Able to go beyond the individual ego: spiritual, peak and mystical experiences
Trap: the forces are not a list of every school by date. Structuralism, functionalism, Gestalt and cognitive psychology are not counted as forces. In counselling books, multiculturalism is also called the "fourth force" (Paul Pedersen); for schools of psychology, the expected answer is transpersonal.

Methods: introspection vs phenomenology

Introspection (structuralism)Phenomenology (Gestalt, existential, humanistic)
What it is"Looking inward": a trained observer reports the basic elements (sensations, images, feelings) of his own conscious experience under controlled lab conditionsDescribing experience as it is actually lived, as a meaningful whole, without breaking it into parts or forcing a theory onto it
WhoWilhelm Wundt used experimental self-observation; Titchener made it a strict, systematic methodRooted in the philosophy of Edmund Husserl; used by Gestalt psychologists and later by existential and humanistic therapists
ExampleShown an apple, the observer must report "red, round, shiny", not "apple"Wertheimer's phi phenomenon (1912): two lights flashed one after the other are seen as one light moving; the movement exists in the experience, not in either light. In therapy: Rogers seeing the world from the client's own frame of reference
StyleAnalytic, breaks experience into elementsHolistic, descriptive
Trap: Titchener called naming the object ("apple") instead of reporting the raw sensations the stimulus error. Introspection fell out of favour because reports were private and trained observers in different labs disagreed; Watson's behaviourism rejected it completely. Both methods rely on first-person experience; the difference is parts (introspection) vs wholes (phenomenology).

7 · Yoga Psychology

  • Follows Samkhya philosophy.
  • Patanjali defined Yoga as: citta vritti nirodhah.

"Yogas citta vritti nirodhah" is Yoga Sutra 1.2 of Patanjali: "Yoga is the stilling (nirodha) of the fluctuations (vritti) of the mind (citta)."

  • Citta: the mind-stuff, the whole field of the mind.
  • Vritti: the waves or modifications that rise in it. Patanjali names five (Sutra 1.6): pramana (valid knowledge), viparyaya (wrong knowledge), vikalpa (imagination, ideas with no real object), nidra (sleep), smriti (memory).
  • Nirodha: restraint, stilling, cessation.

Classic image: a lake. When the surface is choppy you cannot see the bottom; when the waves settle, you see clearly. In the same way, when the vrittis are stilled, the seer rests in its own true nature (Sutra 1.3). The two means are abhyasa (steady practice) and vairagya (non-attachment) (Sutra 1.12).

Trap: here citta means the whole mind. In the antahkarana list below, chitta is only one of four parts (the memory store). Same word, two uses.

Types of Yoga

  • Hatha Yoga
  • Jnana Yoga
  • Bhakti Yoga
  • Karma Yoga
  • Ashtanga Yoga (Patanjali)
PathWhat it isKey practice / text
Hatha yogaPath of the body and breathAsanas, pranayama, mudras, bandhas and cleansing practices (shatkarma); main text Hatha Yoga Pradipika (Svatmarama, about 15th century). Aim: a steady body and breath as preparation for meditation
Jnana yogaPath of knowledgeDiscriminating the real from the unreal (viveka); listening to, reflecting on and meditating on the teaching "I am not the body, I am the Self"; linked to Advaita Vedanta

Trap: Swami Vivekananda (1890s) popularised the four main paths as karma, bhakti, jnana and raja, matched to the active, emotional, intellectual and meditative temperaments. Raja yoga = Patanjali's ashtanga yoga. Hatha yoga shares asana and pranayama with limbs 3 and 4, but it is a separate, later tradition.

Ashtanga Yoga (Patanjali) — Eight elements

  1. Yama: moral instructions
  2. Niyama: self-purification
  3. Asana: posture
  4. Pranayama: rhythmic breath control
  5. Pratyahara: sense withdrawal
  6. Dharana: concentration
  7. Dhyana: meditation
  8. Samadhi: high unitive consciousness

The eight limbs have been divided into two categories:

  • Bahiranga yoga (exterior)
  • Antaranga yoga (interior) — begins at Pratyahara
GroupLimbsWhat they work on
Bahiranga (external)1 Yama, 2 Niyama, 3 Asana, 4 PranayamaConduct with others, self-discipline, body, breath
Bridge5 PratyaharaPulls the senses back from their objects: the turn from outer to inner
Antaranga (internal)6 Dharana, 7 Dhyana, 8 SamadhiThe mind alone; practised together they are called samyama (Yoga Sutra 3.4)
Trap: Patanjali himself (Yoga Sutra 3.7) says dharana, dhyana and samadhi are "more internal than the five before them". So the classical split is 5 bahiranga + 3 antaranga, with pratyahara in the external group. These notes (and some books) start antaranga at pratyahara (4 + 4) because it is the bridge. If an MCQ asks which limbs are antaranga, dharana, dhyana, samadhi is the safest answer.

Yogic Cognition (Antahakarana)

In yogic understanding there are 16 dimensions of a human mind, categorized into 4 parts:

  • Manas: the sensory, thinking mind: takes in what the senses report and keeps weighing "this or that?" (doubt, deliberation)
  • Ahamkara: ego, the "I-maker" that claims experiences as "mine"
  • Buddhi: intellect, the higher mind (wisdom) that judges and decides
  • Chitta: the store of memories and past impressions (samskaras)
PartClassical functionRope-or-snake example (walking at dusk)
ManasSankalpa-vikalpa: taking in and doubting, "is it this or that?""There is a coiled thing on the path. Snake or rope?"
ChittaSmarana: rememberingBrings up memories of ropes, of snakes, and that snakes are dangerous
BuddhiNishchaya: deciding, discriminating"It is not moving and the ends are frayed: it is a rope."
AhamkaraAbhimana: identifying as "I" and "mine""I got scared; I am safe now."
Heads up: the PDF page says "Manas: keeps the memory" and "Chitta: consciousness". That follows Sadhguru's popular (Isha) version, where manas = memory and chitta = pure intelligence. In classical Vedanta (e.g. the Vivekachudamani) and in Indian psychology textbooks, chitta is the memory store and manas is the doubting, sensory mind. Use the classical version in exams. Also note that in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, citta is the whole mind (taken to include manas, buddhi and ahamkara).

Where "16 dimensions" comes from: the line "in yogic understanding there are 16 dimensions of the mind, which fall into four categories" is from Sadhguru's teaching, not from a classical text. That teaching does not publish which of the 16 belong to which of the four parts, and standard Indian psychology texts describe the antahkarana simply as four-fold. For exams, learn the four parts and their functions; there is no standard 16-way split to memorise.

How doshas and gunas shape temperament

Both are Indian "type" theories, a little like Hippocrates' four humours, but they work at two different levels:

Tridosha (Vata, Pitta, Kapha)Triguna (Sattva, Rajas, Tamas)
SourceAyurveda (Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita)Samkhya philosophy; Bhagavad Gita ch. 14 and 17
LevelBody: physical constitutionMind: mental constitution
How it sets temperamentEveryone has all three. The mix that dominates from conception is one's prakriti (constitution), which shapes build, health and temperament.All three are present in every mind. Whichever dominates colours a person's thinking, feeling and behaviour, and the balance can shift with diet, habits and practice.
  • Vata-dominant: lively, quick, creative; under stress anxious, restless, scattered.
  • Pitta-dominant: sharp, focused, ambitious; under stress irritable, angry, critical.
  • Kapha-dominant: calm, steady, loyal; under stress slow, lethargic, possessive.

Example: after failing an exam, a sattvic response is to calmly review the mistakes and plan; a rajasic response is anger, blaming others and frantic activity; a tamasic response is sleeping, giving up and feeling hopeless.

Trap: doshas = body (Ayurveda); gunas = mind (Samkhya). The link between them: the Charaka Samhita calls rajas and tamas the two doshas of the mind, while sattva is the clear, balanced quality. Sattva is the ideal, but the Gita (14.5) says all three gunas bind the self.

Avasthatraya (the three states) and Turiya (the fourth)

Avasthatraya literally means "the three states" (avastha = state, traya = three). The Mandukya Upanishad describes these three and then a fourth, turiya (literally "the fourth"), which is not one more state but the pure consciousness present through all three.

StateWhat happensName of the self in it (Mandukya)
Jagrat (Jagratavastha): wakingConsciousness faces outward; we experience the physical world through the sensesVaishvanara (Vishva)
Swapna (Swapnavastha): dreamingConsciousness faces inward; the mind builds its own world out of stored impressionsTaijasa
Sushupti: deep, dreamless sleep (the notes call it Prajnavastha)No dreams, no desires, no awareness of objects; a unified, blissful restPrajna
Turiya (Turiyavastha): "the fourth"Pure witness-consciousness (sakshi), identical with Atman; it underlies the other three and is not touched by themAtman itself
Heads up: the PDF page says "Avasthatraya (the four states of consciousness)". Strictly, avasthatraya is three states: jagrat, swapna, sushupti. Turiya is the fourth that lies beyond them. "Prajna" is the name of the self in deep sleep, so the standard name of the state itself is sushupti. The Mandukya also maps the sounds A, U, M onto waking, dream and deep sleep, and the silence after AUM onto turiya.

Indriya (Senses)

In Jain thought (Umasvati's Tattvartha Sutra, ch. 2), every sense has two sides, a physical one and a psychic one, and both are needed for actual perception.

TypeMeaningIts two parts
Dravyendriya (physical sense)The material sense organNirvritti: the formed organ itself (e.g. the eye). Upakarana: its supporting, protective parts (e.g. eyelids, eyelashes)
Bhavendriya (psychic sense)The soul's own power to know through that organLabdhi (Labdi; power): the capacity to sense, which appears as knowledge-obscuring karma thins out. Upayoga (manifestation): actually using that capacity, i.e. attending and perceiving

The five senses, each having both a dravya and a bhava side: Chakshu (eyes, sight), Shrotra (ears, hearing), Ghrana (nose, smell), Jihva or Rasana (tongue, taste), Sparsha (skin, touch).

Example: your eyes are healthy (dravyendriya intact) and you have the power of sight (labdhi), but while lost in thought you fail to see a friend waving at you: upayoga, the act of attending, was missing.

Heads up: the PDF lists the five senses under "Dravyendriya or Gyanendriya". In the Jain scheme the five senses are not a subtype of dravyendriya; each of the five has both a physical (dravya) and a psychic (bhava) side, and dravyendriya's own two parts are nirvritti and upakarana. "Jnanendriya" (organ of knowledge, as opposed to karmendriya, organ of action) is a Samkhya and Vedanta term, not the Jain name for dravyendriya. Jains also rank living beings by number of senses, from one-sensed beings such as plants (touch only) to five-sensed beings such as humans.

Characteristics of jiva or self

  • Jiva has bio-energies called prana.
  • Jiva is endowed with consciousness called chaitanya.
  • Jiva is the enjoyer of the fruits of his own actions.
  • Psychic exertion distinguishes the living from the non-living.
  • Jiva is actively engaged in its karmic journey, experiencing the result of its actions and striving towards liberation.
Key: In Jainism, the highest form of pure knowledge a soul can attain is called Kevala Jnana.

Types of self

  • Unsullied self: contains metaphysical energy
  • Sullied self: empirical self

Sufism

  • Dualist philosophy.
  • Believes in the soul.
  • No rebirth.

Layers of Sufism (outer to inner)

  1. Shariat (outward religion or law) — exoteric layer
  2. Tariquat (the path) — esoteric layer
  3. Haqeeqat (the inner truth)
  4. Maarifat (Divine knowledge)

Path in Sufism

  • Tawbah (repentance)
  • Wara (fear of the Lord)
  • Zuhd (renunciation or detachment)
  • Faqr (poverty)
  • Sabr (patience)
  • Tawakkul (trust, surrender)
  • Rida (satisfaction)

Levels of the self or nafs

  1. Nafs Al Ammara — the commanding self / the tyrannical self; two sublevels: the animal self and the satanic self
  2. Nafs Al Lawwama — the blaming self / the regretful self
  3. Nafs Al Mulhamah — the inspired self
  4. Nafs Al Muttma'ina — the secure self / the serene self
  5. Nafs Al Radiyah — the content self / the pleased self
  6. Nafs Al Mardiyyah — the gratified self / pleasing self
  7. Nafs Al Kamilah — the complete self / the pure self

The seven levels describe the ego (nafs) being purified step by step, from a self ruled by desire to a self wholly surrendered to God. Several names come from the Qur'an; the seven-step ladder is a later Sufi teaching model (in English, see Robert Frager, Heart, Self and Soul, 1999).

LevelWhat the person is like
1. Ammara (commanding)Ruled by desire, anger, greed and pride; obeys whatever the ego commands and does not see its own faults (Qur'an 12:53). Its two sublevels: the animal self chases pleasure without caring about right or wrong; the lower satanic self sets itself up against God.
2. Lawwama (blaming)Conscience wakes up (Qur'an 75:2): still slips, but now feels regret, blames itself and repents; swings between good and bad.
3. Mulhamah (inspired)Begins to enjoy prayer and good deeds and to receive inner inspiration (Qur'an 91:8); can still fall back, e.g. into spiritual pride.
4. Mutma'inna (secure / serene)Inner peace (Qur'an 89:27): trusts God, and the ego's desires have largely quietened.
5. Radiyah (content / pleased)Accepts whatever happens, good or bad, as God's will, with patience and gratitude: pleased with God (Qur'an 89:28).
6. Mardiyyah (pleasing)Pleasing to God (Qur'an 89:28): humble, loving, living in service of others.
7. Kamilah (complete / pure)The ego is fully purified; the "perfected human" (insan al-kamil) of Sufi thought.
Trap: the order runs from the commanding self (lowest) to the complete/pure self (highest), and lawwama (2) comes before mulhamah (3). Radiyah = pleased with God; Mardiyyah = pleasing to God. A handy psychology parallel: impulse-driven (id-like) behaviour, then guilt and conscience, then self-transcendence.
Who → what (philosophies): Buddhism → monist, no soul (anatta); Hinduism → dualist, believes in soul (Advaita is monist); Jainism → dualist, believes in soul; Sufism → dualist, believes in soul, no rebirth.
