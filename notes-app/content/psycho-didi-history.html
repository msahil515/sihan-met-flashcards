The full history of psychology as a discipline: the Western chronology from the first psychology course (1875) through positive psychology (1998), the Greek theorists (Hippocrates' humoral theory, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle) and the 17th-century philosophers who framed the mind, the major schools of psychology with their founders and "forces," the parallel history of psychology and clinical psychology in India, and the Indian systems (Yoga, Buddhism, Hinduism, Jainism, Sufism) that form Indian psychology.

2 · Greek Theorists

The Mind–Body Argument

  • Monists: mind and body are a single entity.
  • Dualists: mind and body are separate entities.

Hippocrates — Humoral Theory

Hippocrates was a dualist. His humoral theory linked four bodily humors to four temperaments.

HumorTemperamentCharacter
Black bileMelancholicSad, depressed, poetic and artistic
Yellow bileCholericFiery, energetic, and passionate
BloodSanguineWarm-hearted, cheerful, optimistic, and confident
PhlegmPhlegmaticSlow, quiet, rational, and consistent

Socrates

  • Dualist.
  • Focused on morality.
  • Explained eudemonia: long-lasting contentment, peace and happiness comes from building virtues like honesty, generosity, kindness, altruism, etc.

Plato

  • Dualist.
  • Allegory of the cave and allegory of the chariot.
  • Tripartite theory of the soul: rational (mind), emotional (spirited), and appetites (pleasures).
  • Theory of forms: forms are universals and are the purest representation of things that actually exist.

Aristotle

  • Monist.
  • Gave the first theory of cognitive sciences; described sensation and perception — special sensible and common sensible.
  • Theory of associationism: law of contiguity, law of frequency, law of similarity, law of contrast.
Trap: Hippocrates, Socrates and Plato were dualists; Aristotle was a monist. The four laws of association (contiguity, frequency, similarity, contrast) belong to Aristotle.

3 · 17th-Century Philosophers

René Descartes

  • Famously stated "cogito, ergo sum" ("I think, therefore I am").
  • Innatism: the mind is born with ideas and knowledge.
  • Dualist.
  • Believed the pineal gland to be the seat of the soul.

John Locke

  • Empiricism: all knowledge comes from the senses.
  • Tabula Rasa: individuals are born as a blank slate and all knowledge is acquired from the external world.
  • Rejected innatism.

Immanuel Kant

  • Rejected the soul as the subject matter of psychology.
  • Types of knowledge:
    • A priori knowledge: innate knowledge — rationalism.
    • A posteriori knowledge: empiricism.

William Dilthey

  • Best known for the way he distinguished between the natural and human sciences.
  • Wrote Ideas for a Descriptive and Analytic Psychology (1894).
Who → what: Descartes → innatism + "cogito, ergo sum" + pineal gland; Locke → empiricism + tabula rasa; Kant → a priori / a posteriori knowledge; Dilthey → natural vs. human sciences.

4 · Major Schools of Psychology

Each school is defined by its founder(s), method of study, and central focus. Several are tagged as a "force" in psychology.

School (year)Founder(s)Description
Structuralism (1896) Edward Bradford Titchener Aimed to discover the structural elements of the mind. Method: introspection. Emphasized basic conscious elements.
Functionalism (1896) William James Assumed that thinking, like the senses, developed because it was adaptive — it contributed to our ancestors' survival. The role of consciousness is adaptation.
Psychoanalysis (1900) Sigmund Freud Two levels of reality: conscious and unconscious. Unconscious instincts: eros (life instincts) and thanatos (death instincts). Parts of personality: id, ego, superego. 1st force in psychology.
Gestalt (1912) Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka, Wolfgang Köhler Method: phenomenology. Studied conscious experience and mental processes. Focus on holism.
Behaviourism (1913) John Watson; most famous behaviourist: B. F. Skinner Focus on behaviour only and discredited mental processes. 2nd force in psychology.
Existential (1940s) Rollo May Method: phenomenology. Focus on freedom, meaning, and death. Martin Heidegger's Dasein: Umwelt (nature), Mitwelt (people), Eigenwelt (oneself), Uberwelt (spirituality).
Humanistic (1954) Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow Method: phenomenology. Focused on the potential of individuals and highlighted each person's subjectivity, consciousness, free will and other special human qualities. 3rd force in psychology.
Cognitive (1956) Ulric Neisser Focused on cognition and mental process. Analogy of the brain as a computer.
Transpersonal (1960s) Abraham Maslow and Victor Frankl Concerned with the study of humanity's highest potential, and the recognition, understanding, and realization of unitive, spiritual, and transcendental states of human consciousness. 4th force in psychology.
Key — the four forces: 1st = Psychoanalysis, 2nd = Behaviourism, 3rd = Humanistic, 4th = Transpersonal.
Trap: Behaviourism was founded by John Watson; B. F. Skinner is named as the most famous behaviourist, not the founder.

7 · Yoga Psychology

  • Follows Samkhya philosophy.
  • Patanjali defined Yoga as: citta vritti nirodhah.

Types of Yoga

  • Hatha Yoga
  • Jnana Yoga
  • Bhakti Yoga
  • Karma Yoga
  • Ashtanga Yoga (Patanjali)

Ashtanga Yoga (Patanjali) — Eight elements

  1. Yama: moral instructions
  2. Niyama: self-purification
  3. Asana: posture
  4. Pranayama: rhythmic breath control
  5. Pratyahara: sense withdrawal
  6. Dharana: concentration
  7. Dhyana: meditation
  8. Samadhi: high unitive consciousness

The eight limbs have been divided into two categories:

  • Bahiranga yoga (exterior)
  • Antaranga yoga (interior) — begins at Pratyahara

Yogic Cognition (Antahakarana)

In yogic understanding there are 16 dimensions of a human mind, categorized into 4 parts:

  • Manas: keeps the memory
  • Ahamkara: ego
  • Buddhi: wisdom or higher mind
  • Chitta: consciousness

8 · Indian Psychology

Buddhism

  • Monist philosophy; atheistic.
  • Anatta: no soul.

Three main schools

  • Theravada or Hinayana Buddhism
  • Mahayana Buddhism
  • Vajrayana Buddhism

Four Noble Truths

  • Dukkha (suffering)
  • Tanha (desire)
  • Suffering can be overcome
  • The Eightfold Path is the solution to suffering

The Noble Eightfold Path

  1. Right Understanding
  2. Right Thought
  3. Right Speech
  4. Right Action
  5. Right Livelihood
  6. Right Effort
  7. Right Mindfulness
  8. Right Concentration

Tripitaka

  • Vinaya Pitaka (Discipline Basket)
  • Sutta Pitaka (Discourse Basket)
  • Abhidhamma Pitaka (Higher Teaching Basket)

The Three Jewels (Three Refuges)

  • Buddha (the enlightened one)
  • Dharma (teachings)
  • Sangha (community)

Hinduism

  • Dualist philosophy (Advaita philosophy is monist).
  • Believes in the soul.

Self in the Samkhya Philosophy

Jiva or living being is made up of:

  • Prakriti: concerned with the material world
  • Purusha: represents Atman — metaphysical or cosmic energy

Advaita Philosophy

  • "Advaita" refers to the identity of the self (atman) and the whole (brahman).
  • Considers moksha achievable by removing avidya (ignorance).

Purushartha — the four goals

  • Dharma: values
  • Artha: prosperity
  • Kama: love
  • Moksha: liberation

Tridoshas

DoshaElementsBody parts associated
VataSpace and airSmall and large intestines
PittaFire and waterLiver, spleen, gall bladder, stomach, pancreas
KaphaEarth and waterSinuses, nostrils, throat, bronchi, lungs

Triguna

  • Sattva: intelligent, fortitude, gentle, truthful, benevolent, virtuous
  • Rajas: energy, harsh, angry, excessive activity, strong emotions, inclining towards violence and aggression
  • Tamas: mass, heavy, obstructing, ignorance or lack of knowledge (confused), inactivity, sleep (more), generally dejected always, indecent

Avasthatraya (the four states of consciousness)

  • Jagratavastha
  • Swapnavastha
  • Prajnavastha
  • Turiyavastha

Panchkoshas

  • Annamaya Kosha: food body
  • Pranamaya Kosha: breathing body
  • Manomaya Kosha: mental body
  • Vijnanamaya Kosha: intelligence
  • Anandamaya Kosha: bliss body

Bhagavad Gita

The Gita upholds the necessity of Karma. According to the Bhagavad Gita, the spiritual journey towards self-realization and attainment of peace involves movement across several stages:

  • Abhyasa (exercise)
  • Gyana (knowledge)
  • Dhyana (meditation)
  • Tyaga (renunciation)
  • Shanti (peace)

Jainism

  • Atheistic, dualist philosophy.
  • Believes in the soul.
  • Teachers called tirthankars (24 in number).
  • Gained prominence under Lord Mahavira.

Focus areas

  • Ahimsa
  • Moksha
  • Self-discipline or asceticism

Indriya (Senses)

  • Bhavendriya: Labdi (power), Upayoga (manifestation)
  • Dravyendriya or Gyanendriya: Chakshu (Eyes), Shrotra (Ears), Ghrana (Nose), Jihva (Tongue), Sparsha (Skin)

Characteristics of jiva or self

  • Jiva has bio-energies called prana.
  • Jiva is endowed with consciousness called chaitanya.
  • Jiva is the enjoyer of the fruits of his own actions.
  • Psychic exertion distinguishes the living from the non-living.
  • Jiva is actively engaged in its karmic journey, experiencing the result of its actions and striving towards liberation.
Key: In Jainism, the highest form of pure knowledge a soul can attain is called Kevala Jnana.

Types of self

  • Unsullied self: contains metaphysical energy
  • Sullied self: empirical self

Sufism

  • Dualist philosophy.
  • Believes in the soul.
  • No rebirth.

Layers of Sufism (outer to inner)

  1. Shariat (outward religion or law) — exoteric layer
  2. Tariquat (the path) — esoteric layer
  3. Haqeeqat (the inner truth)
  4. Maarifat (Divine knowledge)

Path in Sufism

  • Tawbah (repentance)
  • Wara (fear of the Lord)
  • Zuhd (renunciation or detachment)
  • Faqr (poverty)
  • Sabr (patience)
  • Tawakkul (trust, surrender)
  • Rida (satisfaction)

Levels of the self or nafs

  1. Nafs Al Ammara — the commanding self / the tyrannical self; two sublevels: the animal self and the satanic self
  2. Nafs Al Lawwama — the blaming self / the regretful self
  3. Nafs Al Mulhamah — the inspired self
  4. Nafs Al Muttma'ina — the secure self / the serene self
  5. Nafs Al Radiyah — the content self / the pleased self
  6. Nafs Al Mardiyyah — the gratified self / pleasing self
  7. Nafs Al Kamilah — the complete self / the pure self
Who → what (philosophies): Buddhism → monist, no soul (anatta); Hinduism → dualist, believes in soul (Advaita is monist); Jainism → dualist, believes in soul; Sufism → dualist, believes in soul, no rebirth.
