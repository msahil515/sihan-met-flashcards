1. Nagy's Family Systems (Contextual Family Therapy)

Core: Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy founded Contextual Family Therapy, built on relational ethics and the multigenerational ledger of fairness.
Exam answer Nagy = Contextual Family Therapy; signature concepts are invisible loyalties, the intergenerational ledger, and relational ethics (therapist uses multidirected partiality).
  • Invisible loyalties — unconscious commitments to the family of origin that drive present behaviour across generations.
  • Ledger of entitlements and indebtedments — a running balance of what each member has given vs. received; injustice ("destructive entitlement") gets passed down.
  • Relational ethics — fairness/trustworthiness is the deepest level of family life (above facts, psychology, and transactions).
  • Four dimensions of relational reality: (1) facts, (2) individual psychology, (3) transactional patterns/systems, (4) relational ethics.
  • Multidirected partiality — the therapist sequentially sides with each member so everyone is heard (not neutral, not one-sided).
Hook: Nagy = "ledger + invisible loyalties + relational ethics."
Trap: don't confuse the family-therapy founders.

Murray BowenBowenian / intergenerationalDifferentiation of self, triangles, genogram
Salvador MinuchinStructuralBoundaries, subsystems, enmeshment/disengagement

Virginia SatirExperiential / communicationCommunication stances, family sculpting, self-esteem

2. CGI Scale (Clinical Global Impression)

Core: a clinician-rated global measure of illness severity and treatment response, scored on simple 7-point scales.
Exam answer CGI = clinician-rated, 7-point global scale of severity (CGI-S) and improvement (CGI-I); on CGI-I, 4 = no change, 1 = very much improved, 7 = very much worse.
  • CGI-S (Severity): 1 = normal/not ill → 7 = among the most extremely ill. Rated relative to the clinician's experience with that disorder.
  • CGI-I (Improvement): 1 = very much improved, 4 = no change, 7 = very much worse (rated against baseline).
  • CGI-E / Efficacy Index: therapeutic effect weighed against side effects.
  • Developed at the NIMH; a quick global anchor in clinical trials.
Trap: CGI is clinician-rated and global; contrast with disorder-specific scales (PANSS, HAM-D, Y-BOCS). "4" means no change on CGI-I, not "moderate."

3. AUDIT Scale (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test)

Core: a WHO-developed 10-item screen for hazardous/harmful alcohol use; total score 0–40.
Exam answer AUDIT = WHO, 10 items, score 0–40, screens current hazardous/harmful drinking; cut-off ≥ 8. (Not CAGE, not MAST.)
  • Items 1–3 = consumption; 4–6 = dependence; 7–10 = alcohol-related harm.
  • Cut-off ≥ 8 indicates hazardous/harmful drinking; higher bands suggest likely dependence (often ≥ 15–20).
  • AUDIT-C = the 3 consumption items only, a brief version.
Trap: AUDIT is WHO, current use, 10 items. Don't confuse with CAGE (4 questions, lifetime), MAST (longer), or SADQ (severity of dependence).

4. RAVLT — Dimensions / What It Measures

Core: the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test assesses verbal learning and memory using a 15-word list across repeated trials.
Exam answer RAVLT measures verbal (auditory) learning and memory — immediate span, learning curve, interference, delayed retention, and recognition (15-word list, sensitive to hippocampal damage).
  • Structure: List A read 5 times (Trials 1–5), interference List B, immediate recall of A (Trial 6), delayed recall (~20–30 min), and recognition.
  • Dimensions captured: immediate memory span (Trial 1), learning curve/acquisition (Trials 1→5), proactive & retroactive interference, retention/forgetting (delayed vs. Trial 5), and recognition memory.
  • Sensitive to medial temporal / hippocampal dysfunction (Alzheimer's, amnesia).
Trap: RAVLT = auditory/verbal list learning. The visual analogue is the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure (visuospatial memory + construction). Don't mix them.

5. Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (India)

Core: India's MHCA 2017 (in force 2018) replaced the Mental Health Act 1987; rights-based, aligned with the UN CRPD.
Exam answer Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (effective 2018, repealed the 1987 Act): decriminalised attempted suicide (Sec 115), introduced Advance Directive + Nominated Representative, bans unmodified ECT.
  • Decriminalised attempted suicide (Sec 115) — presumed to be under severe stress, not punished.
  • Advance Directive — a person may state how they wish to be treated, plus a Nominated Representative.
  • Bans unmodified ECT, ECT for minors (except with board approval), and sterilisation as treatment.
  • Mental Health Review Boards (MHRB); Central & State Mental Health Authorities.
  • Rights to access, confidentiality, community living, and free treatment for the poor/homeless.
Trap: "MHA" = Mental Healthcare Act 2017 in the Indian exam context. Year traps: passed 2017, effective 2018; repealed the 1987 Act. Capacity-based, not custodial.

6. Tests Notified in the Gazette of India

Core: likely tests the RCI Act, 1992 framework — only RCI-recognised qualifications and registered professionals may practise; recognition is notified in the Gazette of India.
Exam answer Legal recognition runs through the RCI Act 1992: recognised qualifications/courses and disability-assessment tools are valid only when notified in the Gazette of India. (Exact stem uncertain — anchor on RCI + Gazette = legal recognition.)
  • The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register and notifies recognised courses (e.g., M.Phil Clinical Psychology) via Gazette notifications.
  • Under the RPwD Act 2016 and assessment rules, the specific tools used for disability certification (intellectual disability, autism, SLD) are notified by Gazette — e.g., IQ + adaptive-behaviour tools standardised on Indian populations.
  • Practical reading: tests usable for legal/certification purposes in India must be officially notified/standardised, not arbitrary.
Flag (moderate confidence): the exact "tests in the Gazette" depend on the question's framing (RCI recognition of qualifications vs. notified disability-assessment tools). Anchor: RCI Act 1992 + Gazette notification = legal recognition.

7. Levodopa (L-DOPA)

Core: a dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier (dopamine itself cannot) and is the gold-standard treatment for Parkinson's disease.
Exam answer Levodopa = a dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier, given with carbidopa, gold-standard for Parkinson's disease (boosts nigrostriatal dopamine; can induce dyskinesias/psychosis).
  • Converted to dopamine by DOPA decarboxylase (AADC); given with carbidopa/benserazide (peripheral decarboxylase inhibitors) to cut peripheral side effects and boost central delivery.
  • Pathway it boosts: nigrostriatal dopamine (substantia nigra → striatum), the system depleted in Parkinson's.
  • Long-term problems: motor fluctuations ("wearing-off", "on-off"), dyskinesias; can induce psychosis/hallucinations.
  • Psych link: the dopamine-excess theory of schizophrenia — too much dopamine activity can produce psychotic symptoms.
Trap: levodopa ≠ dopamine agonist. Antipsychotics (D2 blockers) do the opposite and can cause drug-induced parkinsonism; levodopa can worsen psychosis.

8. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

Core: the standard scale for level of consciousness, especially after head injury. Score range 3 (deep coma) to 15 (fully alert).
Exam answer GCS is scored /15 = Eye (4) + Verbal (5) + Motor (6); minimum 3 (never 0), ≤ 8 = coma/severe (intubate).
ComponentRangeBest response
Eye opening1–44 = spontaneous
Verbal response1–55 = oriented
Motor response1–66 = obeys commands
  • Severity: Mild 13–15, Moderate 9–12, Severe ≤ 8 (≤ 8 = "intubate").
  • Lowest possible = 3, never 0. Devised by Teasdale & Jennett (Glasgow, 1974).
Trap: eye opening max 4, verbal max 5, motor max 6 (4+5+6 = 15). Minimum is 3, not 0.

9. Minuchin — Structural Family Therapy

Core: Salvador Minuchin founded Structural Family Therapy; the family's structure (boundaries, subsystems, hierarchy) maintains symptoms.
Exam answer Minuchin = Structural Family Therapy; key concepts are boundaries (rigid→disengaged, diffuse→enmeshed), subsystems, and the psychosomatic family.
  • Subsystems: spousal, parental, sibling.
  • Boundaries: clear (healthy), rigid → disengagement, diffuse → enmeshment.
  • Psychosomatic family features: enmeshment, overprotectiveness, rigidity, conflict avoidance (studied in anorexia, asthma, diabetes).
  • Techniques: joining, enactment, boundary-making, unbalancing, reframing.
Hook: Minuchin = "maps, boundaries, enmeshment."
Trap: Minuchin (structural, boundaries) vs. Bowen (differentiation/triangles) vs. Haley (strategic/directives) vs. Nagy (contextual/ledger). "Munichin" in recall = Minuchin.

10. Durkheim's Theory of Suicide

Core: Émile Durkheim (1897, Le Suicide) explained suicide sociologically via two axes: social integration and social regulation.
Exam answer Durkheim's 4 types: Egoistic (low integration), Altruistic (high integration), Anomic (low regulation), Fatalistic (high regulation).
TypeCauseExample
EgoisticToo low integrationIsolated, weak social ties
AltruisticToo high integrationSelf-sacrifice for the group (soldier, sati)
AnomicToo low regulationSudden disruption (economic crash, divorce)
FatalisticToo high regulationOppressive control (prisoners, slaves)
Hook: Integration low/high = Egoistic/Altruistic; Regulation low/high = Anomic/Fatalistic.
Trap: Durkheim is sociological, not psychological. Contrast with Freud (aggression inward), Shneidman (psychache), Beck (hopelessness), Joiner (interpersonal theory).

12. National Mental Health Survey (NMHS) — Prevalence

Core: the NMHS of India 2015–16 (led by NIMHANS, Bengaluru) is the landmark Indian epidemiological survey.
Exam answer NMHS 2015–16 (NIMHANS): current prevalence ~10.6%, lifetime ~13.7%, treatment gap ~83%.
  • Current prevalence of mental disorders ~10.6%; lifetime prevalence ~13.7%.
  • Treatment gap very high: overall ~83%; severe mental disorders ~75%.
  • ~1 in 20 Indians suffers from depression; alcohol use disorder notably high in men.
  • Higher burden in urban metros; large gender, age, income gradients.
Trap: lock the headline numbers: current 10.6%, lifetime 13.7%, treatment gap ~83%, year 2015–16, conducted by NIMHANS. Don't quote a WHO global figure.

13. Functional Hallucinations

Core: a hallucination that occurs simultaneously with, and triggered by, a real external stimulus in the same modality — both perceived together.
Exam answer Functional hallucination = a real external stimulus triggers a hallucination in the SAME modality, both perceived together (e.g. voice heard only while the tap runs). Contrast: reflex = cross-modality.
  • Example: hearing a voice only while a tap runs, both the running water and the voice perceived at once.
  • Key distinction from reflex hallucination: in functional, stimulus and hallucination are in the same modality; in reflex, a stimulus in one modality triggers a hallucination in a different one.
TypeDefinition
FunctionalReal stimulus + hallucination, same modality, perceived together
ReflexStimulus in one modality → hallucination in another
ExtracampineHallucination outside the sensory field (seeing behind one's head)
AutoscopicSeeing oneself / a double
Hypnagogic / HypnopompicWhile falling asleep / on waking (can be normal)
Trap: functional = stimulus and hallucination coexist in the same sense; if it stops when the stimulus stops, that's the cue. Reflex = cross-modality.

14. PANSS (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale)

Core: a 30-item clinician-rated scale for schizophrenia symptom severity (Kay, Fiszbein & Opler, 1987).
Exam answer PANSS = 30 items in 3 subscales (Positive 7, Negative 7, General 16), each rated 1–7, total 30–210, for schizophrenia.
  • 3 subscales: Positive (7 items), Negative (7 items), General Psychopathology (16 items).
  • Each item scored 1 (absent) to 7 (extreme); total range 30–210.
  • Requires a semi-structured interview; gold standard in schizophrenia trials.
Trap: 30 items, 7-point items, minimum total 30 (not 0). Contrast with SAPS/SANS (Andreasen, separate positive/negative scales) and BPRS (18–24 items, broader).

15. WASI (Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence)

Core: a brief, screening measure of intelligence yielding FSIQ, from the Wechsler tradition.
Exam answer WASI = brief/abbreviated Wechsler IQ screen, 4 subtests (Vocabulary, Similarities, Block Design, Matrix Reasoning) giving FSIQ. Not the full WAIS/WISC battery.
  • WASI / WASI-II has 4 subtests: Vocabulary, Similarities (Verbal Comprehension); Block Design, Matrix Reasoning (Perceptual Reasoning).
  • Can give a 2-subtest FSIQ (Vocabulary + Matrix Reasoning) or a 4-subtest FSIQ.
  • Ages ~6–90; used when a full battery isn't feasible.
Trap: WASI = abbreviated/screening, not the full battery. Don't confuse with WAIS (full adult), WISC (children), WPPSI (preschool), or WMS (memory). "Weschler" = Wechsler.

16. Attunement (Affect Attunement)

Core: Daniel Stern's concept of affect attunement — the caregiver matching/sharing the infant's internal emotional state (often cross-modally), central to intersubjectivity and the developing self.
Exam answer Affect attunement is Daniel Stern's concept — caregiver cross-modally matches the infant's affect, marking the subjective/intersubjective self (~7–9 mo). (Sihan's stem was garbled; anchor on Stern + intersubjective self.)
  • Part of Stern's senses of self (emergent, core, subjective/intersubjective, verbal). Affect attunement appears with the subjective/intersubjective self (~7–9 months).
  • Distinct from simple imitation: attunement matches the quality/intensity/contour of affect, not the literal behaviour.
  • Related: mirroring (Kohut/Winnicott), mentalization (Fonagy), marked mirroring, contingent responsiveness — all feed secure attachment.
Flag (low confidence on exact wording): Sihan's recall ("attunement workup and another dimension is a part of what") was garbled. Most likely intent: affect attunement as one component of Stern's intersubjective self / dimensions of the self. Anchor: Stern, affect attunement, cross-modal, intersubjectivity.

17. R² in Multiple Regression

Core: R² (coefficient of determination) = the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the set of predictors. Range 0 to 1.
Exam answer R² = the proportion of variance in Y explained by the predictors (coefficient of determination, 0–1); e.g. R² = 0.40 means 40% of variance explained. Use adjusted R² to compare models.
  • R² = 0.40 → the predictors jointly explain 40% of the variance in Y.
  • In multiple regression, R² is the square of the multiple correlation R (correlation between observed and predicted Y).
  • Adjusted R² penalises for the number of predictors — use it to compare models, because raw R² never decreases when predictors are added.
  • 1 − R² = the coefficient of alienation (unexplained variance).
Trap: R² is variance explained, not correlation strength itself and not causation. Adding predictors always inflates R²; adjusted R² can go down. High R² with poor predictors can be spurious/overfit.

18. Floortime (DIR Model) as an Intervention

Core: Floortime is the core technique of the DIR (Developmental, Individual-difference, Relationship-based) model by Stanley Greenspan & Serena Wieder, used mainly for Autism Spectrum Disorder and developmental delays.
Exam answer Floortime is the core technique of the DIR model (Greenspan & Wieder), a developmental, relationship-based, child-led intervention for autism (not behavioural like ABA). (Stem was cut off at "intervention t..." — almost certainly "for autism".)
  • Relationship- and play-based, child-led: the adult joins the child's interest and "opens and closes circles of communication" to expand engagement.
  • Targets functional emotional developmental capacities (regulation, engagement, two-way communication, complex problem-solving, symbolic thinking).
  • Emphasises affect and relationships as the engine of development, not drilled skills.
Trap: Floortime/DIR is developmental & relationship-based. Contrast with ABA (behavioral/reinforcement), TEACCH (structured teaching), PECS (picture exchange), and ESDM (developmental + ABA blend). The cut-off "...intervention t" almost certainly = "for autism."

28. Primary, Secondary, Tertiary Prevention (Caplan)

Core: Gerald Caplan's public-health model of prevention, defined by when the intervention occurs relative to disorder onset.
Exam answer Caplan's levels: Primary = before onset (reduce incidence), Secondary = early detection/screening (reduce prevalence), Tertiary = rehab in established illness (reduce disability).
LevelTimingGoalPsych example
PrimaryBefore onsetReduce incidence (new cases)Parenting programs, stress-management, anti-stigma campaigns
SecondaryEarly stageReduce prevalence via early detection & prompt treatmentDepression screening, suicide-risk screening, early psychosis services
TertiaryEstablished illnessReduce disability, prevent relapsePsychosocial rehabilitation, relapse prevention, vocational rehab
Hook: Primary = prevent (incidence) · Secondary = screen (prevalence) · Tertiary = rehab (disability).
Trap (the favourite compare): don't confuse Caplan's model with Gordon's / IOM framework, which classifies prevention by target population: Universal (everyone), Selective (at-risk groups), Indicated (high-risk individuals with early signs). Caplan = timing; Gordon = who you target. Some add quaternary prevention = protecting patients from over-medicalisation.

29. Primary Auditory Cortex → Heschl's Gyrus

Core / answer: the primary auditory cortex is Heschl's gyrus (transverse temporal gyrus) on the superior surface of the superior temporal gyrus, Brodmann areas 41 and 42.
Exam answer Heschl's gyrus = primary auditory cortex (transverse temporal gyrus, BA 41/42). Not supramarginal, angular, or fusiform.
  • Tonotopic organisation: different frequencies map to different locations (low anterolateral, high posteromedial).
  • Receives input from the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus.
Option on paperWhat it actually is
Heschl's gyrus ✓Primary auditory cortex (BA 41/42) — correct answer
Supramarginal gyrusInferior parietal (BA 40); phonological processing, part of Wernicke's area / language
Angular gyrusInferior parietal (BA 39); reading, writing, semantic integration (lesion → Gerstmann syndrome)
Fusiform gyrusVentral temporal/occipital (BA 37); face & word-form recognition (lesion → prosopagnosia)
Trap: distinguish the areas — Heschl's gyrus = primary auditory (hearing); Wernicke's area (BA 22, posterior STG) = auditory comprehension; Broca's area (BA 44/45, inferior frontal) = speech production. The "supra-something" decoy is the supramarginal gyrus; "fusiform" is faces, not sound.

21. PANSS Profile — High Positive, Moderate Negative, Very Low HAM-D

Core: a patient with prominent positive symptoms, moderate negative symptoms, and a very low Hamilton Depression (HAM-D) score has a psychosis-dominant, mood-minimal picture. The positive symptoms lead and depression is essentially absent → this is schizophrenia, not a depressive or mood disorder.
Exam answer Schizophrenia (NOT depression / not psychotic depression / not schizoaffective). High positive PANSS + low HAM-D = primary psychosis with negligible mood component.
  • What each scale measures: PANSS rates schizophrenia across Positive (P), Negative (N), and General psychopathology (G); HAM-D rates the severity of depression.
  • Reading the profile: high P (delusions, hallucinations, conceptual disorganisation) + moderate N (blunted affect, withdrawal) means active psychosis dominates the picture.
  • Why the very low HAM-D is the deciding clue: a very low HAM-D means depressive symptoms are minimal, so depression is not driving the presentation. You cannot call it psychotic depression or schizoaffective disorder when the mood burden is near zero.
  • Conclusion: psychosis without a meaningful mood component = schizophrenia. The high-positive PANSS is the primary disorder; the low HAM-D actively rules depression out.
DisorderPsychosisMood / HAM-DFit here?
SchizophreniaDominant (high positive symptoms)Minimal / very low HAM-D✓ Best fit
Psychotic depression (MDD with psychotic features)Present but mood-congruent (guilt, nihilism)HIGH HAM-D (severe depression)✗ HAM-D would be high, not low
Schizoaffective disorderPresentProminent mood episode (depressive or manic) concurrently✗ needs a substantial mood episode
Trap: the examiner dangles a mood answer to tempt you. Psychotic depression = mood-congruent delusions + a HIGH HAM-D; schizoaffective = psychosis plus a prominent mood episode; schizophrenia = psychosis dominant with mood minimal/low HAM-D. The single very-low HAM-D is what excludes both depressive options and points to schizophrenia.
