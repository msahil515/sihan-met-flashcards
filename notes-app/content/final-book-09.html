Berlin, 1904. A retired schoolteacher named Wilhelm von Osten puts a question to his horse: what is three plus five? Hans taps his hoof eight times and stops. Von Osten had spent years teaching him, and Hans now seemed able to add, subtract, multiply and divide, to work with fractions, tell the time and answer questions about German words, all by tapping. He answered strangers as well as his owner, so deliberate fraud seemed unlikely. In September 1904 a commission that included the psychologist Carl Stumpf watched him perform and found no trick. The newspapers called him Clever Hans.

Stumpf passed the puzzle to his student Oskar Pfungst, who treated it as a research problem rather than a marvel. Pfungst set out rival explanations (trickery, genuine reasoning, cues from the people present) and tested them by changing one condition at a time. When the questioner knew the answer, Hans was nearly always right; when the questioner did not, Hans was nearly always wrong. With blinkers that hid the questioner, or with the questioner standing far off, Hans failed again. The horse was not counting; he was watching. Pfungst found the signal: once the question was put, the questioner bent forward slightly to watch the hoof, and Hans began to tap; when the right count was reached, the questioner's head lifted a fraction and the tension left his body. Hans stopped at that release. None of the questioners, von Osten included, knew they were doing it.

Pfungst published his study in 1907. He even took the horse's part himself in the laboratory, tapping out answers for human questioners, and found that they gave him the same small, unwitting signals. The lesson has outlived the horse: what an observer expects can leak into the very data meant to test it. This chapter is about the methods science has built to stop that from happening.

9.1 Science and the Research Process: Problems and HypothesesMedium yield · 21 Qs

Why should you believe a psychologist's claim? Not because the psychologist is famous or certain, but to the degree that the claim was reached by a method that could have shown it to be wrong. This section describes that method, the reasoning it uses, the kinds of research it produces, and the first two steps of every study: finding a problem and stating a hypothesis.

Ways of knowing

In "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) the American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce (1839 to 1914) described four ways in which people settle what they believe, and Fred N. Kerlinger (1910 to 1991) made them standard in Foundations of Behavioral Research (1964). By the method of tenacity you hold a belief because you always have; by the method of authority, because a respected source says so; by the a priori method, because it seems self-evidently reasonable (intuition). Only the method of science settles belief by public observation that anyone can repeat, so only science corrects its own mistakes.

Older traditions stand behind the list (Table 9.1). Socrates (about 470 to 399 BCE) used the elenchus, a question-and-answer cross-examination that draws out a person's beliefs until they contradict one another (the Socratic method). Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE) observed and classified nature; René Descartes (1596 to 1650) trusted reason and intuition (rationalism); Galileo Galilei (1564 to 1642) experimented; and Francis Bacon (1561 to 1626) argued in Novum Organum (1620) for building knowledge inductively from observations.

What makes research scientific

Kerlinger defined scientific research as "systematic, controlled, empirical and critical investigation of hypothetical propositions about the presumed relations among natural phenomena". Unpacked, that gives its characteristics.

  • Empiricism: claims are settled by observation, not by argument or rank.
  • Objectivity through systematic, controlled observation: the result should not depend on who observes it.
  • Replicability and reproducibility: the study can be repeated, and gives the same results when it is. When the Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 published psychology studies, about 97 percent of the originals but only about 36 percent of the repetitions were significant.
  • Parsimony: prefer the simpler of two explanations that fit equally well. This is Occam's razor, after William of Ockham (about 1287 to 1347); in animal psychology it is Lloyd Morgan's canon (1894), after Conwy Lloyd Morgan (1852 to 1936): never explain an action by a higher mental faculty if a lower one will do.
  • Falsifiability: Karl Popper (1902 to 1994), in Logik der Forschung (1934), noted that a thousand white swans cannot prove "all swans are white" but one black swan disproves it. A statement is scientific only if some possible observation could show it false; psychoanalysis, which explains any behaviour after the event, was his example of a theory that risks nothing.
  • Determinism and tentativeness: behaviour has natural causes and so is lawful, yet every conclusion is provisional.

A. K. Singh adds three assumptions of science (nature is lawful, behaviour is determined, nature is understandable) and six attitudes of the scientist: open-minded, tolerant of uncertainty, aware of being fallible, cautious (a finding is evidence, never "proof"), sceptical and ethical. The goals of science are to describe, explain, predict and control behaviour.

Exam trap

"A characteristic of good research" is reproducibility of results. The distractors are the opposites of the real criteria: good research has clear objectives, unbiased findings, adequate scope and ethical conduct.

Scientists usually do normal science inside a paradigm, a shared package of theory, methods and assumptions. Anomalies pile up, crisis follows, and a scientific revolution installs a new paradigm (a paradigm shift), as when the cognitive revolution displaced behaviourism.

Induction, deduction and abduction

Induction runs from specific observations to broad generalisations, bottom-up. You interview twenty hostel students in exam week, notice that the poorest sleepers worry most, and generalise. Induction builds theory, so it belongs to the theory-development stage and is typical of exploratory and qualitative research. Its weakness, noted by David Hume (1711 to 1776), is that no number of cases guarantees the rule.

Deduction runs top-down, from theory to hypothesis to the observation that tests it: if the cognitive theory of depression is right, depressed patients should recall more negative words, so you test them. It is typical of quantitative, experimental research. Abduction, Peirce's term, is inference to the best explanation: from a surprising fact to the hypothesis that would best account for it, as when Oskar Pfungst guessed that Clever Hans was reading his questioners (see the chapter opening).

The hypothetico-deductive method joins them: propose a hypothesis, deduce a prediction, test it, then keep, revise or drop the hypothesis. Clark L. Hull (1884 to 1952) built Principles of Behavior (1943) this way. Figure 9.1 shows the cycle.

Exam trap

"Starts with a theory and tests it with observations" is deductive reasoning; the hypothetico-deductive method (the whole conjecture-and-test cycle) is the distractor. "Gathers data, finds patterns, then generates a theory", "specific observations to broader generalisations" and "associated with qualitative research" are all inductive reasoning, used at the theory-development stage, not in formulating hypotheses, collecting data or reviewing literature. Experimental design and quantitative analysis go with deduction; qualitative analysis is a way of handling data, not a direction of reasoning.

Methodology, methods and the steps of research

Research methods are the techniques for collecting and analysing data: an interview, an experiment, a t-test. Research methodology is the wider study of the logic behind choosing them: why this method suits this problem, and what its results can show. As C. R. Kothari puts it in Research Methodology: Methods and Techniques, methods are part of methodology, so methodology has the wider scope.

Research advances theory, informs government policy, guides practice (which therapy works) and, in business, provides insights for decision-making, a role broader than profit and far from merely academic. Kothari's criteria of good research are a clearly defined purpose, a procedure described well enough to be repeated, a design planned for objective results, frankly reported flaws, adequate analysis, and conclusions confined to what the data justify.

Singh's eight steps are: (1) identifying the problem, with the literature review; (2) formulating hypotheses; (3) identifying, manipulating and controlling variables (9.2); (4) the research design (9.4); (5) devices for observation and measurement (9.6); (6) summarising results (9.8); (7) statistical analysis (9.9 to 9.11); and (8) drawing conclusions. Kothari's flow runs: problem, literature survey, hypotheses, research design, sample design (9.3), data collection, execution, analysis, hypothesis testing, generalisation and interpretation, and the report (9.7), which raises the next problem.

Basic research seeks knowledge for its own sake; applied research solves a practical problem. Action research, an applied form, was introduced by Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947), often called the father of modern social psychology, in 1946. It is research done by or with the people in a setting to solve a specific local problem there, in cycles now drawn as plan, act, observe and reflect: a ward team studies why patients miss follow-up visits, changes the reminders and measures again (participatory action research is in 9.6). Evaluation research judges whether a programme works; Michael Scriven (1967) separated formative evaluation, which improves a running programme, from summative evaluation, which judges it at the end.

Exploratory research probes a poorly understood area without firm hypotheses, so it is hypothesis-generating. Descriptive research observes and describes what exists without manipulating variables. Explanatory research (causal research) tests hypotheses about why, most strongly by experiment, a variable-manipulating study. A case study is a design for one case (9.4).

Wilhelm Windelband (1848 to 1915) in 1894 separated sciences seeking general laws from those seeking to understand the unique case. The nomothetic approach (Greek nomos, law) finds laws across many people through experimental and quasi-experimental methods; the idiographic approach (idios, one's own) studies one person in depth through case studies, ethnography and other qualitative methods. Gordon W. Allport (1897 to 1967) brought the pair into personality psychology in 1937 and preferred the idiographic approach (Chapter 4).

Exam trap

Both experimental and quasi-experimental methods are nomothetic; when both are offered, the expected answer is the experimental method. Qualitative methods and ethnography are idiographic. Allport preferred the idiographic approach; factorial design (9.4) and chi-square (9.10) are a design and a test, not approaches. Action research is "research carried out to solve a specific problem": it is applied but need not be longitudinal, so "all of the above" fails. Lewin founded action research, not psychoanalysis, psychophysics or functionalism (Chapter 1).

The research problem

A research problem is a felt difficulty sharpened until data can answer it: "students seem stressed" becomes "Is sleep duration related to test anxiety in first-year students?" Kerlinger's criteria are that it relates two or more variables, is stated clearly as a question, and implies empirical testing. A good research question is clear, answerable, connected to earlier work and worth answering.

Problems come from experience, theory and earlier findings, which reveal a gap, contradictory results, or an isolated unexplained fact, such as the apparent movement that Max Wertheimer (1880 to 1943) studied as the phi phenomenon, which led to Gestalt psychology. Pauline V. Young (1956) named documentary sources, key informants and library sources; Baker and Keller add programmed activities, research proposals, government policies and social values.

A problem should be novel, significant, feasible (time, money, participants), within the researcher's interest and competence, supported by available data and instruments, and ethical. It must also be solvable by empirical observation. An unsolvable problem ("Is there a divinity that shapes our ends?") is metaphysical: unstructured, with terms that cannot be operationally defined and data that cannot be collected. Singh's steps in formulating a problem are: choose a broad area, divide it, select a subarea, raise questions, turn them into objectives, assess their feasibility, and double-check.

Reviewing the literature

The literature is the body of prior work related to a research problem; it is not an archive (a store of historical records) or a repository (a storage place for documents and data). The review of literature evaluates it. Its primary purpose is to provide a theoretical framework, from which hypotheses are deduced; it also avoids duplication, locates gaps, refines the problem and suggests variables, designs and tools.

Singh's five types are primary (traces a topic's development), content (links a study to related work), methodological (compares methods), integrative or systematic (combines empirical results; see 9.5) and theoretical (compares theories). Sources include peer-reviewed journals, books, review articles, abstracts (Psychological Abstracts, begun in 1927, now PsycINFO), indexes, PubMed, dissertations (Shodhganga in India), the internet and grey literature, material without an ISBN or ISSN such as theses and government reports. A review is written as critical synthesis organised by theme, with each source annotated, not as a string of summaries.

Exam trap

The primary objective of a literature review is to provide a theoretical framework. Establishing the researcher's credibility is a secondary purpose Singh mentions; summarising your own findings belongs in the results, and ignoring earlier research is the opposite of a review.

The hypothesis: meaning, criteria and functions

The problem asks; the hypothesis answers. Kerlinger (1973) called it "a conjectural statement of the relation between two or more variables", always declarative: a tentative, testable statement. "Is sleep related to test anxiety?" is a problem, interrogative and not directly testable; "Students who sleep under six hours will report higher test anxiety" is a hypothesis, declarative and testable. Hypotheses can be recast as "if (antecedent), then (consequent)".

A good hypothesis is testable and falsifiable, conceptually clear, specific, consistent with known facts, parsimonious, and related to theory and to available techniques. William J. Goode and Paul K. Hatt (Methods in Social Research, 1952) named three difficulties in formulating one: no clear theoretical framework (or ignorance of it), inability to use that framework logically (Singh renders this as not knowing what theoretical evidence is available), and unfamiliarity with research techniques.

Hypotheses come from theory, observation, earlier research, analogy (learned helplessness in dogs suggested a model of human depression), intuition and culture. A hypothesis guides data collection and analysis (what to collect, what to ignore), delimits the study, suggests the design and links theory to observation. It is never meant to prove the researcher's view or steer the results, and it does not replace the literature review it grows from. A supported hypothesis is retained, never proved.

9.2 Variables, Experimental Control and the Validity of ResearchMedium yield · 22 Qs

Suppose a counsellor at a Mysuru college runs a six-week yoga course for forty anxious students, and their anxiety falls. Did the yoga do it, or did the examinations end, or did the students relax because someone was watching? Deciding needs the language of variables, measurement, control, guards against bias and the idea of validity. (Hypotheses are Section 9.1; designs, Section 9.4.)

Variables and their kinds

A variable is any characteristic that can take two or more values; one with a single value in a study is a constant. A concept is an abstraction from observed events (honesty, courage), and a construct is a concept deliberately invented for scientific use, such as intelligence (Fred N. Kerlinger, 1910 to 1991, Foundations of Behavioral Research, 1964). Measurability is what separates a variable from a concept.

The independent variable (IV) is the presumed cause, manipulated by the experimenter or, where that is impossible, selected. The dependent variable (DV) is the measured outcome, expected to change with the IV. Find the IV by asking what is presumed to cause what, not by word order: in "old age home residents who have heart disease die earlier than those who do not", the IV is having heart disease, the DV is age at death, and the old age home is a constant.

Heart disease cannot be handed out, which illustrates Kerlinger's split between active variables, which the researcher manipulates (dose, instructions), and attribute variables, which participants bring and which can only be measured or selected (age, sex, diagnosis). With an attribute IV random assignment is impossible, so causal claims are weaker. Attribute variables are also called organismic, the O in Robert S. Woodworth's (1869 to 1962) stimulus-organism-response scheme. Table 9.5 lists the other contrasts.

Exam trap

The IV is the presumed cause (having heart disease), not the first-mentioned variable (dying early) or the setting (the old age home, a constant). "Manipulated by the experimenter" defines the IV; "measured as the outcome" defines the DV; "randomly assigned" describes participants, not a variable.

Extraneous, confounding, moderator and mediator variables

An extraneous variable is any variable other than the IV that may affect the DV: subject or organismic variables (age, intelligence, motivation), task variables (difficulty, instructions), environmental variables (noise, light) and situational variables (time of day, the experimenter's manner). One that varies randomly only adds noise. One that varies systematically with the IV and also affects the DV is a confounding variable: it influences both, and its effect cannot be separated from the IV's. If the yoga volunteers were also more motivated, and motivation lowers anxiety, motivation is confounded with treatment.

Reuben M. Baron and David A. Kenny (1986) separated two other third variables (Figure 9.2). A moderator changes the direction or strength of the IV-DV relationship, saying when or for whom: if yoga helps mild but not severe anxiety, severity moderates the effect (statistically an interaction, Section 9.10). A mediator is the mechanism through which the IV acts: yoga slows breathing, which lowers anxiety. An intervening variable is such a link that can only be inferred, never observed, such as learning or fatigue.

Exam trap

A variable that "only affects the DV" is merely extraneous; a confound must also vary with the IV, which is why it "influences both". Hook: mediator = mechanism (how); moderator = more for some (when, for whom).

Operational definitions and measuring the DV

An operational definition defines a variable by the operations used to manipulate or measure it: anxiety is the score on a named inventory; hunger is hours without food. This operationism comes from the physicist Percy Williams Bridgman (1882 to 1961) in The Logic of Modern Physics (1927); Stanley Smith Stevens (1906 to 1973) brought it into psychology in 1935. It makes research objective and replicable, though no single operation captures a whole construct. The DV can be measured by direct observation of behaviour, by indirect physiological measures or reaction time, by judgements about a stimulus, or by self-report. A good DV is reliable, valid and sensitive. Scores bunched at the bottom of a scale because the task is too hard show a floor effect; scores bunched at the top because it is too easy show a ceiling effect. Both hide real differences.

Control and the MAXMINCON principle

Control means holding constant, or otherwise neutralising, the influence of extraneous variables on the DV, so that conditions differ only in the IV. Kerlinger summed up the aim of design in the MAXMINCON principle: maximise systematic (experimental or primary) variance, the variance due to the IV, by making its levels as different as possible; minimise error variance, the random variance from individual differences and measurement error, by reliable measures and steady conditions; and control extraneous (secondary) variance, the systematic variance from other variables, by the techniques that follow.

Exam trap

MAXMINCON reads primary, error, secondary; "true variance" belongs to test theory (Chapter 2). One real paper defines control as holding constant "the possible influence of the dependent variable". The stem is careless (extraneous variables are what is held constant), but control is still the answer. Standard deviation is a statistic (Section 9.8); an experiment is the whole procedure (Section 9.4).

Techniques of control

Elimination removes an extraneous variable (a sound-proof room removes noise). Constancy of conditions holds it at one value for all (same room, time and instructions), at some cost to generality. Matching pairs participants on a relevant variable and splits each pair between groups. Balancing spreads a known variable equally across groups, as with equal numbers of men and women in each.

When the same people serve in every condition, order effects arise: practice, fatigue and carry-over. Counterbalancing varies the order so that each condition appears equally often in each position: complete (every order: 2 for two conditions, 6 for three, 24 for four), partial (a subset, such as a Latin square, Section 9.4) or ABBA (one order, then its reverse). Psychophysics controls primacy and recency effects the same way, by counterbalancing the order of presentation.

The most powerful technique is randomisation, or random assignment: every participant has an equal chance of being placed in any group. It makes the groups alike at the start on known and unknown variables, so that the main difference between them is the treatment, and is the chief guard against selection bias and confounding. That is why an HIV vaccine trial randomly assigned over 16,000 volunteers to vaccine or placebo: not to reduce non-response or sampling variability (Section 9.3), and not to blind anyone. It does nothing about measurement error (reliable measures reduce that), recall bias (faulty memory of past exposures) or publication bias (selective publishing of positive results; both Section 9.5). Figure 9.3 separates it from random sampling.

Finally, a within-subjects design uses participants as their own control: stable individual differences drop out, but order effects enter. Statistical control removes a measured extraneous variable in the analysis (ANCOVA, Section 9.10; partial correlation, Section 9.11). A control group, untreated or given a placebo, shows what would have happened anyway.

Exam trap

Equal probability of being assigned to a treatment group is randomisation. Random sampling decides who enters the study, stratification splits a population into subgroups before sampling, and randomness is mere unpredictability. In the psychophysics item, "pairing" is matching, and randomisation only scatters order effects by chance. "Double-bind technique" is most likely a misprint for double-blind; the double bind proper is a theory of contradictory family messages in schizophrenia (Chapter 8). None of these balances order, so counterbalancing is the answer.

Bias and artefacts

Bias is any influence that distorts a study's results away from the truth. (Control is its remedy; sample and population are the people studied, Section 9.3.) Distortions created by the research situation itself are called artefacts; the lesser ones are in Table 9.6. The best known is the Hawthorne effect: people change their behaviour because they know they are being observed, whatever the intervention.

Key study: The Hawthorne studies (1924 to 1932)

The Western Electric Hawthorne Works near Chicago studied workers' output. In the illumination experiments output rose when lighting was increased, but also in the control group, and sometimes when the light was dimmed. The studies, associated with Elton Mayo (1880 to 1949), were reported by Fritz J. Roethlisberger (1898 to 1974) and William J. Dickson in Management and the Worker (1939); Henry A. Landsberger coined the term in 1958. Reanalyses find the data weaker than the legend.

Martin T. Orne (1927 to 2000) argued in 1962 that participants are active problem-solvers. Demand characteristics are the cues in a setting (rumours, instructions, procedure, the experimenter's manner) that tell participants what is expected and so shape their behaviour. Stephen J. Weber and Thomas D. Cook (1972) described good (confirming), faithful (literal), negativistic (opposing) and apprehensive subjects, the last showing evaluation apprehension, anxiety to be judged favourably (Milton J. Rosenberg, 1965). From the other side, Robert Rosenthal (born 1933), in Experimenter Effects in Behavioral Research (1966), showed that researchers' expectations change how participants are treated and responses scored: the experimenter expectancy effect, or experimenter bias.

Key study: Pygmalion in the classroom (1968)

Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson gave the pupils of Oak School (a pseudonym), a Californian elementary school, an IQ test and told teachers that it had picked out certain pupils, in fact chosen at random (about 20 percent), as about to bloom intellectually. Retested on the same IQ test at the end of the year, these children, especially in grades one and two, had gained more IQ points than their classmates: the Pygmalion effect, a self-fulfilling prophecy. Replications find it real but smaller.

The halo effect, described by Edward Lee Thorndike (1874 to 1949) in 1920, is one salient attribute colouring ratings of others. When a patient's anxiety improves and the rater therefore scores depression as improved too, that is halo: not a ceiling effect (a scale limit), not Hawthorne (a change in the participant) and not a therapeutic effect (a genuine benefit of treatment).

In a single-blind study participants do not know their condition, which controls their expectancy and demand, but the experimenter can still bias the results. The four kinds of validity of a study

Here validity belongs to a study, not a test (Chapter 2). Donald T. Campbell (1916 to 1996) first set internal against external validity in 1957 and, with Julian C. Stanley (1918 to 2005), listed the threats to each in 1963; Cook and Campbell (1979) and William R. Shadish, Cook and Campbell (2002) gave four types:

  • Statistical conclusion validity: is the IV-DV relationship real, or did low power, violated assumptions or many tests mislead?
  • Internal validity: did the IV cause the change in the DV? Campbell and Stanley called it the basic minimum for interpreting an experiment.
  • Construct validity: do the manipulation and measures represent the intended constructs? Giving plain versus barbecue-flavoured chips does not manipulate anxiety.
  • External validity: the extent to which findings generalise to other people (population validity), settings (ecological validity) and times (temporal validity). Glenn H. Bracht and Gene V Glass (1968) split it into population and ecological validity.

The two main kinds trade off: tight laboratory control raises internal validity but makes the setting unlike life, while a busy clinic is realistic but loosely controlled. Researchers establish an effect under control, then replicate it in the field. Alan Bryman, in Social Research Methods (2001), judges studies by reliability (measures of concepts stable on different occasions), replicability (methods stated clearly enough to repeat) and validity (generalising to other social settings is external validity). The researcher's reputation is no criterion.

Exam trap

In the chips item, internal validity tempts, but no rival variable explains the result; the manipulation simply is not anxiety, so construct validity fails. "Statistical validity" loosely means statistical conclusion validity; "empirical validity" is an old name for a test's criterion-related validity (Chapter 2). Causation is internal validity; internal consistency and reliability belong to measures.

9.3 Sampling: Populations, Probability and Non-Probability MethodsMedium yield · 24 Qs

Suppose you want to know how anxious Indian school students feel before their board examinations. You will test a few hundred of the crores who exist, then speak about all of them. Whether that leap is justified depends almost entirely on how the few hundred were chosen.

Populations, samples and parameters

A population (or universe) is the whole, well-defined group about which you want to draw conclusions. The target population is the group you would like to generalise to (all school students in India); the accessible population is the part you can actually reach (students in the Mysuru schools that admit you). Each member about whom data are collected is an element; a sampling unit is whatever is selected at a step of sampling, either an element or a whole group such as a school. The sampling frame is the actual list used for selection (a school register, a voters' list), and the sample is the smaller set chosen from it by some rule or plan. Studying every element is a census; studying a part in order to describe the whole is a sample survey.

A number describing a population is a parameter, written with Greek letters (mean μ, standard deviation σ); the same number computed from a sample is a statistic, written with Roman letters (M or X̄, s). A parameter is fixed but usually unknown; a statistic is known but varies from sample to sample. Sampling exists to estimate the first from the second.

Exam trap

"A statistical measure based upon a population" is a parameter; one based upon a sample is a statistic. Pair the initials: Population with Parameter, Sample with Statistic.

Sampling is needed because it saves time and money, often measures more accurately (a few trained investigators beat an army of hasty ones), is the only option for huge or infinite populations, and is unavoidable when testing destroys what is tested. Its principles: a statistic usually differs somewhat from its parameter; parameters are estimated through sampling distributions; and a random sample mirrors its population more closely as it grows. Its aims: a representative sample, unbiased estimates and a known margin of error. A good sample therefore shows representativeness (population characteristics in the same proportions), adequacy (enough cases), independence (choosing one element does not affect choosing another) and homogeneity (its units are of the same kind as the population's).

Probability versus non-probability sampling

Following the sociologist Hubert M. Blalock (1926 to 1991), whose Social Statistics appeared in 1960, sampling methods fall into three families (Figure 9.4). Probability sampling methods clearly specify the likelihood of inclusion of each element: every element has a known, non-zero chance of selection, and a random mechanism does the choosing. In non-probability sampling that chance cannot be known, because a person (researcher, interviewer, volunteer or friend) does the choosing. Mixed sampling combines features of both: Indian texts following Blalock place systematic sampling here, since only its start is random, and the term also covers designs that join a purposive stage to a random one.

Why does the random mechanism matter? It removes the researcher's personal bias from selection, and because selection follows the laws of chance, the size of the chance error can be calculated (Section 9.9), which is what licenses inferential statistics. Probability sampling is therefore the method most likely to yield a representative sample: likely, not certain, since a random draw can still come out lopsided. Non-probability samples allow neither randomisation nor a calculated sampling error.

Exam trap

A past paper keys "random sampling guarantees a representative sample". Choose it, since the other options (it ensures personal bias, reduces the sample needed, makes data collection harder) are plainly wrong, but know the exact fact: it makes representativeness likely and error calculable, never guaranteed. "Statistical" and "empirical" sampling are not Blalock's families; methods that specify each element's chance of inclusion are probability methods.

Simple random and systematic sampling

Simple random sampling (unrestricted random sampling) is the foundation of the others: every element has an equal and independent chance of selection. Equal means none is favoured; independent means one draw does not alter another's chance. Randomness comes from the lottery or fishbowl method (names on identical slips, drawn blind), a table of random numbers (the most popular: elements are numbered 1 to N, and numbers above N and repeats are skipped) or computer generation for large populations. Returning each drawn element before the next draw is sampling with replacement; psychologists usually sample without replacement. The method needs no knowledge of the population's make-up and its error is easy to compute, but it requires a complete frame and may, by bad luck, miss small subgroups.

Systematic sampling takes every kth (or nth) element after a random start, with the interval k = N/n. For 100 students from a register of 2,000, k = 20, so a random start of 7 gives the 7th, 27th, 47th student and so on. It is quick and spreads the sample evenly, but beware periodicity: if a hostel list runs room by room, four to a room with the prefect always first, k = 4 yields every prefect or none. Chance decides only the start, hence the label mixed in Indian texts following Blalock; it is never a non-probability method.

Stratified random sampling

Suppose a university has 1,000 PhD, 2,000 MA and 7,000 BA students, and research anxiety may differ by level. Stratified random sampling first divides the population into strata, mutually exclusive and exhaustive subgroups formed on a variable related to the study, then draws a random sample from every stratum. In proportionate stratified sampling each stratum gets the same sampling fraction, so a sample of 100 holds 10 PhD, 20 MA and 70 BA students and mirrors the population. In disproportionate stratified sampling small strata are deliberately over-sampled (say 40, 30 and 30) so that each can be analysed on its own, and results are weighted back for the whole. Optimum allocation takes more cases from strata that are larger, more variable and cheaper to sample, giving the most precise estimate for a fixed cost; Jerzy Neyman (1894 to 1981) gave its classic form, weighing stratum size and variability, in 1934.

Stratification guarantees that every important subgroup appears. When strata are internally homogeneous and differ from one another, it is also more precise than a simple random sample of the same size, because chance can no longer hand you too many BA students. Its costs: every element's stratum must be known in advance, and in a homogeneous population it gains nothing.

Exam trap

Stratified sampling is preferred over simple random sampling when important subgroups must be represented. It does not help a homogeneous population, is not chosen to minimise sample size, and needs more knowledge of the frame, not less (with no list of individuals, use cluster sampling). Every nth case is systematic; intact schools are cluster; volunteers are non-probability.

Cluster, multistage and multiphase sampling

No list of every school student in India exists, and a simple random sample would scatter interviewers across thousands of villages. Cluster sampling (area sampling) solves both problems: the population is divided into naturally occurring groups (districts, villages, schools, wards), a random sample of these clusters is drawn, and units inside the chosen clusters are studied. Only a list of clusters is needed. It began in agricultural research and now drives large surveys.

In one-stage cluster sampling everyone in each chosen cluster is studied; in two-stage cluster sampling a random sample is taken within each chosen cluster; in multistage sampling, associated with the British statistician Claus Moser (1922 to 2015), selection descends through more levels. Randomly select 5 districts from each state, then 10 schools per district, then 30 students per school: districts and schools are intact groups picked at random, so this is multistage cluster sampling, the logic of India's National Family Health Surveys.

The price is precision. Students in one school are alike, so each extra student from a chosen school adds less new information than one drawn from the whole country. For the same n, a cluster sample usually has a larger sampling error than a simple random sample, and a stratified sample a smaller one; cluster sampling buys cheapness, not a larger sample. Ideal clusters are miniatures of the population, the reverse of ideal strata (Table 9.9, Figure 9.5).

Non-probability methods

Non-probability methods give up the random mechanism for speed, low cost or access. In all of them the chance of selection is unknown, randomisation is impossible, sampling error cannot be calculated, and generalisation rests on judgement (Table 9.10).

Convenience sampling (accidental or incidental sampling) takes people who are available, volunteer or can easily be recruited: your own classmates, shoppers passing a mall. It is the quickest, cheapest and least controlled method. In a volunteer or self-selected sample people answer an advertisement or online poll; volunteers are usually more interested and motivated than others. In purposive sampling (judgemental sampling) the researcher's knowledge and judgement hand-pick cases thought typical, expert or information-rich, such as senior nurses she considers typical of their wards.

Quota sampling, "a common and poor version of stratified sampling", fixes how many people of each kind are needed to mirror population proportions (60 men and 40 women, say), then lets interviewers fill each quota with whoever they find. The interviewer, not chance, picks people within quotas, so bias enters there. Cheap and needing no frame, it is widely used in market research and opinion polls.

In snowball sampling (chain-referral sampling) a few people with the characteristic of interest recruit others like them, so existing participants recruit future participants. It is the route into hidden populations that have no list, such as drug users or sex workers. In saturation sampling the researcher includes as many available members of a limited group as possible (900 of a school's 1,000 students); in dense sampling all or nearly all of the accessible population is taken. Both are rare. Qualitative purposive strategies (theoretical sampling, maximum variation, typical and extreme case, criterion sampling, sampling to saturation) belong to Section 9.6.

Sampling error, bias and the Literary Digest

Sampling error is the chance difference between a statistic and its parameter; Everet Franklin Lindquist (1901 to 1978) defined it in 1940 as "the difference between a parameter and an estimate of that parameter which is derived from a sample". It implies no mistake. It falls as n rises (the standard error of the mean is σ divided by the square root of n, so quadrupling n halves it) and as population variability falls, is zero in a census, and can be calculated only for probability samples.

Non-sampling error afflicts a census too: coverage error (the frame misses part of the population), non-response error (non-repliers differ from repliers), measurement error (poor questions, interviewer effects) and processing error (coding and entry mistakes). Error that pushes results consistently one way is sampling bias. Random error shrinks as n grows; bias does not, and a bigger biased sample is only a more confident wrong answer.

Key study: The Literary Digest poll (1936)

Question. Who would win the 1936 United States presidential election? Method. The magazine Literary Digest mailed about 10 million ballots to names from its subscriber lists, telephone directories and car registrations; over 2 million came back. Result. It predicted a comfortable win for the Republican challenger; the sitting president won by a landslide, carrying all but two states. A young pollster, George Horace Gallup (1901 to 1984), with a far smaller sample, called it correctly. Why. In the Depression, telephone and car owners were richer than average (coverage bias), and those who returned ballots differed from those who did not (non-response bias). Lesson. Size cannot cure bias.

Exam trap

Random sampling decides who enters the study and supports generalisation (external validity). Random assignment decides who gets which condition and supports causal conclusions (internal validity; Section 9.2). A true experiment needs random assignment and can survive a convenience sample.

How large should a sample be?

The size needed rises with population variability, with the precision and confidence level wanted, with smaller effects to detect, with higher power (conventionally .80), with the number of subgroups to analyse separately and with expected non-response; cost, time and the planned analysis set the ceiling. Rules of thumb call 30 or more a large sample and about 100 adequate for most educational research, but the modern answer is a power analysis before data collection (Section 9.9). A parameter can be estimated as a single value (point estimation) or as a range likely to contain it (interval estimation, the confidence interval); both rest on the sampling distribution, taught in Section 9.9.

9.4 Research Designs: Experimental, Single-Case and Non-ExperimentalMedium yield · 21 Qs

A counsellor in Mysuru believes that slow breathing before class lowers exam anxiety. She could ask 500 students whether they practise it, follow one anxious student as the practice starts and stops, or let chance decide which half of a class practises. Each is a research design, a plan fixing who is studied, what is done to them and when they are measured, and each licenses different conclusions. We begin with the only design that can establish cause.

What makes an experiment

An experiment has three features: manipulation of the independent variable (IV) by the researcher (variables are defined in Section 9.2), control of everything else, and random assignment, in which chance (a lottery, random numbers), not the researcher or the participant, decides who enters which condition. The experimental group receives the treatment; the control group is treated identically but receives no treatment, and shows what would have happened anyway.

Random assignment makes the groups equivalent at the start, spreading every characteristic, even unmeasured ones, evenly by chance. If equivalent groups end unequal on the dependent variable (DV), only the IV can explain it. Creating equivalence among participants is therefore vital for experimental research; the issue does not arise in correlational or descriptive research, where nobody is assigned. John Stuart Mill (1806 to 1873) gave the logic in A System of Logic (1843). By his method of agreement, a circumstance shared by every case of an effect is probably its cause; by his method of difference, if cases with and without the effect differ in only one circumstance, that circumstance is the cause, which is exactly what an experiment arranges. Causal inference needs covariation, temporal precedence (cause before effect) and elimination of alternative explanations. Only the experiment secures all three, so only it licenses cause-and-effect conclusions.

Ronald Aylmer Fisher (1890 to 1962), in The Design of Experiments (1935), gave three principles of experimental design: replication (several units per treatment, so that chance variation can be estimated), randomisation (every participant has an equal chance of assignment to any group) and local control (blocking: grouping similar units so that their differences leave the error term). Homogeneity is not one of the three.

Exam trap

Random selection (sampling, Section 9.3) decides who enters a study; random assignment decides who gets which condition. A survey of 10,000 randomly selected adults finding regular meditators less stressed has no assignment: people chose to meditate, and calmer people may be the ones who do. Large or random samples never turn an observational study into an experiment, so neither this study nor one linking exercise and depression in 30,000 adults supports a causal conclusion.

Campbell and Stanley's designs

Donald Thomas Campbell (1916 to 1996) and Julian C. Stanley (1918 to 2005) catalogued designs in 1963 in a notation still used: R is random assignment, X the treatment and O an observation; each row is a group and time runs left to right. They grouped designs by how well they rule out threats to internal validity such as history and maturation (Section 9.2), as Table 9.11 shows. Pre-experimental designs have no random assignment and little or no comparison; true experimental designs have R and a control group.

A pretest has a hidden cost, pretest sensitisation: a questionnaire on mental illness may make students more responsive to an anti-stigma film that follows. Richard L. Solomon (1949) answered with the Solomon four-group design, which runs the pretest-posttest and posttest-only designs together with four random groups, so the effect of pretesting and its interaction with treatment are measured and controlled. Maturation and selection are already handled by any randomised control group, and researcher bias by blinding (Section 9.2).

A quasi-experiment manipulates the IV but uses intact groups (classes, wards) without random assignment. Its main forms are the nonequivalent control group design, the interrupted time-series design (one group measured many times before and after the intervention) and the regression discontinuity design (Donald Thistlethwaite and Campbell, 1960), which assigns treatment by a cut-off score and looks for a jump in the regression line at the cut-off.

Between, within and mixed designs

In a between-subjects design each participant receives one condition: two randomised groups; a multigroup design (one IV at three or more levels, which shows the shape of its effect); or the matched-groups or randomised block design, in which participants are matched on a variable correlated with the DV and the members of each block are randomly assigned to conditions, Fisher's local control in practice.

In a within-subjects or repeated-measures design everyone receives every condition and serves as his or her own control, so fewer participants are needed and individual differences leave the error term. The cost is order effects (practice, fatigue) and carry-over effects (a drug still acting in the next condition), handled by counterbalancing the order (Section 9.2). A Latin square arranges orders so that each condition appears once in every position: four conditions need four orders instead of 24. Latin squares thus control order effects in repeated-measures designs; they are not correlational, and "equal group sizes" or "one IV with several levels" describe balanced and multigroup designs. A mixed design has a between factor and a within factor (group × time). In a crossover design each participant receives both treatments in sequence, half in each order.

Exam trap

Patients take a placebo for four weeks and a viral medication for four more, or the reverse. The researchers assigned the treatments, so this crossover study is an experiment, and the explanatory variable (the one that explains changes in the response) is the type of treatment; symptom improvement is the response.

Factorial designs

A factorial design studies two or more IVs at once, every level of each combined systematically with every level of the others. Each IV is a factor, each of its values a level, and each combination a treatment combination or cell. Count the numerals for factors and multiply them for cells: 2 × 3 is two factors and six cells; 2 × 2 × 2 is three factors and eight cells.

A main effect is one factor's effect averaged over the levels of the others; a simple effect is its effect at one level of another; an interaction effect exists when one factor's effect depends on the level of another, shown by non-parallel lines (Figure 9.6). There noise adds 2 errors on an easy task and 8 on a hard one: a main effect of 5 errors that hides an interaction only a factorial design can reveal. Two factors give two main effects and one interaction; three give three main effects, three two-way and one three-way interaction (factorial ANOVA, Section 9.10). In a nested design a factor's levels occur within only one level of another: therapists who each deliver only one therapy are nested within it.

Exam trap

IQ (100 or 120), age (30 or 60) and sex make a three-factor (2 × 2 × 2) design: count the IVs, not the levels. "Eight factor" counts cells and "six factor" adds levels.

Laboratory, field and natural experiments

A laboratory experiment, as defined by Leon Festinger and Daniel Katz (1953), is one in which the investigator creates exactly the conditions he wants, controlling some variables and manipulating others: high internal validity, but limited external validity, that is, generalisation to real life (Section 9.2). A field experiment manipulates an IV in a natural setting, as when Irving Piliavin and colleagues (1969) staged a collapse on New York subway trains to see who helped. Its advantage is higher external validity; the laboratory keeps higher internal validity and easier control, and both can use between-participants designs. A field study manipulates nothing but studies relations as they exist in a factory or school; Katz (1953) divided field studies into exploratory (finding the variables) and hypothesis-testing types. In a natural experiment nature or policy does the manipulating: people conceived at the height of the Dutch famine of 1944 to 1945 later showed about twice the usual risk of schizophrenia. With no random assignment, it remains quasi-experimental. Analogue studies are covered in Section 9.5.

Single-case and small-N designs

The single-subject design (single-case or N = 1 design) asks whether a treatment changes one person's behaviour, by measuring it repeatedly while treatment is switched in planned phases; with a few participants it becomes a small-N design. Such designs grew from the intensive study of a few animals by Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990). Murray Sidman's Tactics of Scientific Research (1960) argued that a group average may fit no individual and that generality comes from replication, and Alan E. Kazdin (1982) systematised the designs for clinical work.

Behaviour is recorded (by frequency, duration or time intervals) through a baseline, phase A, until stable, which predicts what would happen untreated; B is the treatment and C a second treatment. An A-B design shows one change and cannot rule out coincidence. The withdrawal design (A-B-A) removes the treatment to see behaviour return; the reversal design (A-B-A-B) reinstates it, the clearest demonstration (Figure 9.7). Withdrawal is unethical for self-injury and useless for skills, such as reading, that do not reverse; the multiple-baseline design avoids it by starting treatment at staggered times across behaviours, participants or settings (Table 9.12).

Non-experimental designs

In non-experimental or observational research variables are measured as they exist and nothing is assigned. The commonest form, correlational research, measures two or more variables in the same people (Section 9.11) and allows prediction but not causation. The directionality problem (does poor sleep cause depression, or the reverse?) means it cannot determine the direction of influence; the third-variable problem means an unmeasured factor such as loneliness may cause both. It needs no random assignment and is among psychology's most used designs. No sample, however large or random, cures these two problems.

Fred N. Kerlinger (1910 to 1991) defined survey research as studying populations by selecting samples, to discover the relative incidence, distribution and interrelations of sociological and psychological variables, but not the causal factors of an event. A survey of an entire population is a census (the Census of India is the classic survey); of a part, a sample survey. Modes are interview, mail, telephone, online and the panel technique (the same respondents surveyed repeatedly). In Kerlinger's ex post facto research ("after the fact"), also called causal-comparative research, the IV has already occurred or cannot be manipulated, so pre-existing groups such as smokers and non-smokers are compared; self-selection and the post hoc fallacy are its risks.

Historical research uses primary sources (eyewitness records) and secondary sources (accounts by people not present). Its evidence faces external criticism, which tests authenticity (is the document genuine?), and internal criticism, which tests credibility (is it accurate, its author honest and informed?). Developmental designs

A cohort is a group born in the same period and sharing its history. The longitudinal design studies the same people repeatedly over time (one group across ten years) and is the best design for change over time; its problems are attrition (selective drop-out), practice effects, cost and time. The cross-sectional design tests different age groups at one time: quick, cheap and covering several ages at once, but limited by the cohort effect, generational differences (less schooling in older groups) mistaken for development. The time-lag design tests one age in different years. K. Warner Schaie (1965) combined these in sequential designs, which test several cohorts on several occasions so that age changes can be told apart from cohort differences; in the commonest, the cross-sequential design, several age groups are tested and then retested over years (Figure 9.8). A case-control design instead compares people with and without an outcome on past exposures (Section 9.5).

Key study: Terman's gifted children (from 1921)

Question: are gifted children frail and maladjusted? Method: Lewis Madison Terman (1877 to 1956) followed about 1,500 California schoolchildren with very high Stanford-Binet IQs from 1921 into old age, the classic longitudinal study. Result: they grew up healthier, better adjusted and more successful than average. Criticism: the sample was teacher-nominated and largely middle class, and Terman helped his participants' careers.

Exam trap

Cohort effects limit cross-sectional designs; attrition and practice effects limit longitudinal ones. Socio-economic factors can confound any comparison but are not the characteristic flaw, age cannot be randomly assigned in either design, and a cross-sectional design studies many ages at once.

9.5 Clinical Epidemiology, Randomised Trials and Evidence SynthesisMedium yield · 27 Qs

How common is a disorder, does a test detect it, and does a treatment work? The designs and numbers come from epidemiology, the study of how disorders are distributed in populations and what determines them; evidence synthesis pools the answers. In epidemiology the independent variable is called the exposure and the dependent variable the outcome.

Counting cases: prevalence and incidence

A disorder's prevalence counts existing cases, old and new together, as a proportion of a population. Its narrowest form, point prevalence, is the number of existing cases on a specific date divided by the total population on that date (40 cases among 2,000 Mysuru adults on 1 March: 2 percent). Period prevalence counts everyone who was a case at any time during a stated period, such as the past 12 months, and lifetime prevalence everyone who has ever met the criteria. By contrast, incidence counts only new cases arising during a period among people free of the disorder at its start, either as cumulative incidence (new cases divided by the population at risk) or as an incidence rate (new cases per person-time at risk, such as per 1,000 person-years).

For a stable condition, prevalence ≈ incidence × average duration. Schizophrenia has a low incidence but lasts for years, so its prevalence is relatively high; a brief adjustment disorder can have many new cases yet a low point prevalence. Incidence is used to find causes, prevalence to plan services. A morbidity rate expresses illness (its incidence or prevalence) per unit of population over a stated time, and a mortality rate gives deaths per unit of population, usually per 1,000 or 100,000 a year.

Exam trap

"Existing cases on a specific date out of the total population" is point prevalence. Plain "prevalence" is less specific, "periodic prevalence" covers a stretch of time, and "incidence" counts new cases only.

Epidemiological designs

The designs differ in where the researcher starts and which way the inquiry runs in time (Table 9.14). An ecological study correlates group-level data, such as suicide and unemployment rates across Indian states; inferring individual behaviour from such correlations is the ecological fallacy, demonstrated by William S. Robinson in 1950. A cross-sectional study measures exposure and outcome at one moment, as prevalence surveys do, so it cannot show which came first.

A case-control study starts from the outcome: cases who have the disorder and matched controls who do not are compared on past exposure. For a rare dissociative disorder and childhood trauma, a cohort would need thousands of children followed for decades, whereas a case-control study starts from the cases a hospital already has: for rare outcomes it is the most feasible design. It cannot measure incidence, so it yields the odds ratio, and it is prone to recall bias, because ill people search their memories harder than healthy controls.

A cohort study classifies people who are free of the outcome as exposed or unexposed and follows them to see who develops it, which gives incidence and the relative risk. A prospective cohort runs forward from now. In the British Doctors Study, begun by Richard Doll (1912 to 2005) and Austin Bradford Hill (1897 to 1991), British doctors were questioned about smoking in 1951, again in 1957, 1966 and repeatedly up to 2001, and their deaths were traced: lung cancer mortality rose with the number of cigarettes smoked. Nobody was assigned to smoke, so the study is observational; it ran forward, so it is prospective. The Framingham Heart Study of a Massachusetts town (from 1948) is another. A retrospective cohort (historical cohort) uses existing records but still reasons from exposure to outcome. Cohorts are slow and costly, and they lose people to attrition.

Measures of association and effect

Any exposure and yes-or-no outcome fit a 2 × 2 table. In a trial, 200 people recovering from depression receive maintenance cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) and 200 receive treatment as usual (TAU); 50 and 80 of them relapse. Risk is the proportion who develop the outcome: 0.25 with CBT, 0.40 with TAU (the control event rate). The relative risk or risk ratio (RR) divides the risks: 0.25/0.40 = 0.625. The odds ratio (OR) divides the odds, odds being those with the outcome over those without: (50/150)/(80/120) = 0.50. A ratio of 1.0 means no association. Above 1 the exposure increases risk (an RR of 2.0 means double the incidence); below 1 it is protective, as CBT is here. A case-control study gives only the OR, there phrased as the odds of exposure among cases over that among controls; it approximates the RR when the outcome is rare.

Absolute measures show size. The attributable risk (risk difference) is the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed; in a trial it becomes the absolute risk reduction (ARR): 0.40 − 0.25 = 0.15. The relative risk reduction (RRR = ARR/control risk = 1 − RR) is 37.5 percent, a grander-sounding figure. The number needed to treat (NNT = 1/ARR) is how many patients must receive the treatment instead of the control for one additional patient to benefit: 1/0.15 = 6.7, rounded up to 7. An NNT of 8 for an antidepressant means treating 8 patients for one extra to benefit; the ideal is 1. The number needed to harm (NNH) is 1 divided by the absolute risk increase of an adverse effect: a side effect in 30 percent on the drug and 10 percent on placebo gives NNH = 5.

Exam trap

NNT = 1/ARR. The distractors 1/RRR, 1/RR, RR × control event rate (that gives the treated group's event rate), OR − 1, OR × 100, RRR × 100 and "enrolled ÷ events" are all wrong. An NNT of 8 does not mean the drug works in 8 percent of patients or that 8 must drop out, and there is no NNT threshold for drug approval. RR or OR = 1.0 means no association: not protective (below 1), not "doubles the risk" (2.0), not "causes the outcome" and not "perfect correlation" (r = 1, 9.11).

Screening and diagnostic accuracy

Against a gold standard such as a clinical interview, a screening test sorts people into four cells (Table 9.15): true positives, false positives (healthy people flagged), false negatives (cases missed) and true negatives. A test's sensitivity, TP/(TP + FN), is the true positive rate: the proportion of real cases it catches. Its specificity, TN/(TN + FP), is the proportion of non-cases it correctly clears. A depression test with sensitivity 90 percent and specificity 70 percent therefore identifies 90 percent of depressed people as depressed and 70 percent of healthy people as healthy. Both are computed among people whose true status is known, so they do not change with prevalence.

The patient asks the reverse: given a positive result, am I ill? The positive predictive value (PPV), the proportion of positive scorers who truly have the disorder, and the negative predictive value (NPV), the proportion of negative scorers who truly lack it, answer it, and they depend on the prevalence or base rate, the pre-test probability. Apply the 90/70 test to 1,000 clinic patients, 20 percent depressed: 180 true and 240 false positives give PPV = 180/420 = 43 percent. Where only 5 percent are depressed, 45 true and 285 false positives give PPV = 14 percent. The test is unchanged; the base rate fell. Paul Everett Meehl (1920 to 2003) and Albert Rosen warned in 1955 that with rare conditions a test's cutting score can make more errors than predicting from the base rate alone.

Lowering a cut-off score raises sensitivity and lowers specificity. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, from signal detection theory, plots sensitivity (y-axis) against 1 − specificity (x-axis) across all possible cut-offs (Figure 9.9). The area under the curve (AUC) runs from 0.5 (chance) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination). Finally, likelihood ratios combine both indices: sensitivity/(1 − specificity) shows how much a positive result raises the odds of the disorder (3 for the 90/70 test), and (1 − sensitivity)/specificity how much a negative result lowers them (0.14).

Exam trap

Sensitivity is not PPV. "A positive test reflects true disease" is PPV, "probability of disease before testing" is prevalence, "true negatives correctly identified" is specificity, and TN/(TN + FN) is NPV. PPV is most affected by prevalence, not by sensitivity or specificity alone or by the number of items (Chapter 2). ROC curves do not plot Type I against Type II error (9.9), predictor against outcome (9.11), item difficulty against discrimination, validity against reliability, score against age, or means against standard deviations.

The randomised controlled trial

A randomised controlled trial (RCT) is a true experiment on patients: participants are randomly allocated to a treatment or a comparison condition and compared on an outcome named in advance. Randomisation gives everyone the same chance of each arm, balancing measured and unmeasured characteristics. Next, allocation concealment hides the upcoming allocation sequence from those enrolling participants until each is irreversibly enrolled, using central randomisation or sealed opaque envelopes, so that a clinician cannot steer a promising patient into the treatment arm; it prevents selection bias. Blinding works after allocation, hiding the arm from the participant (single-blind), also from those treating and assessing (double-blind), and also from the analysts (triple-blind); see 9.2. Psychotherapy trials cannot blind patients or therapists, only assessors and analysts. Controls may receive a placebo (an inert, identical pill) or an active established treatment; psychotherapy trials also use waiting-list, attention-placebo and treatment-as-usual controls (Chapter 7).

Exam trap

Allocation concealment is not blinding. Concealment hides the upcoming allocation from recruiters before enrolment; blinding hides the actual arm after it. "Blinding the patient to the assigned arm", "hiding outcome assessors' identity", "sealing the analysis dataset", "concealing the primary outcome from the statistician" and "keeping investigators unaware of the hypothesis" are all wrong.

Analysing, designing and registering trials

In intention-to-treat analysis (ITT) every participant is analysed in the group to which they were randomised, regardless of adherence, drop-out or crossover. A per-protocol analysis keeps only those who completed the treatment as planned (completers), and as-treated analysis groups people by the treatment they actually received. Drop-outs differ between arms, so discarding them reintroduces selection bias and usually flatters the treatment; ITT preserves randomisation and gives a conservative, real-world estimate. Attrition, the loss of participants, reduces power, and when it differs between arms it destroys the balance that randomisation created.

Exam trap

ITT analyses everyone in their randomised group. "Only completers" and "excluding protocol deviators" are per-protocol; "by actual treatment received" is as-treated; "only responders" or "only the control group" is no analysis at all.

Most trials are parallel-group: each person receives one condition. In a crossover trial each person receives both conditions in random order, with a washout between, which fails when effects carry over (9.4). A cluster randomised trial randomises whole groups such as schools or villages. A superiority trial asks whether the new treatment is better; a non-inferiority trial whether it is no worse than the standard by more than a preset margin; an equivalence trial whether the difference lies within that margin in both directions.

Drug trials run in phases: phase I tests safety and dose in about 20 to 100 mostly healthy volunteers; phase II seeks first evidence of efficacy in up to several hundred patients; phase III runs confirmatory RCTs in roughly 300 to 3,000 patients, the basis for approval; phase IV is post-marketing surveillance for rare and long-term harms. Trial registration records planned outcomes publicly before enrolment; the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI), run by the Indian Council of Medical Research since 2007, has been mandatory since 2009. Finally, efficacy is whether a treatment works under ideal conditions (an explanatory trial) and effectiveness whether it works in routine care (a pragmatic trial; Chapter 7).

Analogue research

In analogue research, a clinical phenomenon is modelled under conditions that only resemble the clinical situation, usually with healthy, non-clinical participants (often students) or simulated conditions: snake-fearful undergraduates standing in for phobic patients, or sadness induced in the laboratory. It offers control, easy recruitment and fewer ethical obstacles, but its findings may not generalise to patients (external validity, 9.2). It is not research on clinical populations, nor an efficacy or comparative effectiveness trial.

Systematic reviews, meta-analysis and the evidence hierarchy

A systematic review uses explicit, pre-specified, reproducible methods to find, appraise and synthesise all the studies on one question, unlike a narrative review whose author picks the studies. A meta-analysis is its statistical step: it summarises the results of many studies by pooling their effect sizes (9.9) into one weighted average. Gene V. Glass (born 1940) coined the term in 1976.

Key study: Smith and Glass (1977)

Hans Eysenck (1916 to 1997) had claimed in 1952 that neurotic patients improve about as often without psychotherapy as with it. Mary Lee Smith and Glass pooled 375 controlled studies of psychotherapy: the average effect size of 0.68 meant that the average treated client was better off than about 75 percent of untreated controls. Critics, Eysenck foremost, replied that pooling poor studies gives a precise but wrong answer ("garbage in, garbage out").

Exam trap

One real paper (IHBAS 2024) keys "to conduct a single large experiment" as the purpose of meta-analysis. That is wrong: a single experiment, however large, is primary research. Meta-analysis exists to summarise findings from multiple studies; it collects no primary data and does not analyse individual case studies.

A forest plot shows each study as a square (its effect, sized by its weight) on a line (its 95 percent confidence interval), with a vertical line of no effect and a diamond for the pooled estimate. Heterogeneity, variation between studies beyond chance, is tested with Cochran's Q and expressed as I2. Pooled effects are inflated by publication bias, the preferential publication of significant results; Robert Rosenthal (born 1933) named its extreme form the file-drawer problem in 1979: null results stay in researchers' drawers. A funnel plot of effect size against precision is symmetrical without bias and lopsided when small null studies are missing.

The hierarchy of evidence for treatment questions runs from systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs at the top, through single RCTs, cohort studies, case-control studies and case series or reports, down to expert opinion.

9.6 Methods of Data Collection and Qualitative ResearchLower yield · 15 Qs

A design (Section 9.4) settles when and on whom you measure; a method of data collection settles how the information reaches you: by watching people, questioning them, handing them a form, or studying what they have produced. This section takes those tools and their errors, then the paradigms beneath them, and ends with qualitative research.

Data sources and observation

Primary data are collected first-hand for the present study, by observation, interview, questionnaire or test. Secondary data were collected by others for another purpose and are re-used: census records, hospital files, archives, published surveys. They are cheap, large and non-reactive, but the researcher controls neither what was recorded nor how accurately.

Observation is the systematic watching and recording of behaviour without manipulating it; it alone does not depend on what people say they do. In participant observation the observer joins the group's life; in non-participant observation the observer stays outside. Structured observation codes predefined categories; unstructured observation keeps open field notes. Naturalistic observation records behaviour in its usual setting (play in a school yard); controlled observation arranges standard conditions (mother and child behind a one-way mirror). Overt observers are known to the group; covert observers are not, which raises consent problems (Section 9.7). James P. Spradley (1933 to 1982), in Participant Observation (1980), described three narrowing phases: descriptive observation, broad, to grasp the field; focused observation, on the processes found relevant; and selective observation, near the end, seeking further evidence for specific practices.

Its two errors are reactivity, behaviour changing because people know they are watched (reduced by habituation and one-way mirrors), and observer bias, seeing what one expects (reduced by blind observers and inter-observer agreement). The participant observer may also "go native", losing critical distance. Time and event sampling of behaviour are covered in Section 9.4.

Interviews and ethnographic inquiry

An interview is a purposeful conversation in which the interviewer asks questions and records the answers. A structured interview fixes the wording and order of every question: reliable and comparable, but rigid. A semi-structured interview follows a topic guide but lets the interviewer rephrase and probe; an unstructured interview fixes only the aim, trading reliability for depth. An in-depth interview is a long one-to-one exploration of experience; a focus group is a moderated discussion among about 6 to 10 similar people whose interaction is itself the data, though dominant members can sway it.

An open-ended question lets respondents answer in their own words ("Tell me about..."); a closed-ended question restricts the answer to yes or no or a fixed choice ("Did you sleep well?"). Probes are neutral follow-ups ("Could you say more?", an expectant pause) that draw out a fuller answer without suggesting one.

Spradley's The Ethnographic Interview (1979) treats the respondent as an informant, an expert on their own cultural world, asked to explain its meanings to an outsider through descriptive, structural and contrast questions. "Can you tell me why this band is so popular among your age group?" is an ethnographic interview question: the teenager explains the culture of the peer group. Other styles of inquiry differ: psychodynamic inquiry (feelings, early relationships, hidden motives: "What does this remind you of?"), task-focused inquiry (completing a defined task), strength-based inquiry (resources and past successes: "What has helped you cope?") and theory-guided inquiry (questions set by one theory, such as cognitive therapy).

The interviewer is part of the instrument, so errors arise from the interviewer's attitudes and expectations, from their sex, age, caste or status, from confusing or overlong questions, coldness and unmotivated respondents, and from interviewers recording similar answers differently. Training and standard wording reduce them.

Exam trap

"Tell me about..." is an open-ended question, not an ethnographic technique. The band question is ethnographic inquiry because it asks an insider to explain a culture.

A questionnaire is answered by respondents themselves; a schedule is asked and filled in by the investigator or enumerator face to face, as in the census, so it suits respondents who cannot read. Items are open (own words; rich but slow to code) or closed: dichotomous (yes or no), multiple choice, ranking, rating, and the contingency question that routes respondents ("If no, go to question 12"). Content analysis, sociometry and Verstehen

Content analysis studies communications (letters, diaries, case notes, films) rather than people. Bernard Berelson (1912 to 1979) defined it in 1952 as "the objective, systematic and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication". Manifest content is what is plainly there; latent content, the underlying meaning, is left to qualitative versions. His method, as set out in his 1954 handbook chapter (the date A.K. Singh gives): specify the universe (the content and its categories), choose the unit of analysis (word, theme, item, character, or space and time) and quantify by counting, ranking or rating.

Sociometry measures an individual's social behaviour and relations within a group. Jacob Levy Moreno (1889 to 1974), founder of psychodrama, introduced it in Who Shall Survive? (1934). Members name whom they would choose for an activity, and the sociogram draws the choices as arrows, showing stars (chosen by many), isolates (chosen by none) and mutual choices.

Verstehen ("understanding") is the interpretive grasp of the meaning an action has for the actor. Wilhelm Dilthey (1833 to 1911) argued in 1883 that the natural sciences explain from outside while the human sciences must understand from inside; Max Weber (1864 to 1920) made Verstehen the method of interpretive sociology. It is an interpretive stance, not a measure of social behaviour; nor is the "sample method", which is simply sampling (Section 9.3). Q-methodology (William Stephenson, 1935), which factor-analyses persons through Q-sorts and bridges the two traditions, is covered in Chapter 2.

Every method rests on a paradigm, a set of basic beliefs. Egon Guba (1924 to 2008) and Yvonna Lincoln (1994) defined paradigms by three questions: ontology (what is the nature of reality?), epistemology (what is the relation between the knower and the known?) and methodology (how should the inquirer find out?). Qualitative versus quantitative research

Qualitative research studies meaning, experience, process and context through words, images and observed action, analysed by interpretation; quantitative research measures variables and tests hypotheses statistically. Qualitative work seeks the insider's view and treats the researcher as the instrument, which calls for reflexivity, an open account of the researcher's influence. The qualitative traditions

Ethnography ("writing about a people") describes a culture or group from the inside through long immersion in its setting, mainly by participant observation and key informants; the researcher's role is to understand from the insider's perspective, not to manipulate variables, test large samples or reanalyse official statistics. The linguist Kenneth Pike (1912 to 2000) coined emic (insiders' own categories) and etic (outsiders' comparative categories) in 1954: "tension" as an idiom of distress is emic, "generalised anxiety disorder" etic. Clifford Geertz (1926 to 2006) made thick description the aim (1973): behaviour recorded with the context that gives it meaning, which alone tells a wink from a twitch of the eyelid.

Key study: Malinowski in the Trobriand Islands (1915 to 1918)

Stranded in Australian territory by the First World War, Bronislaw Malinowski (1884 to 1942) pitched his tent in a Trobriand village off New Guinea, learned the language and joined daily life for about two years. Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) set ethnography's goal: "the native's point of view". Criticism: his diary, published in 1967, revealed contempt for the people he studied.

Grounded theory generates theory from data. Barney Glaser (1930 to 2022) and Anselm Strauss (1916 to 1996) launched it in The Discovery of Grounded Theory (1967). Constant comparison of each new incident with earlier ones drives it; theoretical sampling picks whom to study next from the emerging categories; collection stops at theoretical saturation, when new data add nothing new; and memos record the analyst's developing ideas. The coding stages are those of Strauss and Juliet Corbin (1990), shown in Figure 9.10: open coding breaks the data apart and labels concepts; axial coding weaves the data back together around categories; selective coding integrates everything around one core category.

Exam trap

Phenomenology studies lived experience as it appears to the person. Edmund Husserl (1859 to 1938) asked researchers to describe it after bracketing (epoché), suspending their own assumptions; his former assistant Martin Heidegger (1889 to 1976) replied (1927) that we are always immersed in the world, so all understanding is interpretation (hermeneutic phenomenology). Jonathan Smith's interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA, 1996) follows the hermeneutic line: the researcher makes sense of participants making sense of their experience. Most methods texts call the two lines descriptive (Husserl) and interpretive or hermeneutic (Heidegger) phenomenology. One source used for this book renames them, contrasting a direct approach (Husserlian: a detached researcher lights up the foreground, participants' mental representations) with an indirect approach (Heideggerian: an involved observer, immersed in the everyday world, lights up its background).

Exam trap

"Direct" and "indirect" are not standard labels, and sources disagree on them. The safe fact is that the detached, bracketing approach is Husserl's (descriptive) and the involved one Heidegger's (interpretive). Analysis, trustworthiness and triangulation

Matthew Miles and A. Michael Huberman (1994) saw analysis as three interwoven activities: data reduction (coding and summarising), data display (matrices and networks) and conclusion drawing and verification.

Lincoln and Guba's (1985) trustworthiness replaces the experimental criteria with four parallels: credibility (internal validity), transferability (external validity, judged by the reader), dependability (reliability) and confirmability (objectivity). Credibility comes from prolonged engagement, peer debriefing, triangulation and member checking (returning findings to participants); transferability from thick description; dependability and confirmability from an audit trail of decisions and from reflexivity.

Triangulation, a surveyor's term, studies one question from several positions so that each method's errors are offset by the others. Denzin (1970) named four types: data (several sources, times and places), investigator (several researchers), theory (several theoretical lenses) and methodological (several methods). Uniting qualitative with quantitative methods is therefore triangulation; "coalition" is no research term, a "multidimensional approach" merely studies several aspects of a problem, and co-operative inquiry is Heron's participatory method.

Mixed-methods research integrates both kinds of data on a pragmatist footing. John Creswell's three core designs are convergent (both strands at once, then merged), explanatory sequential (quantitative first, qualitative to explain it) and exploratory sequential (qualitative first, building a tool that a quantitative phase tests).

9.7 Research Ethics and Writing the Research ReportLower yield · 10 Qs

A study is finished twice: once when the last participant leaves unharmed, and again when its findings are written up for others to check. This section follows the scandals that produced ethical rules, the rules themselves, and honest reporting.

Why research needs ethics

Modern research ethics was written in response to abuse. After Nazi physicians' lethal experiments on concentration camp prisoners, the verdict of the Doctors' Trial at Nuremberg (1947) set out ten principles, the Nuremberg Code. Its first point reads: "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential"; another lets the subject stop at any time. In 1964 the World Medical Association adopted the profession's own code, the Declaration of Helsinki, which puts the participant's welfare above the interests of science and later required independent committee review.

From 1932 to 1972 the United States Public Health Service ran the Tuskegee syphilis study: about 600 poor Black men, 399 with syphilis, were told they had "bad blood", and penicillin was withheld even after it became the standard cure. Its exposure in 1972 led to the Belmont Report (1979) and its three principles, each with one application. Respect for persons (treat people as autonomous and protect those with reduced autonomy) is applied through informed consent; beneficence (do no harm, maximise benefits and minimise harms) through risk-benefit assessment; and justice (share research's burdens and benefits fairly) through fair selection of participants, the principle Tuskegee violated most plainly.

Psychology's own debated cases matter here for their ethics only (Section 1.6, Chapter 3). John B. Watson (1878 to 1958) and Rosalie Rayner (1898 to 1935) conditioned fear in an infant, Little Albert (1920), and never removed it. Philip Zimbardo (1933 to 2024) cast 24 male students as guards or prisoners in his Stanford prison study (1971); planned for two weeks, it was stopped after six days as guards turned abusive, with Zimbardo himself acting as superintendent.

Key study: Milgram's obedience study (1963) and Baumrind's critique

Stanley Milgram (1933 to 1984) ordered 40 men, told the study concerned punishment and learning, to give rising "shocks" up to 450 volts to a learner who was really an unharmed confederate; 26 (65 per cent) went to the maximum. Diana Baumrind (1927 to 2018) argued in 1964 that the deception and stress were unjustified, and critics add that prods such as "you have no other choice, you must go on" undercut the right to withdraw. Milgram replied that all were debriefed and 84 per cent were glad to have taken part.

Codes and ethics committees

Psychologists follow the APA Ethics Code, the American Psychological Association's Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (2002, amended 2010 and 2016). Its five aspirational general principles are (A) beneficence and nonmaleficence, (B) fidelity and responsibility, (C) integrity, (D) justice, and (E) respect for people's rights and dignity; beneath them, enforceable standards set specific rules, Standard 8 covering research and publication. In India, the Indian Council of Medical Research issued the ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017), which cover behavioural research in health. Under either system no study may begin until an independent institutional ethics committee (in the United States, an institutional review board or IRB) has approved its protocol. The Rehabilitation Council of India's code for clinical psychologists is in Section 7.1.

Exam trap

The APA has five general principles. Competence and confidentiality are enforceable standards, the usual intruders in "which is NOT a general principle".

Informed consent and voluntary participation

Informed consent is the principle that participants are told enough about a study (its purpose, procedures, duration, risks, benefits, the protection of their data, and their rights) to decide voluntarily whether to take part, and agree before it begins. It needs understandable information, voluntariness and capacity, the ability to understand, weigh and communicate a choice. The consent form is only the signed record of that agreement; a signature extracted with jargon or pressure is not informed consent.

Suppose a Mysuru student reads that your exam-anxiety study takes 20 minutes, may stir up worry (a counsellor's number is given), codes answers by number and can be left at any point without effect on marks. If she then agrees, she has given informed consent; her signed form merely documents it.

Children cannot give legal consent, so a parent or guardian consents and the child gives assent, an age-appropriate agreement of his or her own; a child's refusal is normally respected. An adult lacking capacity needs a legally authorised representative's consent, though a diagnosis alone does not remove capacity. Voluntary participation rules out coercion (a threat to grades, treatment or parole) and undue inducement (a payment large enough to distort judgement about risk). It continues as the right to withdraw at any time, without a reason or penalty, including withdrawal of one's data.

Exam trap

"Told enough about the research to decide whether to take part" and "permission obtained after informing them of the purpose and procedures" both define informed consent, given before participation. The consent form is the record, not the principle. Expectation management (a business phrase) and de-concealment are not research-ethics terms; revealing a deception is dehoaxing, part of debriefing. Random assignment is a design procedure (Section 9.2), not an ethical principle.

Harm, risk and confidentiality

Participants should leave no worse off than they came. Protection from harm covers physical harm but in psychology chiefly psychological harm (anxiety, guilt, lowered self-esteem) and social harm such as stigma. Above minimal risk (no more than in daily life), the committee makes a risk-benefit analysis: are risks as low as possible, outweighed by benefits to participants or society, and covered by safeguards?

The duty most often used to sum up research ethics is ensuring the confidentiality of data: the researcher knows who gave the data but does not disclose it, coding names, storing data securely and reporting only group results. Anonymity is stricter: no one, not even the researcher, can link data to a person, because no identity is recorded. Confidentiality has limits, stated at consent, such as a serious risk of harm to self or others. Ethics asks more than following the law, and manipulating data to fit a hypothesis (falsification) or ignoring consent is its opposite.

Vulnerable populations, people less able to protect their own interests, need extra safeguards: children, prisoners, people with severe mental illness or intellectual disability, pregnant women, the very poor, and anyone dependent on the researcher, such as students or patients. Justice forbids loading risky research onto them, but also excluding them from research that could benefit them.

Exam trap

Confidential means the researcher could identify you but will not; anonymous means nobody can. A face-to-face interview or a longitudinal study can be confidential but never anonymous.

Deception and debriefing

Obedience or conformity cannot be studied if participants know the hypothesis. Deception is misleading participants about a study's purpose or procedures, actively (a cover story, a confederate, false feedback) or passively (withholding the hypothesis). APA Standard 8.07 permits it only when the study has significant scientific, educational or applied value; no effective non-deceptive method is feasible; nobody is deceived about anything expected to cause physical pain or severe emotional distress; and the deception is explained as early as feasible, no later than the end of data collection, with the option to withdraw one's data.

That explanation is debriefing: after participation, participants receive full information about all aspects of the study, including its true purpose and any deception, and are checked for distress. David S. Holmes (1976) named its two parts. Dehoaxing convinces participants that the false information was false (the learner received no shocks); desensitising helps them deal with distress or unwelcome self-knowledge (that they obeyed). Debriefing is good practice even without deception, but cannot excuse an unjustified deception. It is not description or explanation (goals of science, Section 9.1), nor interpretation, which belongs to a report's Discussion. Figure 9.11 places each safeguard on the participant's path.

Animal research and the 3Rs

Animals cannot consent, so animal research ethics rests on justification and welfare. William Russell and Rex Burch, in The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique (1959), proposed the 3Rs: replacement (non-animal methods, such as cell cultures or computer models, wherever possible), reduction (the fewest animals that give a valid answer) and refinement (less pain and distress, through anaesthesia and better housing).

The research proposal

9.8 Measurement Scales, Organising Data and Descriptive StatisticsHigh yield · 58 Qs

Two hundred students in Mysuru have filled in an anxiety questionnaire, and you now own a notebook of numbers that, on their own, say nothing. Before any hypothesis can be tested (Sections 9.9 to 9.11), you must decide what kind of numbers you have, put them in order and describe them. This section does those jobs, ending with the normal curve and the z-score.

Levels of measurement

Measurement is the assignment of numbers to objects or events according to rules. But numbers mean different things: the 2 on a cricketer's shirt, 2nd rank in class, 2°C and 2 kg are all "2", yet only the last can be doubled meaningfully. In 1946 the Harvard psychophysicist Stanley Smith Stevens (1906 to 1973) sorted these meanings into four levels of measurement ("On the theory of scales of measurement", Science). Each level adds one property: identity (a number names a category), magnitude (order), equal intervals (a unit means the same amount anywhere on the scale) and an absolute zero (zero means none of the attribute).

A nominal scale has identity only: labels name unordered categories such as marital status, eye or car colour and gender, and coding "married" as 2 does not make it more than "single" at 1. An ordinal scale adds magnitude: cases are ranked, but the distances between ranks are unknown. Class ranks, socioeconomic status categories and any task in which respondents place items along a continuum by preference or degree of agreement give ordinal data. A single Likert item (strongly disagree 1 to strongly agree 5) is strictly ordinal, though a total over many items is conventionally treated as interval.

An interval scale adds equal intervals (20°C to 30°C is the same rise as 30°C to 40°C), but its zero is arbitrary, a reference point rather than the absence of the quantity: 0°C is just the freezing point of water. Differences are meaningful, ratios are not; 40°C is not twice as hot as 20°C. Celsius temperature, calendar years and most test scores, IQ included, are treated as interval. A ratio scale adds a true zero: height, weight, reaction time and the number of books a person owns (zero books is no books, and 6 is twice 3). The level decides which statistics are permissible (Table 9.23), and each level keeps every statistic of the levels below it (memory hook: NOIR).

Key study: Stevens and the scales of measurement (1946)

Can sensations be measured at all? A committee of the British Association for the Advancement of Science had debated it for years without agreeing, its physicists doubting it (final report, 1940). Stevens replied by defining measurement broadly, as assigning numbers by rule, and by classing scales according to the transformations that leave their information intact: any one-to-one relabelling (nominal), any order-preserving change (ordinal), a linear change, aX + b (interval), and multiplication by a constant (ratio). The result was the four levels and their permissible statistics. Critics such as Frederic Lord (1953, "On the statistical treatment of football numbers") replied that the numbers do not remember where they came from, so scale type should not ban a statistic outright; the debate continues.

Two further classifications cut across the levels. A categorical variable (qualitative) sorts cases into classes; a quantitative one records an amount. A discrete variable takes separate values, usually counts (books owned, so a count can be discrete yet ratio); a continuous variable can take any value in a range (height, reaction time). Nominal variables are always categorical. Ordinal variables are strictly ordered categories, but because ranks are taken to lie on an underlying continuum (anxiety, agreement) they are usually analysed as continuous.

Exam trap

"Ordered variables are considered ____, nominal variables ____" is keyed continuous, categorical. Take the key, knowing that some books call nominal and ordinal variables alike qualitative. "Discontinuous" simply means discrete.

A good measure is reliable, that is, consistent (it gives the same result when the same thing is measured again), valid (it measures what it claims to), practical (cheap, quick, easy to interpret) and sensitive to small differences. Objectivity belongs here too; high cost, low reliability and subjectivity mark a poor measure. Test reliability and validity are treated in Chapter 2.

Processing data and statistical software

Filled-in questionnaires become analysable data through four processing operations, in this order, as set out in C. R. Kothari's Research Methodology. Editing examines raw data to detect and correct errors, omissions and inconsistencies (an age of 210, a blank item). Field editing is done by the investigator soon after the interview; central editing is done at the office, by one editor or team, once all forms are in. Coding assigns numerals or other symbols to answers so that responses fall into a limited number of exhaustive, mutually exclusive categories (male 1, female 2); the key to the codes is the codebook. Classification arranges the data into groups or classes sharing a characteristic, by attribute (urban or rural) or by class interval (ages 20 to 29). Tabulation arranges the classified data in rows and columns: a simple (one-way) table shows one characteristic, a complex table two or more. Graphs, charts and diagrams present the same data visually instead. On computer, data entry is followed by data cleaning: hunting for out-of-range values (7 on a 1-to-5 scale), duplicates and missing-value codes.

Exam trap

Coding assigns symbols (the "assigning numerical values" option); classification groups data into classes. One real paper keys "organising data into categories or groups" for both: it is exact for classification and the nearest option for coding. Editing corrects errors; tabulation uses rows and columns.

IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences), first released in 1968 and owned by IBM since 2009, is psychology's standard package; its primary purpose is data analysis, from descriptive statistics to factor analysis. R, SAS, Stata and the free JASP are alternatives. Microsoft Excel is a spreadsheet for data entry, tabulation, charts and calculation: =AVERAGE(A1:A10) gives the mean; SUM, MAX, MIN, MEDIAN and MODE do what they say; STDEV gives the sample SD, CORREL Pearson's r and COUNT the number of numeric cells. Word (report writing), Photoshop (photo editing), Illustrator (drawing) and InDesign (page layout) are not analysis tools.

Categorical data take a bar graph (separate bars with gaps) or a pie chart (parts of one whole). John Wilder Tukey (1915 to 2000), in Exploratory Data Analysis (1977), added the stem-and-leaf display, which keeps every raw score, and the box plot (a box from Q1 to Q3, a median line and whiskers), good for comparing groups and spotting outliers. A line graph shows change over time; a scatter plot shows the relationship and spread of two continuous variables (Section 9.11).

Measures of central tendency

A measure of central tendency is one score that represents the whole distribution. The arithmetic mean (M = ΣX / N) is the balance point: deviations from it sum to zero, the sum of squared deviations about it is a minimum (least squares), and adding a constant to every score adds it to the mean, while multiplying every score by a constant multiplies the mean by it. Using every score makes it the most stable average across samples, but also lets one extreme score pull it: 4, 7, 7, 9 and 13 have a mean of 8, and adding a 40 lifts it to 13.3. It cannot be computed for an open-ended top class. A combined mean weights each group by its size: 30 boys averaging 60 and 20 girls averaging 70 give (1800 + 1400) / 50 = 64.

The median is the middle score of the ordered set, the 50th percentile (for 4, 7, 7, 9, 13, 40 it is 8). Depending only on position, it resists extreme scores, so it is the average for skewed data: community income, where a few rich households inflate the mean, and anxiety or symptom scores, where most people score low and a few very high. It also suits ordinal data.

The mode is the most frequent score and the only average for nominal data. One peak makes a distribution unimodal and two bimodal; when two or more scores share the highest frequency it is multimodal. The geometric mean (the Nth root of the product of N scores) suits growth rates; the harmonic mean (N divided by the sum of the reciprocals) suits rates such as speeds.

Remember

Symmetrical interval or ratio data: mean. Skewed data, outliers or ordinal data: median. Nominal data: mode.

Exam trap

Range, standard deviation, variance and mean deviation measure variability, not central tendency. Mean deviation is the favourite decoy because it contains the word "mean".

Measures of variability

Two classes may share a mean of 50 while one spans 45 to 55 and the other 10 to 90; variability (dispersion) describes this spread. The range (highest minus lowest score) rests on the two most extreme scores. The interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1) covers the middle 50 per cent; half of it is the quartile deviation or semi-interquartile range, Q = (Q3 − Q1) / 2, the partner of the median. The mean deviation is the average absolute deviation from the mean, Σ|X − M| / N.

The variance squares the deviations instead: for a population σ² = Σ(X − μ)² / N, and the standard deviation (SD, σ) is its square root, back in the original units: the typical distance of a score from the mean. For 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 the mean is 5; the deviations −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4 square to 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16, a sum of squares (SS) of 32. So σ² = 32 / 8 = 4 and σ = 2 (32 is the SS, 2 the SD, 5 the mean).

To estimate a population's variance from a sample, divide SS by N − 1: s² = Σ(X − M)² / (N − 1). Deviations from a sample's own mean run slightly small, so dividing by N underestimates. The fix is Bessel's correction, after the astronomer Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel (1784 to 1846), and N − 1 is the degrees of freedom: once the mean is fixed, only N − 1 deviations can vary freely. For the sample 3, 5, 7: SS = 8 and s² = 8 / 2 = 4, so s = 2 (dividing by N gives 2.67). The coefficient of variation, CV = (SD / M) × 100, compares spread across different units but needs a ratio scale.

Adding a constant to every score shifts the mean but leaves the SD and variance unchanged; multiplying by c multiplies the SD by c and the variance by c². With variance 4 and scores multiplied by 3 and then increased by 10, the new SD is 6 and the new variance 4 × 9 = 36 (not 12, which multiplies the variance by 3 only, nor 46, which also adds the 10).

Remember

Affected by extreme scores: range, mean deviation, variance, SD and the mean. Resistant: IQR, quartile deviation and the median.

Skewness and kurtosis

Skewness is asymmetry, and it is named after the long tail. In positive (right) skew most scores are low and a few high ones trail right (income, a very hard test); in negative (left) skew most are high and a few low ones trail left (a very easy test). The mean, which uses every score's value, is dragged furthest into the tail, the mode stays at the hump and the median lies between. So positive skew gives mode < median < mean and negative skew mean < median < mode, the mean lying left of the median (Figure 9.14). Karl Pearson (1857 to 1936) measured it as Sk = 3(Mean − Median) / SD, zero for a symmetrical curve.

Kurtosis is peakedness relative to the normal curve: leptokurtic (more peaked), mesokurtic (normal) or platykurtic (flatter). The percentile coefficient Ku = Q / (P90 − P10) is 0.263 for the normal curve; above it is platykurtic, below it leptokurtic. On the moment measure the normal curve scores 3, and SPSS prints excess kurtosis (minus 3), so 0 means mesokurtic.

The normal probability curve

The normal probability curve was derived by Abraham de Moivre (1667 to 1754) in 1733 as the limit of the binomial distribution. Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749 to 1827) developed it, and Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777 to 1855) used it in 1809 for errors in astronomical measurement, hence the Gaussian curve. Adolphe Quetelet (1796 to 1874) applied it to human characteristics, and Francis Galton (1822 to 1911) to human ability.

Exam trap

"Who propounded the normal curve?" is keyed Carl Gauss, after whom it is named; mark Gauss, but know that de Moivre came first (1733). Siméon Denis Poisson (1781 to 1840) gave the distribution of rare events, Jacob (James) Bernoulli the law of large numbers (1713), and Wilhelm Reich (1897 to 1957) was a psychoanalyst.

The curve is bell-shaped, symmetrical and unimodal, with mean, median and mode coinciding at the centre; a two-peaked curve cannot be normal. Its tails are asymptotic, nearing the baseline without touching it. The total area under it is 1 (100 per cent), so areas read as probabilities. Its points of inflection lie at ±1 SD, and it is defined by its mean and SD alone.

The areas are therefore fixed (Figure 9.15). On each side, 34.13 per cent of cases lie between the mean and 1 SD, 13.59 per cent between 1 and 2 SD and 2.14 per cent between 2 and 3 SD. About 68 per cent fall within ±1 SD, 95 per cent within ±2 SD (exactly 95 within ±1.96) and 99.7 per cent within ±3 SD.

z-scores, percentiles and standard scores

You score 65 in statistics (class mean 50, SD 10) and 70 in English (mean 60, SD 20). Which is better? A z-score states how many SDs a score lies from its mean: z = (X − M) / SD. Statistics gives z = +1.5 and English z = +0.5, so the lower raw score is the better performance. That is the purpose of z-scores: they let you compare results from different distributions. A z of +1 lies one SD above the mean, −1 one SD below, and 0 at the mean itself. A set of z-scores has mean 0 and SD 1, and because the conversion is linear, a skewed set stays skewed. A z-score shows relative position. It is not a measure of variability (the SD, variance or quartile deviation) or of central tendency, nor the coefficient of variation, nor the standard error (the SD of a sampling distribution, Section 9.9). The z table, derived from the normal distribution, converts z into percentiles.

A percentile rank is the percentage of cases below a score. Quartiles split the distribution into four (Q1 = P25, Q2 = the median, Q3 = P75), deciles into ten. On the normal curve z = 0 is the 50th percentile, +0.5 about the 69th, +1 the 84th, +1.28 the 90th, +1.5 the 93rd, +1.96 the 97.5th, +2 about the 98th and +3 the 99.9th; z = −1 is the 16th and −2 the 2nd. Percentile units are unequal (crowded in the middle), so they are never averaged.

Deviation IQs have mean 100 and SD 15 (David Wechsler's system, Chapter 2): IQ 100 is the 50th percentile, 85 the 16th, 115 the 84th and 130 (z = +2) about the 98th. Older Binet-type tests used SD 16, so an IQ of 136 is z = 36 / 16 = +2.25; the table puts .9878 below it, so about 98.7 to 98.8 per cent score lower.

9.9 Sampling Distributions and the Logic of Hypothesis TestingMedium yield · 37 Qs

At the Rothamsted research station in the 1920s, the biologist Muriel Bristol claimed she could taste whether the milk or the tea had gone into her cup first. Ronald Aylmer Fisher (1890 to 1962) turned the claim into the opening example of The Design of Experiments (1935): eight cups, four made each way, in random order. A pure guesser would sort all eight correctly only 1 time in 70 (p = .014). That is the whole logic of this section: assume nothing is going on, work out how probable the result would be by chance, and reject chance when the result would be rare.

From describing to inferring

Descriptive statistics summarise the scores you have (section 9.8). Inferential statistics reason from a sample to the population it came from, drawing on two things: the sample data, and knowledge of how random processes behave. Their question is how plausible it is that a random process alone, drawing samples from a population in which nothing is going on, could produce results as extreme as those obtained. Inference always runs from the observed sample to the unobserved population, never the reverse.

If a therapy group improves by 6 points and a control group by 4, part of the gap is chance, because two random samples almost never have identical means. What statistics (applied to health data, biostatistics) tells the psychologist is whether an observed difference is larger than chance would usually produce. Only a statistical test can show a reliable difference between groups; norms (a reference group's typical scores), reliability (consistency) and validity (measuring what is claimed) are properties of tests (Chapter 2).

Exam trap

"Inferential statistics make statements about samples based on observing populations" is the FALSE option: it reverses the logic. Statistics does not choose patients for therapy, design interviews or plan therapeutic exercises; it judges whether differences are due to chance.

Probability: the language of chance

A probability runs from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Classical probability is worked out in advance from equally likely outcomes, favourable outcomes divided by all outcomes: a die shows a six with probability 1/6. Empirical probability is the observed relative frequency over many trials: if 30 of 200 discharged patients relapse, p(relapse) is about .15. The addition rule handles "or": for mutually exclusive events p(A or B) = p(A) + p(B), so a one or a two on a die is 2/6. The multiplication rule handles "and": for independent events p(A and B) = p(A) × p(B), so ten heads in a row has probability 1/1024. For dependent events you multiply by a conditional probability, p(B given A); remember it when you meet the p-value.

The binomial distribution gives the probability of each number of successes in N independent two-outcome trials with fixed probability p (mean Np, SD √Npq): ten coin tosses average 5 heads, yet exactly 5 occurs only about one time in four. As N grows the binomial becomes bell-shaped, as Abraham de Moivre (1667 to 1754) showed in 1733. The normal distribution is therefore the probability model of inference: area under it is probability, and since 95 percent lies within ±1.96 SD (section 9.8), a chance value beyond those points has probability .05.

The sampling distribution and the standard error

Draw a random sample of 36 students, compute their mean, replace them, and repeat thousands of times. The means vary, and the theoretical distribution they form is the sampling distribution of the mean. You never build it, but three facts about it let you judge your one real sample. Its mean equals the population mean, μ. Its standard deviation is the standard error of the mean, SE = σ/√N (estimated as s/√N): for IQ (σ = 15), samples of 25 give SE = 3 and samples of 100 give 1.5, so halving the SE takes four times the sample. And its shape is normal once N is large.

That last fact is the central limit theorem: with a sufficiently large sample, conventionally 30 or more (A.K. Singh says above 50), the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal whatever the shape of the population, centred on μ, with a spread that shrinks as N grows (Figure 9.16). De Moivre's coin result was its first case and Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749 to 1827) generalised it. It is why tests on means can use the normal curve even though psychological scores are often skewed.

Sampling error is the gap between one sample's statistic and the parameter (M − μ); it is a random error, unsystematic and averaging to zero. The standard error is the typical size of that gap across all samples. The law of large numbers (Jacob Bernoulli, 1655 to 1705, published 1713) says only that a sample mean gets closer to the population mean as N grows; it says nothing about the shape of the sampling distribution.

Exam trap

The standard error is the SD of the sampling distribution of the mean: not the SD of the sample (which does not shrink with N) and not M − μ (sampling error). As N rises the sampling distribution approaches normal; it does not grow skewed, does not become identical to the population distribution, and its SE gets smaller. "Normal distribution theorem" and "sampling distribution theorem" are invented names.

Estimation and confidence intervals

A point estimate is a single value, such as a sample mean of 52 for μ. An interval estimate, the confidence interval (CI), adds a margin: M ± 1.96 SE for 95 percent and M ± 2.58 SE for 99 percent. With M = 52, SD = 12 and N = 36, SE = 2, so the 95 percent CI runs from 48.08 to 55.92 and the 99 percent CI, wider, from 46.84 to 57.16. Higher confidence, a larger SD and a smaller N all widen the interval.

The population mean is fixed, so the confidence belongs to the procedure: if the sampling were repeated many times, 95 percent of the intervals so built would contain the true mean. If a 95 percent CI for a difference includes zero, the difference is not significant at α = .05 (two-sided): a mean difference of 0.4 with a CI of −0.1 to 0.9 is non-significant. That is not proof of no effect, since the interval also allows 0.9; a wide CI signals an imprecise estimate. For ratios the null value is 1 (section 9.5).

Exam trap

A 95 percent CI is NOT "a 95 percent probability that μ lies in this interval", NOT "95 percent of individual scores fall in it" and NOT "a 95 percent chance of significance". A CI crossing zero means non-significant, not a difference of exactly zero.

The logic and steps of a significance test

A significance test assumes the null hypothesis (H0: no difference or effect), asks how probable the data would be if H0 were true, and rejects H0 in favour of the alternative hypothesis (H1) if they would be rare (wording hypotheses: section 9.1). The steps:

  1. State H0 and H1; choose a one-tailed or two-tailed test.
  2. Set the significance level (α), usually .05 or .01, before seeing data.
  3. Compute the test statistic (z, t, F, chi-square): the observed effect over its standard error.
  4. Compare it with the critical value bounding the critical region, or compare p with α.
  5. Reject H0 or fail to reject (retain) it; report the effect size and CI.

A stress scale has a population mean of 50 and SD of 12; after a mindfulness course 36 students average 55. Then z = (55 − 50)/(12/√36) = 2.5: beyond 1.96, so significant at .05 two-tailed (p about .012), but short of 2.58, so not at .01. Table 9.26 gives the cut-offs. They explain the rough rule that a score is significant when it lies more than two standard deviations from the mean: about 95 percent of a normal distribution lies within two SDs, so a value beyond is in the outer 5 percent, while values within one SD, or between one and two, are ordinary chance results.

The p-value and what it is not

The p-value is the probability of obtaining the observed result, or one more extreme, if H0 is true: the probability, revealed by a statistical test, that a random process (random samples of a given size from a given population) could produce that outcome. If p ≤ α, reject H0: with α = .05, p = .03 is statistically significant and H0 is rejected.

Because p assumes H0 and asks about the data, it is not the probability that H0 (or H1) is true, which would need Bayes' theorem. Nor is it the effect size (a huge sample makes a trivial effect give tiny p), the probability of replication, or power. It is continuous: p = .049 and p = .051 are essentially the same evidence, because .05 is a convention, not a natural boundary. Two distractors share a letter: the q-value, the false-discovery-rate analogue of p used when thousands of tests are run (brain imaging, genetics), and Tukey's studentised range q (section 9.10).

Exam trap

The key is always "the probability of the data (or more extreme) if H0 is true"; the favourite wrong option reverses it, "the probability that H0 is true". Nor is p the magnitude of a correlation or the sample size, and p = .001 does not mean a large, clinically important or certain-to-replicate effect.

Type I and Type II errors

A decision from a sample can go wrong in two ways (Table 9.27). A Type I error (alpha error) is rejecting a true null hypothesis: a false positive. Its probability is α, so the significance level is the probability of a Type I error, in analysis of variance as in every test; it does not certify that F is correct, and the Type II risk is β, not α. A Type II error (beta error) is retaining, or loosely "accepting", a false null hypothesis: a false negative, a real effect missed. Its probability, β, is not chosen; it follows from N, the true effect, α and variability.

Swapped wordings ("accepting a false null" offered as Type I) are the commonest wrong options. Type III error is informal: the right answer to the wrong question, or rejecting H0 but in the wrong direction; "Type IV" is rarer still. Low power, sample size (too small, too large or "large sample bias"), a wide CI, a misused one-tailed test, sampling error, standard error and confounding (section 9.2) are not definitions of these errors, though several change their risk.

Statistical power

Power (1 − β) is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis, of finding an effect that is really there; the convention is at least 0.80. Power rises with a larger true effect, a larger N (it shrinks the SE, pulling the curves of Figure 9.17 apart), a larger, less strict α (at the price of more Type I errors), lower variability (homogeneous samples, standard procedures, repeated measures, reliable measures) and a correctly predicted one-tailed test. It falls when N shrinks, α becomes stricter or variability grows. Power analysis, done before a study, fixes α, the desired power and the smallest effect worth detecting, and solves for N. Jacob Cohen (1923 to 1998) made it routine in Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences (2nd edition, 1988): detecting a medium effect (d = 0.5) with an independent-samples t-test at α = .05, two-tailed, and power .80 needs about 64 people per group.

Significance says an effect is probably not zero; effect size says how big it is, independent of N. Cohen's d is the standardised difference between two means, (M1 − M2)/pooled SD, with benchmarks 0.2 small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. The mindfulness example gives d = 5/12 = 0.42, between small and medium, despite p = .012. The correlation r is also an effect size (.10, .30, .50); its sign gives only direction, so r = −.30 is a medium negative relationship, not "none", not perfect (−1.00) and not causal (section 9.11). 9.10 Comparing Groups: t-Tests, ANOVA and Non-Parametric TestsHigh yield · 57 Qs

A counsellor at a Mysuru college tries three ways of revising for examinations: practice testing, flashcards and plain rereading. A month later the groups' recall scores differ a little. Is the difference real, or the wobble any three groups would show by chance? Section 9.9 gave the logic of significance testing; here are the tools. Choosing one always comes down to three questions: what kind of data, how many groups, and are the groups independent or made of the same (or matched) people?

Parametric or non-parametric?

A parametric test estimates population parameters (means and variances) and rests on four assumptions: the scores come from a normally distributed population; the groups have roughly equal variances (homogeneity of variance); observations are independent of one another; and the dependent variable (DV) is measured on an interval or ratio scale, so that means make sense. The z-test, t-tests, ANOVA's F-test and Pearson's r are parametric. When data are normal with equal variances, a parametric test is the right choice, because it uses the actual distances between scores and so has the most power.

A non-parametric test, also called a distribution-free test, makes few assumptions about the population's shape and works on ranks, signs or frequency counts. Sidney Siegel (1916 to 1961) brought these tests into psychology with Nonparametric Statistics for the Behavioral Sciences (1956). They suit ordinal data (ranks), nominal data (counted categories), very small samples and clearly violated assumptions (heavy skew, outliers, very unequal variances). Rank order is thus the classic non-parametric data, whereas height, weight and Celsius temperature are interval or ratio measures (9.8). Chi-square is not a third family: it is the non-parametric test for nominal frequencies.

The price is power, because ranking discards the distances between scores. Siegel measured the cost as power-efficiency: the sample a parametric test needs, as a percentage of the sample a non-parametric test needs for equal power. If a t-test needs 95 cases to match what the Mann-Whitney test does with 100, Mann-Whitney is 95 percent power-efficient. Under normality the Mann-Whitney and Wilcoxon tests reach about 95 percent; the sign test only about 63 percent in large samples.

Exam trap

"Which is non-parametric data?" Rank order, not height, weight or Celsius temperature (Celsius has an arbitrary zero but equal intervals: interval data). "Distribution-free" is just another name for non-parametric. For normal data with equal variances the answer is parametric tests, never "chi-square tests" or "distribution-free tests".

Checking the assumptions

Normality is tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test (the usual choice) or the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which compares the sample's cumulative distribution with a normal one; it is inspected with a Q-Q plot (quantile-quantile plot), where normal data hug a straight diagonal, and with skew and kurtosis values near zero (9.8). Homogeneity of variance, or homoscedasticity, is tested with Levene's test (Howard Levene, 1960), which stays accurate when data are not normal; Bartlett's test, more powerful but upset by non-normality; or Hartley's F-max, the largest group variance divided by the smallest (near 1 is fine). These checks precede a t-test, an ANOVA or a regression. Independence has no test: it is secured by random sampling and assignment, and its violation is the most damaging.

Repeated-measures ANOVA adds sphericity: equal variances of the difference scores between every pair of conditions. It matters only with three or more conditions, is tested by Mauchly's test, and when violated the df are reduced by the Greenhouse-Geisser correction (more conservative) or the Huynh-Feldt correction (less so). ANCOVA adds homogeneity of regression slopes (9.10.6). Read these tests backwards: their null hypothesis is that the assumption holds, so a non-significant result (p > .05) is good news.

Exam trap

Shapiro-Wilk tests normality, NOT homogeneity of variance, so it is the odd one out beside Levene's and Bartlett's tests. Levene's test checks equal variances, not normality, independence or equal group sizes. Equal group sizes are not an assumption, though they make ANOVA more forgiving of unequal variances.

The t-tests

A z-test compares means using the population standard deviation, which you almost never know (older texts allow it above about 30 cases). Estimating the standard deviation from the sample adds a second source of error, and the statistic then follows the t distribution.

Key study: "Student" at the Guinness brewery (1908)

William Sealy Gosset (1876 to 1937), a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, had to judge barley and stout from tiny samples. He worked out the exact distribution of a mean divided by its estimated standard error. Guinness barred staff from publishing under their own names, so his 1908 Biometrika paper appeared as "Student", hence Student's t. (Some Indian texts print 1915; the paper is from 1908.)

The t distribution is a family of curves, one for each number of degrees of freedom (df). Like the normal curve it is symmetrical and bell-shaped around zero; unlike it, it has heavier tails and a lower peak, so its variability and kurtosis are greater. As df rise it closes in on the normal curve (Figure 9.18), which is why critical t values shrink towards 1.96 in large samples.

There are three t-tests, each with its own df.

  • The one-sample t-test compares one sample mean with a known population value; df = N - 1, since once the mean is fixed the last score cannot vary. N = 20 gives 19; N = 30 gives 29.
  • The independent-samples t-test (unpaired t) compares the means of two separate groups, using their pooled variance. Its df = N1 + N2 - 2: 25 per group gives 48. If Levene's test shows unequal variances, Welch's t-test is used; it does not pool the variances and lowers the df.
  • The paired-samples t-test (dependent or matched-pairs t) is for the same people measured twice, pre and post, or for matched pairs. It tests whether the mean difference score is zero; df = pairs - 1. Each person acts as their own control, so individual differences leave the error term and power rises.

Why not run three t-tests on three groups? Each carries a 5 percent Type I risk (9.9), and the risks accumulate. With c tests the chance of at least one false positive, the family-wise error rate, is 1 - (1 - .05)c: about .14 for three tests and .26 for the six that four groups need. ANOVA tests all the means at once at one alpha.

Exam trap

One-sample df: N - 1. Independent: N1 + N2 - 2 (25 per group gives 48, not 24, 49 or 50). Paired: pairs - 1. The t curve shares only its symmetry with the normal curve; its kurtosis and variability are larger.

One-way ANOVA: partitioning variance

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was developed by Ronald Aylmer Fisher (1890 to 1962) for agricultural trials at Rothamsted Experimental Station and published in Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925); George Waddel Snedecor (1881 to 1974) named its statistic F in Fisher's honour. A one-way ANOVA compares the means of independent groups formed by one independent variable (IV, or factor) on one continuous DV. It is used when more than two means are compared, though it works for two as well, where F = t2.

ANOVA compares means by analysing variances. The deviations of all scores from the grand mean sum to zero, so they are squared and added to give the total sum of squares. Fisher split it into exactly two parts. The between-groups sum of squares reflects how far each group mean lies from the grand mean: treatment effect plus chance. The within-groups sum of squares reflects how far each person lies from their own group mean: since a group was treated alike, this is pure chance or error. SS total = SS between + SS within, and each part has its own df: between = k - 1 (k groups), within = N - k, total = N - 1. Three groups of 10 give 2, 27 and 29.

Each SS divided by its df is a mean square (MS), a variance. MS within is the pooled error variance, and it is the denominator of the F ratio: F = MS between / MS within. If the null hypothesis is true, both estimate the same chance variance and F is about 1; a real effect swells only the numerator. So an F below 1, such as 0.99, can never be significant whatever the df, and as variability due to chance falls, F rises. Figure 9.19 and Table 9.29 work through the revision study.

One-way ANOVA has two sources of variance, each with its own df; "error variance" is the within-groups part, not a third source, and "information variance" does not exist. F = 0.99 is never significant; F is never negative. ANOVA tests means, not "variance only", and unequal variances violate its assumption.

Post hoc tests (a posteriori or multiple comparison tests) compare groups pair by pair while holding the family-wise error rate down. They are used only after a significant F; after a non-significant F you stop. With k groups there are k(k - 1)/2 pairs: 3 for three groups, 6 for four, 10 for five. Factorial, repeated-measures and covariance designs

A factorial ANOVA (two-way or higher) crosses two or more IVs so that every level of one meets every level of the other; a 2 × 3 design has two factors and six cells. It tests a main effect of each IV (averaged over the other) and an interaction effect, present when the effect of one IV depends on the level of another: plotted, the lines are not parallel. Of the familiar techniques only factorial ANOVA detects interactions; a t-test has one two-level IV, chi-square tests association between two categorical variables, and correlation measures how two variables move together. A significant interaction is interpreted before the main effects.

Repeated-measures ANOVA tests the same people under three or more conditions, extending the paired t-test. It accounts for the correlation among each person's scores by removing individual differences from the error term, so it needs fewer people; but it is still parametric, assuming normality, plus sphericity. A randomised block design uses different people grouped into homogeneous blocks (by pre-test, age or IQ) and randomly assigned to treatments within each block, so block differences leave the error term. With one person per treatment per block, error df = (treatments - 1)(blocks - 1): 4 treatments and 5 blocks give 3 × 4 = 12. A mixed (split-plot) ANOVA combines a between-subjects and a within-subjects factor.

Clinical link

Most therapy trials are mixed designs: group (therapy or waitlist) by time (pre, post, follow-up). Evidence that therapy worked is the group × time interaction, not either main effect.

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), also Fisher's, builds a regression into ANOVA. A covariate (a continuous variable such as pre-test score or IQ) predicts the DV, its effect is removed, and groups are compared on adjusted means. This raises power and statistically equates groups that started unequal, though it cannot replace random assignment. ANCOVA keeps ANOVA's assumptions and adds a linear covariate-DV relation, a covariate measured reliably before treatment, and above all homogeneity of regression slopes: the covariate must predict the DV equally in every group, so the regression lines are parallel. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) tests groups on two or more DVs at once, avoiding the Type I error of many separate ANOVAs; its statistics are Wilks' lambda (most reported; smaller means a bigger effect), Pillai's trace (most robust), Hotelling's trace and Roy's largest root. MANCOVA adds covariates. In all these names, M means more than one DV and C means a covariate.

Exam trap

The assumption ANCOVA adds to ANOVA is homogeneity of regression slopes. Homogeneity of variance and homoscedasticity (one idea, two names) belong to both; equal sample sizes to neither. Repeated-measures ANOVA is not non-parametric, still assumes normality and is not limited to two conditions.

Chi-square: comparing frequencies

Nominal data offer no means, only counts of people in categories. For these, Karl Pearson (1857 to 1936) introduced the chi-square test (χ²) in 1900. It compares the observed frequency (O) in each category with the frequency expected (E) under the null hypothesis: χ² = Σ (O - E)²/E. Being squared, it is never negative and its distribution is positively skewed. It is a non-parametric test for categorical frequencies, not for interval, ratio or parametric data.

The goodness-of-fit test asks whether one variable's frequencies match a hypothesised pattern (do students prefer four brands of chai equally?); df = k - 1 for k categories. The test of independence asks whether two categorical variables in a contingency table are associated (is gender related to treatment preference?). Each expected frequency is row total × column total / N, and df = (rows - 1)(columns - 1), because once the margins are fixed only that many cells can vary: 2 × 2 gives 1, 3 × 4 gives 6, 4 × 5 gives 12.

Chi-square needs raw counts (not percentages), each person in one cell only, and an expected frequency of at least 5 per cell. For 2 × 2 tables with small expected values, Frank Yates (1902 to 1994) proposed the Yates' correction for continuity in 1934, subtracting 0.5 from each |O - E| before squaring, while Fisher's exact test computes the exact probability of a small table. Paired yes-or-no data (the same people before and after) need the McNemar test (Quinn McNemar, 1947), which counts only those who changed. Chi-square shows whether an association exists, not its strength; for that report phi (2 × 2), the contingency coefficient or Cramér's V (9.11).

Exam trap

Contingency df is a product, (rows - 1)(columns - 1): 3 × 4 gives 6, not 12, 7 or 11; 4 × 5 gives 12, not 20 or 9. It is not N - 1 (one-sample t) or N - 2 (independent t), and it subtracts 1, not 2.

Rank tests and choosing the right test

Each parametric test has a rank-based partner. The Mann-Whitney U test (Henry Berthold Mann and Donald Ransom Whitney, 1947) pools and ranks the scores of two independent samples and asks whether one group's ranks run higher. It is the non-parametric equivalent of the independent-samples t-test, and identical to the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For two related samples, the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test (Frank Wilcoxon, 1945) ranks the size of each pair's difference and reattaches the signs, using both direction and magnitude; the sign test counts only pluses and minuses (direction) and is weaker. For three or more independent groups, the Kruskal-Wallis H test (William Kruskal and W. Allen Wallis, 1952) is the one-way ANOVA by ranks, H being read against chi-square with k - 1 df. For three or more related samples or repeated measures, the Friedman test of the economist Milton Friedman (1912 to 2006), devised in 1937, ranks conditions within each person: the two-way ANOVA by ranks.

The median test splits independent groups at the combined median and applies chi-square. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample test compares two samples' whole cumulative distributions, so it also catches differences in shape and spread. The Wald-Wolfowitz runs test counts runs of the same group in the pooled, ordered scores; few runs mean the groups differ. Cochran's Q extends McNemar to three or more related yes-or-no samples. 9.11 Correlation, Regression and Factor AnalysisMedium yield · 28 Qs

Does stress steal sleep? Is a satisfied worker a productive one? Such questions concern quantities that rise and fall together, not the group means of Section 9.10. Correlation measures how strongly two variables go together, regression turns that association into a prediction, and factor analysis asks how many underlying dimensions produced a whole table of correlations.

Correlation: direction, strength and form

A correlation is an association between two variables measured on the same people. It has a direction, positive when both rise together (study hours and marks) and negative when one rises as the other falls (stress and sleep quality); a strength, how closely the scores keep to the pattern; and a form, straight or curved. Sir Francis Galton (1822 to 1911), studying heredity, described such "co-relations" in 1888, and his protégé Karl Pearson (1857 to 1936) gave them an exact formula in 1896: the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, r. So r measures association; it is not a mean, a variance or a ratio.

Correlational data are graphed as a scatterplot (scatter diagram), with one variable on each axis and each person a single dot (Figure 9.20). A rising cloud is a positive correlation, a falling cloud a negative one, and a shapeless cloud no relationship. The last panel is a warning: a clear inverted U gives r = 0, because r detects only straight-line relations. Do not confuse the scatterplot with the bell curve or a skewed distribution, which describe a single variable (Section 9.8), or with chi-square, which is a test, not a graph (Section 9.10).

Reading Pearson's r

Pearson's r runs from -1.00 through 0 to +1.00. Its sign gives the direction; its size, ignoring the sign, gives the strength. So r = -0.85 between stress and sleep quality is a strong negative (inverse) relationship, exactly as strong as +0.85: the more stress, the worse the sleep, and the scores keep closely to that pattern. The range of r is never 0 to 1 (that is the range of r2 and of multiple R), never -0.04 to +0.04, and never 0 to 100 or -100 to +100. In 1985 Michelle Iaffaldano and Paul Muchinsky pooled the studies in a meta-analysis (Section 9.5) and found a mean correlation of only about .17. In 2001 Timothy Judge and colleagues, pooling more than 300 samples and correcting for measurement error, put it at about .30. Job satisfaction and performance thus show a weak (modest) positive correlation: not zero, not negative, not strong, and silent on which causes which.

Assumptions, limits and testing r

Pearson's r is the technique for two continuous (interval or ratio) variables, such as stress level and sleep duration. Chi-square (association between categories), the t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Mann-Whitney U test (differences between groups) answer other questions (Section 9.10). Pearson's r assumes linearity; homoscedasticity, an equal scatter of Y at every value of X, whose violation, heteroscedasticity, shows as a fan-shaped cloud; bivariate normality, for significance tests; and independence of cases. Observations that depend on earlier ones, such as daily mood ratings, show autocorrelation: a variable correlated with itself over time. Homogeneity of variance is the group version of homoscedasticity, equal variances across groups. r is also distorted by restriction of range (entrance scores predict grades poorly among admitted students alone, whose scores span a narrow band), by outliers, by curvilinear relations and by pooling unlike groups.

A sample r is tested with t = r√(N - 2) / √(1 - r2) on N - 2 degrees of freedom: r = .30 fails with 20 people but passes with 100. To compare two correlations, Sir Ronald Aylmer Fisher (1890 to 1962) devised Fisher's r-to-z transformation, z' = 0.5 ln[(1 + r)/(1 - r)], which removes the skew in the sampling distribution of r. Fisher's z-test then divides the difference between the two z' values by its standard error, √[1/(N1 - 3) + 1/(N2 - 3)]. A t-test compares two means, ANOVA several, and Pearson's r measures a single association; none compares two correlations.

When the data are ranks or categories, another coefficient is used. The key distinction is between a true dichotomy, two natural categories (alive or dead), and an artificial dichotomy, a continuous variable cut in two (pass or fail from marks). Charles Spearman (1863 to 1945) introduced the rank-difference method in 1904. Spearman's rho, ρ = 1 - 6ΣD2 / N(N2 - 1), where D is each person's difference in ranks, correlates ranks; it therefore suits ordinal data and monotonic (steadily rising or falling) but non-linear relations, and it resists outliers. Kendall's tau, from Maurice Kendall (1938), counts agreeing and disagreeing pairs of ranks and suits small samples with many ties. Partial, semi-partial and multiple correlation

Ordinary r is a zero-order correlation: nothing is held constant. Marital stress may rise with length of marriage partly because long marriages have more children. The partial correlation, r12.3, is the correlation between two variables after a third variable's influence has been removed statistically from both; partialling out the number of children gives the stress and length relation among couples with equally many children. Holding one variable constant gives a first-order partial, two a second-order partial. A semi-partial correlation (part correlation) removes the third variable from only one of the two, usually the predictor; its square is a predictor's unique contribution in multiple regression, and it is never larger than the partial. The multiple correlation (R, from 0 to 1) links one variable with the best weighted combination of several others. Linear (ordinary) correlation, biserial correlation and multiple correlation remove nothing.

Exam trap

"Controlling for", "holding constant" or "subtracting out the influence of" a third variable always means partial correlation.

Simple linear regression

Regression also began with Galton. In 1886 he found that children of very tall parents were tall, but less extremely so, and called it "regression towards mediocrity". This regression to the mean follows whenever two measures correlate imperfectly: extreme scores on one pair with less extreme scores on the other (a threat to validity, Section 9.2).

Simple linear regression predicts a criterion Y from a predictor X with the line Y = a + bX (Figure 9.21). The intercept a is the predicted Y when X is zero; the regression coefficient b is the slope, the change in Y for each one-unit rise in X. b = r(SDY/SDX) and a = MY - bMX, where SD is the standard deviation and M the mean. If hours and marks correlate .60, the SD of marks is 10 and the SD of hours 2, then b = 3 marks per hour; with means of 5 hours and 50 marks, a = 35, so 8 hours predicts 35 + 24 = 59 marks.

The method of least squares, published by Adrien-Marie Legendre (1752 to 1833) in 1805 and developed by Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777 to 1855), chooses the line that minimises the sum of squared residuals (actual minus predicted Y). Their typical size is the standard error of estimate, SDY√(1 - r2). Regression assumes linearity, independence of residuals, normality of residuals and equal residual variance (LINE). Correlation is symmetrical and unit-free; regression is directional and gives an equation in real units. A researcher therefore uses regression to predict the value of one variable from another, not to find a mean, count frequencies or estimate sampling error.

Exam trap

In Y = a + bX the slope is b; a is the intercept, X the predictor and Y the predicted score.

Multiple regression and multicollinearity

Multiple regression uses several predictors, Y = a + b1X1 + b2X2 + ..., each b holding the others constant. Standardised beta weights (β) let predictors be compared. R2 is the variance all predictors explain together, and adjusted R2 corrects its rise with every added predictor. Entry may be simultaneous (all at once), hierarchical (theory-ordered blocks, testing the change in R2) or stepwise (chosen by the computer, which capitalises on chance).

When predictors correlate highly with each other, the result is multicollinearity. Anxiety and neuroticism scores correlating .90 carry the same information, so R2 stays high but the weights become unstable, their standard errors inflate and signs may flip. A variance inflation factor (VIF) above 10 (above 5 on a stricter rule) or a tolerance (1/VIF) below .10 flags it; the cure is to drop or combine predictors.

Exam trap

Multicollinearity is correlation among predictors, not between a predictor and the outcome. Heteroscedasticity is unequal residual spread, autocorrelation is residuals correlated over time, homogeneity of variance is equal group variances, a categorical outcome calls for logistic regression, and "assumptions are violated" defines nothing.

A dichotomous outcome (relapsed or not) calls for logistic regression, which models the log of the odds, estimates weights by maximum likelihood, expresses each as an odds ratio (above 1 raises the odds, below 1 lowers them) and tests it with the Wald test. Regression, the t-test, ANOVA and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) are all cases of the general linear model: factorial designs are analysed by ANOVA, and ANCOVA blends ANOVA with regression, using the covariate's correlation with the outcome (Section 9.10). Mediation and moderation are tested by regression, following Reuben Baron and David Kenny (1986), with the Sobel test of Michael Sobel (1982) for indirect effects (Section 9.2). Discriminant analysis predicts membership of known groups from continuous variables. Path analysis, devised by Sewall Wright (1889 to 1988) in 1921, chains regressions to test a diagram of directional paths, and structural equation modelling (SEM) adds latent variables, joining a confirmatory factor analysis to a path model.

Factor analysis: the dimensions behind correlations

Twenty tests yield 190 correlations, and factor analysis asks how few underlying factors could produce them: if verbal tests correlate among themselves and numerical tests among themselves, two factors may explain the whole table. It serves data reduction and construct validation (Chapter 2). Spearman invented it in 1904 to argue for a general factor, g (Chapter 1). Louis Leon Thurstone (1887 to 1955) extended it in the 1930s into multiple factor analysis, with the goal of simple structure: each variable loading highly on one factor and near zero on the others.

A factor loading is a variable's correlation with a factor; about .30 to .40 or more counts. A variable's squared loadings summed across factors give its communality (h2), and 1 - h2 is its uniqueness. A factor's squared loadings summed down the column give its eigenvalue, the variance it explains in units of one variable. Before factoring, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy should be at least .60 and Bartlett's test of sphericity significant (the correlation matrix is not an identity matrix), with at least 10 cases per variable and ideally 300 or more. Of the extraction methods, principal components analysis (Harold Hotelling, 1933) analyses total variance for data reduction, whereas principal axis factoring and maximum likelihood analyse only common variance, to model latent factors.

Retaining, rotating and confirming factors

How many factors? The Kaiser-Guttman rule (Louis Guttman, 1916 to 1987, and Henry Kaiser) keeps eigenvalues above 1.0, since a factor below 1 explains less than one variable; it tends to keep too many. The scree test of Raymond Cattell (1905 to 1998), published in 1966, keeps the factors above the elbow of the eigenvalue plot, where the slope flattens into "scree", the rubble below a cliff. John Horn's parallel analysis (1965), the most accurate, keeps factors whose eigenvalues beat those of random data. With eigenvalues of 3.5, 1.8, 0.9, 0.7 and 0.5 (Figure 9.22), two exceed 1.0 and two lie above the elbow at factor 3: retain two.

Exam trap

The elbow factor itself is dropped: an elbow at factor 3 means retain two, not three. Five counts every eigenvalue, and one keeps only the largest.

Factor rotation turns the factor axes towards simple structure without changing the variance explained. An orthogonal rotation keeps factors uncorrelated: varimax (Kaiser, 1958) simplifies each factor, quartimax each variable, and equimax compromises. An oblique rotation (promax, direct oblimin) lets factors correlate, as psychological constructs usually do. A second-order factor is extracted from the correlations among first-order factors (general ability above verbal, numerical and spatial factors, a hierarchical model), so it needs an oblique rotation such as promax; orthogonal factors leave no correlations to factor.

Exam trap

Second-order factor analysis needs promax (oblique). Varimax, quartimax and equimax are all orthogonal.

All of this is exploratory factor analysis (EFA): data-driven, with no prior model. By contrast, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), developed by Karl Jöreskog in 1969, tests a pre-specified structure, fixing which items load on which factor, and judges fit by the comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), about .95 or above, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), about .06 or below, and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), .08 or below, with modification indices suggesting changes. It needs no rotation, takes continuous or categorical items, and is the measurement model of SEM. Cluster analysis groups people rather than variables, and multidimensional scaling maps judged similarities between objects in as few dimensions as possible.

The Case of Clever Hans, Revisited

Pfungst's investigation is a compact course in this chapter. He turned a marvel into a research problem with rival, falsifiable hypotheses and preferred the simplest explanation that fitted, as Lloyd Morgan's canon advises (9.1). He varied one condition at a time while holding the others constant, and exposed the questioner's expectation as a hidden variable that undermined every earlier demonstration (9.2). His study of one horse was a single-case experiment (9.4); he observed systematically (9.6) and honestly reported a result that deflated a celebrity (9.7). His verdict rested on counts of right and wrong answers compared across conditions (9.8 to 9.10). And the blinding of modern clinical trials (9.5), in which those who assess outcomes do not know who was treated, exists so that no one plays von Osten again.

Chapter summary

9.1 Science and the Research Process: Problems and Hypotheses. Science differs from tenacity, authority and the a priori method (Peirce, 1877) because it fixes belief by public, repeatable observation. Scientific research is empirical, objective, replicable, parsimonious (Occam's razor, Lloyd Morgan's canon), falsifiable (Popper, 1934) and tentative; Kuhn (1962) described paradigm shifts. Induction builds theory from observations (theory development, qualitative research); deduction tests theory against data; abduction infers the best explanation. Methodology is wider than methods. Research is basic or applied (including Lewin's action research), exploratory, descriptive or explanatory, nomothetic or idiographic (Allport). A clear problem and a literature review (which provides a theoretical framework) yield hypotheses: null or alternative, directional or non-directional, universal or existential.

9.2 Variables, Experimental Control and the Validity of Research. A variable takes two or more values; the independent variable is the presumed cause (manipulated or selected) and the dependent variable the measured outcome. A confound varies with the IV and affects the DV; a moderator changes the strength of an effect and a mediator carries it (Baron and Kenny, 1986). Control follows Kerlinger's MAXMINCON principle (maximise primary, minimise error, control secondary variance), above all through random assignment. Hawthorne, demand (Orne), expectancy and Pygmalion (Rosenthal) and halo (Thorndike) effects distort results; double-blinding controls expectancy on both sides. Studies are judged by statistical conclusion, internal, construct and external validity (Campbell and Stanley, 1963; Cook and Campbell, 1979).

9.3 Sampling: Populations, Probability and Non-Probability Methods. A parameter describes a population and a statistic a sample. Following Blalock (1960), sampling methods are probability, non-probability or mixed. In probability sampling every element has a known, non-zero chance of selection, so sampling error can be calculated and the sample is likely, though not guaranteed, to be representative: simple random, systematic (every kth after a random start), stratified (random sampling within every stratum) and cluster sampling (random choice of whole natural groups, multistage when repeated down levels). In non-probability sampling (convenience, purposive, quota, snowball, saturation, dense) the chance is unknown. Sampling error shrinks as n rises; bias, as in the 1936 Literary Digest poll, does not.

9.4 Research Designs: Experimental, Single-Case and Non-Experimental. Only an experiment, with manipulation, control and random assignment that makes the groups equivalent, licenses cause-and-effect conclusions; Fisher's (1935) principles are replication, randomisation and local control. Campbell and Stanley (1963) classed designs as pre-experimental, true experimental (including Solomon's four-group design, which controls pretest sensitisation) and quasi-experimental (intact groups, no random assignment). Experiments may be between-subjects, within-subjects (order effects controlled by counterbalancing and Latin squares), mixed or crossover; factorial designs reveal interactions. Single-case designs (A-B-A-B, multiple baseline) test a treatment in one person. Correlational, survey (a census is a survey) and ex post facto designs cannot establish cause; longitudinal designs suffer attrition, cross-sectional designs cohort effects.

9.5 Clinical Epidemiology, Randomised Trials and Evidence Synthesis. Epidemiology counts disorder as prevalence (existing cases: point, period, lifetime) or incidence (new cases); prevalence is roughly incidence times duration. Cross-sectional studies give prevalence, case-control studies (best for rare outcomes) give odds ratios, and cohort studies, such as Doll and Hill's prospective British Doctors Study, give relative risk; a ratio of 1 means no association, and NNT = 1/ARR. Sensitivity and specificity belong to the test, but predictive values depend on the base rate (Meehl and Rosen, 1955). Trials rely on randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding and intention-to-treat analysis. Meta-analysis (Glass, 1976) pools many studies; CONSORT reports trials and PRISMA systematic reviews.

9.6 Methods of Data Collection and Qualitative Research. Data are primary or secondary. Observation is participant or non-participant, structured or unstructured, naturalistic or controlled, overt or covert. Interviews run from structured to unstructured, with open-ended questions, probes and Spradley's ethnographic inquiry. A questionnaire is self-answered, a schedule filled in by the enumerator; items must not be double-barrelled, leading or loaded. Berelson's content analysis, Moreno's sociometry and Dilthey's Verstehen follow. Guba and Lincoln's paradigms run from positivism (naive realism) to constructivism (relativism). Qualitative research is naturalistic, inductive and emic; ethnography, grounded theory (open, axial, selective coding) and phenomenology lead its traditions, judged by trustworthiness and strengthened by triangulation.

9.7 Research Ethics and Writing the Research Report. Research ethics grew out of abuse: the Nazi experiments produced the Nuremberg Code (1947), and the Tuskegee study (1932 to 1972) led to the Belmont Report (1979: respect for persons, beneficence, justice). The APA Ethics Code (2002) has five general principles, and in India the ICMR guidelines (2017) require ethics committee approval. Informed consent tells participants enough to decide voluntarily before taking part; debriefing gives full information afterwards (dehoaxing and desensitising). Confidentiality differs from anonymity, deception needs strict justification, and animal research follows the 3Rs. Misconduct means fabrication, falsification and plagiarism; citation is not plagiarism. A research report communicates findings, and its Discussion, not its Results, interprets them.

9.8 Measurement Scales, Organising Data and Descriptive Statistics. S. S. Stevens (1946) gave four levels of measurement: nominal (unordered categories), ordinal (ranks), interval (equal intervals, arbitrary zero, as in Celsius) and ratio (true zero, as in height). Raw data are edited, coded, classified and tabulated, analysed in SPSS or Excel, and shown as frequency distributions and graphs. The mean suits symmetrical data, the median skewed data and the mode nominal data; the SD, the square root of the variance, measures spread. The normal curve holds about 68, 95 and 99.7 per cent of cases within 1, 2 and 3 SD, and z = (X − M)/SD converts any score into a percentile or another standard score.

9.9 Sampling Distributions and the Logic of Hypothesis Testing. Inferential statistics use sample data and the laws of chance to judge whether a result could be due to chance. The sampling distribution of the mean centres on the population mean, its SD is the standard error (σ/√N), and by the central limit theorem it is near normal once N is about 30. A 95 percent CI is M ± 1.96 SE. A test assumes H0 and rejects it when p ≤ α. A Type I error rejects a true H0 (α); a Type II error retains a false one (β). Power is 1 − β; Cohen's d of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 is small, medium and large.

9.10 Comparing Groups: t-Tests, ANOVA and Non-Parametric Tests. Parametric tests (z, t, F) assume normality, equal variances, independence and interval or ratio data; non-parametric or distribution-free tests (Siegel, 1956) handle ranks, categories and small or skewed samples. Gosset's t tests one or two means (df N - 1, N1 + N2 - 2 or pairs - 1). Fisher's ANOVA splits variance into between and within parts, F = MS between / MS within, about 1 under H0; a significant F is followed by post hoc tests or a Bonferroni correction. Factorial ANOVA detects interactions; ANCOVA adds homogeneity of regression slopes. Chi-square handles frequencies, with df (r - 1)(c - 1), and rank tests partner each parametric test.

9.11 Correlation, Regression and Factor Analysis. Correlation (Pearson's r, 1896) measures the direction (sign) and strength (size) of a linear association from -1 to +1. It is graphed as a scatterplot; r squared is shared variance (+0.90 means 81 per cent), and correlation aids prediction but never proves causation. Spearman's rho, point-biserial, biserial, phi and tetrachoric suit other data; partial correlation removes a third variable from both. Regression (Y = a + bX) predicts one variable from another; multiple regression is undermined by multicollinearity among predictors. Factor analysis (Spearman, 1904) reduces correlated variables to a few factors, retained by eigenvalues above 1 or the scree elbow and rotated orthogonally (varimax) or obliquely (promax).

From the past papers

  1. 'Reinforcement will have positive effect on performance,' which type of hypothesis is this? (RML 2024)

    (a) Null and directional (b) Alternate and directional (c) Null and non-directional (d) Alternate and non-directional

    Answer: (b) Alternate and directional. The statement predicts an effect, so it is the alternative hypothesis (the null would predict no effect), and it says which way the effect goes (positive), so it is directional and would be tested one-tailed.

  2. Nomothetic approach of study is involved in (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Qualitative method (b) Experimental method (c) Quasi-experimental method (d) Ethnography

    Answer: (b) Experimental method. The nomothetic approach seeks general laws across many people, and the experiment is its purest method. Quasi-experiments are nomothetic too but are the weaker choice; qualitative methods and ethnography study particular cases in depth and are idiographic.

  3. In which method does the researcher start with a theory and then test it with observations? (RML 2023)

    (a) Inductive reasoning (b) Deductive reasoning (c) Abductive reasoning (d) Hypothetico-deductive method

    Answer: (b) Deductive reasoning. Deduction runs top-down from theory to hypothesis to observation. Induction runs the other way (observations to theory), abduction infers the best explanation for a surprising fact, and the hypothetico-deductive method is the whole cycle of conjecture and test, not the direction of reasoning asked for.

  4. Maximization of ____ variance, minimization of ____ variance, control of ____ variance. Select the appropriate option. (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) secondary, true, error (b) true, primary, secondary (c) primary, error, secondary (d) primary, true, error

    Answer: (c) primary, error, secondary. This is Kerlinger's MAXMINCON principle: maximise primary (experimental) variance due to the IV, minimise error variance, control secondary (extraneous) variance. True variance belongs to test theory, not to this principle.

  5. Which of the following can help control experimenter's bias? (RML 2022)

    (a) Double-blinding (b) Randomly assigning subjects to groups (c) Using control groups (d) Demand characteristics

    Answer: (a) Double-blinding. When the experimenters who treat and assess participants do not know who is in which condition, their expectations cannot shape the results. Random assignment equalises groups and a control group gives a comparison, but the experimenter still knows who got what; demand characteristics are a source of bias, not a control.

  6. A researcher is interested in the impact of anxiety on performance. To manipulate anxiety, some subjects were given plain chips to eat while others were given barbecue flavoured chips to eat. The most obvious problem with this experiment is a lack of: (RML 2022)

    (a) Statistical Validity (b) Empirical Validity (c) Internal Validity (d) Construct Validity

    Answer: (d) Construct Validity. Chip flavour does not manipulate anxiety, so the operation does not represent the construct named. No rival variable explains the result (internal validity), and statistical or empirical validity (an old name for a test's criterion-related validity) do not name this flaw.

  7. A researcher wants to study the prevalence of test anxiety among all school students in India. Due to feasibility, they first randomly select 5 districts from each state, then randomly select 10 schools from each district, and finally randomly select 30 students from each school. This sampling technique is known as: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Stratified Random Sampling (b) Quota Sampling (c) Cluster Sampling (d) Purposive Sampling

    Answer: (c) Cluster Sampling. Intact groups (districts, then schools) are chosen at random at successive levels, which is multistage cluster sampling. Stratified sampling would draw individuals from every stratum, and quota and purposive sampling involve no random selection at all.

  8. Which of the following sampling methods relies on the researcher's judgment to select participants who are "typical" or "representative" of the population, and does NOT allow for the calculation of sampling error? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Simple Random Sampling (b) Stratified Random Sampling (c) Quota Sampling (d) Cluster Sampling

    Answer: (c) Quota Sampling. It is the only non-probability method offered, so its sampling error cannot be calculated; the other three are probability methods. Strictly, a judgement-based choice of typical cases describes purposive sampling, which would be the better answer if it were offered.

  9. A statistical measure based upon a population is known as: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Statistic (b) Parameter (c) Sample (d) None of the above

    Answer: (b) Parameter. A parameter (Greek letters such as mu and sigma) describes a population; a statistic (Roman letters) is computed from a sample, and a sample is a group of people, not a measure.

  10. In experimental design, the principle that ensures every participant has an equal chance of being assigned to any group is: (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Replication (b) Local control (c) Randomization (d) Homogeneity

    Answer: (c) Randomization. Randomisation, one of Ronald Fisher's three principles (The Design of Experiments, 1935), gives every participant an equal chance of assignment to any group. Replication and local control (blocking) are the other two principles; homogeneity is not one of them.

  11. Researchers studying meditation contacted over 10000 randomly selected adults in 10 nations. The results showed that those who meditated on a regular basis were less likely to suffer from stress than adults who meditate less regularly or not at all. Can we conclude that meditation lowers stress? Why? (RML 2021)

    (a) Yes, because the sample size was large (b) Yes, because the adults were randomly selected (c) Yes, because the adults were randomly assigned (d) Not necessarily, because this wasn't an experiment

    Answer: (d) Not necessarily, because this wasn't an experiment. The researchers only measured who already meditated; nobody was randomly assigned, so meditators may differ in other ways that lower stress. A large or randomly selected sample helps generalisation, not causal inference, and option (c) describes something the study did not do.

  12. Longitudinal and cross-sectional designs can be used to study how behaviour changes as a function of age. A disadvantage of cross-sectional studies is that the result can be influenced by : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Random assignment (b) Socio-economic factors (c) Cohort difference (d) Participant attrition

    Answer: (c) Cohort difference. A cross-sectional study compares different age groups at one time, so generational (cohort) differences can masquerade as age changes. Attrition is the problem of longitudinal studies, random assignment plays no part in age comparisons, and socio-economic differences are a general confound rather than the design's characteristic flaw.

  13. A famous study examined the smoking habits of British doctors and whether or not the doctors development various types of cancers. Participants responded to questionnaires about their smoking habits and health in 1951, 1957, 1966 and many more times all the way up to 2001. What type of observation study is this? (RML 2021)

    (a) A retrospective observational study (b) A prospective observational study (c) A sample survey (d) None of these. It's an experimental study.

    Answer: (b) A prospective observational study. This is Doll and Hill's British Doctors Study. Nobody was assigned to smoke, so it is observational rather than experimental, and the doctors were followed forward from exposure (smoking in 1951) to outcome, so it is a prospective cohort, not a retrospective one. A sample survey describes a population at one point in time.

  14. What is the main purpose of using meta-analysis in clinical psychology? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) To summarize findings from multiple studies (b) To conduct a single large experiment (c) To analyze individual case studies (d) To collect primary data

    Answer: (a) To summarize findings from multiple studies. The paper's key gives (b), which is wrong: a single experiment, however large, is primary research. Meta-analysis statistically pools the effect sizes of many existing studies; it neither collects primary data nor analyses individual case studies.

  15. In a cohort study investigating the association between a new psychological intervention and relapse rates in schizophrenia, the risk ratio is calculated as 1.0. This finding indicates that: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) The incidence of relapse in the exposed group is double that of the unexposed group. (b) The intervention has a significant protective effect against relapse. (c) There is no difference in the risk of relapse between the intervention and control groups. (d) The odds of exposure are equal in both the relapse and non-relapse groups.

    Answer: (c) There is no difference in the risk of relapse between the groups. A risk ratio divides the risk in one group by the risk in the other, so 1.0 means equal risks. Double the incidence would be 2.0 and a protective effect below 1.0; (d) is how a case-control study's odds ratio is phrased, not what a cohort's risk ratio measures.

  16. 'Can you tell me why this band is so popular among your age group?' is an example of: (RML 2020)

    (a) psychodynamic inquiry (b) a task-focused inquiry (c) an ethnographic inquiry (d) a strength-based inquiry

    Answer: (c) An ethnographic inquiry. The question treats the teenager as an informant and asks them to explain the culture of their own peer group, as in Spradley's ethnographic interview. Psychodynamic inquiry probes feelings and hidden motives, task-focused inquiry a defined task, and strength-based inquiry the person's resources.

  17. Uniting various qualitative methods with quantitative methods can be called as: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Coalition (b) Triangulation (c) Multidimensional approach (d) Co-operative inquiry

    Answer: (b) Triangulation. Combining methods so that each one's errors are offset by the others is Denzin's (1970) methodological triangulation. Coalition is not a research term, a multidimensional approach merely studies several aspects of a problem, and co-operative inquiry is Heron's participatory method.

  18. Which one of the following does not apply to qualitative research? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Recognition of the role of language and discourse (b) Emphasis on objective truth (c) Understanding of processes (d) Concern for human individuality

    Answer: (b) Emphasis on objective truth. Qualitative research rests on constructivism, which sees realities as constructed and multiple; language and discourse, processes and individuality are its hallmarks, while objective truth belongs to positivist, quantitative work.

  19. 'The ethical principle that research participants should be told enough about a piece of research to be able to make a decision about whether to participate in it.' Which research principle is this a glossary definition of? (RML 2021)

    (a) Informed consent (b) Debriefing (c) Expectation management (d) De-concealment

    Answer: (a) Informed consent. Being told enough to decide, before taking part, is informed consent. Debriefing comes after participation; expectation management and de-concealment are not research-ethics principles (revealing a deception is called dehoaxing).

  20. The principle of ethical research under which the subjects should be given full information about all aspects of the research study after it is done is known as: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Debriefing (b) Description (c) Explanation (d) Interpretation

    Answer: (a) Debriefing. Debriefing gives full information after participation, including any deception (dehoaxing) and help with distress (desensitising). Description and explanation are goals of science, and interpretation belongs to a report's Discussion.

  21. Which of the following is NOT a form of plagiarism? (RML 2023)

    (a) Collusion (b) Citation (c) Patchwriting (d) Ghost-writing

    Answer: (b) Citation. Citing openly acknowledges a source and gives credit to its authors, so it is the cure for plagiarism. Collusion (unauthorised joint work), patchwriting (lightly reworded source text) and ghost-writing (work written by someone else) are all plagiarism.

  22. Arbitrary zero is the characteristic of which scale? (RML 2024)

    (a) Ratio scale (b) Ordinal scale (c) Interval scale (d) Nominal scale

    Answer: (c) Interval scale. The source keys (a), which is wrong: an interval scale such as Celsius has equal intervals but an arbitrary zero, so ratios are meaningless, while a ratio scale is defined by a true zero. Ordinal and nominal scales have no meaningful zero or equal intervals at all.

  23. A person wants to determine the representative income of the people living in a community. He would be best advised to use the (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Mean (b) Median (c) Mode (d) Standard deviation

    Answer: (b) Median. Income is positively skewed: a few very rich households pull the mean upward, while the median, depending only on position, stays with the typical family. The mode is unstable, and the standard deviation measures spread, not central tendency.

  24. In a negatively skewed distribution, the position of the mean with reference to the median is: (RML 2022)

    (a) Left of the median (b) Right of the median (c) Same as median (d) Same as a mode

    Answer: (a) Left of the median. In negative skew the long tail points to low scores and drags the mean furthest into it, so from low to high the order is mean, median, mode. The mean lies right of the median in positive skew, and the three coincide only in a symmetrical distribution.

  25. Type II error occurs : (RML 2022)

    (a) whenever a true null hypothesis is accepted (b) whenever a false null hypothesis is rejected (c) whenever a false null hypothesis is accepted (d) whenever a true null hypothesis is rejected

    Answer: (c) whenever a false null hypothesis is accepted. A Type II error (β) misses a real effect: a false negative. Rejecting a true null (d) is a Type I error; (a) and (b) are the two correct decisions.

  26. The statistical theorem states that given a sufficiently large sample size, the sampling distribution of the mean for a variable will be approximately normally distributed, regardless of that variable's distribution in the Population. What is this definition of? (RML 2021)

    (a) The normal distribution theorem (b) The central limit theorem (c) The sampling distribution theorem (d) The law of large numbers

    Answer: (b) The central limit theorem. With N of about 30 or more, the distribution of sample means is near normal whatever the population's shape. The law of large numbers says only that a sample mean approaches the population mean as N grows; (a) and (c) are not standard theorems.

  27. What is the p-value in statistical testing? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) The probability of the null hypothesis being true (b) The probability of obtaining the observed data if the null hypothesis is true (c) The effect size of the experiment (d) The power of the study

    Answer: (b). p is the probability of data at least this extreme assuming H0 is true. Option (a) reverses the conditional; effect size and power (1 − β) are separate quantities.

  28. Which non-parametric test is equivalent to the independent samples t-test? (RML 2023)

    (a) Mann-Whitney U test (b) Kruskal-Wallis test (c) Wilcoxon signed-rank test (d) Friedman test

    Answer: (a) Mann-Whitney U test. It ranks the pooled scores of two independent groups and is the rank partner of the independent t-test. Wilcoxon signed-rank is for two related samples, Kruskal-Wallis for three or more independent groups, and Friedman for three or more related samples.

  29. Which of the following is a pooled variance estimate that constitutes the denominator of an F-ratio? (RML 2021)

    (a) The between-cells mean square (MSB). (b) The mean of the sampling distribution. (c) The sum of the deviations from the grand mean. (d) The within-cells mean square (MSw).

    Answer: (d) The within-cells mean square (MSw). F = MS between / MS within, and MS within pools the error variance inside the groups. MS between is the numerator; deviations from the grand mean always sum to zero, which is why they are squared; the mean of the sampling distribution plays no part in F.

  30. A researcher wants to calculate the degrees of freedom (df) for a 3 x 4 contingency table in a Chi-square test. The df is: (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) 12 (b) 7 (c) 6 (d) 11

    Answer: (c) 6. For a test of independence df = (rows - 1)(columns - 1) = 2 x 3 = 6. Twelve multiplies the raw dimensions, 7 adds them, and 11 is the number of cells minus 1.

  31. A researcher uses a Scree plot to determine the number of factors to retain in an EFA. The plot shows a sharp drop from eigenvalue 3.5 to 1.8, followed by a gradual plateau where eigenvalues are 0.9, 0.7, and 0.5. According to the Kaiser-Guttman rule and the Scree plot, how many factors should ideally be retained? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) 5 (b) 3 (c) 2 (d) 1

    Answer: (c) 2. Only 3.5 and 1.8 exceed the Kaiser-Guttman cut-off of 1.0, and both lie above the elbow at the third factor, so the two rules agree. Three wrongly keeps the elbow factor, five counts every eigenvalue and one keeps only the largest.

  32. When two variables that are being used as predictors in a statistical analysis have a high correlation with each other, this statistical problem is known as: (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Heteroscedasticity (b) Multicollinearity (c) Autocorrelation (d) Homogeneity of variance

    Answer: (b) Multicollinearity. Highly intercorrelated predictors make regression weights unstable and inflate their standard errors (flagged by a VIF above 10). Heteroscedasticity is unequal residual spread, autocorrelation is residuals correlated over time, and homogeneity of variance is equal variance across groups.

  33. Which of the following correlation methods is used when one variable is continuous and the other is dichotomous? (RML 2023)

    (a) Pearson correlation coefficient (b) Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (c) Point-biserial correlation coefficient (d) Biserial correlation coefficient

    Answer: (c) Point-biserial correlation coefficient. A continuous variable paired with a true (natural) dichotomy calls for point-biserial. Biserial is for an artificial dichotomy cut from a continuous variable, Pearson for two continuous variables and Spearman for ranks.
