Psychological Assessment & Testing

A psychological test is a measuring instrument, and like any instrument it must be (a) consistent and (b) accurate. Consistent = reliability. Accurate = validity. Everything in this chapter is either a way to estimate reliability, a way to estimate validity, a way to interpret scores against norms, or a real-world test built on those foundations. We measure things we cannot see directly (anxiety, intelligence, personality) by building instruments out of items, like a thermometer made of questions, then deciding things with them: special education, army fitness, diagnosis. This chapter locks the psychometric theory first, then the major intelligence, personality, projective, and clinical instruments, with every author, year, item count, scale, score range, and exam trap in one place.

1. Item analysis & test construction

Each test item is a single sensor reading. If a sensor is too easy (everyone passes) it tells us nothing; too hard (no one passes) it tells us nothing; and if it disagrees with the rest of the test, something is wrong with it. That is why every well-built test goes through item analysis.

Difficulty index, p

The difficulty index is the proportion who got an item right.

  • p = (number correct) / (total attempting), range 0 to 1.
  • p = 0.50 is the sweet spot for a free-response item because it discriminates the most.
  • For a 4-option MCQ the optimal p is ~0.625, halfway between chance guessing (0.25) and ceiling (1.00).
  • p above 0.80 = too easy; p below 0.20 = too hard. Both are revision candidates because they cannot separate strong from weak students.

Discrimination index, D

Do high-scorers on the whole test do better on this item than low-scorers?

  • D = p_upper − p_lower, where p_upper = proportion correct in the top 27% of scorers and p_lower = proportion in the bottom 27%. Range −1 to +1.
  • D above 0.40 = item agrees with the test, a good signal.
  • D near zero = random noise; both groups perform alike.
  • Negative D = the bottom group out-performed the top group: the item is mis-keyed, ambiguous, or has a trick strong test-takers over-think.
Trap

Read negative discrimination as a mis-keyed / flawed item to discard immediately, NOT as a merely “hard” item. This is asked every cycle.

2. Reliability (the consistency story)

Reliability is not about whether a test is correct; it is about whether it is consistent. A bathroom scale that reads 75 kg every time is reliable even if your true weight is 70 (that is a validity problem). A scale bouncing between 60, 90, 80 is unreliable, full stop. If a test is unreliable you cannot trust any single score: the same person retested gives a different answer for reasons unrelated to the trait. You are measuring noise.

Definition

Classical Test Theory: Observed Score = True Score + Error. Reliability is the proportion of observed-score variance that is true variance, not error. A reliability of 0.80 says 80% signal, 20% noise.

There are several reliability methods because there are several ways to think about “consistency”:

  • Across time: test the same person twice (test-retest).
  • Across forms: give two equivalent versions (parallel/alternate forms).
  • Within one administration, across items: check items hang together (split-half, Cronbach alpha, KR-20).
  • Across raters: check two judges agree (Cohen kappa for categorical, ICC for continuous).

Test-retest (time stability)

Give the test, give it again later, correlate the two scores. High correlation = stable over time. The standard window is two to four weeks. Too long and the trait itself can change (correlation drops, but the test is not at fault); too short and memory carryover inflates the correlation.

Split-half + Spearman-Brown

Give the test once, split into two halves (often odd vs even items), correlate the halves. By splitting you have halved the test length, and shorter tests are less reliable, so the raw split-half correlation underestimates full-test reliability. Spearman-Brown corrects this:

  • r_full = 2 × r_half / (1 + r_half).
  • Example: r_half = 0.60 → r_full = 1.20 / 1.60 = 0.75. Always apply Spearman-Brown after any split.

Cronbach alpha

Imagine doing every possible split-half and averaging the result: that mean is Cronbach alpha. It is the most common internal-consistency measure for tests with multi-point (e.g. Likert) items. Alpha rises with item count, item homogeneity, and trait variance.

Trap

Alpha is NOT a measure of unidimensionality. A test can have high alpha and still measure two distinct constructs. The MET loves this.

KR-20 (Kuder-Richardson 20)

When items are right-or-wrong (dichotomous, 1/0), Cronbach alpha simplifies to KR-20: same idea, adapted for 0/1 items. So “30-item right-wrong vocabulary test, one administration, which reliability?” → KR-20.

Inter-rater: kappa and ICC

When humans do the scoring (Rorschach protocols, clinical interviews, essay grades), reliability is about rater agreement.

  • Cohen kappa for categorical decisions (OCD: yes/no), corrects for chance agreement. Kappa ≥ 0.81 = almost-perfect; 0.61–0.80 = substantial.
  • Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous ratings (a 0–100 severity score); different ICC forms exist depending on whether raters are random or fixed.
Trap

Kappa is NOT an internal-consistency measure. If a question describes a one-administration internal-structure test, kappa is the wrong answer.

Standard Error of Measurement (SEM)

SEM is the practical payoff of reliability: the band of error around any single observed score.

  • SEM = SD × √(1 − r_xx), where SD is the test SD and r_xx its reliability.
  • Example: SD = 20, reliability 0.81 → SEM = 20 × √0.19 = 20 × 0.436 = 8.72. A score of 100 has a 68% confidence band of roughly 91–109 (±1 SEM).
  • Higher reliability shrinks SEM. SEM is what lets you say two scores differ meaningfully versus fall within the noise band.

3. Validity (the accuracy story)

A test can be perfectly reliable yet measure the wrong thing. Validity asks whether the test measures the construct it claims to. The classical model has three broad evidence types; modern theorists (especially Messick) fold them all into “construct validity,” but the MET still uses the classical breakdown, so know both.

Content validity

Does the test cover the domain it claims to? An anxiety scale that asks only about racing heart but ignores worry, restlessness, and sleep is content-deficient. Established by expert panels who rate item-domain coverage; Lawshe’s CVR is the technical formula, but expert judgment is the core.

Trap

Do not confuse with face validity = whether the test merely looks valid to a layperson taking it. Face validity is the weakest form. A test can have high face validity and poor content validity, or low face validity and rigorous content validity.

Criterion validity

Does the test correlate with a real-world outcome (the criterion)? Two flavors:

  • Concurrent: criterion measured at the same time. E.g. a new depression scale vs an established gold standard (HAM-D) today. Used when a gold standard already exists.
  • Predictive: criterion measured later. E.g. GRE today predicts graduate GPA two years on. Used to forecast future behaviour.
Mnemonic

Predictive = Postponed. Concurrent = Current.

Construct validity

The most abstract form. If the test truly measures Construct X, it should correlate strongly with other measures of X (convergent), weakly with unrelated constructs (discriminant), and behave as theory predicts study after study (nomological network). The classic technique is Campbell & Fiske’s 1959 multitrait-multimethod matrix (MTMM), which tests convergent and discriminant validity simultaneously by measuring multiple traits with multiple methods.

Validity ceiling

High-yield

Validity cannot exceed the square root of reliability. Max validity coefficient between Test A and B = √(r_xx_A × r_xx_B). If A is 0.64 reliable and B is 0.81 reliable, max correlation = √(0.64 × 0.81) = 0.72. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity; unreliable tests cannot be strongly valid.

4. Norms & standard scores

A raw score means nothing alone: “scored 47 on a vocabulary test” tells you nothing until you know what others score. Norms convert raw scores into a frame of reference. The two most useful conversions are percentile ranks and standard scores.

Percentile ranks

The percentage of the norm group scoring at or below the person’s score. A 75th percentile means 75% scored at or below this person. Easy to interpret but ordinal: the gap between the 50th and 60th percentile is far smaller than between the 90th and 99th.

Standard scores

These put raw scores on an interval scale with fixed mean and SD: Standard Score = M + (SD × z), where z is the person’s z-score from the norm group. Memorize these systems cold:

5. Intelligence theories & IQ mechanics

The IQ formula history (memorize the sequence)

  1. Mental Age (MA) introduced by Binet, 1908 revision: the average chronological age that performs at the child’s level.
  2. Ratio IQ = (MA / CA) × 100: William Stern, 1912; applied by Terman in the Stanford-Binet 1916.
  3. Deviation IQ: Wechsler, 1939 (Wechsler-Bellevue I), comparing the examinee to age-mates via a standardized score with fixed mean & SD. Solved the problem that ratio IQ breaks down past 16 because MA stops climbing linearly.
  4. Stanford-Binet switches to deviation IQ in 1960 (Pinneau’s tables), SD = 16.
  5. SB-5 (2003) drops SD to 15 to match Wechsler.
Trap

Do not credit Binet with the IQ formula: Binet rejected a single global score and wanted a clinical descriptive profile. Stern wrote the ratio formula (1912); Terman put it into the Stanford-Binet (1916).

6. Stanford-Binet family

Origin: Binet-Simon (1905, France)

  • Authors: Alfred Binet & Théodore Simon, commissioned by the French Ministry of Public Instruction to identify children needing special schooling.
  • Year: 1905 (original 30-item graded-difficulty scale, “follow a light” up to “abstract definitions”), revised 1908 (introduced mental age) and 1911.
  • Did NOT yield IQ. Binet wanted a clinical descriptive profile, not a single number.

Stanford revisions (the IQ-formula era)

  • 1916 — Stanford-Binet 1st edition. Lewis M. Terman, Stanford. First English adaptation; ~90 items. Adopted Stern’s ratio IQ = (MA/CA) × 100, mean ≈ 100. Age 2–adult.
  • 1937 — SB-LM (2nd ed.). Terman & Maud Merrill. Two equivalent forms L and M. Still ratio IQ.
  • 1960 — SB-LM revised (3rd ed.). Terman & Merrill. Switched to deviation IQ (Pinneau tables), M = 100, SD = 16.
  • 1986 — SB-IV (4th ed.). Thorndike, Hagen & Sattler. Hierarchical structure, 4 area scores (Verbal Reasoning, Abstract/Visual Reasoning, Quantitative Reasoning, Short-Term Memory), 15 subtests. Age 2–adult.
  • 2003 — SB-5 (current). Gale H. Roid, Riverside. SD changed to 15 to align with Wechsler. Age range 2–85+.

SB-5 structure (the version exam-writers mean today)

  • 10 subtests, 5 factors × 2 domains.
  • 5 factors: Fluid Reasoning (FR), Knowledge (KN), Quantitative Reasoning (QR), Visual-Spatial Processing (VS), Working Memory (WM) — same names across both domains.
  • 2 domains: Verbal (5 subtests) and Nonverbal (5 subtests); each factor has one verbal + one nonverbal subtest.
  • 3 IQ scores: Full-Scale IQ (FSIQ), Verbal IQ (VIQ), Nonverbal IQ (NVIQ). All M = 100, SD = 15.
  • Routing subtests: Object Series/Matrices (nonverbal) and Vocabulary (verbal) set entry difficulty.
  • Admin time: ~45–75 min full; 15–20 min abbreviated.

7. Wechsler family

David Wechsler (Bellevue Hospital, NY) built his scales to fix two SB problems: it was age-stratified and clunky for adults, and ratio IQ broke down past 16. His fixes were the deviation IQ (1939) and a point-scale structure where every adult takes the same subtests. Three age bands:

  • WPPSI (Preschool & Primary), ages 2:6–7:7, current WPPSI-IV. Lineage: 1967 → WPPSI-R 1989 → WPPSI-III 2002 → WPPSI-IV 2012.
  • WISC (Children), ages 6:0–16:11, current WISC-V. Lineage: 1949 → WISC-R 1974 → WISC-III 1991 → WISC-IV 2003 → WISC-V 2014 US (2016 UK, 2019 India).
  • WAIS (Adult), ages 16:0–90:11, current WAIS-IV. Lineage: Wechsler-Bellevue I 1939 (11 subtests, first deviation IQ) → W-B II 1946 → WAIS 1955 → WAIS-R 1981 → WAIS-III 1997 (added Processing Speed, 4-index structure) → WAIS-IV 2008 → WAIS-5 2024 (rolling out; exams still default to WAIS-IV).
High-yield

All Wechsler FSIQ & index scores: M = 100, SD = 15. All subtest scaled scores: M = 10, SD = 3, range 1–19.

WAIS-IV (the canonical adult answer)

  • Age range: 16:0–90:11. Total subtests: 15 (10 core + 5 supplemental). FSIQ derived from the 10 core subtests. Admin ~60–90 min.
  • 4 index scores: Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Perceptual Reasoning (PRI), Working Memory (WMI), Processing Speed (PSI).

  • Age range: 2:6–7:7, split into two batteries.

  • Ages 4:0–7:7: 15 subtests, 5 primary indices (VCI, VSI, FRI, WMI, PSI) — same structure as WISC-V. Primary subtests: Information, Similarities, Block Design, Object Assembly, Matrix Reasoning, Picture Concepts, Picture Memory, Zoo Locations, Bug Search, Cancellation.

8. Raven’s Progressive Matrices

  • Author: John C. Raven. Years: SPM published 1938 (developed 1936), CPM 1947, APM 1943.
  • Nature: nonverbal, multiple-choice, pattern-completion; targets Spearman’s g via eductive (meaning-extracting) reasoning.
  • “Culture-fair”: commonly so described because items are abstract shapes, but cultural loading persists (paper-pencil familiarity, formal schooling). “Culture-reduced” is the more honest phrasing. Used widely in cross-cultural research and India.

9. Kaufman, Cattell CFIT & special-purpose scales

Kaufman family

  • Authors: Alan S. & Nadeen L. Kaufman.
  • K-ABC (1983) → KABC-II (2004), ages 3:0–18:11. Built on Luria’s neuropsychological model (simultaneous + sequential processing); KABC-II added CHC interpretation. Yields two global scores: Mental Processing Index (MPI) for Luria-style use and Fluid-Crystallized Index (FCI) for CHC-style use. 5 scales: Sequential, Simultaneous, Planning, Learning, Knowledge. Designed to reduce verbal/cultural loading and assess minority/ESL children fairly.
  • KAIT (1993): ages 11–85, 10 subtests, separates Fluid vs Crystallized IQ explicitly.
  • KBIT-2 (2004): 2 subtests (Verbal Knowledge + Matrices), ~20 min screen.
Trap

The original K-ABC (1983) was pure Luria; KABC-II (2004) yields two indices (MPI + FCI) because it supports both Luria and CHC.

Cattell’s Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT)

  • Author: Raymond B. Cattell. Year: first 1949, revised 1973.
  • Purpose: measure fluid intelligence (Gf) per Gf-Gc theory with reduced cultural/language loading.
  • 3 scales: Scale 1 (ages 4–8 and ID adults, 8 subtests), Scale 2 (ages 8–13 and average adults, 4 subtests), Scale 3 (ages 14+ and above-average adults, 4 subtests).
  • Scales 2 & 3 subtests (same 4): Series, Classifications, Matrices, Conditions (Topology). Forms A + B for retest. Original M = 100, SD = 24; renormed editions use SD 15 or 16.

Special-purpose & nonverbal scales

  • Leiter International Performance Scale — Russell G. Leiter, 1936 → Leiter-R 1997 → Leiter-3 2013. Fully nonverbal, no language; for deaf, ASD, non-English speakers. Ages 3–75+.
  • Goodenough Draw-A-Man (Florence Goodenough, 1926) → Goodenough-Harris Drawing Test (Harris, 1963). Children draw a man (and woman + self in Harris); scored on 64–73 body-detail criteria. Ages 3–15. Drawing-based IQ estimate.
  • Slosson Intelligence Test (SIT, 1963; SIT-R3, 2002) — brief individual screen.
  • Differential Ability Scales (DAS-II, Elliott, 2007) — ages 2:6–17:11, CHC-aligned, popular for LD identification.
  • Woodcock-Johnson Cognitive (WJ-IV, 2014) — explicitly CHC; ages 2–90+; GIA + 9 CHC broad abilities.
  • CTONI-2 (Hammill et al., 2009), UNIT-2 (Bracken & McCallum, 2016), WRIT (Glutting et al., 2000), RIAS-2 (Reynolds & Kamphaus, 2015).

10. Group & infant/developmental scales

Army tests (WWI)

  • Army Alpha (1917): chaired by Robert M. Yerkes (APA president) with Otis, Terman, Thorndike, Goddard, Bingham, Whipple. 212 items across 8 subtests (Oral Directions, Arithmetic Problems, Practical Judgment, Synonym-Antonym, Disarranged Sentences, Number Series Completion, Analogies, Information). For literate, English-speaking recruits.
  • Army Beta (1917): same Yerkes committee. 7 subtests (Maze, Cube Analysis, X-O Series, Digit Symbol, Number Checking, Pictorial Completion, Geometrical Construction). For illiterate / non-English recruits; administered via pantomime.
Trap

Army Alpha = 8 subtests, Army Beta = 7, Bhatia = 5. Yerkes was the chair (not Terman, who was a member but is tied to Stanford-Binet 1916).

Other group tests

  • Otis Self-Administering Tests of Mental Ability (Arthur S. Otis, 1922) — civilian descendant of Army Alpha. → OLSAT (current K–12 screen).
  • Wonderlic Personnel Test (1937) — 50 items, 12 minutes, employment screen (famous for NFL combine use).
  • MAB-II (Jackson, 1998) — group-administrable Wechsler-style. Plus CTMM, Henmon-Nelson, CogAT.

Infant & developmental scales

  • Bayley Scales of Infant Development (BSID) — Nancy Bayley: BSID-I 1969, BSID-II 1993, Bayley-III 2006, Bayley-4 2019. Ages 1–42 months. Bayley-III has 5 scales: Cognitive, Language (Receptive + Expressive), Motor (Fine + Gross), Social-Emotional, Adaptive Behavior.
  • Gesell Developmental Schedules — Arnold Gesell, 1940s. Ages 4 weeks–6 years. 5 fields: Adaptive, Gross Motor, Fine Motor, Language, Personal-Social. Yields Developmental Quotient (DQ) = (DA / CA) × 100.
  • Cattell Infant Intelligence Scale (Psyche Cattell, 1940) — downward extension of Stanford-Binet, ages 2–30 months.
  • Brunet-Lézine (1951) French infant scale; Mullen Scales of Early Learning (1995) birth–68 months; Denver Developmental Screening Test (DDST 1967, Denver-II 1992) screening, ages 0–6.
Trap

Bayley-III = 1–42 months (not just infants). Gesell to 6 years. Cattell Infant Scale 2–30 months. Stanford-Binet starts at 2.

11. Indian intelligence tests

High-yield

High-yield for both MET and NIMHANS. Memorize the author-year-count quadruple for each.

Bhatia’s Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence

  • Author: Chandra Mohan Bhatia. Year: 1955. Age: originally 11–16, extended to early adulthood.
  • 5 subtests: (1) Koh’s Block Design (10 designs), (2) Alexander’s Pass-Along (8 problems), (3) Pattern Drawing (8 designs), (4) Immediate Memory (digits forward + backward + sentence repetition), (5) Picture Construction (5 pictures).
  • Use: built for the Indian context; performance (nonverbal) orientation suits illiterate and rural populations.
Trap

Bhatia having 5 subtests is a chronic MCQ trap. The Pass-Along is Alexander’s (1932), incorporated into Bhatia’s battery — do not say “Bhatia Pass-Along.” Bhatia is 1955, not 1949 (1949 = WISC and CFIT; 1955 = WAIS and Bhatia).

Other Indian instruments

  • Binet-Kamat Test (BKT) — V.V. Kamat, 1934 (Marathi; later Kannada). Ages 3–22, ~80 age-graded tasks. Still used in NIMHANS clinical IQ estimation for Indian children.
  • Malin’s Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC) — Arthur J. Malin, 1969. Ages 6–15. Indian adaptation of WISC (the 1949 original, not WISC-R). 11 subtests.
  • Seguin Form Board — Edouard Seguin (1907 origin), used in India ages 3–7. Nonverbal performance, 10 cut-outs; measures speed of visual-motor coordination. Often misattributed to Indian authors.
  • Mohsin General Intelligence Test (S.M. Mohsin, 1959) — verbal group test, Hindi. Jalota’s Group Test of General Mental Ability (S. Jalota, 1972) — verbal, ages 13+, 100 items.
  • Developmental Screening Test (DST) — Bharath Raj, 1977, ages 0–15. Vineland Social Maturity Scale, Indian adaptation — A.J. Malin, 1965 (Indian Social Quotient).

13. Objective (self-report) personality tests

Definition

Objective tests use structured items (true/false, Likert, forced-choice) with fixed scoring keys. MCQ-writers attack this area four ways: (1) swap the item count between two tests, (2) swap author/year, (3) ask which scale belongs to which test, (4) ask projective vs objective. Lock the quadruple test → items → scales → author/year cold.

MMPI family (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory)

  • MMPI (original, 1943): Starke R. Hathaway (psychologist) & J. Charnley McKinley (psychiatrist), Univ. of Minnesota. 566 true/false items (550 unique + 16 repeats). Method = empirical criterion-keying: items retained only if they discriminated a diagnostic group from normals, not by face validity — the breakthrough versus older content-based inventories. Norms = “Minnesota normals” (724 hospital visitors), later criticised as a narrow white rural Midwest base, driving the MMPI-2 restandardisation. Clinical cutoff originally T ≥ 70.
  • MMPI-2 (1989 restandardisation; the default version exam-writers mean): 567 true/false items. Age 18+, 6th-grade reading level, 60–90 min. Scored as T-scores (M 50, SD 10); clinically significant elevation T ≥ 65.
  • MMPI-2-RF (2008, Ben-Porath & Tellegen): 338 items; 9 Restructured Clinical (RC) scales + demoralization (RCd) + higher-order scales; common variance of demoralization pulled out so each RC scale is more specific.
  • MMPI-3 (2020, Ben-Porath & Tellegen): 335 items, new norms, 52 scales (H-O, RC, Specific Problem, PSY-5).
  • MMPI-A (adolescent, 1992, Butcher et al.): 478 items, ages 14–18; MMPI-A-RF (2016, 241 items).

Validity scales (the L/F/K triad is the high-yield bit):

  • ? (Cannot Say): count of unanswered items; > 30 omissions invalidates the profile.
  • L (Lie): 15 items detecting naive “faking good” (impossibly virtuous claims). High L = unsophisticated impression management.
  • F (Infrequency): 60 items rarely endorsed by normals. High F = random responding, malingering, OR genuine severe pathology (cannot distinguish without more evidence).
  • K (Correction): 30 items measuring subtle defensiveness; added as a correction to 5 clinical scales (1, 4, 7, 8, 9).
  • VRIN (variable inconsistency → random responding), TRIN (all-true/all-false bias), FB (back F — fatigue on later items), Fp (F-psychopathology — separates malingering from genuine pathology among inpatients).

Interpretation: all scales plotted on one graph in T-units; clinicians read code types (e.g. 2-7/7-2 = anxious-depressed; 4-9 = antisocial acting-out). Single-scale interpretation is discouraged.

Trap

MMPI-2 = 567 items, 10 clinical scales (Hs D Hy Pd Mf Pa Pt Sc Ma Si), L/F/K validity, T-scores M50/SD10, cutoff T ≥ 65. Don’t confuse with 16PF’s 16 factors or its Sten scores. L = naive faking-good, F = exaggeration/random/severe, K = subtle defensiveness — don’t swap.

16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire)

  • Author: Raymond B. Cattell (with Tatsuoka & Eber later). Year: 1st ed. 1949; current 5th Edition 1993.
  • Items: 185 (5th ed.); each 3-choice (agree / ? / disagree), except the reasoning subtest (forced-choice). Age 16+, 35–50 min.
  • Construction: factor analysis of the lexicon — Cattell took Allport & Odbert’s ~4500 trait words, reduced to ~160 surface traits, factor-analysed to 16 source traits.
  • Scoring: raw → Sten scores (M 5.5, SD 2, range 1–10): 1–3 low pole, 4–7 average, 8–10 high pole.
  • 5 global factors (second-order, close to Big Five): Extraversion, Anxiety, Tough-Mindedness, Independence, Self-Control. Validity indices: Impression Management (IM), Acquiescence (ACQ), Infrequency (INF).
  • Use: normal-population assessment for selection (I/O), career, couples/family counselling. Not a clinical screen.

NEO Personality Inventory (Five-Factor Model)

  • Authors: Paul T. Costa Jr. & Robert R. McCrae (NIH/National Institute on Aging). Lineage: NEO-PI 1985 → NEO-PI-R 1992 → NEO-PI-3 2010.
  • Items: 240 (NEO-PI-R / NEO-PI-3), 5-point Likert, 8 items × 30 facets. Short form NEO-FFI / NEO-FFI-3 = 60 items (5 domains only, no facets).
  • Age: 17+ (NEO-PI-3 extended to 12). Forms S (self) and R (observer). Scoring: raw → T-scores; no classic validity scales (assumes honest responding).
  • Dominant trait model in academic psychology since the 1990s. Use: research, occupational selection, therapy planning.

EPQ / EPQ-R (Eysenck Personality Questionnaire)

  • Authors: Hans J. & Sybil B.G. Eysenck. Lineage: Maudsley Medical Q (1952) → Maudsley Personality Inventory (MPI, 1959) → Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI, 1964) → EPQ (1975) → EPQ-R (1985) → EPQ-R Short (1992).
  • Items: EPQ 90; EPQ-R 100; Short 48. Yes/No. Age adult; Junior EPQ ages 7–15.
  • PEN dimensions: P — Psychoticism (tough-mindedness, aggression, low empathy; added 1975, the EPI→EPQ move), E — Extraversion (sociability, impulsivity), N — Neuroticism (emotional reactivity), plus L — Lie (social desirability).
  • Biological anchor: Extraversion ↔ cortical arousal (introverts higher baseline), Neuroticism ↔ limbic/ANS reactivity, Psychoticism ↔ dopaminergic function. Eysenck = biology of personality (common MCQ pairing).

MBTI (Myers-Briggs Type Indicator)

  • Authors: Katharine Cook Briggs & daughter Isabel Briggs Myers, built on Carl Jung’s Psychological Types (1921). Year: first 1944, commercial 1962 (ETS); current Form M (1998).
  • Items: Form M = 93 forced-choice; Form Q = 144 (adds 20 facets); Form G (older) 126. Age 14+.
  • 4 dichotomies → 16 types: E/I (energy direction), S/N (information style), T/F (decision style), J/P (lifestyle/outer-world orientation), e.g. INTJ.
  • Critical: scores are categorical (discrete types), NOT dimensional — the main psychometric criticism. Traits are normally distributed; dichotomisation throws away information; ~50% of takers get a different type on retest within weeks.
  • Use: career counselling, team building, coaching. NOT for clinical diagnosis or selection. MET contrast: “Which test claims discrete types instead of continuous traits?” → MBTI.

CPI (California Psychological Inventory)

  • Author: Harrison Gough. Year: 1957 → CPI-434 (1996) → CPI-260 short. Items: 434 / 260, true-false.
  • Scales: 20 “folk-concept” scales (Dominance, Sociability, Empathy, Independence…) under 4 themes (Interpersonal, Self-management, Achievement, Personal Style) + 3 vector scales.
  • Use: explicitly normal populations — the non-clinical complement to MMPI (selection, counselling, leadership).
Trap

CPI = normal populations (Gough’s “folk concepts”); MMPI = clinical populations. Easy to swap.

14. Projective tests

Definition

Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli on the hypothesis that the subject projects unconscious needs, conflicts, and defenses onto them. Less structured scoring, weaker psychometrics, richer clinical material than objective tests.

Rorschach Inkblot Test

  • Author: Hermann Rorschach (Swiss psychiatrist). Year: Psychodiagnostik, 1921 (died 1922 at 37, before refining the system).
  • Stimuli: 10 inkblot cards. 5 achromatic (black/grey: I, IV, V, VI, VII), 2 black + red (II, III), 3 fully chromatic (VIII, IX, X). Shorthand: “5 chromatic + 5 achromatic.”
  • Administration: (1) Free Association / Response phase (“What might this be?”); (2) Inquiry phase (where on the blot, and what features — form, colour, shading, movement — made it look that way).

Exner Comprehensive System (1974), dominant until 2011:

  • Location: W (whole), D (common detail), Dd (unusual detail), S (white space).
  • Determinants: F (form), M (human movement), FM (animal movement), m (inanimate movement); C (pure colour), FC (form-dominated colour), CF (colour-dominated form); C′ (achromatic colour), T (texture), V (vista), Y (diffuse shading); FD (form-dimension), (2) (pair), r (reflection).
  • Content: H, Hd, A, Ad, An, Bl, Sx, Xy… Popularity: P (common response, e.g. bat/butterfly on card V). Form quality: +, o, u, −.
  • Indices: Erlebnistypus (EB) = M : sum-C (Introversive vs Extratensive vs Ambitent); Lambda (L) = pure-form ratio (high = constricted/defensive); Egocentricity Index = (reflections + pairs)/R; SCZI, DEPI, CDI, S-CON.

R-PAS (2011): Meyer, Viglione, Mihura, Erard & Erdberg. Updates Exner norms, drops weak indices, standardises response count (R-Optimised). Increasingly the research standard, but MET still tests Exner.

Trap

Rorschach = 10 inkblots (not 12, 15, or 20). Determinant trick: FC = form-dominated colour vs CF = colour-dominated.

TAT (Thematic Apperception Test)

  • Authors: Henry A. Murray & Christiana D. Morgan, Harvard Psychological Clinic. Year: 1935 (manual 1943).
  • Stimuli: 31 picture cards total — 30 black-and-white ambiguous scenes + 1 blank. 20 cards administered per subject, selected by age (adult/child) and sex (M/F); cards coded e.g. 3BM = card 3 for Boys + Men.
  • Procedure: subject makes up a story for each picture — what led up, what is happening, what characters think/feel, how it ends. Recorded verbatim.
  • Theory: projective hypothesis — subject projects dominant needs (achievement, affiliation, power) and perceived environmental press. Built on Murray’s needs-press theory (Explorations in Personality, 1938).
  • Scoring systems: Murray’s original (code needs + press); SCORS (Westen, 1985, 8 object-relations dimensions); Defense Mechanism Manual (Cramer); McClelland’s nAch scoring.
  • Indian adaptation: Indian TAT by Uma Choudhury (1960), culture-adapted scenes.
Trap

TAT = 31 cards total, 20 administered (not 10, not 20 total). Theory = needs-press. Indian TAT = Uma Choudhury 1960.

15. Adjustment, clinical & Indian inventories

Beck inventories (all by Aaron T. Beck, Univ. of Pennsylvania; share the 0–3 Likert skeleton)

  • BDI / BDI-II: BDI 1961 (Beck, Ward, Mendelson, Mock, Erbaugh); BDI-II 1996, 21 items, 0–3 each, total 0–63, past 2 weeks. Revised to match DSM-IV (added agitation, worthlessness, concentration; removed body image, work difficulty, weight loss, somatic preoccupation). Cutoffs: 0–13 minimal, 14–19 mild, 20–28 moderate, 29–63 severe. 5th–6th-grade reading. Screening/severity, not diagnostic alone.
  • BAI (Beck Anxiety Inventory, 1988): 21 items, 0–3, total 0–63, past week. Cutoffs: 0–7 minimal, 8–15 mild, 16–25 moderate, 26–63 severe. Loads on somatic anxiety (sweating, dizziness, racing heart) to minimise overlap with depression.
  • BHS (Beck Hopelessness Scale, 1974): 20 true/false items. Measures pessimism about the future; more predictive of eventual suicide than depression severity alone. Cutoff ≥ 9 = elevated risk.
  • BSS / BSI (Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation, 1979 clinician / 1991 self-report): 21 items, 0–2 each. First 5 screen desire to live/die and active/passive ideation; if positive, complete remaining 14.
  • BYI (Beck Youth Inventories, 2001/2005): ages 7–18; 5 scales × 20 items = 100 (Depression, Anxiety, Anger, Disruptive Behavior, Self-Concept). Also CBOCI (Clark-Beck OCD Inventory, 25 items) and Beck Self-Concept Test.
Trap

BDI-II = 21 items, 0–3, 0–63, past 2 weeks. BAI = 21 items, past week. BHS = 20 T/F. BSS = 21 items, 0–2. BHS (hopelessness) predicts suicide better than BDI severity.

Bell’s Adjustment Inventory

  • Author: Hugh M. Bell. Student form 1934, Adult form 1938; Indian adaptations by R.K. Ojha (Hindi) and others.
  • Items: Student form 140; Adult form 160. Yes/No/? format.
  • Areas: Home, Health, Social, Emotional (Student form = 4); Adult form adds Occupational (5). Higher score = poorer adjustment; brackets labelled excellent / good / average / unsatisfactory / very unsatisfactory.
  • Use: screening in school/college counselling, common in Indian masters dissertations (why it persists on MET).

16. Aptitude, attitude & interest scales

Aptitude batteries

  • DAT (Differential Aptitude Test): 8 subtests (Verbal, Numerical, Abstract, Mechanical, Space, Spelling, Language, Perceptual). Bennett, Seashore, Wesman.
  • GATB (General Aptitude Test Battery): nine aptitudes, US Employment Service.

Attitude scales

  • Thurstone (1928): equal-appearing intervals; judges scale items 1–11.
  • Likert (1932): summated rating, typically 5-point.
  • Guttman (1944): cumulative — endorsing a stronger item implies endorsing weaker ones.
  • Bogardus: social distance. Osgood semantic differential: bipolar adjectives, 3 factors (evaluation, potency, activity).

Interest inventories

  • Strong Interest Inventory: Holland themes (RIASEC) + occupational scales.
  • Kuder Career Search: forced-choice triads.
  • Holland Self-Directed Search: RIASEC = Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional.

18. Educational measurement basics

  • Achievement = what you have learned, past (WIAT, school finals). Aptitude = what you can learn, future (DAT, SAT). Intelligence = general cognitive capacity (WAIS, SB).
  • Norm-referenced: compare student to a peer group (ranking, selection). Criterion-referenced: compare to an absolute standard (mastery, licensing).
  • Formative: during learning, low-stakes feedback. Summative: end of unit, high-stakes outcome.

High-yield

The trap list to memorize cold:

  1. Validity ceiling = √reliability. Unreliable tests cannot be strongly valid.
  2. Negative discrimination index = mis-keyed item; discard.
  3. Cronbach alpha ≠ unidimensionality.
  4. Wechsler IQ SD = 15; old Stanford-Binet SD = 16; SB-5 (2003+) SD = 15.
  5. Stanine SD = 2, range 1–9. Sten (16PF) M 5.5, SD 2, range 1–10. T-score = 50/10.
  6. Predictive = future criterion; Concurrent = same-time.
  7. Face validity is weakest; content validity = expert judgment of domain coverage.
  8. Kappa is inter-rater, NOT internal consistency. KR-20 is for dichotomous items.
  9. MMPI-2 = 567 items, 10 clinical scales, L/F/K validity, T-scores, cutoff T ≥ 65.
  10. 16PF = 185 items, 16 factors + 5 globals, Sten, Cattell. NEO = 240 items, OCEAN, 30 facets, Costa & McCrae. NEO-FFI = 60.
  11. EPQ-R = 100 items, PEN + Lie; Psychoticism added 1975. MBTI = 16 types, 4 dichotomies, forced-choice, poor retest reliability.
  12. CPI = normal populations; MMPI = clinical.
  13. Rorschach = 10 inkblots (Exner → R-PAS 2011). TAT = 31 total, 20 admin, needs-press. CAT = Bellak 1949, 10 cards. HIT = 45 cards.
  14. Bell’s: Student 140 / 4 areas; Adult 160 / adds Occupational.
  15. Beck: BDI-II 21 (0–3, 0–63, 2 weeks); BAI 21 (1 week); BHS 20 T/F (best suicide predictor); BSS 21 (0–2).
  16. Indian: TAT = Uma Choudhury 1960; WISC adaptation = MISIC (Malin 1969); Bhatia Battery = C.M. Bhatia 1955 (5 subtests); Binet-Kamat = Kamat 1934.
  17. SPM = 60, CPM = 36, APM scored = 36. Bhatia = 5, Army Beta = 7, Army Alpha = 8 subtests.
  18. WAIS-IV = 4 indices; WISC-V = 5 (old PRI split into VSI + FRI). Ratio IQ = Stern 1912, applied by Terman 1916 (not Binet).
  19. Gardner = 8 intelligences (Naturalist added 1995). Cattell appears thrice: 16PF, CFIT, Gf-Gc (with Horn).
  20. Y-BOCS = OCD; PANSS = schizophrenia; MMSE = 30 points, cutoff 23–24; MoCA more sensitive.
