In the early hours of 13 March 1964, Catherine "Kitty" Genovese, a 28-year-old bar manager, parked her car near her flat in Kew Gardens, a quiet neighbourhood of Queens, New York, and started the short walk home. A stranger, Winston Moseley, chased her and stabbed her. A neighbour shouted from a window and Moseley fled, but about ten minutes later he came back, found her lying wounded in a hallway at the back of her building, and attacked her again. She died on the way to hospital.

Two weeks later The New York Times put the killing on its front page. For more than half an hour, it reported, 38 respectable, law-abiding citizens had watched a killer stalk and stab a woman, and not one had telephoned the police during the attack. The story went round the world as proof of big-city apathy. Two young psychologists in New York, John Darley and Bibb Latane, suspected another explanation: perhaps each witness, knowing that others could also hear, assumed that someone else would act. They staged an emergency in the laboratory, reported in 1968, in which students talking over an intercom heard another student apparently have a seizure. The more listeners a student believed were present, the less likely that student was to go for help.

The finding became one of the most famous in psychology, yet the newspaper story behind it was badly wrong. Later investigations, among them a 2007 review by the psychologists Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins, found no 38 watching witnesses: few neighbours saw any part of the attacks, most heard only a cry in the night, and one, Sophia Farrar, went down and held Kitty in her arms as she lay dying. In 2016 the Times itself conceded that its report had exaggerated the number of witnesses and what they saw. One night in Queens thus holds social psychology in small: how the presence of others changes what each of us does, and how a whole society can come to share a story that is not quite true.

3.1 Social Psychology: History, Theories, Concepts and MethodsLower yield · 6 Qs

Why do you drive more carefully with your father beside you? Social psychology asks how other people, present or imagined, shape what we think, feel and do. This section defines the field, traces its history, and covers its sociological roots, theories and methods.

What social psychology studies

The classic definition is that of Gordon Willard Allport (1897 to 1967), from 1954: social psychology studies how the thoughts, feelings and behaviour of individuals are influenced by the actual, imagined or implied presence of others: an audience in the hall, a mother whose frown you picture, a "No smoking" sign left by strangers. Robert A. Baron and Nyla R. Branscombe call it the scientific study of the nature and causes of individual behaviour, feeling and thought in social situations: scientific because evidence settles questions, individual because the unit is the person, even in a crowd.

Behaviour reflects the person-situation interaction: a student silent in class talks freely among friends. Psychological social psychology studies the individual's mental processes, mostly by experiment; sociological social psychology studies how groups, roles and social structure shape people, mostly by surveys and observation, drawing on symbolic interactionism (below). Sociology proper studies groups and societies, and personality psychology stable differences between people (Chapter 4); social psychology asks how situations make different people act alike. Common sense has a proverb for every outcome ("birds of a feather flock together", yet "opposites attract"), and hindsight bias (Section 1.8) makes findings look obvious afterwards; only testing shows which holds when.

Exam trap

"Actual, imagined or implied presence of others" is Gordon Allport (1954), though Baron and Branscombe credit it to his brother Floyd (1924). "Nature and causes of individual behaviour ... in social situations" is Baron and Branscombe.

Founders and landmarks

Two roots, crowds and experiments, fed the field (Table 3.1). Le Bon's crowd mind, Ringelmann's rope pullers (each pulled less hard as the team grew) and Triplett's children, who wound fishing reels faster in pairs than alone in what is usually called the first social psychology experiment, are taught in Section 3.10. The first two textbooks (1908) pulled opposite ways: the British psychologist William McDougall traced social behaviour to inborn instincts, the American sociologist Edward Alsworth Ross to imitation and suggestion in crowds. Floyd Henry Allport (1924) made the field experimental and individual-centred, and Muzafer Sherif (1935, 1936) watched group norms form as people judged the apparent movement of a light in the dark (Section 3.7).

Kurt Lewin

Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947), a Gestalt-trained refugee from Nazi Germany, is called the father of modern experimental social psychology. His field theory (Section 1.2) states B = f(P, E): behaviour is a function of the person and the environment, together the life space, the person plus the psychological environment as it exists for that person at that moment, not society, the group or the physical setting.

Lewin wanted research to change real life. In 1945 he founded the Research Center for Group Dynamics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT); group dynamics is the study of forces within groups. In 1946 he described action research, done with the people affected, as a spiral of steps, each a circle of planning, action and fact-finding about the results (Figure 3.1). A 1946 workshop produced the T-group (training group), whose members learn from frank feedback on their behaviour in the group, spread by the National Training Laboratories (1947) as sensitivity training. His motto: "There is nothing so practical as a good theory."

Exam trap

Life space = person + psychological environment, not society, group or physical environment. Lewin founded action research, not psychoanalysis (Freud), psychophysics (Fechner) or functionalism (James).

Crisis and new directions

After 1945 the field grew around the war's questions: persuasion, prejudice, conformity and obedience (Table 3.1). By the late 1960s a crisis had set in: laboratory experiments on students looked artificial, ethically doubtful and culture-bound, and Kenneth J. Gergen argued in "Social psychology as history" (1973) that its findings describe one society at one moment, not timeless laws. Europeans made the field more social: Henri Tajfel (1919 to 1982) studied group identity (Section 3.5), and Serge Moscovici minorities and shared knowledge. Then came the social cognition era of the 1970s and 1980s (Section 3.2), social neuroscience (the brain activity behind social thought), implicit processes (automatic attitudes outside awareness, measured by the Implicit Association Test, 1998) and cultural psychology (how culture shapes social thought and behaviour). In the 2010s the replication crisis broke: when 100 published psychology studies were repeated in 2015, only about a third gave the original result.

Sociological roots: Durkheim to Mead

Emile Durkheim (1858 to 1917), founder of French sociology, treated society as a reality in its own right. Social facts are ways of acting, thinking and feeling that exist outside individuals and constrain them, such as laws and customs; collective representations are the ideas and symbols a society shares. His most examined idea is anomie: normlessness, a state in which social norms are unclear, weak or no longer apply, so people no longer know what is expected of them. He introduced it in The Division of Labour in Society (1893); in Suicide (1897), anomic suicide rises when sudden change, a crash or a windfall, unsettles the norms. Robert King Merton (1910 to 2003) recast anomie as strain (1938), a gap between cultural goals such as wealth and the legitimate means to them, met by conformity, innovation (illegitimate means such as fraud), ritualism (rules kept, goal dropped), retreatism (both rejected) or rebellion (both replaced).

Exam trap

Anomie = normlessness. The progression from traditional to modern society is modernisation: Durkheim's mechanical solidarity (bonds of sameness) giving way to organic solidarity (interdependent specialists), or Ferdinand Tonnies's (1887) Gemeinschaft (community) giving way to Gesellschaft (society). Adapting to a dominant culture's values is assimilation or acculturation; internalising society's norms is socialisation.

A second root is symbolic interactionism: people act towards things according to the meanings those things have for them, and meanings, and the self, are built in interaction through symbols, above all language. George Herbert Mead (1863 to 1931) founded it; students published his lectures as Mind, Self and Society (1934), and Herbert Blumer named it in 1937. Charles Horton Cooley (1864 to 1929) added the looking-glass self (1902: we imagine how we appear to others, imagine their judgement and feel pride or shame; Chapter 4) and the primary group (1909), such as the family.

Socialisation and resocialisation

Socialisation is the lifelong learning of a society's norms, values and roles until they become one's own: primary in childhood within the family, secondary through school, peers, work and the media, the other agents of socialisation (gender socialisation is in Chapter 6). Merton's anticipatory socialisation is adopting a group's norms before joining it, as a medical aspirant already talks like a doctor.

Resocialisation is unlearning old norms, values and roles and learning new ones after a major transition: college, migration, marriage, the army or prison. A student's first weeks at college, before she finds friends and fits in, are a mild case. It is most intense in the total institutions described by Erving Goffman (1922 to 1982) in Asylums (1961), such as prisons, mental hospitals and barracks, which cut people off from society, manage all of daily life and strip away the old identity.

Clinical link

Long-stay inmates may develop institutionalisation: apathy, lost initiative, submissiveness and dependence on routine. Russell Barton called it institutional neurosis (1959), a product of the institution rather than the illness (Chapter 7).

Exam trap

A newcomer adapting to college, a new country or the army shows resocialisation, not the looking-glass self (a self-image built from imagined judgements), self-awareness (attention turned on oneself) or institutionalisation (the dependent apathy of long-stay inmates).

A social representation is a shared, changing system of values, ideas and practices, a common-sense theory through which a group makes the unfamiliar familiar: a collective successor to Durkheim's collective representations.

Key study: Moscovici and psychoanalysis in France (1961)

Serge Moscovici (1925 to 2014), a Romanian-born French psychologist, asked in La Psychanalyse, son image et son public (1961) how a scientific theory becomes common sense. Surveying Parisians and analysing the Catholic, Communist and popular press, he found each group remaking psychoanalysis by anchoring, fitting the unfamiliar into a familiar category (analysis seen as a kind of confession), and objectification, turning an abstract idea into a concrete thing (a "complex" became something a person has, like a cold). Critics find the concept too broad to test precisely.

Exam trap

Social representations = Serge Moscovici. Michael A. Hogg is social identity and uncertainty-identity theory; Baumeister and Leary proposed the need to belong, and Leary the sociometer.

Random assignment, giving every participant an equal chance of any condition, lets an experiment show cause. Elliot Aronson and J. Merrill Carlsmith (1968) separated experimental realism, how far a study absorbs participants, from mundane realism, how far it resembles everyday life; Milgram's study had much of the first, little of the second. External validity is how far results generalise. A mediating variable explains how or why one variable affects another (a violent film raises aggressive thoughts, which raise aggression); a moderating variable changes when or for whom it does (more in people already angry).

Deception, misleading participants about a study's purpose so they behave naturally, needs two safeguards: informed consent beforehand (what they will do, and that they may leave at any time) and debriefing afterwards, by dehoaxing (revealing the deception) and desensitising (removing distress, such as the belief that one is cruel). The Milgram and Zimbardo controversies made both standard (Section 3.7). Participants also respond to demand characteristics, cues revealing what the experimenter hopes to find (Martin Orne, 1962), and experimenters bias results through expectancy effects (Robert Rosenthal, 1966); double-blind procedures guard against both.

3.2 Social Cognition and First ImpressionsLower yield · 7 Qs

A stranger sits down at your hostel mess table, and within seconds you have judged him friendly, clever or arrogant; from then on you notice what fits. This section follows social thought from the frameworks that store it, through the shortcuts and tilts that bend it, to the impressions we form of others and manage in ourselves.

The social thinker

Social cognition is how we interpret, analyse, remember and use information about the social world. Susan Fiske and Shelley Taylor (1984) called people cognitive misers: with limited capacity and a flood of social information, we save effort with shortcuts and settle for good-enough answers. In their 1991 revision the thinker became a motivated tactician, choosing shortcuts or careful thought according to goals and stakes: you skim a passing stranger but study an interviewer's face.

That choice is dual-process thinking. Automatic processing is fast, effortless, unintended and often unconscious; controlled processing is slow, effortful, deliberate and conscious (System 1 and System 2, Section 1.8). Fatigue and time pressure push us onto the automatic track. The dominant account of how people organise social information in memory and use it to understand behaviour is the schema approach.

Exam trap

"How people organise information in memory and use it to understand behaviour" = the schema approach. A motivational approach explains social thought by needs and goals; a perceptual approach treats knowing people like perceiving objects (Asch's Gestalt view, below); "organisational approach" merely echoes the stem's verb.

A schema is an organised mental framework, built from experience, that holds our knowledge about a person, group, role or event and guides how we take in new information (Bartlett's idea, Section 1.7). Table 3.5 lists the kinds.

Schemas steer attention towards what fits, shape encoding (consistent information is stored readily, though a sharply inconsistent event stands out) and bias retrieval towards schema-consistent details. Recently activated schemas dominate. Priming is a temporary rise in a schema's availability: after a violent film, a driver who takes your parking spot looks aggressive. Automatic priming works below awareness: John Bargh, Mark Chen and Lara Burrows (1996) had students unscramble sentences containing words linked to old age (bingo, Florida), and they then walked down the corridor more slowly. It failed to replicate: Stéphane Doyen and colleagues (2012), timing walkers with infrared sensors, found slowing only when experimenters expected it. A primed schema fades by unpriming once it is expressed in thought or action (Betsy Sparrow and Daniel Wegner, 2006).

Schemas also resist disconfirmation, the perseverance effect. In the classic study of belief perseverance, Lee Ross, Mark Lepper and Michael Hubbard (1975) gave students false feedback that they were good or poor at telling real suicide notes from fakes; told later that the feedback was invented, they still rated their ability in line with it. Confirmation bias (Section 1.8) keeps schemas alive. Even figures of speech prime schemas: in reasoning by metaphor we grasp an abstract idea through a concrete one ("our relationship is on the right track"), and the metaphor shapes the judgement. Worst of all, a schema can make itself come true.

The self-fulfilling prophecy

The sociologist Robert King Merton (1910 to 2003) named and formalised the self-fulfilling prophecy in 1948: "a false definition of the situation evoking a new behaviour which makes the originally false conception come true." A rumour that a sound bank is failing starts a run on it, and it fails. In social psychology the term covers any expectation, about oneself or another, that leads us to act in ways that make the expected event or behaviour more likely (Figure 3.2).

Key study: Pygmalion in the Classroom (1968)

Robert Rosenthal (born 1933) and Lenore Jacobson tested every child in a Californian elementary school and told teachers that about 20 percent, in fact chosen at random, would soon "bloom" academically. Eight months later these children had gained more IQ points than their classmates, most clearly in the first two grades; teachers had given them more warmth and challenge. Critics questioned the IQ measures; later reviews find expectancy effects real but usually small.

A high expectation that raises performance is the Pygmalion effect (Rosenthal effect), after the sculptor whose statue came to life; a low one that depresses it is the Golem effect (Elisha Babad, Jacinto Inbar and Rosenthal, 1982). A perceiver's expectation drawing matching behaviour from the target is behavioural confirmation. Mark Snyder, Elizabeth Tanke and Ellen Berscheid (1977) gave men a photograph, attractive or not, of the woman they would telephone (it was not her); judges hearing only the women's side rated the "attractive" women warmer and more sociable. Carl Word, Mark Zanna and Joel Cooper (1974) found that white interviewers sat farther away and ended interviews sooner with Black applicants; white applicants treated this coolly then performed worse.

Exam trap

Acting on an expectation until it comes true = self-fulfilling prophecy. Confirmation bias only seeks confirming evidence and changes nothing in the world; the base-rate fallacy ignores how common something is (Section 1.8); "motivational reaction" is not a term. Merton formalised it, not Erving Goffman (dramaturgy, below; stigma, 1963; total institutions), Charles Horton Cooley (1864 to 1929; looking-glass self, 1902; primary group) or Émile Durkheim (1858 to 1917; anomie, a state of normlessness).

Heuristics in social judgement

Why shortcuts? Because of information overload: demands exceed our capacity, so we use heuristics, simple rules giving quick, usually adequate judgements (Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, 1974; Section 1.8). Representativeness judges a quiet, bookish neighbour a librarian, ignoring that managers far outnumber librarians (base-rate neglect). Availability judges by ease of recall: people who recalled six of their own assertive acts rated themselves more assertive than those asked for a harder twelve (Norbert Schwarz and colleagues, 1991). Anchoring and adjustment starts from a value, such as an asking price, and adjusts too little. The status quo heuristic ("what is, is good") favours the established: the same chocolate tasted better when said to be on sale since 1937 rather than 2003. The affect heuristic (Paul Slovic) lets a feeling of liking or dread stand in for judging risk and benefit.

On film of the 1992 Olympics, bronze medallists looked happier than silver medallists: silver thinks upward ("I nearly had gold"), bronze downward ("I nearly got nothing"). Ironic rebound arises because an automatic monitoring process keeps checking for the forbidden thought, and so keeps it primed. Terror management theory (Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski, 1986) holds that awareness of certain death creates anxiety we buffer with cultural world-views, self-esteem and supernatural beliefs.

Feeling also steers thought. In mood-congruent judgement we notice, recall and judge in line with our mood (Section 1.7). Using affect as information we ask "how do I feel about it?": people telephoned on sunny days reported more life satisfaction than on rainy days, unless first asked about the weather, which let them discount their mood (Schwarz and Gerald Clore, 1983). Joseph Forgas's affect infusion model (1995) adds that mood seeps into judgements we must construct, but hardly into ready-made or goal-driven ones.

Forming impressions

Impression formation is the combining of information about a person into an overall judgement. Its founding experiment used only adjectives.

Key study: Asch's warm and cold stranger (1946)

Solomon Asch (1907 to 1996), a Gestalt thinker, read participants a stranger's traits: intelligent, skilful, industrious, warm, determined, practical, cautious. Others heard cold instead. The warm stranger was judged far more generous, happy and sociable; swapping polite for blunt changed little. Warm-cold were central traits, which reorganise the whole impression; polite-blunt were peripheral traits. Traits, Asch concluded, form an integrated whole rather than a sum. Critics later showed that centrality depends on context.

Asch also showed a primacy effect: a person described as "intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious" was liked more than one given the same words in reverse, because early traits set the meaning of later ones. Harold Kelley (1950) told students a guest lecturer was "rather cold" or "very warm"; after the same class, the warm group rated him more considerate and sociable, and more of them joined the discussion.

Such leaps rest on implicit personality theories, unspoken beliefs about which traits go together ("warm people are generous"), a term of Jerome Bruner and Renato Tagiuri (1954). Their best-known product is the halo effect: an overall impression, or one salient positive trait, colours ratings of other, unrelated traits. Edward Lee Thorndike named it in 1920: army officers who rated a soldier high on physique also rated him high on intelligence, loyalty and leadership. The negative version is the horns effect. "What is beautiful is good" is a halo from attractiveness (Section 3.6); in rating scales the halo is a rater error (Chapter 2).

Exam trap

Halo effect = an overall impression or one positive trait influencing ratings of other, specific traits. Not judging by group membership (stereotyping, Section 3.5); not initial impressions shaping later judgements (primacy, or anchoring); not overlooking a liked person's faults (a consequence of a halo, not its definition); not crediting oneself for success and circumstances for failure (self-serving bias, Section 3.3); not overestimating how far others share our opinions (false consensus, Table 3.6).

In Norman Anderson's information integration theory (1965) each trait has a value and a weight. An additive model sums them, so adding "punctual" to "brilliant, generous" should help; a weighted averaging model, which the evidence mostly favours, averages them, so the impression dips. By the negativity effect, negative traits weigh more because bad behaviour seems more revealing.

Thin slices and first impressions

How fast and how good is a first impression? Nalini Ambady and Rosenthal (1992) named thin slicing: accurate inferences about people from very brief samples of behaviour. In 1993 they showed students silent clips of college teachers lasting 30 seconds or less (later as little as six); ratings of the clips predicted the teachers' end-of-semester student evaluations. Malcolm Gladwell popularised the idea in Blink (2005). Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov (2006) found that a 100-millisecond look at a face yields trait judgements, trustworthiness above all, that correlate highly with unhurried ones; longer looks mainly raise confidence. Thin slices beat chance but are not infallible.

Exam trap

A teacher who watches two students for under a minute and correctly picks the class leader shows thin slicing. Not social facilitation (better performance of well-learned tasks before others, Section 3.10), not a self-fulfilling prophecy (her expectation did not create the leadership), not the fundamental attribution error (over-explaining others' behaviour by disposition, Section 3.3).

Managing the impressions we make

We also try to control the impressions others form of us: impression management, or self-presentation. Erving Goffman (1922 to 1982) framed it as theatre in The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959). In his dramaturgy, on the front stage we play a role before an audience (the doctor's calm on the ward); back stage we drop it (doctors joking in the staff room). Face-work maintains one's own and others' face, the positive social value claimed in an encounter.

Tactics are self-enhancement, raising one's own appeal (grooming, recounting achievements), or other-enhancement, making the target feel good, chiefly ingratiation, winning liking through flattery and agreement, analysed by Edward E. Jones (1964). Jones and Thane Pittman (1982) classified five strategies by the impression sought (Table 3.7).

3.3 Communication, Nonverbal Cues and AttributionLower yield · 12 Qs

A friend answers your long message with one word: "ok." Is she busy, annoyed, or simply brief? Two processes are at work: communication, which carries meaning from one head to another through words, faces, gestures and distances, and attribution, by which you decide why someone acted as they did. This section follows the message first, then turns to the rules, and the biases, by which we explain behaviour.

The communication process and its models

Communication is the process by which a person or group transmits information, ideas and feelings to another through shared symbols. Every exchange has the same parts (Figure 3.3). The sender, or source, first performs encoding, turning an idea into symbols such as words or a gesture. The message travels through a channel, the medium that carries it (speech, print, a phone screen, the face). The receiver, or recipient, performs decoding, turning the symbols back into meaning, and replies with feedback. Anything that distorts the message on the way is noise.

Exam trap

The sequence is encoding, channel, recipient, decoding: a message must be encoded before it can enter a channel, and received before it can be decoded.

Verbal communication uses words; nonverbal communication uses everything else. In one-way communication (a lecture, a notice) the receiver cannot reply; in two-way communication feedback flows back, which is slower but more accurate. Barriers are semantic (words or jargon understood differently), physical (noise, distance, a poor network), psychological (anxiety, anger, prejudice, inattention) and cultural (different norms for gestures, eye contact and directness).

In the communication networks studied by Alex Bavelas (1913 to 1993) in 1950 and Harold Leavitt (1922 to 2007) in 1951, small groups solved problems by passing notes along fixed links. The centralised wheel, everyone linked only to a hub, was fastest and most accurate on simple tasks, and the hub became leader, but the others were least satisfied. The chain was intermediate. The decentralised circle was slower, with no clear leader but the highest morale, and in the later all-channel network everyone talks to everyone, which suits complex problems (Section 3.10).

Nonverbal channels and personal space

Nonverbal communication is the exchange of information through facial expression, gaze, body movement, posture, touch, voice and even scent, apart from the meaning of words. Such cues are hard to suppress, so hidden feelings leak through them. As Section 1.11 showed, Paul Ekman (born 1934) and Wallace Friesen found six universally recognised expressions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust), with contempt added in 1986, while cultures differ in display rules about when and how strongly an emotion may be shown. The facial feedback hypothesis is also in Section 1.11.

Eye contact usually signals liking, and avoided eyes suggest dislike or shyness, but staring, gaze held regardless of what the other person does, signals hostility and makes people leave. Body language includes posture (an open posture signals ease, frequent self-touching nervousness) and gestures, the most specific being emblems with a fixed meaning in one culture, such as the namaste or the Indian head wobble meaning "all right". Ray Birdwhistell (1918 to 1994) founded kinesics, the study of body movement as communication (Introduction to Kinesics, 1952). Touch conveys affection, care or dominance depending on who touches whom and where. Paralanguage is the voice apart from the words (pitch, tone, loudness, rate, pauses), which is why "fine" can mean its opposite. Even scent is a channel: subtle changes in body odour, such as those across the menstrual cycle, can affect others who do not consciously notice them.

Edward T. Hall (1914 to 2009) coined proxemics (1963), the study of how people use interpersonal distance, and in The Hidden Dimension (1966) described four zones (Figure 3.4). He measured them among Americans; contact cultures (Arab, Latin American) stand closer. In Beyond Culture (1976) he contrasted high-context cultures, where meaning lies largely in the setting, the relationship and nonverbal cues, so speech is indirect (India, Japan, China), with low-context cultures, where explicit words carry it (the United States, Germany). An Indian "I will try to come" may be a polite no.

Detecting deception

Can nonverbal cues expose a lie? Four help. Microexpressions are fleeting facial expressions, lasting a fraction of a second, that leak a concealed emotion before it is masked. Interchannel discrepancies are mismatches between channels: a liar may control the face but not the voice or hands. Eye contact changes: liars blink more, have more dilated pupils, and show too little or unusually much eye contact. Exaggerated expressions, an overdone smile or excessive regret, are the fourth sign, and vocal pitch tends to rise. Yet a meta-analysis of more than 200 studies by Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo (2006) found accuracy of only about 54 percent, barely above chance, and truths were spotted more often than lies because people assume honesty.

Exam trap

Averted eyes do not reliably mark a lie: liars may stare to look honest. No single cue betrays a lie, and most judges stay near chance.

Heider, Jones and Davis: from acts to causes

Attribution is the process by which people infer the causes of their own and others' behaviour. Fritz Heider (1896 to 1988), in The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations (1958), described people as amateur scientists; his naive psychology (common-sense psychology) concerns the implicit rules they use to infer causes. His key distinction: a dispositional attribution (internal) locates the cause in the person (traits, abilities, attitudes, effort), while a situational attribution (external) locates it outside (the situation, other people, the environment, luck, roles, culture). "He failed because he is lazy" is dispositional; "the paper was unusually hard" is situational.

Exam trap

Causes resting on the actor's personality traits are dispositional; situational, environmental and cultural causes are all external. Matching: Heider = implicit rules of attribution; Fred Fiedler (1922 to 2017) = contingency theory of leadership (Section 3.10); Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) = unconscious conflict; Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) = social learning theory.

Edward E. Jones and Keith Davis asked when an act reveals a trait. By their correspondent inference theory (1965), we infer that behaviour corresponds to a stable disposition when it is (1) freely chosen; (2) produces non-common effects, outcomes that only the chosen option yields, which pinpoint the motive; and (3) low in social desirability, since out-of-role behaviour is more revealing: a guest who calls your mother's biryani too salty reveals more than one who praises it. Hedonic relevance (the act affects us) and personalism (it seems aimed at us) strengthen the inference.

Kelley's covariation model and causal schemas

Harold Kelley (1921 to 2003) asked whether a cause lies in the person, the stimulus or the circumstances. His covariation model (1967) says that, observing behaviour over several occasions, we attribute it to the cause it covaries with, using three kinds of information:

  • Consensus: do other people react to this stimulus in the same way?
  • Consistency: does this person react this way to the stimulus on other occasions?
  • Distinctiveness: does this person react differently to other stimuli?

Ananya laughs at a comedian. If few others laugh, she laughs at him every time and she laughs at every comedian (low, high, low), the cause is Ananya. If everyone laughs, she always does and she laughs at no other comedian (all high), the comedian is funny. If she has never laughed at him before (low consistency), a passing circumstance is responsible. Consistency is high in both main patterns, so only consensus and distinctiveness separate internal from external (Table 3.9).

For a single observation, Kelley (1972) proposed causal schemas. By the discounting principle, a cause gets less weight when other plausible causes exist (a film star is paid to praise a brand); by the augmenting principle, it gets more weight when the effect occurs despite an obstacle (a student tops the class while working nights).

Weiner's three dimensions

Bernard Weiner (born 1935), studying achievement from the 1970s, classified causes by locus (internal or external), stability and controllability. Ability is internal, stable and uncontrollable; effort is internal, unstable and controllable; task difficulty is external and stable; luck is external and unstable. Stability shapes expectations, and the dimensions shape emotion: success put down to internal causes brings pride; failure from low ability brings shame, from low effort guilt. Another person's misfortune seen as controllable ("he drank") provokes anger, and an uncontrollable one ("a lay-off") pity and helping (Section 3.8).

Clinical link

In the attributional reformulation of learned helplessness, Lyn Abramson, Martin Seligman (born 1942) and John Teasdale (1978) proposed that people who explain bad events by internal ("it is me"), stable ("it will last") and global ("it spoils everything") causes are most vulnerable to depression (Section 1.6; Chapter 8).

Attributional biases

Our attributions tilt in predictable directions (Table 3.10). The strongest tilt is the fundamental attribution error (FAE), named by Lee Ross (1942 to 2021) in 1977: overestimating dispositional and underestimating situational causes of other people's behaviour. A man arrives late and spills his coffee; we call him clumsy, not delayed and hurried. The correspondence bias, inferring dispositions that correspond to behaviour even when situational causes are present, is usually treated as a synonym (reviewed in 1995 by Daniel Gilbert, born 1957, and Patrick Malone).

Key study: Jones and Harris, the Castro essays (1967)

Method. Jones and Victor Harris had participants read essays for or against Fidel Castro, telling some that the writer chose the side and others that it was assigned. Result. Even with assigned sides, pro-Castro writers were judged fairly pro-Castro. Conclusion. People underweight a situational constraint they know about. Criticism. Readers may assume nobody argues well for a view he rejects, but the bias replicates across many tasks.

Why does it happen? The actor is perceptually salient while the situation fades into the background. In Gilbert's two-step model we first make an automatic dispositional inference, then effortfully correct it for the situation; under cognitive load the correction fails.

For our own behaviour the tilt reverses. The actor-observer effect (Jones and Richard Nisbett, 1971) is attributing our own behaviour to situations and others' to dispositions: he trips because he is clumsy, I trip because the floor was wet. The actor sees the situation; the observer sees the actor. Michael Storms (1973) reversed the effect by showing actors and observers videotapes shot from the opposite viewpoint. The self-serving bias concerns our own outcomes: successes go to internal causes, failures to external ones (an unfair paper, bad luck); it protects self-esteem and is weaker in collectivist cultures.

The just-world hypothesis of Melvin Lerner (1965, 1980) holds that people need to believe the world is orderly, predictable and fair, a place where people get what they deserve. An innocent person's suffering threatens the belief, which is restored by deciding the victim deserved it: victim blaming. In Lerner and Carolyn Simmons's experiment (1966), women who watched a fellow student receive apparent electric shocks, and could not end or compensate her suffering, derogated her. Zick Rubin and Letitia Anne Peplau measured the belief with their Just World Scale (1975).

Exam trap

Just-world victim blaming comes from the need to believe that the world is orderly, predictable and fair, not from sadism, not from the FAE itself (it looks dispositional, but the motive is protecting the belief), and not from downward social comparison with someone worse off (Section 3.6).

Culture and applications

Is the FAE truly fundamental? It is strongest in individualist cultures, which see the person as an independent agent, and weaker in collectivist ones, which see behaviour as embedded in roles and relationships.

Indian context

Joan Miller (1984) compared Hindu Indians in Mysore with Americans, aged 8, 11, 15 and adult, asking them to explain good and bad acts of people they knew. American adults gave far more dispositional explanations; Hindu adults gave more situational ones (roles, relationships, circumstances). At age 8 the groups barely differed and the gap grew with age: attributional style is learned from culture.

Similarly, Michael Morris and Kaiping Peng (1994) found that The New York Times explained two 1991 American mass murders by the killers' personalities, while a Chinese-language newspaper stressed their situations (isolation, conflicts at work, easy access to guns).

Because attributions shape feelings and effort, changing them helps. In attribution retraining, Timothy Wilson and Patricia Linville (1982) showed worried first-year students evidence that grades usually improve after the first year, inviting an unstable explanation for poor grades; they later earned better grades and dropped out less than controls. Attributional therapy for depression teaches people to credit themselves for success and to stop blaming lasting flaws for failure. Happy couples credit a partner's kindness to disposition and excuse lapses as situational; distressed couples do the reverse (Section 3.6). And computer-mediated communication, stripped of faces and tone, gives the correspondence bias free rein: a terse "ok." reads as rudeness, not haste.

3.4 Attitudes, Persuasion and Cognitive DissonanceLower yield · 15 Qs

Advertisements, election rallies and anti-smoking posters all bet that changing attitudes changes behaviour. This section follows attitudes from their origins to behaviour, persuasion, resistance and dissonance.

What an attitude is

In 1935 Gordon Willard Allport (1897 to 1967) called the attitude "the most distinctive and indispensable concept in contemporary American social psychology". An attitude is a learned, fairly lasting evaluation of an attitude object (a person, group, thing or idea) along a like-dislike dimension. The tricomponent model (ABC model) of Milton J. Rosenberg and Carl Hovland (1960) gives it three parts: affect (feelings: "chai cheers me up"), behaviour or conation (action tendencies: "I stop at the stall daily") and cognition (beliefs: "tea is good for me").

Attitude strength combines extremity, certainty, accessibility (how fast the attitude comes to mind) and importance. Ambivalence, holding positive and negative evaluations of one object at once, weakens an attitude's hold on behaviour. Explicit attitudes are conscious and self-reported; implicit attitudes are automatic evaluations measured from reaction times, as in the Implicit Association Test (Section 3.5), and the two can disagree.

Daniel Katz (1903 to 1998) named four functions of attitudes in 1960: utilitarian or adjustive (gaining rewards, avoiding punishments), knowledge (organising a confusing world), ego-defensive (protecting self-esteem from threatening truths, as when scorn for an outgroup props up a shaky self) and value-expressive (displaying central values). In 1956 M. Brewster Smith, Jerome Seymour Bruner and Robert W. White had named object appraisal (sizing up objects for one's interests), social adjustment (easing relations with valued people) and externalisation (projecting inner conflicts onto outside objects). Measurement belongs to Section 2.8: Bogardus's social distance scale (1925), Thurstone's equal-appearing intervals (1928), Likert's summated ratings (1932), Guttman's cumulative scale and Osgood's semantic differential (1957).

How attitudes form

Most attitudes are learned (Section 1.6). In classical conditioning a neutral object takes on the feeling of what accompanies it: Arthur W. Staats and Carolyn K. Staats (1958) paired nationality names such as "Dutch" and "Swedish" with pleasant or unpleasant words, and the pleasantly paired names were later rated more favourably. In subliminal conditioning the pairing works without awareness: Jon A. Krosnick and colleagues (1992) flashed pleasant or unpleasant pictures too briefly to be noticed before photographs of a stranger, who was then liked more after the pleasant ones.

In instrumental conditioning, views that are praised or agreed with are strengthened, which is why children echo their parents' opinions until adolescence. We also copy the attitudes of models (observational learning) and adopt those of reference groups, the groups whose standards we judge ourselves by, through social comparison, which Leon Festinger (1954) described as evaluating our own opinions and abilities against other people's. Mere exposure is taught in Section 3.6. Identical twins share more attitudes than fraternal twins, and Abraham Tesser (1993) found highly heritable attitudes more accessible and harder to change. Online word of mouth (reviews, ratings, posts) now carries the weight of peers.

Exam trap

Praise or agreement from a friend strengthens an expressed attitude, never weakens it: positive reinforcement raises the likelihood of whatever it follows.

When attitudes predict behaviour

Key study: LaPiere's travels (1934)

In the early 1930s, a time of open anti-Asian prejudice, Richard Tracy LaPiere (1899 to 1986) toured the United States with a young Chinese couple. Of about 250 hotels and restaurants, one refused them. Afterwards he wrote to the same places asking whether they would serve Chinese guests, and over 90 percent of those replying said no. Critics note that different staff may have answered the letters.

In 1969 Allan W. Wicker's review found attitude-behaviour correlations rarely above .30. Research then asked when attitudes do predict behaviour.

Strong attitudes predict best. Extremity often reflects vested interest: John Sivacek and William D. Crano (1982) found that 47 percent of students who would be barred by a higher drinking age agreed to campaign against it, against 12 percent of unaffected students. Certainty (clarity plus a sense of correctness), personal relevance and knowledge about the object also help. So does direct experience. During a Cornell University housing shortage, Dennis T. Regan and Russell H. Fazio (1977) found that students forced to sleep on lounge cots acted on their attitudes (petitions, committees) far more consistently than students who had only heard about it, and Fazio and Mark P. Zanna (1981) showed that direct experience makes attitudes clearer and more accessible. Attitudes formed by indirect experience predict worst.

Specificity must also match. The principle of compatibility of Icek Ajzen and Martin Fishbein (1977) requires attitude and behaviour to correspond in action, target, context and time. In Andrew Davidson and James Jaccard's (1979) study, general attitude towards birth control correlated .08 with women's pill use over the next two years, but attitude towards using the pill in that period correlated .57. Finally, situational constraints and perceived norms intervene: Deborah A. Prentice and Dale T. Miller (1993) found Princeton students privately less comfortable with heavy drinking than they believed their peers were, and the men's attitudes drifted towards that imagined norm.

Exam trap

Behaviour is hardest to predict from attitudes formed by indirect experience (hearsay, media). Direct experience, personal relevance, knowledge of the object, certainty and matching specificity all strengthen the link.

Reasoned action, planned behaviour and MODE

For deliberate behaviour, the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) makes behavioural intention the immediate predictor of behaviour. Intention rests on the attitude towards the behaviour (beliefs about its outcomes, weighted by their value) and the subjective norm (what important others want, weighted by the motivation to comply). Ajzen (1985, 1991) added perceived behavioural control, the belief that one can perform the act, close to Albert Bandura's self-efficacy, making the theory of planned behaviour; control affects intention and also behaviour directly. It is the standard model of health behaviours such as condom use and quitting smoking; the health belief model and protection motivation theory are in Section 7.9.

Spontaneous behaviour works differently. In Fazio's attitude-to-behaviour process model (1986), an event automatically activates an accessible attitude, which together with knowledge of norms shapes how the situation is defined, and that definition drives behaviour. His MODE model (1990: Motivation and Opportunity as DEterminants) unites the accounts: people deliberate only when motivated and given the opportunity (time, capacity); otherwise accessible attitudes act automatically.

Exam trap

Planned behaviour's predictors of intention are attitude, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control, often worded as self-efficacy (RML 2023). Genetics, personality, socio-economic status, cultural beliefs, social support, health literacy, physical environment, emotional intelligence and coping are not in the model.

Two routes to persuasion

Why do these factors work? In the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) of Petty and Cacioppo (1981, 1986), elaboration is thinking about a message's arguments, and it depends on motivation (personal relevance, need for cognition) and ability (time, knowledge, no distraction). With both, people take the central route: systematic processing with careful attention to argument quality, giving durable change that resists attack and predicts behaviour. Without them they take the peripheral route, using mental shortcuts triggered by cues such as an attractive source, the number of arguments or a good mood, and the change is temporary (Figure 3.5). In a 1981 study by Petty, Cacioppo and Goldman, argument quality decided students' views on a comprehensive-examination proposal due next year, but source expertise decided them when it was a decade away.

Shelly Chaiken's heuristic-systematic model (1980) contrasts systematic processing with heuristic processing by rules of thumb ("experts are right"), and lets both run at once.

Exam trap

Systematic processing is the central route and involves careful attention; heuristic processing is the peripheral route and uses mental shortcuts. The ELM has exactly two routes: there is no "emotional" or "situational" route (IHBAS 2020).

Resisting persuasion

Audiences also push back. Jack Williams Brehm (1928 to 2009) defined reactance in 1966 as the unpleasant state, and motivation, to restore a freedom that is threatened, often by wanting or doing the forbidden thing more; hard selling may even produce a boomerang effect, change opposite to the one advocated. Reactance is not compliance (changing behaviour at a direct request), conformity (following group norms) or obedience (following authority), all in Section 3.7, nor social comparison (3.4.2).

Forewarning of persuasive intent aids resistance: Jonathan L. Freedman and David O. Sears (1965) found teenagers warned ten minutes ahead of a talk against teenage driving were persuaded less than those warned two minutes ahead or not at all, because they had time for counterarguing, generating thoughts that refute the message. People also practise selective avoidance of challenging information and selective exposure to agreeable information. In inoculation (McGuire, 1961), a weak attack plus its refutation (refutational defence) protects a belief better than supportive arguments alone, as a vaccine does. Charles G. Lord, Lee Ross and Mark R. Lepper (1979) showed biased assimilation: supporters and opponents of capital punishment read the same mixed evidence, each rated the agreeing study as stronger, and both became more extreme (attitude polarisation). Ego depletion, tiredness after a demanding task, weakens resistance.

Exam trap

Reactance (Brehm, 1966) is the motivation to restore a threatened freedom, so the restricted option grows more attractive. It is not complying with requests or authority, following norms or the majority, the effect of persuasive messages, or comparing one's abilities with others.

Clinical link

Lecturing a client to stop drinking can provoke reactance; motivational interviewing (Section 7.6) avoids it by letting the client voice the reasons for change.

Consistency theories and cognitive dissonance

Much resistance reflects a drive for consistency. In the balance theory of Fritz Heider (1896 to 1988; 1946, 1958), a person (P), another person (O) and an object (X) form a triad of positive or negative relations. It is balanced when the product of the three signs is positive, and imbalanced, motivating change, when it is negative (Figure 3.6). In the congruity theory of Charles E. Osgood and Percy H. Tannenbaum (1955), when a source endorses an object we rate differently, as when an admired cricketer promotes a disliked brand, both evaluations move towards each other.

Leon Festinger (1919 to 1989) proposed cognitive dissonance theory in 1957. Dissonance is the aversive tension of holding inconsistent cognitions, especially of acting against one's own attitude; it grows with the importance of the cognitions and with the ratio of dissonant to consonant ones. People reduce it by:

  • changing the attitude, the usual outcome when the behaviour cannot be undone;
  • changing the behaviour;
  • adding consonant cognitions and seeking supportive, not conflicting, information;
  • trivialisation, deciding the inconsistency does not matter (Linda Simon, Jeff Greenberg and Brehm, 1995);
  • self-affirmation, bolstering self-worth through other values while the inconsistency stays (Claude Mason Steele, 1988);
  • denying responsibility.

Take a case. Arvind detests smoking; his grandfather died of lung cancer. At a party his friends smoke, and so does he. "Smoking is deadly" and "I smoked" clash, both matter to him, and he feels dissonance. He cannot unsmoke the cigarette, so he may soften his attitude ("one is harmless"), add a cognition ("I did it for my friends"), blame the pressure, or vow never again. This is not effort justification (valuing what cost effort), an increased drive to smoke, or drive reduction (Hull's need-reduction principle, Section 1.10).

Exam trap

Cognitive dissonance is Festinger's theory, not Milgram's (obedience), Asch's (conformity) or Zimbardo's (prison simulation), and not operant conditioning, classical conditioning or social learning theory (Section 1.6). Trivialisation and self-affirmation reduce dissonance, not compliance, reactance or self-regulation (control of one's own behaviour towards goals). Dissonance is likeliest when one must decide or act against one's beliefs, not when thoughts and behaviour agree, after praise or during happy memories.

Dissonance paradigms and rival explanations

Key study: Festinger and Carlsmith (1959)

Festinger and James Merrill Carlsmith had students do an hour of dull tasks (turning pegs, loading spools), then paid them $1 or $20 to tell the next participant the tasks were interesting. The $20 group, like controls who told no lie, later rated the tasks dull; the $1 group rated them more enjoyable. Lacking adequate external justification, they reduced dissonance by changing their attitude. This is induced compliance (forced compliance) with insufficient justification, and its result is the less-leads-to-more effect.

3.5 Stereotypes, Prejudice and Intergroup RelationsLower yield · 13 Qs

In 1954 two groups of eleven-year-old boys at an Oklahoma camp came to loathe each other within days, though nothing divided them but a name and a tournament. How do ordinary people come to think less of whole groups, and what undoes it?

Stereotype, prejudice and discrimination

Bias has three components, matching the ABC of attitudes (Section 3.4). A stereotype is cognitive: a belief about the traits typical of a group's members, what the journalist Walter Lippmann called "pictures in our heads" (Public Opinion, 1922). Daniel Katz and Kenneth Braly (1933) measured them with an adjective checklist: Princeton students agreed strongly on the traits of ten ethnic groups, including groups they had seldom or never met. Prejudice is affective: a negative attitude toward people because they belong to a group, in Gordon Willard Allport's words (The Nature of Prejudice, 1954) "an antipathy based upon a faulty and inflexible generalization". Discrimination is behavioural: unfair negative action toward a group's members.

William Graham Sumner (Folkways, 1906) named the in-group ("we") and the out-group ("they") and defined ethnocentrism, judging other groups by the standards of one's own, placed at the centre and above the rest. Allport ranked prejudice in action in five steps of rising harm (Allport's scale): antilocution (hostile talk), avoidance, discrimination, physical attack and extermination. Prejudice is named by its target: racism, sexism and ageism (against the old; coined by Robert Neil Butler in 1969). In India, casteism is prejudice and discrimination based on caste, a hereditary, endogamous ranking (untouchability is abolished by Article 17 of the Constitution), and communalism joins loyalty to one's own religious community with hostility toward others.

Exam trap

Stereotype = belief, prejudice = feeling, discrimination = action. "Pictures in our heads" is Lippmann, not Allport. Allport's scale starts with antilocution, not avoidance. Ethnocentrism is Sumner (1906).

Where prejudice comes from

Early explanations looked inside the prejudiced person. Theodor Adorno, Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson and Nevitt Sanford (The Authoritarian Personality, 1950) described an authoritarian personality: rigid, conventional, submissive to authority and hostile to minorities, shaped by harsh, punitive parenting, and measured by the F-scale (F for fascism). Its modern heirs are Bob Altemeyer's right-wing authoritarianism (1981: authoritarian submission, authoritarian aggression, conventionalism) and Jim Sidanius and Felicia Pratto's social dominance orientation, a preference for a hierarchy in which some groups rank above others.

Scapegoat theory holds that frustration is displaced onto a weak, visible minority (Section 3.9): Carl Hovland and Robert Sears (1940) found that lynchings in the American South rose as cotton prices fell. In the social learning view, children absorb prejudice from parents, peers and media and are rewarded with approval for voicing it.

At the group level, realistic conflict theory (named by Donald Thomas Campbell in 1965, argued by Muzafer Sherif in 1966) holds that competition for scarce resources breeds hostility. Often what hurts is relative deprivation, having less than one feels entitled to compared with others: in The American Soldier (1949), Samuel Stouffer and colleagues found fast-promoted Air Corps men less satisfied with promotion than slow-promoted military police. Walter Garrison Runciman (1966) separated egoistic (personal) from fraternal (group) deprivation; the fraternal kind fuels prejudice and collective action such as protest. Ted Robert Gurr (Why Men Rebel, 1970) traced rebellion to the gap between what people expect and what they get.

Key study: The Robbers Cave experiment (1954)

Question. Does competition create intergroup hostility, and what removes it? Method. Sherif and colleagues took 22 unacquainted boys aged about 11 to 12, in two matched groups, to a summer camp at Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma. Phase 1, in-group formation: each group lived apart and named itself (the Eagles, the Rattlers). Phase 2, competition and friction: a tournament with prizes for the winners. Phase 3, reduction of conflict. Result. Competition brought name-calling, a burned flag and cabin raids. Mere contact (shared meals, films) failed and even made things worse: meals became food fights. Hostility fell only with superordinate goals, which both groups wanted but neither could reach alone: restoring the water supply, pooling money to rent a film, pulling a stalled truck. Criticism. The boys were all white, Protestant and middle class, and staff stage-managed events. The report appeared in 1961.

Exam trap

Superordinate goals requiring cooperation reduced the hostility. Mere contact failed, more competition is what created the hostility, and no dominant leader was assigned. Robbers Cave is not Sherif's autokinetic study of norms (Section 3.7).

Social identity theory

Sherif's boys traded insults as soon as they learned of each other, before any contest. Is competition even needed? Henri Tajfel (1919 to 1982), a Polish Jew who lost most of his family in the Holocaust, looked for the minimal conditions for discrimination. In the minimal group paradigm (Tajfel, Billig, Bundy and Flament, 1971), Bristol schoolboys were told they had been grouped by a preference for Paul Klee or Wassily Kandinsky (in truth, at random), then divided points worth money between two anonymous others. Groups with no history, contact or competition still produced in-group favouritism, and many boys chose to maximise the difference between groups even at a cost to their own group's absolute gain.

Tajfel and John Turner (1947 to 2011) explained this with social identity theory (1979, 1986): a large part of our self-concept and self-esteem comes from the groups we belong to. Besides a personal identity (one's own traits, achievements and preferences) we have a social identity derived from group memberships (Indian, Kannadiga, psychology student). Three processes link membership to bias (Figure 3.7): social categorisation into in-groups and out-groups, social identification with the in-group, and social comparison with out-groups. Since self-esteem rides on the comparison, we seek positive distinctiveness: our group must come out ahead. Prejudice thus arises from categorisation itself, with no clash of interests required.

When the comparison is unflattering, members may leave for a higher group (social mobility, if boundaries are permeable), change the comparison by picking a new dimension or a lower group (social creativity: "poorer but kinder"), or challenge the higher group directly (social competition). The self-categorisation theory of Turner and colleagues (1987) explains when we see ourselves as individuals and when as interchangeable group members: the category that best fits the situation becomes salient. Marilynn Brewer's optimal distinctiveness theory (1991) holds that we balance a need to belong against a need to be different, so we identify most with groups that are inclusive yet distinctive.

Exam trap

In social identity theory, identity and prejudice come from group membership and categorisation into in-groups and out-groups, driven by the need for a positive social identity. Wrong options: competition for scarce resources (realistic conflict); birthplace, prejudice experienced, economic status, personal achievements, preferences or attributes (personal identity); internal or external attributions (Section 3.3); cognitive dissonance (Section 3.4); social facilitation (Section 3.10); genetic kin selection (Section 3.8); operant reinforcement (Chapter 1).

How stereotypes work in the mind

Categorisation also distorts perception. In the out-group homogeneity effect "they are all alike": George Quattrone and Edward Ellsworth Jones (1980) found Princeton and Rutgers students generalised more from one rival student's choice to his whole university than from an in-group member's; the reverse is in-group differentiation. An illusory correlation is a perceived group-trait link the data do not support: David Hamilton and Robert Gifford (1976) gave a large and a small group the same proportion of undesirable acts, yet people paired the two distinctive things, minority and rare bad acts, and judged the small group worse. Thomas Pettigrew's ultimate attribution error (1979) extends the fundamental attribution error (Section 3.3) to groups: an out-group's failings are put down to its nature, its successes to luck.

Stereotypes persist because they save effort: C. Neil Macrae, Alan Milne and Galen Bodenhausen (1994) found that people given stereotype labels recalled more traits and did better on a simultaneous second task, so stereotypes are energy-saving devices. They also resist disconfirmation. Renee Weber and Jennifer Crocker (1983) compared bookkeeping (each exception nudges the stereotype), conversion (one dramatic case overturns it) and subtyping (exceptions are fenced off as atypical, leaving it intact); disconfirmations spread over many typical members changed it most.

The stereotype content model of Susan Fiske, Amy Cuddy, Peter Glick and Jun Xu (2002) places groups on warmth and competence, each combination evoking its own emotion (Figure 3.8). Stereotypes can also impair performance through stereotype threat, the fear of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group. Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995) found that Black Stanford students scored below equally able White students on hard verbal items only when these were presented as a test of ability; Steven Spencer, Steele and Diane Quinn (1999) found the same for women on a hard mathematics test.

Automatic and controlled processes

Nearly everyone knows the cultural stereotypes; is everyone then prejudiced? Patricia Devine's (1989) dissociation model says no. High- and low-prejudiced White Americans knew the stereotype of Black Americans equally well, and after stereotype-related words were flashed too fast to see, both judged an ambiguous man as more hostile: activation by automatic processing (fast, unintended, outside awareness) happens in everyone. Given time to list their thoughts, however, low-prejudiced people wrote more egalitarian ones. Devine concluded that low-prejudiced people use controlled processing (slow, deliberate, effortful) to inhibit the activated stereotype and replace it with egalitarian beliefs, much like breaking a bad habit.

Automatic associations can be measured without asking. The Implicit Association Test (IAT) of Anthony Greenwald, Debbie McGhee and Jordan Schwartz (1998) times how fast people sort faces and words with two response keys. Being faster when one group shares a key with "good" words than with "bad" words reveals an implicit, unconscious association (implicit prejudice or stereotyping) that people may be unwilling or unable to report. In the bona fide pipeline of Russell Fazio and colleagues (1995), a briefly shown Black or White face primes a good-or-bad judgement of adjectives; faster responses to negative words after Black faces reveal an automatic negative attitude.

Exam trap

Low-prejudiced people show controlled processing that refutes the stereotype. Automatic processing with involuntary stereotyping is the first stage, shared by all and left unchecked by high-prejudiced people; "automatic, voluntary" is self-contradictory; controlled processing prevents involuntary stereotyping rather than causing it. The IAT measures implicit associations by reaction time: not verbal or hidden mathematical ability, and not dream content (psychoanalysis).

Reducing prejudice

Allport's contact hypothesis (1954) holds that contact reduces prejudice only under four conditions: equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation and support of authorities, law or custom. That is why mere contact during conflict failed at Robbers Cave. Segregation, minimal contact and competition keep prejudice alive.

In Elliot Aronson's jigsaw classroom (first used in 1971 in newly desegregated schools in Austin, Texas; book The Jigsaw Classroom, 1978), each child in a mixed group learns one piece of the lesson and must teach it, so every child needs every other; liking and self-esteem rose. 3.6 Affiliation, Attraction and Close RelationshipsLower yield · 7 Qs

Think back to your first weeks in a hostel: most of the friends you made were probably the people whose rooms were nearest yours. Why do we seek company at all, why these people rather than others, and what keeps a relationship alive or ends it? This section moves from the need to affiliate, through attraction and the theories of close relationships, to love, break-up and loneliness.

The need to affiliate

Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary argued in 1995 that the need to belong, a drive to form and keep a few lasting, positive relationships, is a fundamental human motive. People differ in its strength; the stable trait is the need for affiliation (nAff), listed by Henry Murray and measured by David McClelland (Section 1.10). The older word is gregariousness, the urge to be with others and live in groups. William McDougall listed a gregarious instinct in 1908, but gregariousness is now classed as a social motive: a secondary, learned or psychosocial motive, like affiliation, achievement and power, that arises and is satisfied in interaction with others. Biological (primary) motives such as hunger and thirst serve bodily survival instead.

Exam trap

Gregariousness has a social motive. "Biological" tempts because McDougall called it an instinct, but instinct theory collapsed (Section 1.10). "Psychological" is not a standard separate class, and personal motives are an individual's own habits, interests and aspirations.

Key study: Schachter's anxiety and affiliation experiment (1959)

Stanley Schachter (1922 to 1997) had women students met by a fictitious "Dr Gregor Zilstein", who warned of electric shocks that would be painful (high anxiety) or feel like a tickle (low anxiety). Offered a wait alone or with others, about two-thirds of the high-anxiety women chose company, against about one-third of the low-anxiety women, and they wanted only others facing the same shocks: "misery doesn't just love any kind of company, it loves only miserable company". No shocks were given.

Why do frightened people want each other? All three usual answers hold: we affiliate to obtain information about what is happening, to reduce anxiety, and to compare ourselves with similar others. Craig Hill (1987) found four affiliation motives: positive stimulation, emotional support, attention and social comparison.

The need shows most sharply when thwarted. Ostracism, being ignored and excluded, was studied by Kipling Williams with Cyberball (2000), an online ball-tossing game in which the other "players" soon stop throwing to the participant; minutes of exclusion by strangers lower mood and threaten belonging, self-esteem, control and meaningful existence. Feelings shape liking too: in the reinforcement-affect model of Donn Byrne and Gerald Clore (1970) we like people who make us feel good and, by classical conditioning, even strangers merely present when something pleasant happens.

Social comparison

Leon Festinger (1919 to 1989) proposed social comparison theory in 1954: people are driven to evaluate their opinions and abilities, and when no objective standard exists they compare themselves with others, preferably similar others. A mark of 85 percent means little until you know how your classmates scored. Upward social comparison, with someone better off, can inspire but often brings envy; downward social comparison, with someone worse off, was described by Thomas Wills (1981) as a route to self-enhancement when self-esteem is threatened. Abraham Tesser's self-evaluation maintenance model (1988) adds closeness and relevance: if your cousin tops the medical entrance and medicine matters to you, the comparison process stings and you may distance yourself; if she wins a dance contest you do not care about, the reflection process lets you bask in her glory.

Social media supply an endless upward stream of other people's best moments. Andrew Przybylski and colleagues (2013) defined the fear of missing out (FOMO) as a pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent; it goes with heavier social media use and lower mood. FOMO is a social comparison phenomenon.

Exam trap

FOMO is linked to social comparison, not classical conditioning (learning by pairing stimuli), operant extinction (a response fading when reinforcement stops) or "cognitive dissonance only" (discomfort from inconsistent cognitions, Section 3.4).

Nearness and familiarity

The plainest force of attraction is nearness. In 1950 Festinger, Schachter and Kurt Back studied Westgate West, married-student housing at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology where flats were assigned at random. Of next-door neighbours, 41 percent were named as close friends, against 22 percent of those two doors away and 10 percent of those at the far end of the corridor. Functional distance, how often architecture brings paths together, mattered too: residents beside the stairs and mailboxes made more friends upstairs. Mady Segal (1974) found that police trainees seated alphabetically befriended those with neighbouring surnames. This is the propinquity effect: the more we see and interact with a person, the more likely he or she is to become our friend or partner.

The mechanism is largely the mere exposure effect of Robert Zajonc (1968): repeated exposure to a stimulus, without interaction or reward, increases liking for it, whether nonsense words, Chinese-like characters or faces. Richard Moreland and Scott Beach (1992) had four women attend a class 0, 5, 10 or 15 times without speaking; students liked them more the more often they came. Theodore Mita and colleagues (1977) found that women preferred mirror images of their own faces, the version they see daily, while friends preferred the true image. A stimulus disliked at first, however, can become more disliked.

Exam trap

"The more we see and interact with someone, the more likely they become our friend or partner" is the propinquity effect. The exposure effect is narrower (mere exposure to any stimulus raises liking) and is its mechanism; matching and reciprocity are taught below. For mere exposure, "performing better when observed" is social facilitation, "attitude change after inconsistent behaviour" is cognitive dissonance and "riskier group decisions" is the risky shift (Sections 3.4 and 3.10).

Looks, similarity and reciprocity

Appearance acts first. Karen Dion, Ellen Berscheid and Elaine Hatfield (then Walster) showed in 1972 that attractive people are judged kinder, more sociable and more successful: the what is beautiful is good stereotype. At a 1966 computer dance at the University of Minnesota, Walster and colleagues paired students at random; the only predictor of wanting another date was the partner's attractiveness. Judith Langlois and Lori Roggman (1990) found computer-averaged faces more attractive than most faces composing them (averageness); symmetry also attracts. Yet most people must settle: the matching hypothesis (Walster and colleagues 1966; tested by Berscheid and colleagues 1971) holds that we pair with partners of roughly our own attractiveness to avoid rejection, and Bernard Murstein (1972) found real couples closer in attractiveness than chance.

Similarity matters more over time. Theodore Newcomb measured transfer students' attitudes by post, housed them together, and found that those initially similar became friends (The Acquaintance Process, 1961). Byrne's "bogus stranger" experiments gave his law of attraction (1971): attraction rises with the proportion of similar attitudes. Milton Rosenbaum's repulsion hypothesis (1986), that dissimilarity repels while similarity adds nothing, overstates the case, though dissimilarity does weigh slightly more. Robert Winch's (1958) complementarity of needs, opposites attracting (dominant with submissive), has found little support.

We also like those who like us (reciprocity of liking). In the gain-loss effect of Elliot Aronson and Darwyn Linder (1965), participants who overheard a confederate's evaluations liked her most when they turned from negative to positive, more than when consistently positive, and least when they turned from positive to negative.

Exchange, equity and investment

Why do people stay in some relationships and leave others? Social exchange theory, from John Thibaut and Harold Kelley (1959) and George Homans (1961), treats a relationship as a trade: rewards minus costs. The outcome is judged against the comparison level (CL), what one feels one deserves from past relationships, which sets satisfaction, and the comparison level for alternatives (CLalt), the best outcome available elsewhere, which decides staying. Self-disclosure

Relationships deepen through self-disclosure, revealing personal information about oneself to another. Sidney Jourard (1926 to 1974), in The Transparent Self (1964), saw being fully known by someone as a mark of healthy personality; his Jourard Self-Disclosure Questionnaire asked how much people had told mother, father, friends and spouse about areas such as tastes, work, money and body. Irwin Altman and Dalmas Taylor's social penetration theory (1973) pictures the self as an onion: relationships grow in breadth (range of topics) and depth (intimacy), moving from outer layers to the core. Disclosure invites matching disclosure (disclosure reciprocity), and we like those who confide in us.

T. L. Morton (1978), followed by the textbook of Shelley Taylor, Letitia Anne Peplau and David Sears, separated descriptive self-disclosure, revealing facts and information about oneself (where you grew up), from evaluative self-disclosure, revealing one's personal feelings, opinions and judgements (that you feel lonely). Evaluative disclosure is more intimate and comes later.

Exam trap

IHBAS 2020 keyed "descriptive self-disclosure" for communicating one's feelings. By the standard definitions that is evaluative; descriptive disclosure is about facts. Learn the standard pair, and know that this paper's key reversed it. "Prescriptive" and "non-evaluative" self-disclosure are not standard types.

When relationships fail

Rusbult classified responses to dissatisfaction as active or passive and constructive or destructive: exit (active, destructive: leaving), voice (active, constructive: discussing problems), loyalty (passive, constructive: waiting faithfully) and neglect (passive, destructive: letting things decay). Steve Duck (1982) traced dissolution through four phases: intrapsychic (brooding privately on the partner's faults), dyadic (confronting the partner), social (telling friends and family) and grave-dressing (building an acceptable story of the break-up). John Gottman's "four horsemen" are taught with couple therapy in Chapter 7.

Loneliness

Peplau and Daniel Perlman (1982) defined loneliness as the unpleasant experience that occurs when a person's network of social relationships is deficient in quantity or quality. It is subjective: one can be lonely in a crowd. Robert Weiss (1973) separated two kinds. Emotional isolation (emotional loneliness) is the lack of a close, intimate attachment to one specific person, felt by a widow though friends surround her. Social isolation (social loneliness) is the lack of a wider network of friends and associates, felt by a newcomer to a city even in a happy marriage.

Exam trap

Emotional isolation means no deep attachment to one specific person; social isolation means too few friends or associates. Incompetence, inadequacy and low self-esteem are correlates of loneliness, not Weiss's types.

The UCLA Loneliness Scale of Daniel Russell, Peplau and colleagues (1978, revised 1980) has 20 statements such as "I lack companionship". Loneliness goes with depression, poorer health and shorter life (John Cacioppo and colleagues), and lonely people often benefit from social skills training (starting conversations, disclosing appropriately) and from challenging the negative expectations that keep them withdrawn.

3.7 Social Influence: Conformity, Compliance, Obedience and PowerLower yield · 18 Qs

Other people change what you do all day, often without a word. You join the queue at a chai stall, lend a friend your notes, fall silent when the invigilator orders it. This section follows influence from the unspoken norm through direct requests to outright commands, then turns to the roles and the bases of power behind them.

Kinds of social influence

Social influence is any effort by one or more people to change the behaviour, attitudes or feelings of others (persuasion, changing attitudes by messages, is in Section 3.4). Three forms differ in how directly pressure is applied (Table 3.18). Conformity is changing your behaviour or beliefs to match a group or its norms, although nobody has asked you to. Compliance is changing in response to a direct request, which you may accept or refuse. Obedience is changing in response to a command from an authority. Textbooks add symbolic social influence: absent people still steer us through our mental image of them.

Herbert Kelman (1958) graded how deep the change goes. In compliance (his sense) a person goes along outwardly to gain reward or avoid punishment, and only while watched. In identification he adopts the behaviour to be like an admired person or group. In internalisation he adopts it because it fits his own values, and it lasts. The same contrast appears as public compliance (going along outwardly while privately disagreeing) versus private acceptance (truly coming to believe what the group believes).

Exam trap

"Changing your behaviour to match others in a group" is conformity. Norming is a stage of Bruce Tuckman's group development (forming, storming, norming, performing; Section 3.10); standardisation means uniform test procedures (Chapter 2). Conformity is change under group pressure with nobody asking; compliance answers a direct request. Conformity is not "always involuntary", and neither term means obedience.

Social norms

Social norms are rules indicating how people are expected to behave in a situation. Explicit norms are written or stated ("No Parking"); implicit norms are unstated yet known to all (do not talk during a concert). Robert Cialdini, Raymond Reno and Carl Kallgren (1990) distinguished descriptive norms, what most people actually do, from injunctive norms, what is approved or disapproved, what ought to be done. In their littering study, people found a handbill on their car windscreen. More dropped it where the ground was already littered; fewest did where the ground was clean and a confederate conspicuously dropped one, because the lone litterbug highlighted the descriptive norm that nobody litters here. Making the injunctive norm salient (a passer-by picking up litter, in a follow-up) cut littering even in a littered setting. Their focus theory of normative conduct holds that a norm guides behaviour only when it is focal, salient at the moment of acting.

Prescriptive norms tell people what they ought to do (greet elders); proscriptive norms tell them what not to do (do not cheat). A perceived norm is a person's belief about what others do or approve, which may be wrong: students who overestimate their classmates' drinking drink more. Societies whose norms break down suffer anomie (Section 3.1).

Exam trap

Norms telling people how they ought to behave are prescriptive (injunctive) norms. "Idealistic norms" is not a standard type; descriptive norms describe what is done; perceived norms are beliefs about norms.

Sherif and Asch: the classic studies

How does a norm form? Muzafer Sherif (1935, 1936) used the autokinetic effect: in a dark room a stationary point of light seems to move, because there is no frame of reference. Alone, people gave widely different estimates of the movement. In small groups over several sessions their estimates converged on a shared group norm, which each person kept when later tested alone. In an ambiguous situation people used one another as information and privately accepted the answer. Robert Jacobs and Donald Campbell (1961) had confederates set an exaggerated norm, then replaced members one at a time; the arbitrary norm was handed down for several "generations" after the confederates had gone. Solomon Asch (1951, 1956) asked the harder question: will people conform when the answer is obvious?

Key study: Asch's line judgements (1951, 1956)

Method: one real participant sat among about seven confederates and said aloud, near the end of the row, which of three comparison lines matched a standard line (Figure 3.10). On 12 of 18 trials, the critical trials, every confederate gave the same wrong answer. Result: alone, people almost never erred (under 1 percent of judgements); in the group, conformity occurred on about 37 percent of critical trials. About three quarters (76 percent in Baron's account) conformed at least once; about a quarter never did. Conclusion: the answer was obvious, so people yielded to avoid standing out: normative influence towards an incorrect majority, mostly public compliance. Criticism: only male American students were tested, and such conformity has since declined.

Asch's variations show what drives the effect. Conformity rose with the size of the majority up to three or four, then levelled off. A single dissenting partner cut it sharply, even one giving a different wrong answer: unanimity is what matters. Written, private answers cut it steeply too. Richard Crutchfield (1955) mechanised the method: people in separate booths saw others' supposed answers as lights on a panel, so no confederates were needed. Rod Bond and Peter Smith's (1996) meta-analysis of 133 Asch-type studies in 17 countries found more conformity in collectivist cultures and a decline in the United States since the 1950s.

Exam trap

Asch tested the limits of conformity (not social perception, leadership or coercive power) and showed normative influence towards an incorrect majority: not obedience (nobody gave orders), the bystander effect or diffusion of responsibility (Section 3.8), or cognitive dissonance (Section 3.4). Private answers lowered conformity. Sherif's dot of light: ambiguity, informational influence, norm formation (not obedience or implicit bias, Section 3.5).

Why people conform

Morton Deutsch and Harold Gerard (1955) named two motives. Normative social influence rests on the desire to be liked and accepted; it produces public compliance and is strongest when a valued group is watching, as in Asch's study. Informational social influence rests on the desire to be correct; it produces private acceptance and is strongest when the situation is ambiguous or we lack expertise, as in Sherif's study.

Conformity rises with the group's cohesiveness (how much we are attracted to it and want to belong), its size up to a point, its unanimity, its members' status or expertise, public responding, a difficult or ambiguous task, and a collectivist culture. Gender differences are small: Alice Eagly and Linda Carli's (1981) review found women slightly more influenceable, mainly under face-to-face group pressure. Conformity keeps traffic and queues orderly, but its downside is that it can lead ordinary people into cruelty (see the power of roles).

Exam trap

Normative influence is the desire to be accepted and liked; informational influence, the desire to be correct. "Personal beliefs and values" describe internalisation; "power and authority" describe obedience and the bases of power.

Resisting the majority; minority influence

Conformity pressure can be resisted. C. R. Snyder and Howard Fromkin (1980) proposed a need for uniqueness; later studies found that people told they are "exactly average" resist the majority more. The related desire for individuation, to stand out as a distinct person, works the same way. People reminded of a time they held power over others conform less, and pressure that threatens freedom can provoke reactance (Section 3.4).

Can a minority sway a majority? Serge Moscovici, Elisabeth Lage and Martine Naffrechoux (1969) showed groups of four participants and two confederates blue slides. When the confederates consistently called every slide "green", about 8 percent of the majority's judgements became "green", and in a later private test participants labelled more blue-green discs green; an inconsistent minority had almost no effect. In his conversion theory (1980) Moscovici argued that majorities win quick public compliance, whereas a consistent minority makes people think and produces slower, genuine private conversion. Edwin Hollander (1958) explained safe dissent: by conforming early a member earns idiosyncrasy credits, goodwill he can later spend on deviation. The most basic influence may be emotional contagion, the automatic catching of another's mood, which Elaine Hatfield, John Cacioppo and Richard Rapson (1994) traced to our mimicry of others' faces and postures.

Cialdini worked undercover among "compliance professionals" (salespeople, fund-raisers, advertisers) and in Influence (1984) traced their tactics to six principles: reciprocity (we repay favours and concessions), commitment and consistency (we act in line with what we have agreed to), social proof or social validation (we copy similar others), liking or friendship, authority (we defer to real or apparent experts) and scarcity (we want what is rare or running out). Cults use these principles at full strength. Robert Jay Lifton (1961), studying Chinese Communist "thought reform", described such total environments, including milieu control (control of all communication). At Jonestown, Guyana (1978), more than 900 followers of Jim Jones died in a mass murder and suicide he ordered. Talking with recruits' families is exactly what cults avoid.

Obedience: Milgram's experiments

Would you shock an innocent stranger because a man in a laboratory coat told you to? Stanley Milgram (1933 to 1984) asked this partly to understand how ordinary people carried out the Holocaust.

Key study: Milgram's obedience study (1963)

Method: at Yale, 40 men aged 20 to 50 joined a supposed study of punishment and memory. A rigged draw made each the teacher; the learner, a likeable confederate, sat strapped in next door. For each error the teacher moved one switch up a generator from 15 to 450 volts in 15-volt steps, labelled up to "Danger: severe shock" and "XXX". No real shocks were given. At 300 volts the learner pounded on the wall, then fell silent. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter used four standard prods, from "Please continue" to "You have no other choice, you must go on." Result: psychiatrists had predicted that about one in a thousand would go to the end. All 40 reached 300 volts and 26 (65 percent) went to 450 volts, many sweating, trembling and protesting. Conclusion: most ordinary people obey a legitimate authority even to the point of apparently harming another person.

The power of roles

Key study: The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)

Method: Philip Zimbardo, with Craig Haney and Curtis Banks, randomly made 24 psychologically healthy male students guards or prisoners in a mock prison in Stanford's psychology basement. Prisoners were "arrested" at home and known only by numbers; guards wore uniforms and mirrored sunglasses. Result: the guards became abusive, and the prisoners, after a brief rebellion, passive and distressed. Planned for two weeks, the study was stopped after six days. Conclusion: social roles and situational forces can turn ordinary people cruel. Criticism: demand characteristics (guards may have acted as they thought was wanted), Zimbardo's double role as researcher and prison superintendent, and ethical harm.

In the BBC Prison Study (2006), Stephen Reicher and Alexander Haslam found that guards who did not identify with their role did not turn tyrannical, and prisoners who shared a group identity challenged them: roles shape behaviour when people identify with them. In The Lucifer Effect (2007) Zimbardo applied his analysis to American soldiers' abuse of Iraqi prisoners at Abu Ghraib.

Exam trap

"The power of roles" is Zimbardo's prison experiment, showing situational forces, not genetics, conditioning or reinforcement schedules (Chapter 1). 3.8 Prosocial Behaviour: Why and When People HelpLower yield · 15 Qs

In March 1964 a young woman was stabbed to death in New York while, the newspapers said, 38 neighbours did nothing. Was this proof that city people no longer cared? Two young psychologists suspected that the number of witnesses was itself the problem. Their answer, the bystander effect, is the heart of this section. First, though: why does anyone help at all?

Prosocial behaviour, helping and altruism

Any action that benefits others, such as sharing, comforting, donating or rescuing, is prosocial behaviour. Prosocial behaviour that intentionally benefits a particular person is helping. Defined by motive rather than act, altruism is helping with no expected reward, often at a cost to oneself, purely for the other's welfare. In cooperation, people work together towards a goal that benefits all of them, so nobody need be the giver. A student who lends her notes is helping; if she expects nothing back and loses revision time, she may be acting altruistically; if two friends split the syllabus and swap notes, they are cooperating.

Does true altruism exist? The doctrine of psychological egoism says no: every helpful act ultimately serves the helper, by winning praise, avoiding guilt or feeling good. This egoism versus altruism debate was fought hardest over empathy.

Empathy and the egoism versus altruism debate

The act of perceiving, understanding and responding to another person's emotional state and ideas is empathy. Its emotional side, affective empathy, is feeling with the person; cognitive empathy, or perspective taking, is accurately understanding what they think and feel; empathic concern is the warm, other-oriented compassion that follows. Mark H. Davis measured these with his Interpersonal Reactivity Index (1980), whose four scales are perspective taking, fantasy, empathic concern and personal distress (one's own anxiety at another's suffering). Feeling concern or pity for someone, rather than feeling with them, is sympathy. Empathy is a foundation of helping, and a core condition a counsellor offers a client (Chapter 7).

Exam trap

"Perceiving, understanding and responding to the emotional state and ideas of another person" is empathy, not sympathy. Transference (a client redirecting feelings about past figures onto the therapist) is a therapy term (Chapter 7); displacement (shifting an impulse onto a safer target) is a defence mechanism (Chapter 4).

C. Daniel Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis (1981; The Altruism Question, 1991) holds that empathic concern produces truly altruistic motivation, a wish to relieve the other's suffering for their sake, whereas personal distress produces only an egoistic wish to escape. To tell the two apart, Batson made escape easy.

Key study: Elaine and the shocks (Batson and colleagues 1981)

Women students watched "Elaine" react badly to electric shocks and were asked whether they would take her remaining shocks. Some were led to feel high empathy (Elaine shared their values). With easy escape they could leave after watching two of ten trials; with difficult escape they had to watch all ten. Low-empathy participants helped when escape was hard but mostly left when it was easy, as egoists would. High-empathy participants helped at high rates even when they could walk away. Criticism: they might still be avoiding guilt.

Robert Cialdini and colleagues replied with the egoistic negative-state relief model (1981, 1987): seeing suffering, or being sad for any reason, makes us feel bad, and we help to lift our own mood. Empathic people helped less when told that a drug had "fixed" their mood, so helping could not make them feel better. The empathic joy hypothesis (K. D. Smith, J. P. Keating and Ezra Stotland 1989) says we help for the pleasure of seeing the person relieved: empathic participants helped only if they expected to learn how their help had worked. Batson kept finding helping that these motives cannot explain, and most psychologists now accept that both altruistic and egoistic motives exist.

Genes, status and norms

How could a costly urge to help survive natural selection? William D. Hamilton's kin selection theory (1964) answers that helping a relative helps copies of your own genes, so helping is favoured when relatedness multiplied by the benefit exceeds the cost. Eugene Burnstein, Christian Crandall and Shinobu Kitayama (1994) found that people would help close kin more than distant kin or strangers, and young relatives more than old ones, most clearly when life was at stake. For non-relatives, Robert Trivers proposed reciprocal altruism (1971): helping pays when the favour is likely to be returned.

Two accounts are frankly strategic. According to competitive altruism (Charlie Hardy and Mark Van Vugt 2006), people help to gain status: players gave more when giving was public, and the most generous were preferred as partners. Helping an out-group to reduce its threat to one's in-group is defensive helping (Arie Nadler and colleagues 2009): students offered more help to a rival school said to outscore them, as if to keep it dependent.

Finally, norms. The norm of reciprocity (Alvin Gouldner 1960) says we should help those who helped us; the social responsibility norm (Leonard Berkowitz and L. R. Daniels 1963) says we should help those who depend on us, whether or not they can repay. A personal norm, one's own felt moral obligation to act, predicts helping better than vague social norms.

The bystander effect

In the early hours of 13 March 1964, Kitty Genovese (1935 to 1964), aged 28, was stabbed near her apartment building in Kew Gardens, Queens, New York. The New York Times reported that 38 neighbours had seen or heard the attack without calling the police. The figure was later shown to be exaggerated (few saw the attack clearly, and some did call or go to her), but the story shocked the country, and commentators blamed big-city apathy.

Bibb Latané (born 1937) and John Darley (1938 to 2018) suspected that the witnesses failed to act because there were so many of them. Their experiments (1968) and book The Unresponsive Bystander (1970) established the bystander effect: the more bystanders present at an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help, and the slower the help that comes. People are more likely to help when alone.

Key study: a seizure over the intercom (Darley and Latané 1968)

New York University students talked over an intercom from separate booths. One voice, a tape, began choking and begging for help as if having a seizure. Participants believed that only they, or they and one other, or they and four others could hear. Before the fit ended, 85 percent of those who thought they were alone left the booth to help, 62 percent with one other listener, and 31 percent with four others (Figure 3.11). Non-helpers were not apathetic; many were trembling. Criticism: a distressing deception.

In the smoke-filled room (Latané and Darley 1968), students filling in questionnaires saw smoke pour through a vent. Alone, 75 percent reported it; beside two passive confederates who kept writing, 10 percent did; in groups of three naive students, someone reported it in 38 percent of groups. In the lady in distress study (Latané and Judith Rodin 1969), a woman was heard falling next door: 70 percent of people alone went to help, but only 7 percent of those sitting with an unresponsive confederate.

Exam trap

The research followed the murder of Kitty Genovese, not Martin Luther King Jr. (assassinated 1968), Emmett Till (a Black teenager lynched in 1955) or Malala Yousafzai (shot by the Taliban in 2012). People help more when alone; seeing others not help makes intervention less likely. Groups did notice the smoke a little later, but the effect is about helping, not noticing.

Two obstacles carry the bystander effect. The first, diffusion of responsibility (step 3), is the spreading of responsibility across all the bystanders, so that each feels less personally obliged and assumes someone else will act. In the intercom study nobody could see anyone else, so diffusion alone explains the result. The second, pluralistic ignorance (step 2), occurs when bystanders use the behaviour of others to decide whether help is needed and mistakenly conclude that it is not. Among strangers, nobody knows for sure what is happening; each stays calm to avoid looking foolish, reads the others' calm as evidence that all is well, and the whole group hesitates and does nothing, as in the smoke-filled room. A third obstacle, audience inhibition (evaluation apprehension), is the fear of being judged for overreacting or helping clumsily.

Push a familiar scene through the model. A woman is beaten in a busy street while a crowd films it on phones and nobody calls for help. Each sees many others who could act (diffusion), sees nobody stepping in and half concludes it is a private quarrel (pluralistic ignorance), and fears looking foolish (audience inhibition). That is the bystander effect, not social loafing or group polarisation.

Exam trap

The two classic explanations are diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance; asked for one, the key is diffusion of responsibility. Pluralistic ignorance is not ignoring others or eyewitnesses, nor being suggestible about the cause: it is relying on others and wrongly deciding, from their calm, that help is unnecessary. "Someone else will do it" is diffusion; "nobody looks worried, so it must be nothing" is pluralistic ignorance.

Weighing costs: the arousal: cost-reward model

Why do bystanders sometimes help at once? In 1969 Irving Piliavin, Judith Rodin and Jane Allyn Piliavin staged collapses on New York subway trains. A man with a cane who seemed ill was helped in 95 percent of trials, usually within seconds, and larger groups did not help less; a man who smelled of alcohol and carried a bottle was helped in only about half. Their arousal: cost-reward model (developed fully in 1981) says that seeing someone in distress causes unpleasant arousal, which bystanders reduce by whichever response costs least. The costs of helping (effort, danger, embarrassment) are weighed against the costs of not helping (guilt, blame, the victim's suffering). The drunk man raised the costs of helping and, seeming responsible for his plight, lowered the guilt of walking away (Figure 3.12).

Situations that raise or lower helping

We help people who are similar to us, partly because similarity raises empathic concern. We help less when victims seem responsible for their plight: in Bernard Weiner's attribution analysis (Section 3.3), a controllable cause such as drinking invites anger, an uncontrollable one such as illness invites sympathy and help.

Key study: the Good Samaritan (Darley and Batson 1973)

Princeton Theological Seminary students, some preparing a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan, were told they had time to spare, were on time, or were already late. On the way they passed a man slumped and groaning in a doorway. Of those in no hurry, 63 percent helped; moderate hurry, 45 percent; great hurry, 10 percent. The topic of the talk made no reliable difference. Being in a hurry, not character, decided who stopped.

Stanley Milgram (1970) proposed urban overload: city dwellers face so much stimulation that they screen much of it out, people in need included, and helping is lower in big cities than in small towns.

Mood matters. Alice Isen and Paula Levin (1972) found that students given a cookie were more willing to help, and that people who had just found a dime in a public telephone were far more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers (about 88 against 4 percent). This feel good, do good effect fades if helping would spoil the mood. Guilt also raises helping, as people repair their self-image.

Goodness is contagious. Motorists stopped more often for a woman with a flat tyre if they had just passed another woman being helped (James Bryan and Mary Ann Test 1967): a prosocial model shows what to do and that helping is the norm. After playing a prosocial video game such as Lemmings, 57 percent helped pick up spilt pencils, against 33 percent after a neutral game (Tobias Greitemeyer and Silvia Osswald 2010). Watching an act of kindness produces elevation, an uplifted feeling described by Jonathan Haidt (2000) that makes the watcher want to help.

Gratitude raises future helping. Adam Grant and Francesca Gino (2010) had students comment on a stranger's job application letter; when he thanked them, about twice as many helped again. Being thanked raised the helpers' self-worth (feeling valued by others) and self-efficacy (feeling competent), but only self-worth carried the rise in helping.

Exam trap

Gratitude raises helping by enhancing helpers' self-worth and self-efficacy; pairs built on self-esteem or self-confidence are wrong. If asked which one did the work, it is self-worth.

Helping falls after social exclusion, which drains empathy (people told they would end up alone gave less to a student fund; Jean Twenge and colleagues 2007); in darkness and other conditions of anonymity, which remove the approval helping earns (Chen-Bo Zhong and colleagues 2010); and when people think of their time in money terms (Sanford DeVoe and Jeffrey Pfeffer 2010).

Who helps, and how help is received

The altruistic personality is a consistent tendency to help others across situations, rather than helping only when one feels like it, calculating costs and benefits, or being ready only in emergencies. Hans Werner Bierhoff, Renate Klein and Peter Kramp (1991) compared people who had given first aid at road accidents with people who had not, and found five components in the helpers: empathy, belief in a just world, social responsibility, internal locus of control and low egocentrism. Samuel Oliner and Pearl Oliner (1988) interviewed more than 400 rescuers of Jews in Nazi Europe: compared with non-rescuers, their parents had modelled caring and disciplined by reasoning, and they felt responsible for people beyond their own group. In volunteering, Allen Omoto and Mark Snyder (1995) found five motives among AIDS volunteers (values, understanding, personal development, community concern, esteem enhancement); the self-oriented ones best predicted who kept serving.

Help is not always welcome. The threat-to-self-esteem model (Nadler and Jeffrey Fisher 1986) holds that aid carries both support (someone cares) and threat (you are inferior and dependent). It threatens most when the helper is a similar peer, the task is central to one's self-image and the help cannot be repaid; then people resent help or avoid seeking it. Nadler also contrasted dependency-oriented help, which hands over the full solution, with autonomy-oriented help, which gives the tools to solve the problem oneself. The helper's perceived motive matters too: help that seems freely chosen and caring is welcomed; help that seems forced by a role or given for show is resented.

Are helping and aggression, then, opposites? Not quite. Their motives overlap (people help to gain status or keep rivals dependent), their acts can look alike (a surgeon's knife), and unwanted help can harm. Section 3.9 takes up aggression.

3.9 Aggression: Its Roots, Causes and ControlLower yield · 3 Qs

A batsman given out wrongly smashes his bat against the boundary board; a schoolgirl spreads a cruel rumour; a hired killer shoots a stranger without anger. All three are aggression, yet their roots differ. This section traces those roots, the triggers, and the methods that actually reduce aggression.

What counts as aggression

Robert A. Baron and Deborah R. Richardson (1994) define aggression as behaviour directed toward the goal of harming another living being who is motivated to avoid such treatment. So hateful thoughts alone are not aggression (it is behaviour); accidental harm is not, though a punch that misses is (it is intended); and kicking a door or a wanted surgical cut is not (the target must be living and unwilling). Anger is an emotion, aggression a behaviour: you can be furious and do nothing, and the hired killer aggresses without anger.

The main distinction is by goal. In hostile aggression (reactive or affective aggression) harm is the end in itself: hot, impulsive, a reply to provocation. In instrumental aggression (proactive aggression) harm is a means to another end, such as money or status: cold and planned. Table 3.24 adds the other cuts. The term relational aggression comes from Nicki R. Crick and Jennifer K. Instinct, evolution and biology

The oldest answer is that aggression is built in. Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) proposed in 1920 a death instinct, thanatos, opposing the life instinct (eros): a drive toward self-destruction that is turned outward as aggression (Chapter 4). The ethologist Konrad Lorenz (1903 to 1989), in On Aggression (1966), saw an inherited fighting instinct that spaced animals out and let the strongest males breed. His hydraulic model pictured aggressive energy building like water in a tank until a releasing stimulus, or eventually none at all, lets it out. Well-armed animals evolved ritualised fighting (stags shove with locked antlers rather than gore) and appeasement gestures (a beaten wolf bares its throat) that stop the winner's attack; weakly armed humans evolved weak inhibitions, and our weapons now kill beyond the reach of appeasement. Critics note that aggression varies too much across cultures to be one pressure-cooker drive. Both views fed the catharsis idea (3.9.8).

Evolutionary psychology recasts instinct as inherited potential: aggression that won mates and resources was passed on. It predicts that young men commit most violence, mostly against rival men (male competition), and that sexual jealousy is a leading cause of violence between partners.

The clearest chemical finding is that low serotonin activity, shown by low levels of its breakdown product 5-HIAA in cerebrospinal fluid, is linked to impulsive aggression; serotonin helps restrain impulses. Testosterone correlates modestly with dominance and aggression. Table 3.25 sets out the usual distractors. The amygdala fuels anger, the hypothalamus organises attack and the prefrontal cortex applies the brakes (Chapter 5). Genes act through environments: in the New Zealand cohort of Avshalom Caspi and colleagues (2002), maltreated boys became antisocial adults far more often if they carried the low-activity form of the MAOA gene, which codes the enzyme that breaks down serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine; without maltreatment the variant carried little risk.

Frustration-aggression and its reformulation

Social psychologists moved the drive's source outside the person. The frustration-aggression hypothesis, put forward at Yale in 1939 by John Dollard, Leonard W. Doob, Neal E. Miller, O. H. Mowrer and Robert R. Sears, claimed that frustration, the blocking of a goal (Section 1.10), always leads to aggression, and that aggression always comes from frustration. Neither holds: frustration often brings sadness or problem solving, and the hired killer is not frustrated. Miller (1941) soon withdrew the first claim: aggression is only one of several responses to frustration. The group also explained the target. When the frustrator is powerful or absent, aggression shifts to a safer target (displacement: scolded by his boss, a clerk shouts at his children); when that target is a weak minority blamed for the trouble, displacement becomes scapegoating (Section 3.5).

Leonard Berkowitz (1926 to 2016) reformulated the hypothesis in 1989, in what became his cognitive neoassociationist model. Frustration causes aggression only to the extent that it produces negative affect, so unexpected or unfair frustrations provoke most. And any aversive event (pain, heat, foul smells, noise) does the same. Negative affect automatically primes both fight (rudimentary anger) and flight (rudimentary fear); the situation, past learning and later appraisal decide which wins (Figure 3.13). Stimuli associated with violence act as aggressive cues, tipping an angry person toward fight.

Key study: The weapons effect (Berkowitz and LePage, 1967)

In the study by Berkowitz and Anthony LePage, male students, angered or not by a partner's electric shocks, could shock him back while a shotgun and a revolver, or badminton racquets, or nothing lay nearby. Angered men beside the guns gave the most shocks. "The finger pulls the trigger, but the trigger may also be pulling the finger." Some replications failed, but meta-analyses support a modest weapons effect.

Arousal from elsewhere also feeds aggression. In Dolf Zillmann's excitation transfer theory (1971), arousal fades slowly, so arousal left over from one event (a near miss on the Mysuru ring road, a gym session) is misattributed to the next, and a minor annoyance produces rage, especially when the leftover arousal goes unnoticed.

Exam trap

The reformulation is Berkowitz's (1989), not Dollard's (the 1939 original), Miller's (the 1941 softening) or Lazarus's (Richard Lazarus: appraisal and stress, Section 1.11). Weapons effect: Berkowitz and LePage; leftover arousal: Zillmann.

Learning, scripts and the General Aggression Model

Learning theories ask where the particular ways of aggressing come from. Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) answered: largely from watching others.

Key study: The Bobo doll experiments (1961 to 1965)

Bandura, Dorothea Ross and Sheila A. Ross (1961) let 72 nursery-school children watch an adult punch, kick and shout at an inflatable Bobo doll, or play quietly. Left with the doll, those who had seen aggression copied the model's specific acts and words, unrewarded. Filmed and cartoon models worked almost as well (1963). In 1965, children who saw the model punished (vicarious punishment) imitated less than those who saw it rewarded (vicarious reinforcement), yet a reward for reproducing the acts made all groups equal: consequences to the model affect performance, not learning. Criticism. Bobo is built to be hit.

This is observational learning through modelling, with no direct reinforcement of the learner (Bandura's four processes are in Section 1.6).

Exam trap

Bobo doll = observational learning of aggression through modelling. Not operant conditioning (no child was reinforced for hitting), not classical conditioning of fear (Little Albert, Section 1.6), not sensory deprivation (the McGill isolation studies, Section 1.5).

Repeated observation builds aggressive scripts (L. Rowell Huesmann): stored programmes for how a conflict unfolds and how to respond ("if disrespected, hit back"), retrieved fast when provoked. The General Aggression Model of Craig A. Anderson and Brad J. Bushman (2002) integrates these theories (Figure 3.14): person and situation inputs shape the present internal state (affect, cognition, arousal); a quick appraisal yields impulsive action, a considered one thoughtful action; and each outcome feeds back, making the next aggressive episode easier.

Heat, alcohol and other aversive conditions

Aversive conditions act as Berkowitz predicted. The hot temperature effect is real: across 50 US cities from 1950 to 1995, hotter years brought more murders and assaults but not more property crime (Anderson and colleagues). Yet in Baron's laboratory, very hot, angry participants became less aggressive. The negative affect escape model of Baron and Paul A. Bell explains this: discomfort raises aggression up to a moderate level, beyond which escape takes priority, an inverted U. Alcohol raises aggression in both sexes. In the alcohol myopia account of Claude M. Steele and Robert A. Josephs (1990), alcohol narrows attention to the salient cue (the insult) and pushes out distant ones (consequences, norms). Crowding, uncontrollable noise and pain act likewise, through negative affect.

Bullying, cyberbullying and workplace aggression

Dan Olweus, pioneer of school research in Norway (Bullying at School, 1993), defined bullying as intentional, repeated aggression against a victim with less power. He distinguished bullies, victims (often anxious and friendless) and bully-victims (provocative victims who also bully, and fare worst); his whole-school programme of supervision, clear rules and consistent action roughly halved bullying. Online, cyberbullying follows victims home and reaches vast audiences; anonymity and the unseen victim produce online disinhibition, a cousin of deindividuation (Section 3.10). Workplace aggression is mostly verbal, indirect and covert, and perceived injustice often triggers it.

Preventing and controlling aggression

Punishment deters only when it is prompt, certain, strong and seen as justified, conditions courts rarely meet; unfair punishment acts as fresh provocation, and physical punishment models aggression. The catharsis hypothesis, rooted in Freud, adopted by Dollard's group (1939) and revived by Lorenz, holds that venting anger safely (punching a pillow, shouting) drains the drive and reduces later aggression. It fails.

Key study: Venting feeds the flame (Bushman, 2002)

Bushman angered about 600 students with harsh feedback on their essays. One group then hit a punching bag while thinking about the critic, a second while thinking about fitness, and a third sat quietly. When all could blast the critic with noise, the rumination group was angriest and most aggressive; doing nothing produced the least aggression.

What works? Cognitive interventions: a sincere apology; pre-attribution, a warning that someone is ill or stressed, so later rudeness is blamed on circumstances; and a delay that lets arousal fade. Exposure to non-aggressive models who stay calm under provocation. Social skills training in negotiating and complaining without attack. Baron's incompatible response technique, which induces empathy or humour, feelings that cannot coexist with anger. Strengthened self-regulation, which fatigue, stress and alcohol deplete (Baumeister). And forgiveness, which ends the cycle of revenge.

Exam trap

Which method is not supported? Catharsis: venting raises aggression. "Severe punishment always deters" is also false.

Your family, your entrance batch and your cricket team all shape how hard you try, what you decide and whom you follow. What turns people into a group, how do groups grow, how do others change our effort, why do groups decide badly, and what makes a leader effective?

What makes a group

A group is two or more people who interact, depend on one another for their goals, see themselves as belonging together and share a structure of roles and norms. People at a bus stop share a place, not a group. Entitativity, Donald T. Group structure and membership

Every lasting group develops a structure with four parts. Roles are behaviours expected of a position. Kenneth Benne and Paul Sheats (1948) distinguished task roles that move the work on (initiator, information seeker, coordinator, evaluator-critic), maintenance roles or socio-emotional roles that hold members together (encourager, harmoniser, compromiser, gatekeeper) and self-oriented roles that serve the individual at the group's cost (aggressor, blocker, recognition seeker, dominator). Role differentiation follows as members specialise, often one leading the task while another keeps the peace. Status is rank within the group; high-status members talk more and may deviate more. Norms are the group's rules, often unspoken. Cohesiveness is the sum of forces holding members in: liking, commitment to the task and pride. Cohesive groups demand more conformity (Section 3.7) and satisfy members more, but perform better only when their norms favour effort, and very high cohesion feeds groupthink.

People join for belonging, goals, self-knowledge, status and identity (Section 3.5), and pay in freedom, time and effort. A high price even raises a group's value: women who passed an embarrassing initiation liked a dull group more (Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills, 1959), a dissonance effect (Section 3.4).

Richard Moreland and John Levine (1982) described group socialisation as five phases in which group and person evaluate each other: investigation (prospective member), socialisation (new member), maintenance (full member), resocialisation (marginal member, as commitment falls) and remembrance (ex-member). Entry, acceptance, divergence and exit mark the transitions. Sometimes the whole group breaks. A schism is the splintering of a group into distinct factions. Fabio Sani (2005) found that clergy who left the Church of England after it first ordained women in 1994 felt the change had subverted its identity, and that their protests would go unheard.

Exam trap

Splintering into factions is a schism. A chapter is a local branch, division an everyday word and deviation one member breaking a norm.

Sociometry: mapping who chooses whom

A hostel's room list shows its official structure; how would you find the real one? Jacob Levy Moreno (1889 to 1974) answered with sociometry, a method of measuring an individual's social behaviour and relations in a group from the attractions and rejections among its members. He set it out in Who Shall Survive? (1934), with Helen Hall Jennings, after regrouping a girls' training school by the residents' own choices.

In the sociometric test each member privately names whom he or she would choose, and sometimes reject, for working, living or playing together. Answers fill a sociometric matrix, choosers down the side and the chosen across the top; a column total divided by the number of other members (N minus 1) is a sociometric index of choice status. Drawn out, the matrix becomes a sociogram, a diagram of choices and rejections that shows attraction, social distance and patterns of choice within a group (Figure 3.15). A star receives many choices, an isolate none; a neglectee receives few but is not rejected; a rejectee draws rejections. A mutual pair choose each other, a chain runs A to B to C, and a clique chooses within itself. The sociogram thus captures implicit and explicit communication and interaction patterns; teachers use it to find isolated children. Moreno also founded psychodrama (Chapter 7).

Exam trap

Sociometry, not the sample method (Chapter 9), the interview method or the Verstehen method (interpretive understanding of social action, Wilhelm Dilthey, Max Weber), measures social behaviour in a group. A sociogram does not measure IQ, a leader's personality or economic status. A genogram is a three-generation family tree (Chapter 7), an ecomap a family's links with its environment (Section 3.11), a cognitive map Tolman's mental map (Section 1.6).

How groups develop

In 1965 Bruce Tuckman reviewed studies of therapy, training and laboratory groups and proposed four stages; with Mary Ann Jensen in 1977 he added a fifth (Figure 3.16). In forming, members orient themselves, are polite and unsure, and depend on the leader. In storming, conflict and resistance surface as members struggle over leadership, roles and the group's definition. In norming, cohesion grows, norms and roles are agreed, and the group clarifies its purpose and strategy for reaching its goals. In performing, the work gets done. In adjourning, the group disbands, often with a sense of loss.

Real groups do not always march in step. Connie Gersick (1988) found that project teams with deadlines show punctuated equilibrium: inertia, a burst of change near the midpoint of their time, then focused work. The Boston model used in social group work is in Section 3.11.

Exam trap

Proximity is not a stage of group formation; it is a cause of attraction (Section 3.6). "Clarified its purpose and strategy" is norming; "struggle over leadership and group definition" is storming. The order is forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning.

Performing in front of others

Does an audience help you or hurt you? The question is as old as experimental social psychology.

Key study: Triplett's fishing reels (1898)

Norman Triplett noticed in cycling records that riders were faster when paced or racing a rival than when riding alone against the clock. He then had children wind a fishing reel as fast as possible, alone or beside another child doing the same; many wound faster in company, though some did worse. Published in 1898, it is often called the first social psychology experiment. Criticism: the gains were small and uneven.

Floyd Henry Allport named the effect social facilitation (1920, 1924), better performance when others are present, and called working side by side on the same task coaction. Yet others sometimes made people worse, called social inhibition or impairment. Robert Zajonc resolved the puzzle in 1965 with drive theory: the mere presence of others raises arousal, and arousal strengthens the dominant response, the one most likely in that situation. On a simple or well-learned task the dominant response is correct, so performance improves; on a complex or new task it is likely wrong, so performance worsens (Figure 3.17).

Push a case through it. Tim has trained his voice for years but has hardly danced, and his school musical needs both. The audience raises his arousal: his singing improves and his dancing deteriorates. An audience helps most on easy, well-learned tasks, not novel, difficult, creative or first-time ones. Zajonc, Alexander Heingartner and Edward Herman (1969) found it even in cockroaches: watched by other roaches, they ran a straight runway faster but a maze more slowly.

Two rivals refine the account. In evaluation apprehension (Nickolas Cottrell and colleagues, 1968), concern about being judged does the arousing: a blindfolded audience produced no facilitation. In distraction-conflict theory (Robert S. Baron, 1986), attending to both task and onlookers creates conflict that arouses and narrows attention. The arousal-performance curve is the Yerkes-Dodson law (Section 1.10).

Exam trap

Triplett used fishing reels. Rope pulling is Ringelmann, the shock generator Milgram (Section 3.7), a maze with food reward animal learning (Section 1.6). Diffusion of responsibility explains bystanders not helping (Section 3.8).

Social loafing and deindividuation

Now pull a rope with seven others: nobody can tell how hard you pull. The French engineer Max Ringelmann found that the bigger the team, the less each member pulled (published 1913). Part of this Ringelmann effect is coordination loss, but Alan Ingham and colleagues (1974) showed that blindfolded people who merely believed others were pulling also pulled less: motivation falls too.

That loss is social loafing: less effort when individual efforts are pooled than when working alone. Bibb Latané, Kipling Williams and Stephen Harkins (1979) had blindfolded students in headphones clap and shout alone or in supposed groups of two, four or six: each person's output fell as the group grew. Steven Karau and Williams's collective effort model (1993) explains why: people work hard only when they expect their effort to matter for an outcome they value. Loafing therefore falls when each contribution is identifiable and evaluated, the task is challenging, appealing or involving, members are friends or committed, effort is believed to bring valued rewards, and the group is small. A teacher such as Miss Pooja should grade both the group and each member; group grades alone, random assignment or optional presentations leave individual effort hidden.

Exam trap

"Groups loaf less when evaluation apprehension is absent" is false: removing the fear of judgement lets people loaf. Social loafing's other name is the Ringelmann effect; the free-rider effect is a related but separate term (Table 3.28). Group decision making

Do several heads beat one? James H. Davis (1973) showed that group decisions follow social decision schemes: majority wins on opinions, truth wins when one member can demonstrate a correct answer, and first shift, where the group follows the first member to change position, as in deadlocked juries.

Groups are often more extreme, not more moderate. In 1961 James Stoner found that groups recommended riskier courses than their members had alone, the risky shift, but later studies found that groups that began cautious became more cautious. Serge Moscovici and Marisa Zavalloni (1969) showed the general rule: French students' liking for de Gaulle and dislike of Americans both grew after discussion. This is group polarisation: discussion pushes members towards a more extreme version of their initial leaning, through persuasive arguments (more arguments heard for the favoured side), social comparison (wanting to look better than average on the valued side) and social identity (converging on the group's prototype).

Exam trap

More extreme group decisions are group polarisation. "Extremity" is not a technical term, groupthink is cohesive suppression of dissent, and conformity is yielding to a norm (Section 3.7).

Irving Janis, in Victims of Groupthink (1972; revised 1982), analysed fiascoes such as Kennedy's Bay of Pigs invasion (1961) and the ignored warnings before Pearl Harbor (1941); later analysts added the 1986 Challenger launch. Groupthink is a mode of thinking in highly cohesive groups in which the drive for unanimity overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives: collective decision making that discourages dissent and leads to poor decisions. Antecedents are high cohesion, insulation, a directive leader, high stress and no methodical procedures. The eight symptoms are an illusion of invulnerability (excess optimism and risk), collective rationalisation (warnings explained away), belief in the group's morality, stereotyped views of out-groups, direct pressure on dissenters, self-censorship of doubts, an illusion of unanimity (silence read as agreement) and self-appointed mindguards who screen out contrary information. Remedies: the leader stays impartial and withholds his or her view early, appoints a devil's advocate or a subgroup to critique the plan, invites outside experts, uses subgroups and holds a second-chance meeting.

Exam trap

To curb groupthink, the leader should ask a subset of the group to critique the plan. Team-building raises cohesion, stating his view first steers the group, and forbidding argument silences dissent: all make it worse. Groupthink is not individual thinking, brainstorming or mere peer pressure, and it yields poor decisions.

Groups also waste what members know. Garold Stasser and William Titus (1985) spread facts so that the best student-body candidate stood out only if members pooled unshared facts, a hidden profile; groups discussed mainly shared facts (biased information sampling) and chose worse. In brainstorming, popularised by Alex Osborn, members produce many ideas with criticism postponed, yet as many people working alone produce more, because of production blocking: one person speaks at a time and ideas fade while waiting. The nominal group technique has members write ideas silently, share them in turn and vote privately; the Delphi technique, from the RAND Corporation, polls experts anonymously over rounds with feedback until opinion converges. Escalation of commitment is continuing to invest in a failing course to justify past costs.

Cooperation, conflict and fairness

In a social dilemma, each person gains by acting selfishly but all lose if everyone does. In the prisoner's dilemma, two suspects questioned apart each do best by betraying the other, yet both do worse if both betray than if both stay silent. Garrett Hardin's tragedy of the commons (1968) is the many-person version: each herder adds cows to the shared pasture until it is ruined, as with groundwater or fish stocks today. Communication, trust, small groups and sanctions raise cooperation.

Conflict yields to bargaining, best ending in an integrative agreement that gives both sides more than a split by trading on issues each values differently; to superordinate goals that neither side can reach alone (Sherif's Robbers Cave, Section 3.5); and to Charles Osgood's GRIT (graduated and reciprocated initiatives in tension reduction, 1962), small announced concessions that invite reciprocation. Conflict is often about fairness: distributive justice is fair outcomes, judged in J. Stacy Adams's equity theory (1965) by comparing our outcome-to-input ratio with others'; procedural justice is fair procedures; transactional or interactional justice is courteous treatment and honest explanation.

Leadership

Leadership is influencing group members towards shared goals. The great-person theory held that leaders are born with special traits, but Ralph Stogdill's 1948 review found traits only weakly linked to leadership: a leader in one situation may not lead in another. Attention turned to what leaders do.

Key study: Lewin, Lippitt and White (1939)

Kurt Lewin, Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White had adults lead clubs of ten-year-old boys in three styles. Under autocratic leaders, who made every decision, boys worked while watched but stopped when the leader left, and grew hostile or apathetic. Under democratic leaders, who guided group decisions, work was more original, continued without the leader, and the boys liked this style best. Under laissez-faire leaders, who left the boys alone, little was done. Criticism: small American samples.

The Ohio State studies, using the Leader Behaviour Description Questionnaire (LBDQ), found two independent dimensions: initiating structure (organising work and roles) and consideration (warmth and concern for subordinates); one leader can be high on both. The Michigan studies contrasted job-centred with employee-centred supervisors. Robert Blake and Jane Mouton's managerial grid (1964) crosses concern for production with concern for people, from impoverished (1,1), country club (1,9), authority-compliance (9,1) and middle of the road (5,5) to the ideal, team management (9,9).

Contingency theories match leader to situation. In Fred Fiedler's contingency model (1967), leaders rate their least preferred co-worker (LPC): rating even that person kindly (high LPC) marks a relationship-oriented leader, rating harshly (low LPC) a task-oriented one. Situational favourableness comes from three dimensions: leader-member relations, task structure and position power (the leader's formal power and authority to reward and punish); good relations count most. Task-oriented leaders do best in very favourable or very unfavourable situations, relationship-oriented leaders in between; since style is fixed, Fiedler advised changing the situation. Kurt Lewin wanted social psychology to change lives, through action research that studies a problem while trying to solve it (Section 3.1). Health, environment, work and happiness

Why does the same stress make one person ill and leave another well? Partly because of other people. Social support is the perception or experience of being loved, cared for and part of a network of mutual help. Sheldon Cohen and Thomas Ashby Wills (1985) contrasted two models. In the main-effect model, social integration benefits health at any level of stress. In the stress-buffering hypothesis, support protects chiefly under high stress, by making the stressor seem less threatening and by softening the reaction to it. Loneliness is in Section 3.6, and health behaviour models in Chapter 7.

Places matter too (personal space is in Section 3.3). Daniel Stokols (1972) separated density, a physical count of people per unit of space, from crowding, the unpleasant feeling that there are too many people, with lost control and privacy: a festival is dense yet enjoyable, while a lift with three strangers feels crowded. In 1962 John B. Calhoun let rats breed with ample food but limited space. Their society collapsed into a behavioural sink of neglected young, soaring infant mortality, aggression and withdrawal. In people, density harms mainly when it removes control. Irwin Altman (1975) described territoriality, the marking and defending of places: primary territories are owned and central to life (a home), secondary territories are used regularly but shared (a canteen table) and public territories are open to anyone for a while (a park bench). Noise, likewise, harms most when it is unpredictable and uncontrollable.

At work, social psychology informs motivation (Herzberg, Section 1.10), the Hawthorne effect, in which people change their behaviour because they know they are being studied (Chapter 9), and leadership (Section 3.10). Ed Diener (1946 to 2021) defined subjective well-being as a person's own evaluation of life: high life satisfaction, frequent positive feelings and infrequent negative ones.

Eyewitness testimony

Why do defence lawyers so often protest the use of eyewitness testimony? Witnesses are not especially forgetful, and poor eyesight is rarely the issue; the trouble is that a memory can be vivid, confident and false. Source monitoring is the process of judging where a memory came from: seen, heard about or imagined (Marcia Johnson and colleagues, 1993). A source monitoring error attaches a genuine memory to the wrong origin, so a face seen in a mugshot album, on the news or as an innocent bystander is taken for the culprit's. In one celebrated case a rape victim identified the psychologist Donald Thomson, who had been on the live television programme she was watching when she was attacked: his alibi was the source of her memory. Misleading questions act the same way. In the 'smashed' versus 'hit' study of Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer (1974; Section 1.7), the wording of a question changed what witnesses remembered, down to broken glass that was never there.

A confident witness is not usually right: across studies, confidence predicts accuracy only weakly, and it grows after the event. In the post-identification feedback effect (Gary Wells and colleagues, 1998), witnesses told 'Good, you identified the suspect' later claimed a better view and more certainty, even when they had picked an innocent person. Some sources of error the legal system cannot control. In weapon focus a visible weapon draws attention away from the face; in own-race bias people recognise faces of their own race more accurately than faces of other races; and high stress during the crime usually impairs identification. Other sources it can control, chiefly the lineup (Table 3.30).

Juries, profiling and rehabilitation

In the story model of Nancy Pennington and Reid Hastie (1986), jurors faced with conflicting evidence build a narrative of what happened from the evidence and their own knowledge of how such events go, then choose the verdict that fits the most coherent story; evidence presented in story order persuades best. Pretrial publicity against the accused raises guilty verdicts, and instructions to ignore it help little. Attractive defendants are more often acquitted and given lighter sentences, unless their looks aided the crime, as in a swindle. Stereotypes about race, sex and class bias verdicts (Section 3.5), though careful deliberation weakens them.

Offender profiling infers the characteristics of an unknown offender from the crime. FBI agents such as Robert Ressler and John Douglas interviewed convicted murderers and built a top-down typology. Organised offenders plan, control the scene, bring and remove a weapon and are socially competent; disorganised offenders act on impulse, leave a chaotic scene and a weapon of opportunity, and are socially inadequate. David Canter's investigative psychology works bottom-up, from statistical patterns across many solved cases; his 1986 profile helped catch England's 'railway rapist'. Support for the organised-disorganised split is weak.

Profiling and the cognitive interview help to catch offenders; rehabilitation tries to change them. After Robert Martinson's 'nothing works' (Key study), meta-analyses from the late 1980s built the 'what works' evidence. Successful programmes are community-based rather than institutional, keeping offenders tied to work, family and prosocial peers; they are cognitive-behavioural, structured and multimodal. They follow the risk-need-responsivity (RNR) principles of Don Andrews and James Bonta (1990): match treatment intensity to the risk of reoffending (risk); target criminogenic needs, the changeable causes of crime such as antisocial attitudes, antisocial peers and substance misuse (need); and teach in the way the offender learns best (responsivity). Punishment alone does not reduce reoffending.

Key study: Martinson and 'nothing works' (1974)

Question: does rehabilitation reduce reoffending? Method: Martinson and colleagues reviewed 231 controlled evaluations of correctional programmes. Result: 'with few and isolated exceptions', rehabilitation had had no appreciable effect on recidivism; the press shortened this to 'nothing works', and policy turned to punishment. Criticism: many programmes were weak or badly run, and Martinson himself withdrew the strong claim in 1979.

Exam trap

A quality of successful offender rehabilitation is being community-based. Cognitive interviews and offender profiling are investigative tools, not treatment, and a focus on the relationship with parents is not a defining quality.

Social work: definition, roots and methods

The 2014 global definition of the International Federation of Social Workers (IFSW) and the International Association of Schools of Social Work (IASSW) calls social work a practice-based profession and academic discipline that promotes social change and development, social cohesion, and the empowerment and liberation of people. Its lens is the person-in-environment: problems arise between people and their families, neighbourhoods and institutions, so help may target either side.

It has two roots. The Charity Organisation Society (London, 1869) sent 'friendly visitors' to investigate applicants and coordinate relief, the seed of case work. The settlement houses placed educated volunteers among the poor to press for reform: Toynbee Hall (London, 1884, under Samuel Barnett) and Hull House (Chicago, 1889, founded by Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr), the seed of group work and community organisation.

The primary methods, or direct methods, are social case work, social group work and community organisation; the secondary or auxiliary methods are social welfare administration, social action and social work research. Direct practice, face-to-face work with clients, has historically been conceptualised as case work and case management, as individual psychotherapy (clinical social work) and as generalist practice. Policy practice, shaping laws and agency rules, is indirect or macro practice.

India's first school of social work, and Asia's, was the Sir Dorabji Tata Graduate School of Social Work, opened in Bombay in 1936 under the American missionary Clifford Manshardt; in 1944 it was renamed the Tata Institute of Social Sciences. The Delhi School of Social Work followed in 1946, and psychiatric social work training grew up at Ranchi and at NIMHANS, Bengaluru.

Exam trap

'Tata Institute of Social Profession' and 'Tata Institute of Social Service' are invented, and the Tata Institute of Social Sciences is the 1944 name; the first school was the Sir Dorabji Tata Graduate School of Social Work. Policy practice is not a form of direct practice.

Social case work

Social case work, the oldest method, helps one person or family at a time with problems of social functioning, through a professional relationship and community resources. Its founder, Mary Ellen Richmond (1861 to 1928), gave it a method in Social Diagnosis (1917), which taught workers to gather evidence systematically before judging, and defined it in What Is Social Case Work? (1922) as the processes that develop personality through adjustments consciously effected, individual by individual, between people and their social environment.

The process runs in phases (Figure 3.18): intake (can this agency help?), study (gathering the facts), assessment or social diagnosis (defining the problem and its causes), intervention or treatment, evaluation, and termination with follow-up. Content analysis, the coding of themes in texts, is a research technique, not a phase. The worker's techniques are interviewing (the central one), observation, counselling, supportive techniques (reassurance, encouragement), home visits, collateral contacts (speaking, with consent, to relatives, teachers or employers), environmental modification (changing the client's surroundings, such as arranging a pension) and referral. Lobbying legislators belongs to social action, not case work.

By the 1930s case work had split into the diagnostic school, built on Freud, and the functional school, built on Otto Rank's will therapy (Table 3.31). Florence Hollis developed the diagnostic school into the psychosocial approach, and Charlotte Towle's Common Human Needs (1945) taught that every welfare applicant has the same basic needs as anyone else.

Social group work

Social group work uses the group itself as a helper: members gain from one another as well as from the worker. Grace Longwell Coyle (1892 to 1962), whose Social Process in Organized Groups appeared in 1930, established group work as a method within social work. Gisela Konopka (1910 to 2003) later carried it into treatment settings such as institutions and child guidance clinics. Harleigh B. Trecker listed ten principles: planned group formation, specific objectives, a purposeful worker-group relationship, continuous individualisation, guided group interaction, democratic group self-determination, flexible functional organisation, progressive programme experiences, resource utilisation and evaluation.

Three models dominate. The social goals model, from the settlements, builds social consciousness and responsible citizenship. The remedial model of Robert Vinter uses the group to treat individual members, with the worker as change agent. The reciprocal, or mediating, model of William Schwartz makes the group a mutual-aid system, with the worker mediating between members and between the group and society.

Ronald Toseland and Robert Rivas distinguish formed groups, deliberately convened by a worker or agency for an identified purpose, usually with a professionally trained leader and an agency auspice (a support group for caregivers), from natural groups, which come together spontaneously out of friendship, shared interest or circumstance (a circle of friends, a street gang). All groups share certain properties: a purpose, stages of development and criteria for their composition, along with structure, norms, roles and cohesion (Section 3.10). No group needs an 'analytic base'.

Some members serve the group in hidden ways. Lawrence Shulman described the scapegoat, blamed for what the group dislikes in itself; the deviant member, whose odd behaviour often voices what others feel; the gatekeeper, who blocks or diverts the group, by joking or changing the subject, whenever talk nears a painful topic; the internal leader, who takes initiative from within; the defensive member, who denies having a problem; the quiet member; and the monopoliser, who dominates the talk. Groups also change over time. The Boston model of James Garland, Hubert Jones and Ralph Kolodny (1965) has five stages: pre-affiliation (approach and avoidance), power and control, intimacy, differentiation and separation. Tuckman's forming to adjourning sequence is in Section 3.10.

Culturally sensitive practice and child welfare

Cultures differ in measurable ways. Surveying IBM employees in 40 countries, Geert Hofstede (1980) found four dimensions: power distance (acceptance of unequal power), individualism-collectivism (a self defined as an independent 'I' or by the 'we' of family and group), masculinity-femininity (competition and achievement versus care) and uncertainty avoidance (discomfort with ambiguity, eased by rules). Long-term orientation and indulgence-restraint were added later. Collectivism brings more conformity (Section 3.7), a weaker fundamental attribution error (Section 3.3) and social striving (Section 3.10).

Ethnic-sensitive practice (Wynetta Devore and Elfriede Schlesinger, 1981) uses descriptive knowledge specific to different racial, cultural and ethnic groups in every phase of practice, so that the worker grasps the client's 'ethnic reality', the outlook shaped jointly by ethnic group and social class. Three neighbouring terms differ: ethno-psychotherapy (Georges Devereux) joins psychoanalysis and anthropology to treat clients from other cultures; sensitivity training is the T-group method (Section 3.1); and matching assigns a worker of the client's own ethnicity. Cultural competence (Derald Wing Sue and colleagues) combines awareness of one's own biases, knowledge of the client's worldview and culturally appropriate skills.

How does a worker learn a world not his own? The ethnographic interview of the anthropologist James Spradley (1979), adapted for social work by James Leigh (1998), treats the client as a cultural guide who explains what things mean in their world: 'Can you tell me why this band is so popular among your age group?' A psychodynamic inquiry ('What does that remind you of from childhood?') seeks unconscious conflict; a task-focused inquiry ('What could you do before next week?') seeks concrete steps; a strengths-based inquiry ('How have you coped so far?'), from the strengths perspective of Dennis Saleebey (1992), seeks resources rather than deficits. The ecomap of Ann Hartman (1978) draws the family in a circle linked to school, work, services and friends by lines marking strong, weak or stressful ties; the genogram charts three or more generations of a family.

Exam trap

Descriptive knowledge specific to racial, cultural and ethnic groups is ethnic-sensitive practice, not ethno-psychotherapy, sensitivity training or matching. Asking a client to explain what something means in their world is ethnographic inquiry.

Child maltreatment takes four forms: physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional abuse and neglect, the failure to provide for a child's basic needs. Neglect has several categories: physical neglect (inadequate food, housing, clothing or hygiene); health or medical neglect (failing to immunise, to seek medical care or to follow medical directives); inadequate supervision (children left unattended); educational neglect; and emotional neglect. Using licensed day care is responsible substitute care, not neglect. Sexual offences against children in India fall under the POCSO Act 2012 (Chapter 7).

Chapter summary

3.1 Social Psychology: History, Theories, Concepts and Methods. Social psychology studies how individuals' thoughts, feelings and behaviour are influenced by the actual, imagined or implied presence of others (Gordon Allport, 1954). It grew from Le Bon's crowd (1895), Triplett's first experiment (1898) and the 1908 texts of McDougall and Ross; Kurt Lewin, its modern father, added the life space (person plus psychological environment), group dynamics and action research. A crisis around 1970, European work and the social cognition era followed. From sociology came Durkheim's anomie (normlessness), symbolic interactionism, socialisation and resocialisation, status and role. Its theories include Moscovici's social representations (1961); its methods run from observation to experiment, with debriefing after deception.

3.2 Social Cognition and First Impressions. Social cognition is how we interpret, remember and use social information. As cognitive misers (Fiske and Taylor, 1984) we lean on automatic processing, schemas (scripts, self-schemas, stereotypes), priming and heuristics. Schemas persist (belief perseverance) and can fulfil themselves: Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy (1948), the Pygmalion effect (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968) and the Golem effect. Social thought tilts towards negativity, optimism, the planning fallacy, counterfactuals, magical thinking, false consensus and the illusion of control. Asch's central traits (warm-cold) and the halo effect (Thorndike, 1920) shape impressions; thin slices (Ambady and Rosenthal) are surprisingly accurate; impression management follows Goffman's dramaturgy and Jones and Pittman's strategies.

3.3 Communication, Nonverbal Cues and Attribution. Communication runs encoding, channel, recipient, decoding, with feedback and noise; the classic models are Lasswell (1948), Shannon and Weaver (1949), Schramm (1954) and Berlo's SMCR (1960). Nonverbal channels include face, gaze, body language, touch and paralanguage; Hall's proxemics sets intimate, personal, social and public zones, and lies are detected with only about 54 percent accuracy. Attribution theory began with Heider (1958): dispositional versus situational causes. Jones and Davis explained correspondent inferences, Kelley used consensus, consistency and distinctiveness, and Weiner added stability and controllability. The fundamental attribution error (Ross 1977), actor-observer effect, self-serving bias and just-world hypothesis tilt our explanations, more so in individualist cultures.

3.4 Attitudes, Persuasion and Cognitive Dissonance. An attitude is a learned evaluation of an object with affective, behavioural and cognitive parts (Rosenberg and Hovland, 1960), serving Katz's utilitarian, knowledge, ego-defensive and value-expressive functions. LaPiere (1934) exposed the attitude-behaviour gap; strong, specific attitudes formed by direct experience predict best, and the theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen) adds perceived control to attitude and subjective norm. Persuasion depends on source, message and audience (the Yale approach) and runs by the central or peripheral route (Petty and Cacioppo). Reactance (Brehm, 1966), forewarning and inoculation aid resistance. Festinger's cognitive dissonance (1957) explains why $1 beat $20; Bem's self-perception theory is its main rival.

3.5 Stereotypes, Prejudice and Intergroup Relations. A stereotype is a belief about a group, prejudice a negative attitude (Allport, 1954) and discrimination unfair action. Prejudice has been traced to the authoritarian personality, displaced frustration, social learning, realistic conflict (at Robbers Cave only superordinate goals ended hostility) and relative deprivation. Tajfel's minimal groups showed that categorisation alone breeds in-group favouritism; social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979) says much of our self-esteem comes from our groups. Low-prejudiced people inhibit automatically activated stereotypes by controlled processing (Devine, 1989); the IAT measures implicit bias. Social roles (Eagly) shape gender stereotypes, which build glass ceilings and sticky floors. Contact reduces prejudice under Allport's conditions.

3.6 Affiliation, Attraction and Close Relationships. The need to belong (Baumeister and Leary 1995) is fundamental; gregariousness is a social motive. Schachter (1959) showed that anxiety raises affiliation with others in the same plight, and we affiliate for information, anxiety reduction and social comparison (Festinger 1954), which also explains FOMO. Attraction grows from propinquity and mere exposure (Zajonc 1968), looks, matching, similarity and reciprocity. Exchange, equity and investment theories explain staying or leaving. Self-disclosure deepens relationships (descriptive facts, evaluative feelings). Love is passionate or companionate, triangular (Sternberg 1986) and shaped by attachment style. Weiss separated emotional from social loneliness.

3.7 Social Influence: Conformity, Compliance, Obedience and Power. Social influence takes three forms: conformity (matching a group with no request), compliance (yielding to a direct request) and obedience (following an authority's command). Norms may be descriptive or injunctive, prescriptive or proscriptive. Sherif's autokinetic study showed norms forming through informational influence; Asch's line studies showed normative conformity to an obviously wrong majority (about 37 percent of critical trials). Consistent minorities can convert majorities (Moscovici). Cialdini traced compliance tactics such as the foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face to six principles. In Milgram's study 65 percent obeyed to 450 volts; Zimbardo's prison study showed the power of roles. French and Raven named six bases of power.

3.8 Prosocial Behaviour: Why and When People Help. Altruism is helping with no expected reward, at a cost to oneself. Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis (the Elaine study) claims true altruism; Cialdini's negative-state relief model and the empathic joy hypothesis are egoistic, while kin selection (Hamilton), reciprocal altruism (Trivers), competitive altruism, defensive helping and norms add other motives. After the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese, Latané and Darley showed the bystander effect: people help more when alone (seizure study, 85 versus 31 percent). Their five-step model locates its two classic causes, diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance. Costs (Piliavin), hurry, mood, models, gratitude, the altruistic personality and the threat-to-self-esteem model complete the picture.

3.9 Aggression: Its Roots, Causes and Control. Aggression is behaviour aimed at harming a living being who wants to avoid it (Baron and Richardson, 1994): hostile or instrumental, physical or verbal, direct, indirect or relational. Freud's thanatos and Lorenz's hydraulic instinct gave way to biology (low serotonin, MAOA with maltreatment) and to the frustration-aggression hypothesis (Dollard and colleagues, 1939), softened by Miller (1941) and reformulated by Berkowitz (1989) as negative affect plus aggressive cues (the weapons effect). Bandura's Bobo doll studies showed observational learning; the General Aggression Model integrates these. Provocation, exclusion, media violence, honour cultures, heat and alcohol raise aggression; catharsis fails, while apology, incompatible responses and self-regulation help.

3.10 Groups: Structure, Performance, Decisions and Leadership. A group is two or more interacting, interdependent people with a shared identity and a structure of roles, status, norms and cohesiveness. Moreno's sociometry (1934) maps choices in a sociogram of stars, isolates and cliques. Groups pass through Tuckman's forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning. Others' presence brings social facilitation on easy tasks and impairment on hard ones (Triplett 1898; Zajonc 1965); pooled effort brings social loafing (Ringelmann), reversed as social striving in collectivist cultures; anonymity brings deindividuation. Discussion causes group polarisation, and cohesion with insulation causes Janis's groupthink. Leadership theory runs from traits and styles to Fiedler's contingency model and transformational leadership.

3.11 Applied Social Psychology: Law, Culture and Social Work. Social support protects health, chiefly as a stress buffer (Cohen and Wills, 1985); crowding is felt, density is counted (Stokols). Defence lawyers challenge eyewitnesses because source monitoring errors cause false identifications and confidence predicts accuracy weakly; double-blind sequential lineups and the cognitive interview help. Jurors build stories; effective rehabilitation is community-based, cognitive-behavioural and follows risk-need-responsivity. Social work grew from the Charity Organisation Society and settlements. Mary Richmond founded case work (intake to follow-up); Fischer (1973) doubted its effectiveness. Grace Coyle established group work; Trecker gave its principles and Shulman its member roles. India's first school was the Sir Dorabji Tata Graduate School of Social Work (1936).

  1. Who is credited with elaborating the "social representation theory"? (RML 2022)

    (a) Serge Moscovici (b) Michael A. Hogg (c) Roy F. Baumeister (d) Mark E. Leary

    Answer: (a) Serge Moscovici. Moscovici launched social representations theory in 1961 with his study of how psychoanalysis became French common sense, through anchoring and objectification. Hogg is known for social identity and uncertainty-identity theory; Baumeister and Mark R. Leary for the need to belong (1995), and Leary for the sociometer theory of self-esteem.

  2. Which one refers to the behaviour that an individual engages in while enacting a role? (RML 2020)

    (a) Role taking (b) Role playing (c) Playing at a role (d) Role expectation

    Answer: (b) Role playing. Role playing (role enactment) is the actual behaviour of the person performing a role. Role taking is Mead's mental act of seeing a situation from another's viewpoint, playing at a role is a young child's make-believe, and role expectation is what other people expect of the occupant.

  3. Some students experience an intense period of adaptation when they leave their old school environment and start college, especially during those initial days before they find companions, start to fit in and feel comfortable. This process is best described as: (RML 2021)

    (a) Looking-glass self (b) Self-awareness (c) Institutionalisation (d) Re-socialisation

    Answer: (d) Re-socialisation. Resocialisation is unlearning old norms and roles and learning new ones after a major transition such as starting college. The looking-glass self is Cooley's self-image built from others' imagined judgements, self-awareness is attention turned on oneself, and institutionalisation is the apathetic dependence of long-stay inmates.

  4. A tendency to act in a way that is consistent with one's own belief, expectation, or cognition about an event or behavior, thereby increasing the likelihood that the event or behavior will occur, is termed as: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Self-fulfilling prophecy (b) Confirmation bias (c) Motivational reaction (d) Base-rate fallacy

    Answer: (a) Self-fulfilling prophecy. Acting on an expectation in ways that make it come true is Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy (1948). Confirmation bias only seeks evidence that fits a belief and changes nothing in the world, the base-rate fallacy ignores how common something is, and 'motivational reaction' is not a term.

  5. What is the halo effect? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) The tendency to judge others based on their group membership (b) The influence of a positive trait on the perception of other traits (c) The tendency to overlook negative behaviors in individuals (d) The effect of initial impressions on subsequent judgments

    Answer: (b) The influence of a positive trait on the perception of other traits. Thorndike (1920) named the halo effect: one salient positive trait or an overall impression colours ratings of unrelated traits. Judging by group membership is stereotyping, the effect of initial impressions is the primacy effect (or anchoring), and overlooking faults is a consequence of a halo, not its definition.

  6. A teacher watches two students interact for less than a minute and correctly infers who is the class leader. This example best illustrates: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Social facilitation (b) Self-fulfilling prophecy (c) Thin slicing (d) Fundamental attribution error

    Answer: (c) Thin slicing. Ambady and Rosenthal showed that very brief samples of behaviour (silent clips of 30 seconds or less) yield accurate judgements. Social facilitation is better performance of well-learned tasks before others, the teacher's expectation did not create the leadership, and the fundamental attribution error over-explains behaviour by disposition.

  7. Arrange the process of communication in sequence (RML 2020)

    (a) coding, decoding, recipient, channel (b) channel, encoding, recipient, decoding (c) channel, decoding, recipient, encoding (d) encoding, channel, recipient, decoding

    Answer: (d) encoding, channel, recipient, decoding. The sender first encodes the idea into symbols, the message travels through a channel to the recipient, who then decodes it. The other options send a message through the channel before it is encoded or decode it before it is received.

  8. The tendency of people to attribute their own behavior to external causes and other’s behavior to internal causes is referred to as : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Self-serving bias (b) Fundamental attribution error (c) Actor-Observer effect (d) Stereotyping

    Answer: (c) Actor-Observer effect. Jones and Nisbett (1971): as actors we explain our own behaviour by the situation, as observers we explain others' by their dispositions. The fundamental attribution error concerns only other people's behaviour, the self-serving bias concerns our own successes and failures, and stereotyping is a generalised belief about a group.

  9. The "just-world hypothesis" explains victim-blaming as a result of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) People's inherent sadism and desire to see others suffer. (b) The need to believe that the world is orderly, predictable, and fair. (c) A fundamental error in attributing behavior to dispositional factors. (d) The process of social comparison with a less fortunate other.

    Answer: (b) The need to believe that the world is orderly, predictable, and fair. For Melvin Lerner, an innocent person's suffering threatens this belief, so observers restore it by deciding the victim deserved it. Observers are distressed rather than sadistic, the dispositional blame is the result rather than the cause, and downward social comparison is a different process.

  10. Arvind is a college student who has strong negative feelings about smoking cigarettes. His grandfather died of lung cancer and he believes smoking is disgusting. One night at a party, Arvind's friends all go outside for a cigarette and he decides to smoke as well. What will he most likely experience as a result? (RML 2021)

    (a) Justification of efforts (b) Increased drive to smoke (c) Cognitive Dissonance (d) Drive Reduction

    Answer: (c) Cognitive dissonance. Acting against a strong, important attitude creates Festinger's aversive tension. Effort justification is a different paradigm (valuing what cost effort), nothing suggests a stronger urge to smoke, and drive reduction is Hull's motivation theory.

  11. In which of the following scenarios would it be most difficult to predict behaviour from attitudes? (RML 2021)

    (a) When the attitude has a great deal of personal relevance for the individual (b) When the attitude is formed by indirect experience (c) When the individual has a lot of knowledge about the attitude object (d) When the attitude is formed by the individual's direct experience

    Answer: (b) When the attitude is formed by indirect experience. Hearsay-based attitudes are weak and less accessible. Personal relevance, knowledge of the object and direct experience (Regan and Fazio, 1977) all strengthen the attitude-behaviour link.

  12. In Social Psychology, reactance is : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) The tendency to comply with direct requests (b) The motivation to restore freedom when it is threatened (c) The effect of persuasive messages on attitude change (d) The tendency to follow social norms without question

    Answer: (b) The motivation to restore freedom when it is threatened. Brehm (1966) defined reactance this way; it often makes the forbidden option more attractive. Complying with requests is compliance, following norms is conformity, and message-driven attitude change is persuasion.

  13. According to social identity theory, prejudice arises primarily due to : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Competition for scarce resources (b) Cognitive dissonance (c) Categorization into in-groups and out-groups (d) Social facilitation

    Answer: (c) Categorization into in-groups and out-groups. Tajfel's minimal groups showed favouritism with no resources at stake, so categorisation plus the need for positive distinctiveness is enough. Competition for scarce resources is Sherif's realistic conflict theory; cognitive dissonance (Festinger) and social facilitation (performance in the presence of others) are unrelated.

  14. Low-prejudiced people engage in: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Automatic information processing resulting in involuntary stereotyping (b) Controlled information processing resulting in refuting stereotype (c) Controlled information processing resulting in involuntary stereotyping (d) Automatic information processing resulting in voluntary stereotyping

    Answer: (b) Controlled information processing resulting in refuting stereotype. Devine (1989) found stereotypes are activated automatically in high- and low-prejudiced people alike; low-prejudiced people then use controlled processing to inhibit them. Option (a) is the first stage common to all; (c) and (d) pair the processes wrongly.

  15. The phenomenon where a qualified individual is prevented from advancing to higher positions in their career due to an invisible barrier based on gender or minority status is called: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) The Matthew Effect (b) The Glass Ceiling Effect (c) The Halo Effect (d) The Pygmalion Effect

    Answer: (b) The Glass Ceiling Effect. The glass ceiling is an invisible barrier of attitudes and organisational bias near the top, popularised by a 1986 Wall Street Journal article. The Matthew effect is accumulated advantage (Merton, 1968); halo and Pygmalion effects concern impressions and expectations, not career barriers.

  16. 'The more we see and interact with a person, the more likely he or she is to become our friend or sexual partner.' This denotes: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Propinquity effect (b) Exposure effect (c) Matching hypothesis (d) Reciprocity

    Answer: (a) Propinquity effect. Nearness and frequent interaction breed friendship (Festinger, Schachter and Back 1950). The exposure effect is narrower (mere repeated exposure to any stimulus raises liking) and is its mechanism; matching is choosing partners of similar attractiveness; reciprocity is liking those who like us.

  17. Emotional isolation means: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Feeling a lack of deep emotional attachment to one specific person (b) Suffering from a lack of friends/associates (c) Suffering from feelings of incompetence (d) Feeling a sense of inadequacy and low self-esteem

    Answer: (a) Lack of deep attachment to one specific person. Robert Weiss (1973) separated emotional isolation from social isolation, the lack of a wider network of friends and associates (option b). Incompetence and low self-esteem are correlates of loneliness, not its types.

  18. Which concept is closely related to FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) in social media research? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Social comparison (b) Classical conditioning (c) Operant extinction (d) Cognitive dissonance only

    Answer: (a) Social comparison. FOMO (Przybylski and colleagues 2013) arises from constant upward comparison with others' curated experiences, in the tradition of Festinger's social comparison theory (1954). Conditioning, extinction and dissonance are learning and consistency processes, not comparison.

  19. Which of the following is an example of the power of roles? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Robber's Cave Experiment (b) Milgram's message experiment (c) Zilstein's shock research (d) Zimbardo's prison experiment

    Answer: (d) Zimbardo's prison experiment. Students randomly made guards or prisoners took on their roles so fully that the study was stopped after six days. Robbers Cave studied intergroup conflict, Milgram's message experiment was the small-world study, and Dr Zilstein was the fictitious experimenter of Schachter's affiliation study.

  20. What is normative social influence? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Influence based on the desire to be correct and accurate (b) Influence based on the desire to be accepted and liked by others (c) Influence based on personal beliefs and values (d) Influence based on perceived power and authority

    Answer: (b) The desire to be accepted and liked. Deutsch and Gerard (1955) contrasted it with informational influence, the desire to be correct (option a). Personal values describe internalisation, and power and authority describe obedience.

  21. According to Cialdini, techniques for gaining compliance rest to a large extent on six basic principles. These do NOT include: (RML 2022)

    (a) Scarcity (b) Social validation (c) Friendship/liking (d) Reward

    Answer: (d) Reward. The six principles are reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social validation (social proof), friendship or liking, authority and scarcity.

  22. The bystander effect, where the presence of others inhibits helping, is primarily explained by two factors: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Social loafing and deindividuation (b) Diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance (c) Social comparison and normative influence (d) Group polarization and groupthink

    Answer: (b) Diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance. These are Latané and Darley's two classic mechanisms: each bystander assumes someone else will act, and each reads the others' calm as a sign that nothing is wrong. The other pairs are group phenomena of other kinds (Sections 3.6, 3.7 and 3.10).

  23. A state that occurs when bystanders in an emergency situation use the behaviour of others to determine whether help is actually required is called: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Pluralistic ignorance (b) Group conformity (c) Group compliance (d) Crowd behavior

    Answer: (a) Pluralistic ignorance. Bystanders look to one another to decide whether help is needed and, seeing calm faces, wrongly conclude it is not. Conformity and compliance are yielding to group pressure or to a direct request, and crowd behaviour is the collective action of a large gathering.

  24. Altruistic Personality is characterized by: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Offering help only when one feels like (b) Consistently helping others (c) Calculating the cost and benefits of helping (d) Readiness to help in an emergency

    Answer: (b) Consistently helping others. The altruistic personality is a stable tendency to help across situations; Bierhoff, Klein and Kramp (1991) found its components to be empathy, belief in a just world, social responsibility, internal locus of control and low egocentrism. Helping only when in the mood, cost-benefit calculation and readiness only in emergencies describe situational helping.

  25. Who reformulated the "frustration-aggression hypothesis"? (RML 2022)

    (a) Dollard (b) Miller (c) Berkowitz (d) Lazarus

    Answer: (c) Berkowitz. Leonard Berkowitz (1989) recast it: frustration leads to aggression only to the extent that it produces negative affect, with aggressive cues tipping the balance. Dollard and colleagues proposed the original (1939), Miller only softened it (1941), and Richard Lazarus is known for appraisal and stress.

  26. Albert Bandura's Bobo doll experiment is a classic demonstration of: (RML Practice 1)

    (a) Operant conditioning through direct reinforcement (b) Observational learning of aggression through modeling (c) Classical conditioning of fear (d) The effects of sensory deprivation

    Answer: (b) Observational learning of aggression through modelling. Children copied an aggressive adult's specific acts without ever being reinforced, so it is not operant conditioning; classical conditioning of fear is Little Albert, and sensory deprivation is the McGill isolation research.

  27. Low levels of which neurotransmitter leads to aggression (Entrance Mock 1)

    (a) Serotonin (b) Dopamine (c) Epinephrine (d) Norepinephrine

    Answer: (a) Serotonin. Low serotonin activity (low 5-HIAA in cerebrospinal fluid) is linked to impulsive aggression. Dopamine concerns reward and movement, while epinephrine and norepinephrine mark arousal in any strong emotion.

  28. Which of the following is not a stage of group formation? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Storming (b) Proximity (c) Norming (d) Performing

    Answer: (b) Proximity. Tuckman's stages are forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning. Proximity is a cause of interpersonal attraction, not a stage of group development.

  29. Tim would like to participate in his high school's musical. He has been taking vocal lessons for years and is an excellent singer, but does not have much experience as a dancer. The director gives him a part where he has to sing and dance. Based on the theory of social facilitation, how will the presence of an audience affect Tim's performance? (RML 2021)

    (a) His dancing will improve, but his singing will be impaired (b) Both his singing and dancing will improve (c) His singing will improve, but his dancing will be impaired (d) Both his singing and dancing will be impaired

    Answer: (c) His singing will improve, but his dancing will be impaired. By Zajonc's drive theory, an audience raises arousal, which strengthens the dominant response: correct on a well-learned task (singing), often wrong on an unpractised one (dancing). Options (b) and (d) ignore that the effect depends on how well learned the task is.

  30. A group of scientists is deciding on the use of live animals in research; most of the group has used live animals. How might the leader reduce groupthink? (RML 2021)

    (a) Make a cohesive team-building exercise a mandatory part of the meeting (b) Relate his opinion at the beginning of the discussion (c) Ask a subset of individuals in the group to critique animal research (d) Ask the group members not to argue with one another's opinions

    Answer: (c) Ask a subset to critique animal research. Janis's remedies include a devil's advocate or critical subgroup. Team-building raises cohesion, a leader stating views first steers the group, and forbidding argument suppresses dissent, so (a), (b) and (d) all worsen groupthink.

  31. Defense attorneys often protest the prosecutor's use of eyewitness testimony because: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Eyewitnesses are more prone to forgetting (b) A confident witness is usually right (c) Source errors may cause an eyewitness to make a false identification (d) Poor eyesight of eyewitnesses tends to invalidate their testimony

    Answer: (c) Source errors may cause an eyewitness to make a false identification. A face met elsewhere (a mugshot, the news, a bystander) is misattributed to the crime. Witnesses are not unusually forgetful, confidence predicts accuracy only weakly and grows after feedback, and eyesight is rarely the issue.

  32. Which of the following is not a part of the social case work process? (RML 2020)

    (a) Content analysis (b) Treatment (c) Assessment (d) Evaluation

    Answer: (a) Content analysis. Case work runs intake and study, assessment (social diagnosis), treatment or intervention, evaluation, termination and follow-up. Content analysis, coding themes in texts, is a research technique.

  33. Who among the following established group work as a method within the profession of social work? (RML 2020)

    (a) Mary Richmond (b) Charlotte Towle (c) Grace Coyle (d) Geisla Konopka

    Answer: (c) Grace Coyle. Her Social Process in Organized Groups (1930) founded group work as a method. Richmond founded case work, Towle wrote Common Human Needs, and Konopka carried group work into treatment settings later.
