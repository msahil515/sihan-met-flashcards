1. History of social psychology

The one idea. Social psychology is the scientific study of how the actual, imagined or implied presence of others influences an individual's thoughts, feelings and behaviour. Its history is a chain of named "firsts" and named "founders", and the paper loves to test who did what, in what year.

The foundation (late 19th to early 20th century)

FigureYearContribution
Norman Triplett1897–1898Credited with the first research article in social psychology. He noticed cyclists raced faster against others than against a clock, and designed a careful controlled experiment to study it. This is the origin of social facilitation.
Max Ringelmann1880s (published 1913)Found individuals performed worse on simple tasks such as rope-pulling when working with others. This is the origin of social loafing (the "Ringelmann effect").
William McDougall1908Published an early social psychology textbook; helps establish the field.

Floyd Allport1924His textbook is taken as establishing social psychology as a distinct experimental field.
Trap

Triplett = social facilitation (first article, 1897–98). Ringelmann = social loafing (1880s, published 1913). Don't swap them. Establishing the field as a discipline is attributed to McDougall (1908), Ross (1908) and Floyd Allport (1924).

1930s–1950s: the field finds its problems

  • 1936: Gordon Allport (younger brother of Floyd, the 1924 textbook author) and others formed the Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues (SPSSI). Muzafer Sherif published groundbreaking experimental research on social influence (the autokinetic-effect norm-formation studies).
  • Kurt Lewin fled the Nazis and emigrated to the US in the early 1930s. His central contribution: behaviour is a function of the interaction between the person and the environment (the interactionist perspective), often written B = f(P, E).
  • 1950s: Gordon Allport (1954) published The Nature of Prejudice. Solomon Asch (1951) demonstrated how willing people are to conform to an obviously wrong majority. Leon Festinger introduced two landmark theories: social comparison (1954, how people learn about themselves by comparing to others) and cognitive dissonance (1957, how attitudes change through one's own behaviour).

1960s–1990s: crisis and pluralism

  • Stanley Milgram's obedience research (early/mid 1960s) linked the post-WWII era to the coming era of social revolution, showing individuals' vulnerability to the destructive commands of authority.
  • Strong disagreements of the period (the "crisis of social psychology") were largely a reaction against the dominant method of the day, the laboratory experiment.
  • Mid-1970s onward = an era of pluralism: laboratory and field experiments used side by side; a "hot" perspective (emotion and motivation) alongside a "cold" perspective (cognition); and the rise of international, cross-cultural and multicultural perspectives.

Social psychology in a new century

  • Social cognition dominated the final quarter of the field's first century: the study of how people perceive, remember and interpret information about themselves and others.
  • Social neuroscience emerged as a subfield studying the relationship between neural and social processes.
  • Behavioural genetics (the study of how genes affect behaviour) triggered research on, e.g., whether aggression is heritable and the role of genes in sexual orientation.
  • Evolutionary psychology uses evolutionary principles to understand behaviour (e.g., asking how the psychological mechanisms underlying jealousy may have evolved from natural-selection pressures).
  • Increasing emphasis on testing the universal generality vs cultural specificity of theories via cross-cultural and multicultural research.
High-yield

Triplett (facilitation, first article) · Ringelmann (loafing) · McDougall/Ross 1908 + Floyd Allport 1924 (founding) · Lewin B = f(P,E) · Sherif (norm formation) · Asch 1951 (conformity) · Festinger 1954 social comparison / 1957 dissonance · Milgram 1960s (obedience) · Gordon Allport 1954 Nature of Prejudice. New century: social cognition, social neuroscience, behavioural genetics, evolutionary psychology, cross-cultural work.

2. Theories of social psychology

The grand frameworks the field uses to explain social behaviour. Learn each by its core construct and its named author.

Field theory (Kurt Lewin)

Lewin, whose name is most associated with field theory, was a student of the founders of the Gestalt movement. Others (Tolman 1932, Wheeler 1940, Lashley 1929, Brunswik 1949) have been called field theorists, but none dealt specifically with social behaviour, so Lewin is taken as the best representative.

Six principal attributes of field theory: (1) use of a constructive method; (2) a dynamic approach; (3) emphasis on psychological processes; (4) analysis based on the situation as a whole; (5) a distinction between systematic and historical issues; (6) a mathematical representation of the psychological situation (Lewin favoured topology).

Definition

Life space = the person plus the psychological environment as it exists for him: the totality of all psychological factors influencing the individual at any given moment. A thing "exists" in the life space if it has demonstrable effects. Lewin held that the properties of the field at the present time are the only determinants of behaviour (an ahistorical orientation).

  • Regions / life spheres: major parts of the life space (needs, profession, family, friendships), further differentiated into smaller units. Differentiation depends on age, intelligence and experience.
  • Two added dimensions: fluidity (how distinguishable and how permeable regions are) and the reality–irreality dimension (reality = objective aspects; irreality = imagery, fantasy, hopes and fears; the psychological future has both a level of reality, "what is expected", and irreality, "hopes and fears").
  • Behaviour = locomotion: behaviour is any change in the life space, conceived as a locomotion of the person within it (bodily or psychological). The space of free movement is limited by barriers (boundaries that resist locomotion).
  • Force: "that which causes change" (Lewin, 1936), with direction, strength and point of application (vector + arrow). The combination of all forces on a point is the resultant force. Valence describes whether a goal region is attractive (positive) or repulsive (negative); the strength of force is a function of valence and psychological distance. The change of force strength with distance is the goal gradient (it rises faster as one nears a negative goal than a positive one).
  • Types of force: driving, restraining, induced (from another's wishes), own-need, and impersonal (situational requirements).
  • Tension systems: tension is aroused when a person accepts task completion as a goal; it is discharged by completing the task and seeks equilibrium with neighbouring regions. This explains why incompleted tasks are recalled and resumed more than completed ones (the Zeigarnik effect). Substitute satisfaction can discharge tension; its effectiveness depends on similarity, difficulty, and permeability of boundaries.

Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger, 1957)

Follows Heider's balance theory and the consistency tradition: the core principle is the tendency toward cognitive consistency. Two cognitions are dissonant if, considered alone, the obverse of one would follow from the other; consonant if one follows from the other.

  • Four sources of dissonance: logical inconsistency; cultural mores; opinion generality (a specific opinion clashing with a more general one); past experience.
  • Magnitude depends on the importance of the elements and the weighted proportion of relevant cognitions that are dissonant; the maximum possible dissonance equals the total resistance to change of the least resistant element.
  • Consequences: dissonance is uncomfortable and motivates reduction; the person also actively avoids dissonance-increasing information.
  • Decisions: dissonance is an inevitable consequence of decision; post-decision dissonance grows with the importance of the decision, the attractiveness of the unchosen option, and the cognitive overlap of the options.
  • Forced compliance: public compliance without private change. The smaller the reward/punishment (just sufficient to elicit compliance), the greater the dissonance, hence the greater the attitude change.
  • Selective exposure is curvilinearly related to dissonance: moderate dissonance maximises information seeking/avoidance.
  • Social support: dissonance from disagreement varies with the number of agreeing others, importance of elements, relevance and attractiveness of the disagreer, and extent of disagreement.
Trap

Less-leads-to-more. In forced compliance, a smaller incentive produces more attitude change (you can't blame the small reward, so you change your attitude instead). This is the opposite of a reinforcement prediction, and is the classic dissonance exam item.

Psychodynamic approach: Freud's group psychology

Freud viewed society as the product of the individual personalities within it. Four major works: Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1922), Civilization and its Discontents (1930), Totem and Taboo (1913), and The Future of an Illusion. Core ideas: society inhibits and represses instinctual impulses; the family is the fundamental agent of socialisation and the prototype for all later social relationships; groups form as a re-enactment of the individual's libidinal ties to parents; social justice arises from envy and hostility among members; law and religion protect society against man's aggressive and sexual impulses.

Rapaport's (1959) metapsychological points of view on Freud's system: the empirical (subject matter is behaviour), the Gestalt (all behaviour is the total expression of all structures and forces), the genetic (mental phenomena have historical antecedents; earlier behaviour can be reactivated), the dynamic (all behaviour is ultimately drive-determined, primarily a broad sexual/libidinal drive), the economic (behaviour regulated by distribution and flow of psychic energy), the structural (id/ego/superego intervene between arousal and investment), and the adaptive (behaviour is shaped by, and shapes, external and bodily reality).

Sociobiological theory (Edward O. Wilson, 1975)

In Sociobiology: The New Synthesis, Wilson outlined a framework for the biological basis of social behaviour. As a branch of evolutionary biology, it uses demographic parameters and population genetics to predict social organisation across species. Its key contribution is ultimate causation (the selective forces operating over generations) as distinct from proximate causation (mechanisms within an individual's lifetime). Sociobiology differs from evolutionary psychology: both use ultimate causal explanations, but evolutionary psychology models the underlying information-processing circuitry (cognitive programs), whereas sociobiology steps straight from selective forces to behaviour without specifying the cognitive programs.

Social cognitive theory (Albert Bandura)

Began as Social Learning Theory (SLT) in the 1960s; became Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) in 1986. Learning occurs in a social context through the dynamic, reciprocal interaction of person, environment and behaviour. Constructs: reciprocal determinism, behavioural capability (knowledge + skill to perform), observational learning (modelling), reinforcements, expectations (and expectancies, the subjective value of outcomes), and self-efficacy (added when SLT became SCT; confidence in one's ability to perform a behaviour).

High-yield

Self-efficacy is the construct added when Social Learning Theory became Social Cognitive Theory (1986). The first five constructs (reciprocal determinism, behavioural capability, observational learning, reinforcements, expectations) were already in SLT.

3. Social perception

Social perception = the process through which we seek to know and understand other people: how we learn about others, communicate without words, attribute causes, and form impressions.

Nonverbal communication

To understand others' emotional states we rely on nonverbal communication ("an unspoken language of facial expressions, eye contact, and body movements and postures"). The basic channels are:

  • Facial expressions — the primary channel of emotion.
  • Eye contact — high contact signals liking/interest; staring = continued steady gaze regardless of the recipient's response (often a sign of hostility).
  • Body language — cues from posture, position and movement of the body.
  • Touching — can induce positive reactions when appropriate, negative when not.
  • Paralanguage / paralinguistic cues — tone and inflection of voice (not the words themselves).
  • Body chemistry — even subtle olfactory cues (e.g., menstrual-cycle chemical changes) can be transmitted to others.
Definition

Facial feedback hypothesis: the facial expressions we show not only reflect our internal feelings but feed back to influence our subjective emotional experience. Evidence: participants given Botox (which paralyses facial muscles) reported less negative reaction to negative film clips and less positive reaction to mildly positive clips than those given Restylane (a non-paralysing control).

Recognising deception

  • Microexpressions: fleeting facial expressions lasting only a few tenths of a second.
  • Interchannel discrepancies: inconsistencies between cues from different channels (liars struggle to control all channels at once).
  • Eye contact: liars often blink more and show more dilated pupils.
  • Exaggerated facial expressions: e.g., smiling more broadly than usual.
  • Linguistic style: when lying, the pitch of the voice often rises, response latency increases, and speakers start–stop–restart sentences more.

Attribution: understanding causes of behaviour

Attribution = our efforts to understand the causes behind others' (and sometimes our own) behaviour.

Jones & Davis (1965): correspondent inference theory

We are most likely to conclude that behaviour reflects stable traits (reach a correspondent inference) when the behaviour: is freely chosen; yields distinctive, non-common effects (effects caused by one specific factor but not others); and is low in social desirability.

Kelley's theory of causal attribution (covariation model)

To answer "why?" we focus on three kinds of information:

DimensionMeaning
ConsensusExtent to which other people react to the same stimulus in the same way (higher = more people react alike).
ConsistencyExtent to which this person reacts the same way to the stimulus on other occasions / over time.
DistinctivenessExtent to which this person reacts differently to other, different stimuli.

Other dimensions of causal attribution concern whether causes are stable over time and controllable. People who believe strongly in God, or whose culture accepts complex causality, more often attribute improbable but important events to "what was meant to be" (fate). The level of interpretation we use is action identification: low-level interpretations focus on the action itself, higher-level on its ultimate goals.

Sources of attributional bias

  • Correspondence bias / Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE): the tendency to explain others' actions as stemming from dispositions even when situational causes are present. Reasons: dispositional causes are more salient; we give situational causes insufficient weight; we begin by assuming behaviour reflects character, then under-correct for the situation.
  • Actor–observer effect: we attribute our own behaviour to external/situational causes but others' behaviour to internal causes.
  • Self-serving bias: we attribute positive outcomes to internal causes and negative ones to external causes (especially strong for negative events).
Trap

FAE = bias about others (over-attribute to disposition). Actor–observer = the self–other split (own behaviour situational, others' dispositional). Self-serving = the good–bad split for one's own outcomes (success internal, failure external). Distinguish all three.

4. Group & social influence

Social influence = efforts by one or more persons to change the behaviour, attitudes or feelings of others. It can even be symbolic (occurring when others are not present and make no direct attempt). The three main forms are conformity, compliance and obedience.

Social facilitation and social loafing

Social facilitation: the tendency to perform simple/well-learned tasks better when others are present; more precisely, the strengthening of dominant (most likely) responses in the presence of others (on some tasks the presence of others hinders performance).

  • Robert Zajonc: increased arousal enhances performance on easy tasks (dominant response correct) but promotes incorrect responding on complex tasks (dominant response wrong) — e.g., people do worse on hard anagrams when anxious.
  • Why? Nickolas Cottrell → evaluation apprehension (we wonder how observers are judging us). Sanders, Baron & Moore → distraction–conflict ("driven by distraction": attention conflict overloads cognition, causing arousal). Zajonc → mere presence produces arousal even without evaluation or distraction.

Social loafing: the tendency to exert less effort when pooling efforts toward a common goal than when individually accountable. Free riders benefit from the group but give little in return.

High-yield

The reconciling rule: when being observed increases evaluation concern, social facilitation occurs; when being lost in a crowd decreases evaluation concern, social loafing occurs. Loafing is reduced when the task is challenging/appealing/involving, when members see others as unable to contribute, when high effort is seen to bring rewards, and when members are friends or feel indispensable.

Conformity

Definition

Conformity = changing attitudes/behaviour to adhere to existing social norms (rules indicating how we should behave). Acceptance = conformity that involves both acting and believing in accord with social pressure.

  • Solomon Asch first systematically studied conformity: participants judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard. When confederates gave identical wrong answers, real participants tended to give the same wrong answer; three-quarters conformed at least once.
  • Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison study showed the powerful impact of norms attached to social roles: participants came to behave like real guards and prisoners.
  • Two motives for conformity: normative social influence (the desire to be liked) and informational social influence (the desire to be right/accurate).
  • Factors: cohesiveness, group size, and the type of norm operating (descriptive = what most people do; injunctive = what is approved/expected).
  • Emotional contagion: we catch others' emotions; if they are dissimilar to us in important ways, we may show counter-contagion (opposite emotions).
  • Nonconformity is encouraged by the desire to attract a mate, by power (powerful people depend less on others and take others' perspectives less), and by the desire to be unique.
  • Minority influence: minorities can sway majorities by being consistent, avoiding appearing rigid/dogmatic, and arguing a position consistent with current social trends.

Compliance (Robert Cialdini)

Cialdini studied "compliance professionals" and identified six basic principles of compliance: friendship/liking, commitment/consistency, scarcity, reciprocity, authority, social validation (consensus).

TacticUnderlying principleHow it works
IngratiationFriendship/likingGet others to like us (flattery, self-promotion, improved appearance, positive nonverbal cues, small favours, incidental similarity) so they agree more readily.

Trap

Foot-in-the-door (small → large) is based on consistency; door-in-the-face (large → small) is based on reciprocity. Lowball and foot-in-the-door both start small but lowball involves changing the deal after acceptance.

Obedience (Stanley Milgram, 1963, 1965, 1974)

The clearest, most dramatic evidence for obedience. Teacher-participants were told to increase shock strength each time the "learner" erred (the learner never actually received shocks). When teachers hesitated, the experimenter used graded prods: "Please continue"; "The experiment requires that you continue"; "It is absolutely essential that you continue"; "You have no other choice; you must go on."

Reasons for destructive obedience:

  • Transfer of responsibility onto the experimenter (the authority figure, not the participant, would be responsible).
  • Badges/signs of authority that remind people of the norm "obey the people in charge".
  • Immediacy of the victim: when the victim was unseen/unheard, obedience was higher.
  • Gradual escalation of orders yields more obedience than sudden escalation; situations move quickly.

Social power (French & Raven, 1959)

Power = the capacity to influence others while resisting their attempts to influence you. John French and Bert Raven (1959) identified five bases of social power (reward, coercive, legitimate, referent, expert); Raven (1965, 1993) later added a sixth (informational power).

Persuasion

Persuasion = changing attitudes through messages. Most persuasive communicators are credible, physically attractive, and seem not to be trying to persuade. Fear appeals that are too frightening tend not to work; positively framed messages are often more effective.

Definition

Elaboration-Likelihood Model (ELM): persuasion occurs by two routes. The central route (systematic processing) involves careful attention to message content (argument strength matters), used when motivation and capacity are high. The peripheral route (heuristic processing) uses mental shortcuts and peripheral cues (communicator attractiveness/expertise), used when ability, capacity or motivation is low.

Electronic word-of-mouth (eWoM) persuasion depends on communicator credibility, consistency among reviewers, and consistency with prior beliefs.

Resistance to persuasion is increased by: reactance (negative reaction to threats to personal freedom, which can boomerang into greater opposition), forewarning (knowing someone will try to change us), selective avoidance, active counterarguing, and attitude inoculation (exposure to weak attacks so refutations are ready for stronger ones). Ego-depletion from prior self-regulation undermines our ability to resist persuasion (and makes us less honest).

5. Group dynamics

A group = a collection of people perceived to be bonded together in a coherent unit to some degree.

Types and structure of groups

  • Common-bond groups: face-to-face interaction; members bonded to each other.
  • Common-identity groups: face-to-face interaction often absent; members linked via the category rather than each other.
  • Entitativity: the extent to which a group is perceived as a coherent whole. Entitative groups interact often, are important to members, share goals, and are similar in important ways.

Key components of groups:

ComponentMeaning
StatusGained for many reasons (physical characteristics to conformity to norms); higher for prototypical or senior members; high-status members are more idiosyncratic/variable and report conforming less.
RolesSets of behaviours expected of those in particular positions; powerful when internalised as part of identity; enacting a role well can feel authentic.
NormsRules/expectations about how members should behave. Feeling rules govern emotions (in collectivist groups: maintain harmony even at personal cost; in individualist groups: value standing out).
CohesivenessAll forces causing members to remain. Cohesive groups have solidarity, see themselves as homogeneous, support and cooperate with ingroup members, pursue group over individual goals, have high morale, and perform better. An outgroup/competitive threat increases cohesion.

Group development and socialisation

  • Bruce Tuckman (1965) five-stage developmental sequence: forming (orientation), storming (conflict over goals/practices), norming (consensus, cohesion, common identity), performing (smooth functioning, shared norms), adjourning (the group dissolves).
  • Moreland & Levine's group socialisation model: three basic processes (evaluation, commitment, role transition) leading to five phases. Transition criteria often become formalised as rites of passage / initiation rites serving three functions: symbolic, apprenticeship, loyalty elicitation.
  • Costs and benefits of joining: benefits include self-knowledge, progress toward goals, enhanced status, and a means of social change (when a politicised collective identity forms). Costs include loss of personal freedom and demands on time/energy/resources. After painful initiations people justify their effort and show increased positive attitudes toward the group. A schism (splintering into factions) causes emotional distress and loss of identification.

Coordination, cooperation and fairness

  • Cooperation = working together for shared goals; often fails because of social dilemmas (individuals gain by defection, but if all defect everyone loses). Negative interdependence (one's gain = another's loss) lowers cooperation.
  • Conflict = perceiving that others have taken or will take actions incompatible with one's interests; faulty attribution can drive it. Bargaining and superordinate goals (goals both sides seek that tie their interests together) reduce conflict.
  • Perceived fairness has three forms: distributive justice (fairness of outcomes/share of rewards), procedural justice (fairness of the procedures used to distribute), and transactional justice (how the treatment is delivered — whether distributors explain/justify decisions and show respect).

Group decision-making

  • Group polarization: groups make more extreme decisions than individuals. Two reasons: members want views more prototypical (hence more extreme) than average; and persuasive arguments in discussion shift members further in the dominant direction.
  • Groupthink (Janis): a tendency for highly cohesive groups to assume they can do no wrong and to reject information contrary to the group's view. Groups also reject criticism more from outgroup than ingroup members.
  • Hidden-profile / shared-information bias: members fail to share unique information; discussion focuses on what everyone already knows. A remedy is to keep members unaware of others' views before discussion.
  • Brainstorming does not produce more creativity than individuals working alone; but debate about ideas does stimulate more creative idea production.

Deindividuation

Definition

Deindividuation = a psychological state of reduced self-awareness brought on by external conditions such as being an anonymous member of a large crowd, encouraging unrestrained, antisocial action.

It is especially likely when people are in a large group, physically anonymous, and aroused/distracted. The diminished self-awareness and self-restraint increase responsiveness to the immediate situation (negative or positive). Deindividuation is less likely when self-awareness is high.

High-yield

Polarization = more extreme. Groupthink = cohesive groups suppress dissent (Janis). Deindividuation = anonymity/arousal → reduced self-awareness → unrestrained behaviour. Brainstorming in groups is not more creative than individuals alone.

6. Leadership styles

Leadership = getting group members to achieve the group's goals.

Trait and situational perspectives

  • Great person theory: attributes effective leadership to innate or acquired individual characteristics. The best Big Five predictors of effective leadership are being extraverted, open to experience, and conscientious.
  • Situational perspective: different styles are effective in different situations; the interaction of leader behaviour and situational factors matters.

Lewin's classic leadership styles

StyleBehaviour
AutocraticOrganised activities, gave orders, was aloof, focused exclusively on the task.
DemocraticCalled for suggestions, discussed plans, behaved like an ordinary member.
Laissez-faireLeft the group to its own devices, intervened minimally.

The two leadership dimensions (Ohio State, LBDQ)

The Leader Behaviour Description Questionnaire (LBDQ) drew a distinction between two dimensions: initiating structure (define objectives and organise members' work toward them — task-oriented) and consideration (concern for subordinates' welfare and harmonious relationships — relationship-oriented). A single person can be high on both, and such a person is a particularly effective leader.

Contingency theories

  • Fiedler's contingency theory: measures leadership style with the Least Preferred Coworker (LPC) scale (rate the person you least like working with on dimensions like pleasant–unpleasant, friendly–unfriendly). Effectiveness is contingent on three situational dimensions, in descending importance: (1) leader–member relations, (2) task structure, (3) position power (intrinsic authority of the leader's position).
  • Normative decision theory (NDT): focused on leadership in group decision-making; identifies three strategies — autocratic (no subordinate input), consultative (input sought, leader decides), group decision-making (leader and subordinates as equal partners). Effectiveness is contingent on leader–subordinate relations and task clarity/structure.
  • Path–goal theory: a leader's main function is to motivate followers by clarifying the paths to their goals; also classifiable as a transactional leadership theory. It distinguishes two LBDQ behaviours: structuring (most effective when followers are unclear about goals/how to reach them — new, difficult, ambiguous tasks; backfires as "meddling" when tasks are well understood) and consideration (most effective when the task is boring/uncomfortable, but not when followers are already engaged and motivated, where it can seem distracting).
High-yield

Fiedler = LPC scale + three situational dimensions (leader–member relations > task structure > position power). Ohio State LBDQ = initiating structure (task) vs consideration (relationship). Path–goal = a transactional theory; structuring helps unclear tasks, consideration helps boring tasks.

7. Prosocial behaviour

Definition

Prosocial behaviour = actions by individuals that help others with no immediate benefit to the helper.

Motives for helping

  • Empathy–altruism hypothesis: because of empathy, we help those in need out of empathic concern. Empathy = the capacity to experience others' emotional states, feel sympathy, and take their perspective, with three components: emotional empathy (sharing feelings), a cognitive component (empathic accuracy, perceiving thoughts/feelings accurately), and empathic concern (concern for another's well-being).
  • Negative-state relief model: we help to relieve and reduce our own negative emotional discomfort.
  • Empathic joy hypothesis: helping stems from the positive reactions recipients show, and the positive feelings this induces in helpers.
  • Competitive altruism: we help to increase our own status and reputation.
  • Kinship selection theory: we help relatives because it increases the chance our genes are passed on.
  • Reciprocal altruism theory: we help non-relatives because helping is usually reciprocated, indirectly aiding our own survival.
  • Defensive helping: helping to reduce the threat an outgroup poses to one's ingroup.

Responding to emergencies: the bystander effect

A bystander's response can range from apathy to heroism. Because of diffusion of responsibility, the more bystanders present, the less likely each is to help and the greater the delay (the bystander effect). Pluralistic ignorance is the tendency to do nothing because, not knowing what is happening, each bystander relies on others' (inactive) cues. The bystander effect is weaker among people who belong to the same group.

Definition

Darley & Latané (1968) five-step decision model: whether a bystander helps depends on five sequential decisions — (1) notice the event, (2) interpret it as an emergency, (3) assume responsibility, (4) know how to help, and (5) decide to implement the help. Failing any step means no help is given.

Factors and emotions in helping

  • We help those similar to us more (similarity mostly affects the emotional, not the cognitive, component of empathy); we help those we like and those not responsible for their need.
  • Helping is increased by exposure to prosocial models and by playing prosocial video games (which prime prosocial thoughts).
  • Gratitude increases helping by enhancing helpers' self-worth and self-efficacy.
  • Positive feelings increase helping; negative feelings tend to reduce it (though both can enhance or inhibit depending on the situation). Feelings of elevation (being inspired by others' kind acts) increase helping.
  • Empathy is weaker across group boundaries. Three types of perspective taking: "imagine other", "imagine self", and fantasy (empathy for a fictional character).
  • Factors that reduce helping: social exclusion (reduces capacity for empathy), darkness/anonymity, and putting an economic value on one's time.
  • Reactions to being helped: positive when help seems to stem from the helper's positive feelings (autonomous motivation); far less positive when help seems forced (controlled motivation). Low-status group members are reluctant to receive help from high-status groups, especially when seeking equality.

8. Aggression

Definition

Aggression = behaviour directed toward the goal of harming another living being who is motivated to avoid such treatment.

Theoretical perspectives

  • Drive theories (Berkowitz 1989; Feshbach 1984): aggression stems from externally elicited drives to harm. The most famous is the frustration–aggression hypothesis (Dollard, Doob, Miller, Mowrer & Sears, 1939), which made two sweeping claims: (1) frustration always leads to some form of aggression, and (2) aggression always stems from frustration. Both are now considered false: frustration is a strong elicitor only under limited conditions.
  • Social learning perspective (Bandura, 1997): we are not born with aggressive responses; we acquire them through direct experience or observation of others.
  • General Aggression Model (GAM) (Anderson & Bushman, 2002): two input classes — situational factors (frustration, provocation, aggressive models, discomfort) and person factors (irritability, pro-violence attitudes, hostile attribution tendency, aggression-related skills). These act through three processes: arousal, affective states (hostile feelings), and cognitions (hostile thoughts). Depending on appraisals and restraining factors, the person then engages in thoughtful or impulsive action.

Causes of aggression

  • Provocations: actions perceived as stemming from malicious intent — condescension (a very powerful source), harsh/unjustified criticism (especially personal criticism), and teasing.
  • Excitation-transfer theory: heightened arousal can increase aggression if it persists beyond its original situation and is misattributed as anger in a new context.
  • Social exclusion: rejection initiates a hostile cognitive mind-set, making us perceive ambiguous actions as hostile.
  • Media violence increases aggression via priming of aggressive thoughts, weakening of restraints, and desensitization. Violent video games increase aggressive cognition, affect and behaviour, and reduce empathy/prosocial behaviour (people play them for competence and mastery, not the violent content).

Cultural, personality and situational factors

  • Cultures of honor: cultures with strong norms that aggression is appropriate in response to insults to one's honor; impact especially apparent for sexual jealousy. Evolutionary view: men feel jealousy over sexual infidelity (paternal uncertainty), women over emotional infidelity (need for mate's assistance in child rearing); manhood is "precarious" and threats to it may trigger aggression to restore it.
  • TASS model (Traits As Situational Sensitivities): personality traits interact with situational factors; personal traits enhance aggression only when situational factors (e.g., provocation) are above threshold, and individual differences are eliminated when the situation is strong (high provocation).
  • Type A behaviour pattern: more irritable and aggressive than Type B; more likely to engage in hostile aggression (prime objective = harming the victim) but not more instrumental aggression (aggression to attain other goals such as resources or praise).
  • Narcissism: overinflated self-worth; react with exceptionally high aggression to ego-threatening feedback; view themselves as victims of others' transgressions.
  • Gender: males are more aggressive overall, but the difference is highly situation-dependent and eliminated under strong provocation. Males use more direct aggression, females more indirect; gender differences are smaller and more complex than once thought.
  • Situational determinants: high temperatures increase aggression up to a point (then decline); alcohol increases aggression in both sexes (reduces information-processing capacity).

Bullying and reducing aggression

Bullying = repeated assault on others with little/no power to retaliate. Motives: holding power, being part of a "tough" high-status group, and feeling better about oneself. Among boys, power and group motives predict bullying (not feeling depressed); for girls, all three motives do. Few children are solely bullies or victims; many switch roles. Bullies make hostile/dispositional attributions; bullies and bully-victims favour responding with aggression and have lower self-esteem. Cyberbullying = repeated online assaults (insults, exclusion, blackmail).

Reducing aggression:

  • Punishment works only when it is prompt, certain, strong, and perceived as justified.
  • Catharsis hypothesis (venting reduces aggression) is mainly false — venting tends to increase subsequent aggression.
  • Self-affirmation (bolstering self-esteem) can reduce aggression in high-narcissism people by preventing ego threats from triggering aggression.
Trap

The frustration–aggression hypothesis (Dollard et al., 1939) and the catharsis hypothesis are both classic "this is now considered FALSE" exam items. Hostile aggression (harm is the goal) vs instrumental aggression (harm is a means to another goal) is a frequent distinction.

9. Theories of intergroup relationship

How relations between groups produce prejudice, conflict and collective action.

Minimal group experiments & Social Identity Theory (Tajfel)

Tajfel and associates set out to define an intergroup situation where social categorisation occurs on a trivial criterion but none of the usual conditions of intergroup conflict are present. The conditions were: no face-to-face interaction, complete anonymity of group membership, no instrumental/rational link between criteria and rewards, no utilitarian value of the rewards to the subject, and a number of allocation options.

The first minimal group experiment (Tajfel, 1970): 64 British schoolboys (14–15) did a dot-estimation task and were assigned (actually randomly) to "overestimator/underestimator" or "more/less accurate" groups. They allocated real money to anonymous in-group and out-group members (identified only by code numbers) via a matrix offering strategies: Fairness (F), Maximum Joint Profit (MJP), Maximum In-group Profit (MIP), and Maximum Difference in favour of the in-group (MD).

Result: subjects favoured the in-group, endorsing a combination of MD and MIP most often — even at the expense of absolute in-group profit. Tajfel concluded that mere social categorisation produces discriminatory intergroup behaviour without any of the normal conditions of conflict.

Definition

Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner): people are motivated to maintain or achieve a positive self-identity. Three central processes: social categorisation (segmenting the world to impose order and provide a locus of self-identification), social identity (the part of the self-concept derived from group membership, with its value and emotional significance), and social comparison (comparing in-group to out-group, drawing on Festinger's 1954 theory). The goal is positive psychological group distinctiveness: an identity perceived as distinct and positive vis-à-vis relevant comparison groups. Negative social identity → dissatisfaction → (if cognitive alternatives are perceived) attempts to change the situation.

Social change and social mobility

A strategy for social change is embarked upon only when cognitive alternatives to the existing situation are perceived, which depends on two factors: stability–instability (can the hierarchy be changed?) and legitimacy–illegitimacy (is the situation just?). A disadvantaged group perceiving cognitive alternatives may: be absorbed into the dominant group, redefine the negatively evaluated characteristic, create new dimensions of comparison, or directly compete. Social mobility assumes the system is flexible and permeable, permitting free movement of individuals.

Relative deprivation theory

Definition

Relative deprivation = a perceived discrepancy between attainments ("what is") and expectations/entitlements ("what ought to be"). It is always relative to other conditions; deprivation is not absolute.

  • James Davies (1969) J-curve model: when attainments suddenly fall short of rising expectations, relative deprivation is acute, producing collective unrest — "revolutions of rising expectations".
  • Gary Runciman (1966) distinguished egoistic relative deprivation (deprivation relative to similar individuals) from fraternalistic relative deprivation (comparison with dissimilar others / other groups). They are independent. Walker & Mann (1987): fraternalistic deprivation predicts willingness for militant protest; egoistic deprivation predicts personal stress symptoms (headaches, indigestion, sleeplessness).
  • Fraternalistic deprivation translates into protest only with: strong ingroup identification, belief that action is feasible, perceptions of injustice (procedural injustice especially potent), and a particular ingroup–outgroup comparison.

Social protest and collective action

Response to deprivation often involves sustained social protest. Sympathisers hold supportive attitudes that don't automatically become behaviour; they can be mobilised into activists. Klandermans views protest as intergroup behaviour occurring in "multiorganisational fields", with four steps to participation: becoming part of the mobilisation potential, becoming a target of mobilisation attempts, developing motivation to participate, and overcoming barriers to participation.

Realistic conflict theory (Muzafer Sherif)

A key feature of intergroup behaviour is ethnocentrism, whose origins (Sherif 1962) lie in the nature of intergroup relations: where groups compete over scarce resources, relations become conflictual and ethnocentric. Tested in the famous Robber's Cave experiments (field experiments at summer camps, 1949/1953/1954):

  • Phase 1: boys arrived and formed friendships in camp-wide activities.
  • Phase 2: camp split into two groups (breaking friendships); isolated groups developed their own norms and status differences.
  • Phase 3: intergroup competitions generated fierce hostility that generalised beyond the contests.
  • Phase 4: introducing superordinate goals (desired by both but unachievable alone) gradually improved relations through cooperation.

Cooperation, competition and social dilemmas

DilemmaStructure
Prisoner's dilemmaTwo guilty suspects questioned separately; each can confess for immunity. If both confess = moderate sentence; if neither = light sentence. A "two-person, mixed-motive, non-zero-sum game" (a zero-sum game = one's gain is always the other's loss).
Trucking gameTwo players run trucking companies; a short shared one-lane route causes repeated head-on conflict and wasted time arguing.
Commons dilemmaAn n-person prisoner's dilemma: cooperation by all benefits all, competition by all harms all. The classic example (100 farmers, 100-cow common) yields the tragedy of the commons when each adds an extra cow.

Equity theory

Equity theory specifies conditions for intergroup conflict and its resolution. Justice = a socially defined standard for evaluating resource distribution. People define justice from inputs (contributions: attributes, abilities, efforts) and outcomes (tangible/intangible rewards or punishments). For justice, outcomes need not be equal; the ratio of outcomes to inputs must be equal (Ox/Ix = Oy/Iy). Walster, Berscheid & Walster (1973) revised the formula to permit negative inputs/outcomes (using absolute values). Perceived inequity causes psychological distress; equity can be restored by actual restoration (changing real inputs/outcomes) or psychological restoration (cognitively distorting the perceived inputs/outcomes).

Balance theory (Fritz Heider, 1946)

The p-o-x (balance) theory: originally about the sentiments of a person (p) toward another (o) and an impersonal object (x) belonging to o; later expanded to general interpersonal relations. Heider proposed two relation types: unit relations (U / not-U: elements seen as belonging together or not, governed by Gestalt factors of similarity, proximity, common fate, good continuation, set, past experience) and sentiment relations (L / DL: liking, admiring vs disliking, rejecting).

  • Balance in dyads: balanced when both relations are positive or both negative; imbalanced when one is positive and one negative.
  • Balance in triads: balanced when all three relations are positive, or when two are negative and one positive; imbalanced when one is negative and two positive; ambiguous when all three are negative. (Always judged from p's viewpoint.)
  • Consequences of the balance tendency: preference for balanced states, induction of new relations to produce balance, and pressure to change imbalanced states (by altering either sentiment or unit relations).
Mnemonic

Triad balance = "multiply the signs to +." A triad is balanced when the product of the three signs is positive (+++ or +−−). One negative + two positives gives a product of −, which is imbalanced and feels uncomfortable.

Social exchange theory (George Homans, 1961)

Homans explained elementary face-to-face interaction as the psychological exchange of rewards (positive reinforcements) and costs (negative reinforcements) between two persons. He limited the theory to behaviour that is social, directly reacted to by the other party (not a third party), and actual (not a mere norm). His unit of analysis was the elementary face-to-face social interchange; the dyad and small group are the building blocks of larger social units. Three descriptive terms: activity, sentiment, interaction; two key variables: quantity (frequency) and value (degree of reinforcement).

Homans' propositions of human exchange:

  • Success: the more often an action is rewarded, the more likely it is performed (a derivative of Thorndike's law of effect).
  • Stimulus: the more similar present stimuli are to past rewarded ones, the more likely the action (stimulus generalisation / secondary reinforcement).
  • Value: the more valuable the result, the more likely the action.
  • Deprivation–satiation: the more often a reward has recently been received, the less valuable further units become.
  • Aggression–approval (5a/5b): unexpected non-reward/punishment → anger and aggression; expected (or greater-than-expected) reward → pleasure and approving behaviour.
  • Rationality: a person chooses the action whose value × probability of success is greatest.

Rule of distributive justice: a person in exchange expects rewards proportional to costs, and net profits proportional to investments; evaluations of "just return" are based on past exchange experience, which sets up an expected "exchange rate".

High-yield

Tajfel = social identity / minimal groups (MD & MIP). Sherif = realistic conflict / Robber's Cave / superordinate goals. Heider = balance (p-o-x). Homans = social exchange (rewards/costs, six propositions). Davies = J-curve; Runciman = egoistic vs fraternalistic relative deprivation; Walster et al. = equity ratio.

The contact hypothesis & reducing prejudice

Prejudice (a negative attitude toward an outgroup) is reduced by intergroup contact under favourable conditions (the contact hypothesis, traced to Gordon Allport's 1954 Nature of Prejudice): equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support. Robber's Cave demonstrated that contact alone was insufficient — superordinate goals requiring cooperation were what reduced hostility.

10. Applying social psychology

How social-psychological principles are applied to clinical/health work, the law, and the environment.

Social psychology in clinical / health settings

  • Illusory correlations: the tendency to see correlations where none exist. Clinicians may perceive illusory correlations during diagnosis; hindsight explanations breed overconfidence; erroneous diagnoses can be self-confirming because interviewers seek and recall confirming information. This underscores the need for rigorous testing of intuitive conclusions and statistical prediction.
  • Depressive realism: mildly depressed people make more accurate (rather than self-serving) judgments. Depressed people have a negative, pessimistic attributional/explanatory style, attributing failure to stable, global, internal causes. Depression is a vicious circle in which negative thoughts elicit self-defeating behaviours.
  • Loneliness (common in individualistic societies) and shyness (a form of social anxiety marked by self-consciousness) are similar vicious circles in which negative feelings elicit off-putting behaviour. Loneliness can prompt nostalgia, which reminds people of social connections.
  • Health psychology studies how people decide they are ill, explain symptoms, and seek/follow treatment, and the links among illness, stress and a pessimistic explanatory style.

Therapy and social relations

  • Consistent with the attitudes-follow-behaviour principle, several therapies prescribe action: we can influence feelings indirectly through behaviour. The vicious circle of depression/loneliness/anxiety can be broken at any point — by changing the environment, training constructive behaviour, or reversing negative thinking (social skills training and explanatory-style therapy).
  • Improvements are best maintained when people attribute them to internal factors under their continued control rather than to the treatment program. Changing clients' attitudes requires persuasion; therapists act as expert, trustworthy communicators.
  • Social relations and health: close, supportive relationships lower risk of illness and premature death and foster happiness (e.g., married adults report being happier and are at less risk for depression), partly by helping people confide intimate emotions.

Social psychology in law

  • Eyewitness testimony accuracy is impaired by many factors. Assertive witnesses are more likely to be believed, although assertiveness does not reflect actual certainty. The human eye is not a video camera.
  • Misinformation effect: incorporating misleading post-event information into one's memory so the false information is believed true. Repeated retelling lets errors creep in. To reduce errors, let witnesses recount without interruption and encourage them to visualise the scene and their emotional state.
  • Juror judgments: with ambiguous evidence, jurors interpret it through preconceived biases and sympathise with attractive/similar defendants. Pretrial publicity and inadmissible evidence often influence jurors despite judicial instruction (especially when the admonition comes after an impression is formed). Lawyers use jury consultants; disqualifying death-penalty opponents itself produces a biased jury. Group deliberation can entrench and polarise views, though it may make jurors more lenient when evidence is weakly incriminating.

Social psychology and the environment

  • Humanity can prepare for a sustainable future by increasing technological efficiency, creating incentives, and changing actions and attitudes to control population and moderate consumption.
  • People in rich nations report greater happiness than those in poor nations, yet materialism fails to satisfy, explained by two principles: the adaptation-level phenomenon (adapting to a level of stimulation and reacting only to changes from it) and social comparison (evaluating oneself against others).
  • To build a satisfying, sustainable future, individuals and societies can promote close relationships, belief-based social networks, positive thinking habits, and engaging activity (flow).
High-yield

Applied themes: illusory correlation + self-confirming diagnosis (clinical judgment); depressive realism + pessimistic explanatory style (stable/global/internal); attitudes-follow-behaviour therapy + internal attribution of improvement; misinformation effect + eyewitness fallibility (law); adaptation-level + social comparison + flow (environment/well-being).
