1. Issues of gender

The one distinction to never blur. The whole topic rests on separating two words the public uses interchangeably. Sex is the biological distinction between male and female, fixed at conception by the chromosomes: the male karyotype is XY and the female karyotype is XX. Gender is the culturally constructed categorisation of maleness and femaleness, that is, the social meanings of masculinity and femininity. Sex is given by biology; gender is built by culture.

Definition

Gender = the culturally constructed categorisation of maleness and femaleness (masculinity / femininity). Sex = the biological distinction of male (XY chromosome) and female (XX chromosome).

Gender stereotypes

A gender stereotype is a widely held belief about the abilities, personality traits, and social behaviour of females and males (the source attributes this definition to Weiten, 2001). Stereotypes are not the same as differences that actually exist; they are beliefs about the sexes, which may be exaggerated, inaccurate, or self-fulfilling. They feed the discrimination patterns described later in this book (glass ceiling, maternal wall, etc.).

Minimalist vs maximalist views of gender differences

How big are the psychological differences between men and women? The field splits into two camps:

  • Maximalists believe in huge, fundamental differences between men and women. On this view the sexes are deeply, qualitatively distinct.
  • Minimalists believe there are few fundamental differences between them. On this view the apparent gaps are small, situational, or socially produced rather than essential.
Trap

Maximalist = MAXimum difference; minimalist = MINimum difference. Maximalists emphasise large, fundamental sex differences; minimalists emphasise that the genuine fundamental differences are few. Do not confuse the camps because the names sound technical.

High-yield

Sex = biological (XY male, XX female). Gender = cultural construction of masculinity/femininity. Gender stereotype = widely held belief about males'/females' abilities, traits and social behaviour (Weiten, 2001). Maximalists = big differences; minimalists = few fundamental differences.

2. Types of feminism

The source lists five branches of feminism. Each answers the same question, "what is the root of women's oppression and how do we end it?", in a different way. Learn them by their diagnosis and their strategy, because that is what is tested.

BranchCore idea / diagnosisStrategy
Liberal feminismWorks within the structure of mainstream society. Its roots reach back to social-contract theory of government, the idea instituted by the American Revolution.Integrate women into the existing structure (equal access, equal rights) rather than overthrow it.
Radical feminismSeen by many as the "undesirable" element of feminism, yet it is actually the breeding ground for many of the ideas that other branches shape and pound out. Holds that women's oppression is the deepest, original oppression.Tackle the root system of male domination (patriarchy) directly; the source ties consciousness raising to this branch.
Socialist feminismThe result of Marxism meeting radical feminism. Marxism recognises that women are oppressed and attributes the oppression to the capitalist / private-property system.The only way to end women's oppression is to overthrow the capitalist system.
Cultural feminismRetreated to vanguardism, working instead to build a women's culture. Some of this effort has had real social benefit.Create and value a distinct women's culture rather than fight for integration.
Eco-feminismMuch more spiritual than political or theoretical in nature. May or may not be wrapped up with Goddess worship and vegetarianism. Its basic tenet: a patriarchal/hierarchical society will exploit its resources without regard to long-term consequences, as a direct result of the attitudes fostered in such a society.Link the domination of women to the domination of nature; resist both.
Mnemonic

"Liberals Lobby (work within), Radicals Root out patriarchy, Socialists Smash capitalism, Cultural Creates a women's culture, Eco links women & Earth." — L-R-S-C-E.

High-yield

Socialist feminism = Marxism + radical feminism, blames the capitalist/private-property system. Radical feminism is the "breeding ground" of feminist ideas and is the home of consciousness raising. Liberal feminism = integration within the system. Eco-feminism = spiritual, links patriarchy to environmental exploitation.

3. Feminist psychology

Feminist psychology aims to understand the individual within the larger social and political aspects of society. Its defining move is to refuse to study a person in isolation: a woman's distress is read against the background of her social and political situation, not just her internal makeup.

Key figures named in the source

  • Karen Horney — Feminine Psychoanalysis. Horney challenged Freud from within psychoanalysis, replacing his "penis envy" account of female development with a social and cultural reading of women's psychology. She is the named author of a feminine psychoanalysis, foregrounding women's experience rather than treating it as a deviation from a male standard.
  • Edward Strecker — "Mom-ism". Strecker is associated with the concept of Mom-ism, a (controversial, mother-blaming) idea critiqued within feminist psychology for pathologising motherhood and women's roles.

Consciousness raising

Consciousness raising is closely tied with radical feminism. It aims to weed out discrimination and segregation based on sex, and it maintains a key radical-feminist claim: women's oppression is not a by-product of capitalist oppression but a "primary cause" in its own right. In other words, against socialist feminism (which traces oppression back to capitalism), consciousness raising treats the oppression of women as primary, not derivative.

Trap

Both socialist feminism and consciousness raising mention capitalism, but they take opposite sides. Socialist feminism says women's oppression comes from capitalism. Consciousness raising (radical) insists it is not a by-product of capitalism but a primary cause.

High-yield

Feminist psychology = understand the individual within the larger social and political context. Karen Horney = feminine psychoanalysis; Edward Strecker = "Mom-ism"; consciousness raising = radical feminism, oppression of women as a primary cause.

4. Workplace barriers: the glass-ceiling family

The source gives four named "invisible barrier" effects that describe how discrimination keeps women out of, or trapped in, particular places in the labour market. They are routinely tested as a matching set, so learn each by its precise location and mechanism.

EffectWhat it describes
Glass ceiling effectWhen women can't reach higher ranks, pay or positions because of discrimination, limiting beliefs, etc. The barrier is at the top, invisible but real.
Maternal wallThe stereotypes and forms of discrimination encountered by working mothers and by mothers seeking employment. Bias triggered specifically by motherhood.
Sticky floor effectA discriminatory employment pattern that keeps workers, mainly women, in the lower ranks of the job scale. The barrier is at the bottom; they cannot rise off the floor.
Glass-cliff effectWhen a woman is promoted to a leadership role of an organisation that is about to collapse, set up to be a scapegoat for the failure. A precarious, high-risk promotion.
Trap

Ceiling vs floor vs cliff. Glass ceiling = blocked at the top. Sticky floor = stuck at the bottom. Glass cliff = promoted, but onto a failing organisation so she takes the blame. Maternal wall = bias specifically against mothers.

Mnemonic

Top blocked = ceiling; bottom stuck = floor; promoted-to-fall = cliff; mother-penalty = wall.

5. Feminist therapy

Feminist therapy is a type of therapy based on viewing individuals within their sociocultural context. It carries the central commitment of feminist psychology into the consulting room: the client's problems are understood against her social and cultural situation, not treated as purely intrapsychic faults.

Two features that define the approach

  • Not just for women. Feminist therapy is not confined to women; it addresses gender-role conformities for clients of any gender, that is, the costs of rigidly conforming to prescribed gender roles.
  • An egalitarian relationship. The client–therapist relationship is meant to be as egalitarian as possible, with both sides communicating on equal ground and sharing experiences. The therapist deliberately flattens the usual power hierarchy of treatment, which is itself a therapeutic statement about power.
High-yield

Feminist therapy = individuals understood within their sociocultural context; not confined to women (it addresses gender-role conformity in general); the therapeutic relationship is deliberately egalitarian, with shared, equal-ground communication.

6. Issues in traditional therapies

Feminist therapy arose as a critique of biases built into traditional therapy. The source names three problems.

Gender bias

Many traditional therapies assume that women should follow sex-roles in order to be mentally healthy. They treat gender differences as entirely biologically based and therefore encourage female clients to be submissive, expressive, and nurturant in order to achieve fulfillment. Mental health is thus equated with conformity to a feminine role.

Androcentrism

Androcentrism means treating being male as the norm. Feminine traits are then viewed as a deviation from the norm and as a deficiency on the part of women. The male is the yardstick; the female is measured as a falling-short of it.

Definition

Androcentrism = taking the male as the standard/norm, so that feminine traits appear as a deviation from, and a deficiency relative to, that norm.

Intrapsychic assumptions

Traditional therapy locates the trouble inside the client. As a result, therapists tend to blame clients for their symptoms, even when the client may in fact be coping admirably in a difficult and oppressive situation, and they may pathologise normal responses to oppressive environments. A reasonable reaction to an unjust situation gets relabelled as the client's disorder.

Trap

The intrapsychic assumption is the engine of victim-blaming: it treats a healthy reaction to an oppressive environment as a personal pathology. Feminist therapy's correction is the slogan "the personal is political" — relocate the problem from inside the person to the social situation.

7. Principles of empowerment & feminist techniques

Principles of empowerment

Feminist therapy works from three empowerment principles:

  • Personal is political. A client's private distress is connected to wider social and political conditions; her problems are not purely individual failings.
  • Egalitarian relationships. Therapy models equal power between client and therapist rather than expert-over-patient.
  • Value the female perspective. Women's experiences and ways of knowing are treated as valid and valuable, not as deviations to be corrected.

Techniques

  • Sex-role analysis — a critique of cultural conditioning: examining how the client has been shaped by the gender roles society imposed on her, and what those roles cost her.
  • Power analysis — examining power differentials: making visible the unequal power between the client and others (partners, employers, institutions) and how it bears on her difficulties.
  • Assertiveness training — teaching the client to express needs and set boundaries, countering the trained submissiveness that gender bias rewards.

The source also flags poverty alongside these techniques, signalling that material deprivation is part of the social context feminist therapy must address, which leads to the next major area.

High-yield

Empowerment principles: personal is political, egalitarian relationships, value the female perspective. Techniques: sex-role analysis (critique of cultural conditioning), power analysis (examining power differentials), assertiveness training.

8. The psychology of poverty

Poverty is a condition in which a person or community is deprived of, or lacks, the essentials for a minimum standard of wellbeing and life. It is an emerging area for psychology because deprivation shapes development, stress, health and mental disorder, and because the intrapsychic-assumption trap above (blaming individuals for circumstance) bites hardest here.

Types of poverty

TypeMeaning
Absolute povertyThe scarcity of basic food, clean water, health, shelter, education and information. People in absolute poverty struggle to survive and suffer many child deaths from preventable diseases like malaria, cholera and water-contamination-related diseases.
Relative povertyDefined from a social perspective: living standard compared to the economic standard of the surrounding population. It is therefore a measure of income inequality, often measured as the percentage of the population with income below some fixed proportion of median income.
Situational povertyA temporary poverty caused by an adverse event such as an environmental disaster, job loss, or severe health problem.
Generational povertyPoverty handed down from one generation to the next. It is more complicated because there is no easy escape: people are trapped and cannot access the tools needed to get out. The source quotes Jensen (2009): it "occurs in families where at least two generations have been born into poverty… not equipped with the tools to move out of their situation."
Rural povertyOccurs in rural areas with population below 50,000, where there are fewer job opportunities, less access to services, less support for disabilities, and fewer quality education opportunities.
Urban povertyOccurs in metropolitan areas with population over 50,000. Marked by limited access to health and education, inadequate housing and services, a violent and unhealthy environment due to overcrowding, and little or no social-protection mechanism.
Trap

Absolute vs relative poverty. Absolute = lacking the basics for survival (food, water, shelter), an objective threshold. Relative = below the standard of those around you, a measure of inequality tied to median income. And note the population line: rural < 50,000, urban > 50,000.

High-yield

Six types: absolute (no basics), relative (below surrounding standard = inequality, vs median income), situational (temporary, from an adverse event), generational (handed down, ≥2 generations born into it — Jensen 2009), rural (pop < 50,000), urban (pop > 50,000).

9. Wellbeing & self-growth

This is the positive-psychology heart of the unit: instead of asking what makes people ill, it asks what makes life good and what helps people flourish. It opens with the two great traditions of what "wellbeing" even means.

Two conceptions of wellbeing

Eudaimonic wellbeingHedonistic wellbeing
Equates wellbeing withThe cultivation of personal strengths and contribution to the greater good, acting in accordance with one's deeper nature and values.Pleasure and happiness.
Key featuresActing in accordance with one's inner nature and deeply held values; the realization of one's true potential; the experience of purpose or meaning in life.Maximising positive feeling and enjoyment, minimising pain.
Definition

Eudaimonic wellbeing = wellbeing as living in line with one's true nature and values, realising one's potential, and finding meaning. Hedonistic wellbeing = wellbeing as pleasure and happiness.

Mnemonic

"Hedonist chases the feeling (pleasure/happiness); eudaimon chases the fulfilment (meaning/potential/values)." The two F's tell them apart.

10. Models of wellbeing

The source lists four wellbeing models. Learn the named author and the component list of each.

The holistic view of wellbeing

A holistic model treats wellbeing as multi-domain. The source names the domains as emotional, intellectual, spiritual, occupational, social, and physical wellness. Health is not just absence of illness but balance across all these domains.

Subjective wellbeing — Diener's tripartite model

Subjective wellbeing (SWB) consists of three components (Diener's tripartite model):

  • Cognitive evaluation of life satisfaction (a judgement of how one's life is going);
  • Presence of positive affect;
  • Relative absence of negative affect.

SWB is the standard operationalisation of the hedonic tradition: a satisfied judgement plus lots of good feeling and little bad feeling.

Carol Ryff's Six-Factor Model of Psychological Well-Being

The Six-Factor Model of Psychological Well-Being was developed by Carol Ryff. It identifies six factors that contribute to an individual's psychological wellbeing, contentment and happiness. This is the leading eudaimonic model. The six factors are:

  • Autonomy
  • Environmental Mastery
  • Personal Growth
  • Positive Relations with Others
  • Purpose in Life
  • Self-Acceptance
Mnemonic

Ryff's six: "Autonomy, Environmental mastery, Personal growth, Positive relations, Purpose, Self-acceptance" → A-E-P-P-P-S ("A-E and three P's and S").

PERMA model — Seligman

The PERMA model was given by Martin Seligman and has five measurable elements:

  • Positive emotions
  • Engagement
  • Relationships
  • Meaning and purpose
  • Accomplishment
Trap

Don't cross the authors: Diener = the tripartite subjective wellbeing model (life satisfaction + positive affect + low negative affect). Ryff = the six-factor psychological wellbeing model. Seligman = PERMA (five elements). Ryff has SIX factors; PERMA has FIVE.

High-yield

Diener → SWB, 3 parts (life-satisfaction cognition + positive affect + relative absence of negative affect). Ryff → 6 factors (Autonomy, Environmental Mastery, Personal Growth, Positive Relations, Purpose in Life, Self-Acceptance). Seligman → PERMA (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment). Holistic view = 6 wellness domains.

11. Character strengths & virtues

Character Strengths and Virtues (CSV) is described as a groundbreaking handbook that compiles the work of researchers to create a classification system for widely valued positive traits. It is, in effect, positive psychology's answer to the diagnostic manual: instead of cataloguing disorders, it catalogues what is best in people. (CSV was authored by Peterson and Seligman; it is often called the "un-DSM".)

The system contains 6 classes of virtues that are made up of 24 character strengths. The six virtue classes are universally valued across cultures, and the 24 strengths are the measurable routes to expressing them.

High-yield

Character Strengths and Virtues = a classification of widely valued positive traits, structured as 6 virtues built from 24 character strengths. Think of it as the positive-psychology counterpart to the DSM.

12. Post-traumatic growth

Post-traumatic growth (PTG) is the counterpart to PTSD: the same struggle that can break a person can, far more often, change them for the better. The source stresses the asymmetry: most trauma survivors report growth after their experience, while only a few develop PTSD.

Definition

Post-traumatic growth (a term coined by Tedeschi and Calhoun) = the positive psychological change experienced as a result of the struggle with highly challenging life circumstances.

The five areas of post-traumatic growth

  • Appreciation of life — a renewed sense of the value of being alive.
  • Relating to others — deeper, closer relationships.
  • Personal strength — a sense of "if I survived that, I am stronger than I knew."
  • New possibilities — new paths, goals and opportunities open up.
  • Spiritual, existential or philosophical change — a transformed worldview or faith.
Trap

PTG is not simply the absence of PTSD, and it does not mean the trauma was good. It is genuine positive change that grows out of the struggle with adversity. Note the key statistic the source emphasises: growth is the common outcome; PTSD is the rare one.

High-yield

PTG coined by Tedeschi & Calhoun = positive psychological change from struggling with highly challenging circumstances. Five areas: appreciation of life, relating to others, personal strength, new possibilities, spiritual/existential/philosophical change. Most survivors grow; only a few develop PTSD.

13. Cyberpsychology

Cyberpsychology studies the psychological effects of the internet and digital technology on human behaviour. The source focuses on two phenomena: why people behave badly online (online disinhibition) and how internet use becomes a disorder (internet addiction).

Online disinhibition

Cyberbullying occurs due to a phenomenon called online disinhibition: people say and do things online that they would not say or do face-to-face, because the usual social brakes are loosened. The anonymity, invisibility and distance of the screen lower the inhibitions that normally restrain hostile behaviour. The source credits Suler with identifying the areas (components) of online disinhibition.

Definition

Online disinhibition (Suler) = the loosening or abandonment of the social restrictions and inhibitions that would normally be present in face-to-face interaction, occurring when people are online. It is the mechanism behind cyberbullying.

Internet addiction disorder (IAD)

The source states that the APA has included internet addiction as a disorder in DSM-5, and lists four components essential for a diagnosis of IAD:

  • Excessive internet use — using the internet far more than intended, losing track of time.
  • Withdrawal — feelings of tension, anger or depression when access is denied.
  • Tolerance — needing more and more (better equipment, more hours, more content) to get the same satisfaction.
  • Adverse consequences — negative effects on work, relationships, health and functioning.
Mnemonic

The four IAD components map onto the classic addiction tetrad: E-W-T-A — Excessive use, Withdrawal, Tolerance, Adverse consequences.

Trap

The source follows the deck's claim that internet addiction was "included as a disorder in DSM-5." In the actual DSM-5, only Internet Gaming Disorder appears, and only in Section III as a condition for further study, not as a fully recognised diagnosis. For this paper, answer per the source (four components: excessive use, withdrawal, tolerance, adverse consequences) but know the real DSM-5 nuance.

High-yield

Cyberbullying is driven by online disinhibition (areas identified by Suler). Internet addiction disorder has four diagnostic components: excessive internet use, withdrawal, tolerance, adverse consequences.
