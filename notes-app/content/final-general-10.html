In a primate laboratory at the University of Wisconsin in Madison, at the end of the 1940s, a young rhesus monkey sat in its home cage fiddling with a small wooden board. Screwed to the board was a puzzle made of three pieces of hardware: a metal pin, a hook and a hasp. To undo the hasp, the monkey had to pull the pin first, then lift the hook, and only then swing the hasp open. There was nothing behind the hasp. No raisin, no peanut, no sip of juice. The monkey had been fed and watered as usual. Yet it worked at the puzzle, again and again, and when the experimenters put the pieces back together it took them apart again.

The experimenters were Harry Harlow, Margaret Kuenne Harlow and Donald Meyer, and in 1950 they reported what they had seen. Monkeys left with assembled puzzles for about two weeks became fast, nearly error-free solvers. Monkeys given the same puzzles already taken apart, so that there was nothing to solve, did not. Harlow even reported that bringing food into the test tended to disrupt the monkeys' smooth performance rather than improve it. The monkeys, the authors concluded, were driven by the activity itself. They called it a manipulation drive.

Why should this small observation have caused such a stir? Because in 1950 the most powerful theory in American psychology said it should not happen. According to that theory, an animal learns a response only when the response reduces a biological need: hunger, thirst, pain. Harlow's monkeys were not hungry, not thirsty and not in pain. What need was being reduced? If you have ever done the newspaper Sudoku on a bus to Mysuru, or stayed up past midnight to finish a crossword that nobody will ever check, you already know the answer the theory could not give.

This chapter is about that puzzle: why animals and people do what they do. You will meet instincts, drives, arousal, incentives, needs, expectancies and the self, roughly in the order psychology met them. Keep Harlow's monkeys in mind as you read; we will come back to them at the end of the chapter.

10.1 What Motivation IsMedium yield · 7 Qs

The word motivation comes from the Latin movere, "to move". In psychology it refers to the processes that start behaviour, give it direction, set its intensity and keep it going until it ends. Notice the four parts of that definition, because questions are built on them. Activation is the starting of behaviour (you get up from your desk). Direction is the choice of one goal over others (you walk to the kitchen, not the television). Intensity is how much effort goes in (you stride, you do not stroll). Persistence is how long the behaviour continues in the face of obstacles (you keep searching after finding the biscuit tin empty).

No one has ever seen a motive. You see a student studying until 2 a.m., and you infer that something inside is pushing or pulling him. Motivation is therefore a hypothetical construct, an intervening variable placed between what we can observe (hours without food, a promised reward) and what we can measure (speed, effort, persistence). This is why the history of motivation is a history of competing guesses about what that invisible something is.

Need, drive, motive, incentive and goal

Five words recur in every theory in this chapter, and the exam treats them as distinct. Take one example, thirst, and follow it through.

A need is a state of deficiency or lack within the organism. After a long afternoon in a Mysuru summer, your body is short of water. That shortage is a physiological need, and it exists whether or not you feel it. A drive is the aroused, tense psychological state that a need produces and that energises behaviour. You feel thirsty, restless and irritable. Need is the biological condition; drive is its psychological consequence. The two are not perfectly correlated: a person can have a need without a strong drive (old people can become dangerously dehydrated without feeling very thirsty), and a drive can outlast the need.

A motive is the broader term for any internal state that arouses and directs behaviour towards a goal. In practice "drive" is reserved for biologically based states and "motive" for learned, psychological and social ones, such as the motive to achieve or to be liked. John Atkinson used "motive" in a narrower sense still: a stable personality disposition that a person carries from situation to situation.

An incentive is an external stimulus or object that attracts (positive incentive) or repels (negative incentive) the organism. A cold bottle of water in a shop window is an incentive; so is the thought of the chai stall after the exam. A goal is the end state towards which motivated behaviour is directed, the incentive as it is represented and pursued: reaching the water and drinking it. The commonly taught shorthand is that drives push from inside and incentives pull from outside.

The motivational cycle

Put the five terms in order and you get the motivational cycle: a need gives rise to a drive; the drive energises instrumental behaviour, that is, goal-directed activity that is a means to an end; the behaviour reaches the goal or incentive; reaching the goal reduces the drive and brings relief; and after a while the need builds up again and the cycle restarts. Figure 10.1 draws the cycle for thirst. The cycle is a useful teaching device, but notice that it is drawn from one theory, drive reduction, which you will meet in Section 10.3. Much of the rest of the chapter is about behaviour that does not fit it.

Classifying motives

The most common classification divides motives by their origin. The primary motives, also called biological, physiological or unlearned motives, are rooted in the body's survival needs and are present without learning: hunger, thirst, the need for oxygen, sleep, avoidance of pain, temperature regulation, elimination, sex and, in many textbooks, the maternal drive. Most of them are homeostatic (they correct a bodily imbalance); sex and the maternal drive are the usual exceptions, because no tissue is depleted when they are unsatisfied.

The secondary motives, also called psychosocial, social, acquired or learned motives, develop through experience and culture: achievement, affiliation, power, approval, aggression, curiosity about a particular subject, the wish for money. They vary enormously between people and between cultures. Between the two sits a group that some textbooks (following Clifford Morgan and Richard King) call general or unlearned non-homeostatic motives: curiosity, activity, manipulation and the need for contact comfort. They seem to be inborn, like primary motives, yet they repair no tissue deficit. Harlow's monkeys belong here. Table 10.1 sets out the three classes side by side.

The personality theorist Henry Murray (you will meet him properly in Section 10.6) drew the same line with his own words. His viscerogenic needs are the primary needs tied to bodily events (air, water, food, sex, urination, avoidance of heat, cold and harm). His psychogenic needs are the secondary needs that arise from mental life and are only loosely connected to the body (achievement, affiliation, dominance, autonomy, order).

Motives can also be classified by awareness: conscious motives are ones the person can report, and unconscious motives (the core of Freud's theory) drive behaviour without the person knowing why. Finally, the American personality psychologist Gordon Willard Allport (1897 to 1967) pointed out in 1937 that a learned motive can cut loose from its origins. A man who went to sea to earn a living may, when rich, still long for the sea. Allport called this functional autonomy: a means can become an end in itself, so today's motive need not be explained by the need that started it.

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

A second, cross-cutting distinction concerns where the reward lies. The desire to do something for its own sake, because the activity itself is interesting, enjoyable or satisfying, is called intrinsic motivation. The desire to do something for a separable outcome (money, marks, praise, or the avoidance of punishment) is called extrinsic motivation. A child who reads comics under the blanket is intrinsically motivated; the same child reading the prescribed textbook to escape a scolding is extrinsically motivated.

A classic past-paper example: a toddler goes to great lengths to open a box that she knows is empty. Nothing is inside, so there is no external payoff; the opening is the reward. That is intrinsic motivation. It is not homeostasis (no bodily balance is being restored), not extrinsic motivation (there is no separable reward) and not secondary motivation (nothing learned or social is being served). The two kinds of motivation can interact in surprising ways, and Section 10.7 shows how an external reward can sometimes kill an internal interest.

Clinical link

The intrinsic-extrinsic distinction is used diagnostically. In malingering a person deliberately produces or exaggerates symptoms for an obvious external incentive (money, avoiding duty, obtaining drugs). In factitious disorder the person also deliberately produces symptoms, but in the absence of obvious external rewards; the motive is internal, typically to occupy the sick role. Same behaviour, different motive, different diagnosis. (The disorders themselves are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.)

10.2 Instinct TheoriesMedium yield · 5 Qs

The first scientific answer to the motivation question was the oldest folk answer dressed in new clothes: we do things because we are born to do them. A spider spins a perfect web the first time it tries; a newly hatched turtle heads for the sea. Darwin's theory of evolution (1859) made such inborn behaviour respectable, because a behaviour that helped ancestors survive could be inherited just as a beak or a wing could. Around 1900 it seemed natural to explain human motivation the same way.

What counts as an instinct

An instinct is a complex, unlearned pattern of behaviour that is characteristic of every member of a species and that appears in full form the first time the triggering situation arises. Four criteria follow from the definition: the behaviour is unlearned (it appears in animals reared in isolation), species-specific or universal within the species, stereotyped (performed the same way each time) and complex (more than a simple reflex such as the knee jerk). Keep these criteria in mind; you will see that most of what was called "instinct" in human beings fails them.

William James (1842 to 1910), in his Principles of Psychology of 1890, defined instinct as "the faculty of acting in such a way as to produce certain ends, without foresight of the ends, and without previous education in the performance." James then made a claim that surprised his readers: human beings do not have fewer instincts than animals, they have more. His list included sucking, crying, imitation, rivalry, pugnacity, sympathy, fear, acquisitiveness, constructiveness, play, curiosity, sociability, shyness, modesty, jealousy and parental love. Humans look less instinctive, James argued, because our many instincts conflict with one another and because habit quickly modifies them; an instinct, once acted on, gets bound to particular objects and loses its blind quality.

McDougall's hormic psychology

The theorist most closely tied to the instinct theory of motivation is the British-born psychologist William McDougall (1871 to 1938), who moved to Harvard in 1920 and later to Duke University. In An Introduction to Social Psychology (1908) McDougall argued that instincts are the prime movers of all human activity: without them, he wrote, the organism would be like a clock without a mainspring.

For McDougall an instinct was an inherited disposition with three aspects, corresponding to the old three-part division of mind. It has a cognitive aspect (the animal perceives and pays attention to certain objects), an affective aspect (perceiving the object arouses a particular emotion) and a conative aspect (the animal feels an impulse to act). Each major instinct was paired with a primary emotion, and Table 10.3 shows the famous pairs. Over time, emotions organise around particular objects into sentiments (love of one's country, hatred of a rival), and the most important of these, the self-regarding sentiment, gives character its unity.

His system is called hormic psychology, from the Greek horme, meaning an urge or impulse, because it put purposive striving at the centre of psychology. McDougall insisted that behaviour is purposive: it is directed towards goals, it persists, it varies its means when blocked, and it stops when the goal is reached. This put him in open conflict with John B. Watson's behaviourism, and the two men debated publicly in 1924.

Exam trap

"The instinct theory of motivation was developed by" has the keyed answer William McDougall, even though William James also wrote about instincts and Freud built his theory on them. James described instincts; McDougall made them the systematic foundation of motivation and social behaviour. Maslow is the bait from the wrong school (humanistic). A second trap: McDougall's purposive (hormic) psychology is not Edward Tolman's purposive behaviourism. Both stress goals; McDougall explained goals by instincts, Tolman by learned expectancies (Section 10.4).

Freud's instincts: life and death

Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) also put inborn forces at the root of motivation, though his are better described as drives. His German word was Trieb, a driving force or impulse, which his English translators rendered as "instinct". Freud said every instinct has four features: a source (a bodily need or excitation), an aim (to remove the excitation, which is felt as pleasure), an impetus (the amount of force or pressure it exerts) and an object (the thing or person through which the aim is achieved). Of the four, only the object is learned and changeable, which is why the same hunger can be satisfied by idli in Mysuru and bread in Vienna.

In his final theory, set out in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920), Freud grouped all instincts into two classes. The life instincts (Eros) serve the survival of the individual and the species: hunger, thirst and above all sex. Their energy is the libido. The death instinct, which his followers named Thanatos, is an unconscious wish to return to the inorganic state; turned outwards it appears as aggression and destructiveness. Freud developed the idea after the slaughter of the First World War, and he read war and violence as evidence of it. The investment of psychic energy in an object, person or idea he called cathexis. The reservoir of all these unorganised instinctual drives is the id, which operates on the pleasure principle. (The id, ego and superego, the defence mechanisms and the psychosexual stages are taught in the Personality book; here we need only Freud's view of motivation.)

Exam trap

"According to Freud, human beings are motivated by powerful innate forces known as" has the answer instincts, not needs (Maslow, Murray), not drives (Hull's term, though it is a fair translation of Trieb) and not the id (the id is the reservoir of the instincts, not the forces themselves). "Wars and violence are evidence of" the death instinct; libido is the energy of the life instincts. Freud himself did not use the word Thanatos in his published writing; it was popularised by his followers.

Ethology: fixed action patterns and sign stimuli

While instinct theory was fading in human psychology, it was reborn in zoology. The new discipline was ethology, the study of animal behaviour in the natural environment, with an emphasis on its evolution and function. Its founders, the Austrian Konrad Lorenz (1903 to 1989), the Dutch-born Nikolaas Tinbergen (1907 to 1988) and the Austrian Karl von Frisch (1886 to 1982), shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1973.

The ethologists replaced the vague word "instinct" with precise terms. A fixed action pattern is an unlearned, stereotyped sequence of movements that, once started, runs to completion. A sign stimulus (or releaser) is the simple, specific feature of the environment that triggers it, and the hypothesised neural detector that responds to the sign stimulus was called the innate releasing mechanism. Tinbergen found that a male three-spined stickleback in breeding season attacks crude models of fish as long as their underside is red, while ignoring a realistic model without the red belly. Lorenz and Tinbergen showed that a greylag goose that sees an egg outside its nest stretches its neck and rolls the egg back with the underside of its bill; if the egg is removed mid-roll, the goose finishes the rolling movement over empty ground. A herring gull chick pecks at the red spot on its parent's bill, and the pecking brings up food. Lorenz also described imprinting, the rapid attachment of newly hatched geese to the first moving object they see, during a brief sensitive period (taught in the Learning and Developmental chapters).

Why instinct theory declined

As an explanation of human motivation, instinct theory had collapsed by the late 1920s, for three reasons.

First, naming is not explaining. Why do people form groups? Because of a gregarious instinct. How do we know there is a gregarious instinct? Because people form groups. The argument is circular: the "instinct" is simply the behaviour renamed and then offered as its own cause. Second, the lists grew out of control. Every new behaviour seemed to earn its own instinct, and in 1924 the sociologist Luther Lee Bernard surveyed the literature and counted nearly six thousand proposed human instincts, a reduction to absurdity. Third, the evidence pointed to learning. Anthropologists showed that supposedly universal "instincts" such as pugnacity or acquisitiveness varied enormously between cultures, and the Chinese psychologist Zing-Yang Kuo (1898 to 1970) showed in 1930 that even a cat's "instinctive" killing of rats depended on rearing: most kittens that watched their mothers kill rats became rat killers, while few kittens reared together with rats ever killed one.

The idea did not die completely. Ethology showed that fixed action patterns are real in many animals, and modern evolutionary psychology asks how natural selection shaped human motives such as mate preferences and fear of snakes. But psychologists stopped treating a list of instincts as an explanation, and looked for a mechanism. The mechanism they found was the drive.

Remember

Instinct theory explained behaviour by naming it. Its replacement, drive theory, tried to say how a bodily need turns into action. Whenever a question asks why instinct theory fell, the expected answer is circularity (naming is not explaining), followed by cultural variability and the role of learning.

10.3 Drives, Homeostasis and ArousalMedium yield · 7 Qs

Drive theory begins not in psychology but in physiology, with a simple observation: the inside of a healthy body stays remarkably constant while the world outside changes. The air around you may be at 45 degrees on a Rajasthan afternoon or at 5 degrees on a Shimla morning, yet your core temperature stays near 37 degrees Celsius. How?

Homeostasis

The French physiologist Claude Bernard (1813 to 1878) argued in the second half of the nineteenth century that the cells of the body live in an internal sea of fluids, which he called the milieu intérieur (internal environment). The constancy of this internal environment, he wrote, is the condition for a free and independent life. The American physiologist Walter Bradford Cannon (1871 to 1945) of Harvard gave the idea its modern name: he coined the term homeostasis in 1926 and popularised it in his book The Wisdom of the Body (1932). (Some books date the term to 1932, the year of the book; the word itself first appeared in print in 1926.) Homeostasis is the tendency of the body to maintain a stable internal state by detecting deviations and correcting them.

The usual model is a thermostat. A set point is the value the system tries to maintain (the temperature you dial in). When a sensor detects a deviation from it, a correction is switched on (the heater), and when the set point is restored the correction is switched off. This is negative feedback: the output of the system feeds back to reduce the deviation that produced it. Some corrections are automatic and need no behaviour at all (sweating, shivering). Others require the whole organism to act: you put on a sweater, walk into the shade or drink water. Those behavioural corrections are what a motivation theory must explain, and that is where Clark Hull came in.

Hull's drive-reduction theory

The word "drive" entered psychology in 1918, when Robert Sessions Woodworth (1869 to 1962) of Columbia University used it in Dynamic Psychology for the energy that powers behaviour, as distinct from the mechanisms that steer it. The theory built on the word belongs to Clark Leonard Hull (1884 to 1952) of Yale, whose Principles of Behavior (1943) was for a decade the most influential book in American psychology.

Hull's drive-reduction theory runs as follows. A physiological need (a departure from homeostasis) creates a drive, an aroused state of tension that energises behaviour. The organism becomes active, and any response that happens to reduce the drive is reinforced, that is, strengthened, so that it is more likely to occur the next time the drive appears. Reinforcement, for Hull, simply is drive reduction. The goal of motivated behaviour is a return to a state of low tension.

Two features of the theory are often examined. First, drive energises but does not direct. What decides which response the hungry rat makes is habit strength, the learned stimulus-response connection built up by past reinforcement; drive only multiplies it. Second, drive is general: Hull believed that the energy from any drive pooled together and energised whatever habit was strongest at the moment. Hull expressed all this in a formula. In its later form (by 1952) it reads, in simplified notation:

Reaction potential SER = SHR × D × K × V (minus inhibition)

Because the terms multiply, if any one of them is zero the response does not occur. A rat that has learned a maze perfectly (high habit strength) but has just eaten (drive near zero) will sit in the start box. Hull added that reaction potential must exceed a minimum value, the reaction threshold (SLR), before any response appears at all, and that above the threshold its strength shows in four measurable ways: a shorter response latency, a greater response (reaction) amplitude, meaning the vigour or size of the response, a higher probability of responding and greater resistance to extinction. "The greater the intensity of a stimulus, the greater the probability that a learned response will be elicited" is Hull's stimulus-intensity dynamism (V). Incentive motivation (K) is about the size of the reward, not the strength of the stimulus. Some coaching notes wrongly gloss V as "need"; need belongs to D. Response amplitude and reaction threshold are genuine Hullian terms too, but they name a measure of the response and the minimum reaction potential needed to produce it, not the effect of stimulus intensity. Also note that the word "drive" was introduced by Woodworth (1918), but drive-reduction theory is Hull's (1943), and homeostasis is Cannon's term (Bernard's concept was the milieu intérieur).

Primary and secondary drives

Hull distinguished primary drives, which arise from tissue needs (hunger, thirst, pain, temperature, sex), from secondary drives, which are acquired through learning when a neutral stimulus is repeatedly associated with a primary drive. The clearest demonstration came from Neal Elgar Miller (1909 to 2002), Hull's colleague at Yale, in 1948. Rats were shocked in a white compartment and escaped through a door into a black one. Later, with no shock at all, the rats still hurried out of the white compartment, and they learned a completely new response (turning a wheel to open the door) to get out. Fear had become a learned drive, and reducing it was reinforcing enough to teach new behaviour.

The same logic underlies Orval Hobart Mowrer's (1907 to 1982) two-factor theory of avoidance learning (1947). Factor one is classical conditioning: a warning signal paired with shock comes to evoke fear. Factor two is operant conditioning: the avoidance response is reinforced because it reduces that fear. Mowrer's theory explains why phobic avoidance is so persistent: each escape from the feared situation reduces anxiety, and so strengthens the avoidance (the Learning chapter develops this).

What drive reduction cannot explain

Drive-reduction theory works well for thirst after a cricket match. It works badly for much else, and the failures pushed psychology towards new ideas.

Animals and people seek stimulation. Harlow's monkeys took puzzles apart for nothing (the opening story). Robert Butler showed in 1953 that rhesus monkeys in a closed box would learn a discrimination task for the sole reward of opening a small window and looking out at the laboratory, and would keep doing so for hours. Rewards need not reduce any need. Fred Sheffield and Thornton Roby reported in 1950 that rats learned a maze to obtain saccharin, a sweetener with no nutritional value whatever. Reducing stimulation can be unbearable. At McGill University in 1954, in Donald Hebb's laboratory, Woodburn Heron and his colleagues (Bexton, Heron and Scott) paid students a generous daily wage to lie in a quiet cubicle wearing translucent goggles, gloves and cardboard cuffs, with almost nothing to see, hear or touch. By drive-reduction logic this should have been bliss. Most students quit within two or three days, and many reported difficulty thinking and vivid hallucinations. We eat when we are not hungry, as anyone who has accepted a second sweet at a wedding knows. And people deliberately raise their tension: they climb mountains, ride roller coasters and watch horror films.

These findings did not abolish the concept of drive; they showed that tension reduction is only half the story. Sometimes organisms want less stimulation, and sometimes more. The next idea took that seriously.

Optimal arousal theory

Psychologists use arousal to mean the general level of physiological and psychological activation of the organism, running from deep sleep at one end to frantic excitement at the other. It is regulated by the brainstem's reticular activating system (the Attention and Consciousness chapter, Chapter 5, covers its anatomy). The optimal arousal theory holds that each organism tries to maintain a moderate, optimal level of arousal: when arousal is too low (boredom) it seeks stimulation, and when arousal is too high (anxiety, overload) it seeks to reduce it. The theory was set out most influentially by the Canadian psychologist Donald Olding Hebb (1904 to 1985) in a 1955 paper, "Drives and the C.N.S." (the initials punned on "central nervous system" and "conceptual nervous system"). Hebb argued that arousal acts as a general drive and that performance is best at an intermediate level of it. The theory explains sensory-deprivation distress, curiosity and thrill seeking in one stroke, which drive reduction could not.

Optimal arousal theory found its classic evidence in a study that was forty years old by the time Hebb wrote.

Key study: Yerkes and Dodson's dancing mice (1908)

Question. Does stronger motivation always produce faster learning? Method. Robert Mearns Yerkes (1876 to 1956) and John Dillingham Dodson (1879 to 1955) at Harvard trained "dancing mice" to choose the white compartment of a discrimination box and avoid the black one; a wrong choice brought an electric shock of weak, medium or strong intensity. They also varied how hard the brightness discrimination was. Result. Learning was fastest at an intermediate shock, not the strongest one, and the harder the discrimination, the weaker the shock that produced the fastest learning. Conclusion. The relation between motivation and performance is not linear but rises and then falls, and its peak depends on task difficulty. Criticism. The original study was about shock strength in mice, not "arousal" or "anxiety" in people; the name "Yerkes-Dodson law" and the arousal interpretation came decades later, and the human evidence for a neat inverted U is weaker than textbooks suggest.

The Yerkes-Dodson law, as it is now taught, has two parts. First, the relation between arousal (or anxiety) and performance is an inverted U: performance improves as arousal rises from low to moderate, peaks, and then declines as arousal rises further. Second, the optimal level depends on the task: for simple or well-learned tasks the optimum is relatively high, while for complex, difficult or new tasks the optimum is lower. Figure 10.2 shows both curves. In everyday terms, a little exam anxiety sharpens you, and a lot wrecks you, and it wrecks you sooner on a hard numerical problem than on writing your roll number.

Why does too much arousal hurt? One answer came from J. A. Easterbrook in 1959: as arousal rises, attention narrows to fewer cues (his cue-utilisation hypothesis). At moderate arousal, irrelevant cues are dropped and performance improves; at high arousal, relevant cues are dropped as well and performance falls, and complex tasks, which need more cues, suffer first.

The same arousal logic explains a social phenomenon. Norman Triplett noticed in 1898 that cyclists rode faster when racing others than when racing the clock. Robert Zajonc (1923 to 2008) explained such social facilitation in 1965 with a drive theory: the mere presence of others raises arousal, and arousal strengthens the dominant response, the response most likely in that situation. On an easy or well-learned task the dominant response is the correct one, so an audience improves performance; on a new or difficult task the dominant response is often an error, so an audience impairs it. (Social facilitation and social loafing are developed in the Social Psychology book.)

Exam trap

"The Yerkes-Dodson law states that between performance and anxiety there is" an inverted-U shaped relationship. "Curvilinear" is not false (an inverted U is a kind of curve), but it is less specific, and when both appear the precise answer wins. "Linear" and "M-shaped" are simply wrong. The second half of the law is examined too: on a complex task, performance is best at a moderate arousal level, with the optimum lower than on a simple task. Never choose "very high arousal" or "independent of task difficulty". An option such as "punishment severity and learning rate" describes what the 1908 mouse experiment actually varied, but when "arousal and performance as an inverted U" is offered, that is the law as it is taught.

Sensation seeking

If each of us has an optimal arousal level, it need not be the same level for everyone. Marvin Zuckerman (1928 to 2018) built a research programme on that idea. In his definition, sensation seeking is the tendency to seek varied, novel, complex and intense sensations and experiences, and to take physical, social, legal and financial risks for the sake of them. Zuckerman's Sensation Seeking Scale (first version 1964) measures four components: thrill and adventure seeking (desire for risky physical activities such as skydiving), experience seeking (novel experiences through the mind, travel, music and a non-conforming lifestyle), disinhibition (seeking release through parties, drinking and varied sexual partners) and boredom susceptibility (intolerance of repetition and routine). High sensation seekers are over-represented among extreme-sport enthusiasts, early drug users and people who drive fast; biologically they tend to have low levels of the enzyme monoamine oxidase (MAO). Zuckerman's work turned optimal arousal from a principle about all organisms into a dimension on which individuals differ.

10.4 The Pull of the Goal: Incentives, Expectancies and Opponent ProcessesLower yield · 2 Qs

Drive and arousal theories look inside the organism for the source of motivation. Yet a well-fed guest still eats the gulab jamun because it is there. The theories in this section shift the spotlight outwards, to the goal, and then inwards again to what the person expects about the goal.

Incentive theory: push versus pull

The incentive theory of motivation holds that behaviour is motivated primarily by the pull of external rewards and the push away from external punishments, rather than by internal tension. Drives push; incentives pull. The theory explains why behaviour depends on the quality of what is on offer, and not only on how deprived the organism is.

Two classic observations showed incentives at work. In 1928 Otto Tinklepaugh let monkeys watch while he hid a piece of banana under a cup; then, out of sight, he swapped it for lettuce, which monkeys normally eat readily. When a monkey lifted the cup and found lettuce, it searched about, shrieked and refused the lettuce. The monkey had been working for a specific expected incentive, not for "food". In 1942 Leo Crespi trained rats to run for different amounts of food and then switched them. Rats shifted from a large to a small reward ran more slowly than rats that had always received the small reward, and rats shifted upwards ran faster than rats always given the large one. These contrast effects forced Hull to add K, incentive motivation, to his equation.

Pinel and Barnes (Biopsychology) apply the same idea to eating with their positive-incentive perspective: we are drawn to eat mainly by the anticipated pleasure of eating, which depends on the food's taste, our past experience with it, the time since we last ate, and the presence of other people, rather than by energy deficits. Section 10.9 returns to it. Modern neuroscience has refined the idea further. Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson distinguish the "wanting" of an incentive (its motivational pull, which depends on dopamine) from its "liking" (the pleasure actually felt). Their incentive-sensitisation theory of addiction (1993) proposes that drugs progressively increase wanting without increasing liking, which is why people with addiction can crave a drug they no longer enjoy.

Exam trap

"In incentive theory, motivation is primarily driven by" external rewards. The baits each belong to another theory: intrinsic satisfaction (self-determination theory), biological drives (Hull), self-efficacy beliefs (Bandura). Likewise "drive theory primarily explains motivation as a process to" reduce physiological needs; seeking external rewards is incentive theory, setting long-term goals is goal-setting theory, and increasing social connections is affiliation.

Expectancy-value theories

Incentives do not pull equally hard on everyone. A promotion pulls hard on someone who thinks he can get it and who wants it, and hardly at all on someone who has given up or who does not care. The label expectancy-value theory covers the family of theories holding that the strength of motivation depends on two things multiplied together: the person's expectancy that an action will lead to an outcome, and the value the person places on that outcome. Because the terms multiply, motivation is zero if either is zero: a highly valued goal that seems impossible does not motivate, and neither does a certain outcome that nobody wants.

The idea has several authors. Edward Chace Tolman (1886 to 1959) argued against Hull that animals learn expectancies about what leads to what (his "purposive behaviourism"), and that incentives decide whether this knowledge is shown in performance (latent learning is covered in the Learning chapter). Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947) described goals as having positive or negative valence in a person's psychological field. Julian Bernard Rotter (1916 to 2014), in Social Learning and Clinical Psychology (1954), wrote the most quotable version: the potential for a behaviour to occur in a given situation is a function of the person's expectancy that the behaviour will be reinforced and the reinforcement value of the outcome to that person. Rotter's full theory has four variables: behaviour potential, expectancy, reinforcement value and the psychological situation (the situation as the person perceives it). The same multiplicative logic reappears in John Atkinson's theory of achievement (Section 10.6), in Victor Vroom's expectancy theory of work (Section 10.10) and in Jacquelynne Eccles's model of students' choices, in which value is split into interest, importance, usefulness and cost.

Exam trap

A bank item reads: "The ______ framework is based on the expectancy, demand and incentive concepts", with the options behavioural, cognitive, social learning and supportive. The wording comes from organisational-behaviour textbooks, which describe three frameworks for explaining behaviour at work: the cognitive framework (built on Tolman's expectancies), the behaviouristic framework (built on reinforcement) and the social learning or social cognitive framework (Rotter, Bandura), which combines the two. The bank keys social learning; because expectancy is also the defining idea of Tolman's cognitive approach, "cognitive" has its defenders, so read the options carefully. "Supportive" is not a framework at all but one of Keith Davis's models of organisational behaviour (autocratic, custodial, supportive, collegial). Whatever the key, expectancy and incentive are the shared ideas of Tolman, Rotter and every expectancy-value theory in this section.

The opponent-process theory of acquired motivation

Why do people keep doing things that hurt at first, like parachuting or running marathons, and keep taking drugs that have stopped giving them pleasure? In 1974 Richard Lester Solomon (1918 to 1995) and John D. Corbit proposed an answer in their opponent-process theory, which Solomon later (1980) called the opponent-process theory of acquired motivation.

The theory assumes that the brain is built to resist strong emotional departures from neutrality. Any stimulus that produces a strong feeling arouses a primary process (the a-process), which produces the initial state, called state A (pleasant or unpleasant). The a-process automatically triggers an opponent process (the b-process), which produces the opposite feeling, state B. The two have different time courses. The a-process is quick, closely tied to the stimulus and does not change with repetition. The b-process is sluggish: it starts late, builds slowly and decays slowly after the stimulus stops. What we feel is the difference between them. So during the stimulus, state A peaks and then settles to a lower level (as the b-process grows), and when the stimulus ends, the a-process stops at once and the lingering b-process is felt on its own as an after-reaction in the opposite direction.

The crucial assumption is that the b-process, unlike the a-process, grows stronger with use: with repeated exposure it starts sooner, becomes larger and lasts longer. Figure 10.3 shows the consequence. After many exposures, state A becomes weak (this is tolerance), and state B becomes strong and long-lasting (this is withdrawal, or in the case of frightening activities, exhilaration). Motivation shifts from seeking A to escaping B.

Solomon and Corbit's favourite examples make the theory concrete. A novice parachutist is terrified during the first jump (state A) and dazed and stony-faced on landing; a veteran feels only tense eagerness during the jump and, afterwards, an exhilaration that lasts for hours (a strengthened state B). A first dose of an opiate brings intense euphoria (A) followed by mild craving (B); after hundreds of doses the euphoria is faint and the withdrawal is agonising, so the person takes the drug mainly to escape B. The same pattern, the theory claims, describes the comfort of a loved one's presence and the grief of separation, and the relief after a sauna.

Clinical link

Opponent-process theory gives the clinician a vocabulary for addiction: tolerance is a growing b-process, withdrawal is the b-process exposed when the drug wears off, and craving is the motive to escape B. It also explains why the risk of relapse peaks during early abstinence, when B is strongest. Siegel's conditioned-tolerance research (in the Learning chapter) adds that the b-process can become conditioned to the cues that usually accompany the drug.

Exam trap

There are two "opponent-process theories" in psychology. Ewald Hering's opponent-process theory (1878) is a theory of colour vision (red-green, blue-yellow, black-white; see the Sensation chapter). Solomon and Corbit's (1974) is a theory of acquired motivation and emotion. Match the author to the domain. In Solomon's theory it is the b-process, not the a-process, that strengthens with repetition.

10.5 Hierarchies of Need: Maslow, Alderfer and Other ListsHigh yield · 17 Qs

By the 1950s American psychology had two grand theories of human nature. Psychoanalysis, the "first force", saw people as driven by unconscious sexual and aggressive instincts; behaviourism, the "second force", saw them as products of conditioning. Both, critics complained, had built their pictures of humanity from neurotic patients and hungry rats. The self-styled "third force", humanistic psychology, set out to study healthy, growing people, and to put free will, personal growth and the innate tendency to fulfil one's potential at the centre of psychology. Its two founders were Abraham Harold Maslow (1908 to 1970) and Carl Rogers (whose self theory is taught in the Personality book). Of the two, Maslow is the motivation theorist.

There is a neat link to the opening of this chapter. Maslow did his doctoral research at the University of Wisconsin under Harry Harlow, studying dominance in monkeys. He left convinced that motivation had to be studied in its highest as well as its lowest forms.

The hierarchy of needs

Maslow first set out his theory in the paper "A Theory of Human Motivation" in Psychological Review (1943) and developed it in the book Motivation and Personality (1954). His hierarchy of needs arranges human needs in five levels, from the most basic to the most distinctively human:

  1. Physiological needs: food, water, oxygen, sleep, warmth, sex. These are the most powerful of all when unmet.
  2. Safety needs: security, stability, protection, order, freedom from fear and chaos. Maslow pointed to children's preference for routine and adults' preference for a steady job and insurance.
  3. Belongingness and love needs: friends, a partner, children, membership in a group, acceptance. (Some exam questions call this level "social needs".)
  4. Esteem needs: self-respect (competence, mastery, independence) and respect from others (status, recognition, appreciation).
  5. Self-actualisation: the need to become everything one is capable of becoming. "What a man can be, he must be," Maslow wrote.

The organising principle is prepotency: lower needs are stronger than higher needs whenever they are unmet, so they must be at least partly satisfied before the higher needs take over the direction of behaviour. A starving man dreams of food, not of fame. Once food is reliable, safety becomes the dominant concern; once safety is secure, loneliness begins to hurt, and so on upwards. The lower a need stands in the hierarchy, the greater its strength and priority, so physiological and safety needs, at the base, are the lower needs (management writers call them the lower-order needs, and belongingness, esteem and self-actualisation the higher-order needs); the four levels below self-actualisation together form the deficiency needs described below. Maslow called all five levels basic needs, and he described them as instinctoid: inborn in a weak, instinct-like way, although the behaviours used to satisfy them are learned.

Two points of precision are often lost in retelling. First, the hierarchy is not all-or-none. In the 1943 paper Maslow suggested that an average person might be about 85 per cent satisfied in physiological needs, 70 per cent in safety, 50 per cent in love, 40 per cent in esteem and 10 per cent in self-actualisation. A higher need emerges gradually as lower ones are partly met. Second, Maslow himself acknowledged exceptions, such as the martyr who gives up life for an ideal, or the creative person who pursues work through poverty. The familiar pyramid diagram, incidentally, is not Maslow's own drawing; it was added later by management writers. Figure 10.4 uses it because it is how the theory is examined.

Exam trap

Memorise the order bottom to top: physiological, safety, belongingness and love (social), esteem, self-actualisation. Distractors swap safety and physiological, or put esteem above self-actualisation. "Which is NOT a level of Maslow's hierarchy?" has the answer ego, which belongs to Freud's structural model. "Maslow considers physiological and safety needs as" lower needs. The bait "greatest potency needs" is cleverly half-true: Maslow did call the lower needs prepotent (strongest when unmet), but the category name for physiological and safety needs is lower (lower-order) needs. "Transformational" is a leadership term (Burns, Bass), not Maslow's.

Deficiency needs, growth needs and metamotivation

In Toward a Psychology of Being (1962) Maslow drew his most important distinction. The deficiency needs (D-needs) are the four lower levels. They arise from a lack, their satisfaction removes tension, and once satisfied they stop motivating; a fed person is not motivated by food. Behaviour driven by them is D-motivation, and it works much like Hull's drive reduction. The growth needs or being needs (B-needs) are different. They arise not from a lack but from the wish to grow; their satisfaction does not reduce tension but often increases it, and the more they are satisfied, the stronger they become.

Maslow called the motivation of self-actualising people metamotivation (also B-motivation): they are not striving to fill a gap but are drawn towards states of being he called metaneeds or B-values, such as truth, goodness, beauty, wholeness, aliveness, uniqueness, justice, order, simplicity, playfulness and meaningfulness. When metaneeds are frustrated, the result is not a neurosis in the ordinary sense but a metapathology: cynicism and mistrust when truth is denied, apathy and loss of zest, a feeling of meaninglessness and despair. Maslow also described the Jonah complex, the fear of one's own greatness that keeps people from attempting to reach their potential.

Later Maslow added to his list. In Motivation and Personality he discussed cognitive needs (to know and to understand) and aesthetic needs (for order, symmetry and beauty), which some textbooks insert between esteem and self-actualisation to make a seven-level hierarchy. And in his late writing (around 1969, and in the posthumous The Farther Reaches of Human Nature, 1971) he wrote of self-transcendence: a level beyond self-actualisation at which a person seeks to serve something beyond the self, such as others, a cause, nature or the sacred. An eight-level version (adding cognitive, aesthetic and transcendence needs) circulates widely.

Self-actualisers and peak experiences

The term self-actualisation was coined by the German neurologist Kurt Goldstein (1878 to 1965) in The Organism (1939), for the single master motive of any organism to realise its capacities. Maslow made it famous. He studied people he judged to be self-actualising, including historical figures such as Abraham Lincoln, Thomas Jefferson, Albert Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt, William James, Jane Addams and Albert Schweitzer, and concluded that they made up 1 per cent or less of the population. They shared characteristics such as an efficient perception of reality, acceptance of self and others, spontaneity, a focus on problems outside themselves, a need for privacy, autonomy, a continued freshness of appreciation, deep relationships with a few people, a democratic character, a philosophical sense of humour and creativity. They also reported peak experiences: moments of intense ecstasy, wonder and awe, resembling mystical experiences, in which the sense of self is transcended. (A questionnaire measure of self-actualisation, Shostrom's Personal Orientation Inventory, is described in the Assessment book.)

Its look-alikes: self-concept (one's organised picture of oneself, Rogers), ideal self (who one thinks one should be, Rogers), self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to perform a task, Bandura). The motivation proposed for self-actualisers is metamotivation, not D-motivation (and "E-motivation" and "F-motivation" are invented options). Goldstein coined "self-actualisation"; Maslow popularised it; Rogers spoke of an "actualising tendency" in all organisms.

Evaluating Maslow

Maslow's hierarchy is probably the best-known motivation theory in the world, and one of the least well supported. Four criticisms recur. Method: his self-actualisers were chosen by Maslow himself, according to his own values, so the description of self-actualisers partly describes Maslow's heroes. Evidence: in 1976 Mahmoud Wahba and Lawrence Bridwell reviewed the research and found little support for the ranking of the needs or for the idea that satisfying one level activates the next. Counter-examples: starving artists, hunger strikers and soldiers who die for comrades put higher needs before lower ones. Culture: the ladder reflects an individualistic, Western ideal in which personal fulfilment is the summit; in collectivist cultures, belonging and duty to the group may rank higher, and self-actualisation may be understood in relational terms. A survey of 123 countries by Louis Tay and Ed Diener (2011) found that the needs Maslow named are indeed universal and do predict well-being, but that people gain well-being from social and respect needs even when their basic needs are not fully met: the needs operate largely independently rather than as a strict ladder.

Indian context

Indian psychologists have often compared Maslow's hierarchy with the four purusharthas, the aims of life in Hindu thought: artha (material welfare), kama (pleasure and desire), dharma (duty and right conduct) and moksha (liberation). The tradition of the renunciant who sets aside bodily comfort in pursuit of liberation is a standard counter-example to strict prepotency, and Maslow's late idea of self-transcendence is the part of his theory closest to Indian ideas of self-realisation. (Indian psychology is covered in Chapter 2.)

Alderfer's ERG theory

The American organisational psychologist Clayton Paul Alderfer (1940 to 2015) tested Maslow's ideas with workers and in 1969 proposed a simpler, more flexible alternative, ERG theory. He collapsed the five levels into three. Existence needs cover Maslow's physiological and material safety needs (pay, food, working conditions). Relatedness needs cover interpersonal safety, belongingness and the external part of esteem (being respected by others). Growth needs cover the internal part of esteem and self-actualisation.

Alderfer then changed the rules of movement. Like Maslow, he accepted a satisfaction-progression principle: satisfying a lower need tends to make the next need more important. Unlike Maslow, he allowed more than one need to operate at once, and he did not insist that lower needs be satisfied before higher ones can matter. And he added the frustration-regression principle: when a higher need is persistently frustrated, the person regresses and puts more effort into the lower need that is easier to satisfy. A clerk with no prospect of growth in his job may become intensely concerned with pay and with office friendships. ERG theory has found more empirical support than Maslow's strict hierarchy, particularly in organisations.

Maslow was not the only theorist to propose a list of basic human needs, and exam setters like to mix the lists. 10.6 Psychosocial Motives and AchievementMedium yield · 8 Qs

Maslow's theory says that once you are fed, safe and loved, you strive for esteem and growth. It says much less about why some people strive far harder than others. That question, about individual differences in learned social motives, was taken up at Harvard, beginning with one remarkable man.

Murray: needs and press

Henry Alexander Murray (1893 to 1988), trained in medicine and biochemistry before turning to psychology, directed the Harvard Psychological Clinic and in Explorations in Personality (1938) set out his personology, a theory of personality built around motivation. A need, for Murray, is a force in the brain that organises perception, thinking and action so as to change an unsatisfying situation; it can be aroused from within or by the environment, it is accompanied by a particular feeling, and it gives behaviour a direction. He distinguished the viscerogenic (bodily) needs from the psychogenic (psychological) needs you met in Section 10.1, and manifest needs, expressed openly in behaviour, from latent needs, expressed only in fantasy and dreams. His list of psychogenic needs, about twenty in the version most textbooks reproduce, is the ancestor of every later list. Table 10.6 gives the most examined ones; note Murray's "n" prefix for need.

Murray's second great idea was that needs cannot be understood apart from the environment. A press is an environmental force or situation that helps or hinders the satisfaction of a need: a strict father, a rival, a supportive teacher. He distinguished alpha press, the environment as it objectively is, from beta press, the environment as the person perceives it, and it is beta press that drives behaviour. To measure needs that people may not be able or willing to report, Christiana Morgan and Murray published the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) in 1935. The person tells stories about ambiguous pictures of people, and the needs and presses of the story's hero are scored as projections of the storyteller's own. The TAT, its logic and its rivals (the Rorschach inkblots, Jung's word association test, sentence completion tests) are taught in the Assessment book. What matters here is that the TAT became the standard measure of achievement motivation, and that Murray's needs were later measured by questionnaires such as Allen Edwards's Personal Preference Schedule.

In Murray's system the need for affiliation (n Aff) is the need for social interaction and companionship; power appears in Murray's list as n Dominance (the TAT-scored need for power, n Pow, of McClelland's school descends from it); recognition is n Recognition; achievement is n Ach. "Press" is environmental, "need" is personal, "thema" is their combination.

McClelland: achievement, power and affiliation

David Clarence McClelland (1917 to 1998) took three of Murray's needs and made them the focus of a forty-year research programme. In The Achievement Motive (1953), written with John Atkinson, Russell Clark and Edgar Lowell, he developed a way of scoring TAT stories for achievement imagery: concern with a standard of excellence, unique accomplishment or long-term involvement. The resulting need for achievement (nAch) is a learned, relatively stable desire to accomplish difficult tasks and to meet standards of excellence through one's own effort.

People high in nAch show a characteristic profile. They prefer tasks of moderate difficulty and moderate risk, where success is possible but not certain; they like personal responsibility for outcomes; they want concrete, rapid feedback on how well they are doing; and they are innovative and future-oriented. They are not the gamblers who bet everything on long odds, nor the timid who pick sure things. McClelland argued that such people make good entrepreneurs, and that nAch is learned in childhood: in Marian Winterbottom's studies of the 1950s, mothers who expected early independence and mastery from their sons tended to have sons high in nAch.

In The Achieving Society (1961) McClelland went further, claiming that the level of achievement imagery in a nation's children's stories predicted its later economic growth. If nAch is learned and drives economic development, then perhaps it can be taught.

Indian context: the Kakinada experiment

McClelland put this idea to its most famous test in India. In the mid-1960s, working with the Small Industry Extension Training (SIET) Institute in Hyderabad, his team ran achievement motivation training courses for businessmen in Kakinada, a port town in Andhra Pradesh. Participants learned to think, talk and set goals like high achievers. In follow-up comparisons, the trained men started more new businesses, invested more and created more jobs than comparable untrained men. The work was reported in McClelland and David Winter's Motivating Economic Achievement (1969) and remains the best-known Indian study in motivation psychology.

McClelland's other two needs complete his theory of needs (also called acquired-needs or learned-needs theory). The need for power (nPow) is the desire to influence, control and have an impact on others; McClelland and David Winter distinguished personalised power (power used for self-aggrandisement) from socialised power (power used for the benefit of the group). The need for affiliation (nAff) is the desire to establish and maintain warm, friendly relationships. McClelland found that the most effective managers in large organisations show a leadership motive pattern: high nPow, low nAff and high self-control. A manager with very high nAff, by contrast, finds it hard to make unpopular decisions. Dan McAdams later added an intimacy motive, the desire for warm, close, communicative relationships, as a fourth social motive often scored from the same stories.

Exam trap

McClelland's three needs are achievement, affiliation and power, and McClelland insisted they are learned (acquired), not innate. The baits come from neighbouring theories: "safety, esteem and self-actualisation" (Maslow), "existence, relatedness and growth" (Alderfer), "autonomy, competence and relatedness" (Deci and Ryan). The author of the "hierarchical theory of motivation" is A. H. Maslow, not McClelland, Atkinson or O. H. Mowrer (Mowrer is the two-factor avoidance theorist).

Atkinson: the motive to succeed and the motive to avoid failure

Why should high achievers prefer tasks of moderate difficulty? McClelland's colleague John William Atkinson (1923 to 2003) answered with an expectancy-value theory, published in 1957, that is one of the most elegant in psychology. Atkinson proposed that every achievement situation arouses two opposing tendencies.

The tendency to approach success (Ts) is the product of three terms: the motive to succeed (Ms, a stable personality disposition, the capacity to feel pride in accomplishment), the probability of success (Ps, the person's estimate of the chance of succeeding at this task) and the incentive value of success (Is, the pride that success would bring). Atkinson's key insight was that Is is not independent of Ps: success at an easy task brings little pride, and success at a hard one brings a lot. So Is = 1 minus Ps, and

Ts = Ms × Ps × (1 − Ps).

The tendency to avoid failure (Taf) is built the same way from the motive to avoid failure (Maf, the capacity to feel shame at failure, usually measured by a test-anxiety questionnaire), the probability of failure and the negative incentive of failure (failing an easy task is the most shameful). The resultant tendency is the difference between the two, and it works out to (Ms − Maf) × Ps × (1 − Ps). Now push some numbers through, as in Table 10.7.

Fear of success

Atkinson's theory was built almost entirely on men; women's achievement data were messy and were often set aside. In her 1968 doctoral research at the University of Michigan, Matina Souretis Horner (born 1939) asked why. She gave students the opening line of a story to complete. Women were given: "After first-term finals, Anne finds herself at the top of her medical school class." Men were given the same line about John. About two-thirds of the women wrote stories full of conflict and loss: Anne was lonely, unpopular, unfeminine, or deliberately dropped her grades; a few stories even had her physically disfigured. Fewer than one in ten of the men wrote such stories about John.

Horner proposed a fear of success (the motive to avoid success): a learned expectation, stronger in women in her culture, that success, especially in competitive, traditionally male fields, would bring social rejection and doubts about one's femininity. Her paper in the Journal of Social Issues (1972) made the idea famous. Later research was less kind. When men wrote about a man succeeding in a field seen as feminine (nursing, for instance), they produced just as much negative imagery, which suggested that the stories reflected cultural stereotypes about who belongs where, not a stable motive peculiar to women. The concept survives as an example of how social norms shape achievement behaviour. Horner herself went on to become, at 32, the president of Radcliffe College.

Weiner: attributions and achievement

The newest strand of achievement research asks not "how strong is your motive?" but "how do you explain your successes and failures?" Its roots lie in the work of the Austrian-American social psychologist Fritz Heider (1896 to 1988), who argued in The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations (1958) that people act like naive scientists, explaining behaviour by internal (personal) or external (situational) causes. Such explanations are called attributions.

A related idea came from Julian Rotter. His locus of control (1966) is a generalised expectancy about what controls the outcomes in one's life. People with an internal locus of control believe that outcomes depend on their own effort and ability; people with an external locus of control believe that outcomes depend on luck, fate or powerful others. Rotter measured it with his Internal-External (I-E) Scale. A student who believes his results depend on his own preparation rather than on luck or an easy examiner has an internal locus of control. (Low self-efficacy is a different idea, a belief that one cannot perform a task, below; the inferiority complex is Alfred Adler's concept, in the Personality book.)

Bernard Weiner (born 1935) of the University of California, Los Angeles, built these strands into an attribution theory of achievement motivation, developed through the 1970s and set out in full in 1985. Weiner proposed that the causes people give for success and failure differ along three dimensions. Locus (borrowed from Rotter) asks whether the cause is internal or external to the person. Stability asks whether the cause is stable over time or variable. Controllability, added later, asks whether the person can control the cause. Table 10.8 places the four classic causes.

Each dimension has its own consequences. Locus governs self-esteem emotions: success attributed to internal causes produces pride, while success attributed to luck produces only gratitude or surprise. Stability governs expectancy: if you fail because of a stable cause (low ability, a hard examiner), you expect to fail again and lose hope; if you fail because of an unstable cause (insufficient effort, bad luck), you can expect to do better next time. Controllability governs social emotions: we feel guilt about our own failures due to controllable causes (I did not study) and shame about uncontrollable ones (I am not clever enough); and we feel anger towards others whose failure was controllable and pity or sympathy towards those whose failure was not.

Work it through with a student who has failed a statistics test. If he says, "I am useless at maths" (internal, stable, uncontrollable), he feels shame, expects to fail again and is likely to give up. If he says, "I did not practise enough problems" (internal, unstable, controllable), he feels guilty but hopeful and is likely to work harder. Same failure, different attribution, opposite motivation. Seligman's learned helplessness research, reformulated in 1978 by Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale, applies the same logic to depression: people who attribute bad events to internal, stable and global causes are at risk (the Learning chapter and the Disorders book develop this).

Exam trap

Weiner's three dimensions are locus, stability and controllability. Do not add "globality" (that is the third dimension of the learned-helplessness reformulation, not Weiner's). Locus of control is Rotter's generalised expectancy (1966); locus of causality is Weiner's dimension (borrowed from Rotter). Effort is the only one of the four classic causes that is both internal and controllable, which is why attribution retraining teaches students to attribute failure to effort.

10.7 Intrinsic Motivation, Competence and the SelfHigh yield · 9 Qs

We now return to the puzzle in Harlow's monkey cages. If no drive was being reduced, what was going on? Over the 1950s a group of psychologists began to argue that organisms have built-in motives to explore, to understand and to master their surroundings, and that these motives are as basic as hunger. From that argument grew the modern study of intrinsic motivation.

Curiosity and exploration

Harry Frederick Harlow (1905 to 1981) is better known for his studies of infant monkeys and their cloth and wire "mothers" (in the Developmental book), but his puzzle studies came first. He concluded that a manipulation drive and an exploratory drive are primary, unlearned motives, and in a 1953 paper, "Mice, Monkeys, Men, and Motives", he mocked the attempt to derive all human motives from hunger and thirst. Robert Butler's window-opening monkeys (Section 10.3) showed a matching visual exploration drive.

The most systematic account of curiosity came from the British-Canadian psychologist Daniel Ellis Berlyne (1924 to 1976), in Conflict, Arousal and Curiosity (1960). For Berlyne, curiosity is a motive aroused by stimuli with certain collative variables, so called because the organism must collate (compare) the stimulus with what it already knows: novelty, complexity, surprisingness, incongruity and uncertainty. Moderately novel and complex stimuli are the most attractive, in line with optimal arousal theory. Berlyne distinguished perceptual curiosity (the urge to look at or touch something new) from epistemic curiosity (the desire for knowledge), and two kinds of exploration: specific exploration, aimed at resolving uncertainty about a particular thing (what is making that noise?), and diversive exploration, aimed simply at relieving boredom (flicking through television channels). Maslow's cognitive needs to know and understand describe the same territory from the humanistic side.

Competence or effectance motivation

In 1959 Robert White (1904 to 2001) of Harvard published a long review in Psychological Review called "Motivation Reconsidered: The Concept of Competence". White pulled together the evidence on exploration, play, manipulation and mastery and argued that all of it reflected a single motive: the urge to interact effectively with the environment. He called this effectance motivation, and the feeling it produces, the satisfaction of having an effect, the feeling of efficacy. Watch an infant drop a spoon from a high chair again and again, delighted each time it clatters: the infant is not hungry, it is learning that it can make things happen. White's idea of competence motivation is the direct ancestor of two modern theories: Bandura's self-efficacy and Deci and Ryan's need for competence.

Self-efficacy

Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021), a Canadian-born psychologist at Stanford, introduced self-efficacy in a 1977 Psychological Review paper. Self-efficacy is a person's belief in his or her capability to organise and carry out the actions required to achieve a particular goal. It is specific to a task (you may have high self-efficacy for cooking biryani and low self-efficacy for public speaking), and it affects which tasks people choose, how much effort they put in, how long they persist in the face of difficulty and how anxious they feel. Bandura distinguished it from outcome expectancy, the belief that a given behaviour will produce a given outcome. A student may be sure that solving past papers would raise his marks (high outcome expectancy) and still doubt that he can make himself do it (low self-efficacy).

Bandura named four sources of self-efficacy, in order of power. Mastery experiences (performance accomplishments) are the strongest: succeeding at a task builds belief, especially after overcoming difficulty. Vicarious experiences come from watching similar others succeed ("if she can do it, so can I"). Verbal persuasion is encouragement from others, weaker on its own. Physiological and emotional states are interpreted as signs of capability: a pounding heart before a viva may be read as incapacity unless one learns to read it as readiness.

Self-efficacy is the motivational heart of Bandura's social cognitive theory (set out in Social Foundations of Thought and Action, 1986), the expansion of his earlier social learning theory. Its organising model is triadic reciprocal causation, also called reciprocal determinism: personal factors (beliefs, expectations, biology), behaviour and the environment all influence one another, so none is simply cause and none simply effect. Bandura's work on observational learning, with its attention, retention, reproduction and motivation processes, belongs to the Learning chapter.

Exam trap

"The reciprocal causation model has been described under" social cognitive theory (Bandura), not drive-reduction theory, not constructivist theory, and not "two-factor theory". Be ready for the many two-factor theories: Herzberg (work: motivators and hygiene, 1959), Mowrer (avoidance learning: classical plus operant, 1947), Schachter and Singer (emotion: arousal plus cognitive label, 1962) and Spearman (intelligence: g plus s, 1904). "Social cognitive theory highlights which factor in motivation?" Self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is not self-esteem (a global evaluation of one's worth) and not locus of control (a general belief about what controls outcomes).

When rewards backfire: the overjustification effect

If intrinsic motivation is real, what happens when you pay someone to do what they already enjoy? Common sense says you get more of it. Edward L. Deci (born 1942) tested this in a study published in 1971. College students worked on Soma puzzles, interesting three-dimensional block puzzles, over three sessions. In the second session, half of them were paid a dollar for each puzzle solved; in the third session nobody was paid. The key measure came when the experimenter left the room for a few minutes, leaving the puzzles and some magazines: how long did students spend on the puzzles in this free-choice period? Students who had been paid spent less free time on the puzzles after the payment stopped than students who had never been paid.

Key study: Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's magic markers (1973)

Question. Can an expected reward undermine a child's interest in an activity the child already enjoys? Method. Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett observed preschool children at a Stanford nursery school and selected those who, during free play, spent time drawing with magic markers. Each child was then invited to draw in one of three conditions: expected award (told in advance that drawing would earn a "Good Player" certificate with a gold seal and ribbon), unexpected award (given the same certificate as a surprise afterwards) or no award. Result. One to two weeks later, the markers were put out again in the classroom. Children in the expected-award group spent about half as much free time drawing as children in the other two groups, and the drawings they had made for the award were judged lower in quality. Unexpected awards did no harm. Conclusion. An expected, tangible reward for an already enjoyable activity can undermine intrinsic interest. Criticism. The effect depends heavily on conditions; rewards that are unexpected, verbal, or informative about competence do not undermine, and some reviewers (Cameron and Pierce, 1994) argued that the effect is narrower than often claimed.

The authors called the result the overjustification effect: when a person is given more external justification for an activity than is needed (a reward for something already enjoyed), the person attributes the behaviour to the reward ("I draw for certificates") rather than to interest ("I draw because I like it"), and when the reward stops, so does the behaviour. A meta-analysis of 128 experiments by Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan (1999) confirmed that expected, tangible rewards contingent on doing a task reliably reduce intrinsic motivation, while positive verbal feedback tends to increase it. For parents and teachers in a culture of rewards for marks, the message is not "never reward", but "reward in a way that informs rather than controls, and do not pay children for what they already love".

Self-determination theory and cognitive evaluation theory

Deci and his Rochester colleague Richard M. Ryan (born 1953) built these findings into self-determination theory (SDT), presented in Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior (1985) and now one of the most widely researched motivation theories. SDT proposes three innate, universal psychological needs whose satisfaction is essential for growth, intrinsic motivation and well-being. Autonomy is the need to feel that one's behaviour is self-chosen and volitional, that one is its origin rather than a pawn. Competence is the need to feel effective in dealing with the environment (White's idea). Relatedness is the need to feel close to and cared for by others. Social conditions that support these needs foster intrinsic motivation; conditions that thwart them undermine it.

The first part of SDT to be developed was cognitive evaluation theory (CET), which explains when external events help or harm intrinsic motivation. Any reward, deadline, praise or surveillance has two aspects. Its controlling aspect pressures the person to behave in a particular way and shifts the perceived locus of causality from internal to external, lowering autonomy and intrinsic motivation. Its informational aspect tells the person about his competence, and if the information is positive it raises intrinsic motivation. Expected tangible rewards are usually experienced as controlling; unexpected praise for good work is usually experienced as informational. That is the theory behind the magic-marker results.

SDT does not treat extrinsic motivation as a single thing. Its organismic integration theory arranges motivation along a continuum of increasing self-determination, shown in Table 10.9 for a student preparing for an entrance examination. The more a person internalises the value of an externally prompted activity, the more autonomous, persistent and satisfying the motivation becomes.

Flow

Intrinsic motivation at its most intense has a name. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934 to 2021), a Hungarian-born psychologist at the University of Chicago, began by asking painters why they worked for hours on canvases they then lost interest in once finished. He went on to interview rock climbers, chess players, dancers and surgeons, and in Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975) and Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (1990) he described flow: a state of complete absorption in an activity, in which action and awareness merge, attention is fully focused, self-consciousness disappears, the sense of time is distorted (hours pass like minutes) and the activity becomes autotelic, rewarding in itself. To study flow in everyday life he and Reed Larson developed the experience sampling method, in which people carry a pager (now a phone) and record what they are doing and feeling whenever it signals at random moments.

The central condition for flow is a balance between challenge and skill, at a level that stretches the person, together with clear goals and immediate feedback. When the challenge exceeds skill, the person feels anxiety; when skill exceeds the challenge, boredom. Figure 10.5 shows the "flow channel" and how a learner moves through it.

The consistency motive: cognitive dissonance

One more cognitive motive deserves a place here, because it appears in motivation questions although its home is social psychology. Leon Festinger (1919 to 1989) proposed in A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance (1957) that people are motivated to keep their thoughts, beliefs and actions consistent. In his account, cognitive dissonance is the unpleasant state of tension that arises when a person holds two inconsistent cognitions, or acts against his own beliefs; like a drive, it motivates the person to reduce it, usually by changing an attitude, changing the behaviour, or adding a justifying thought. Arvind, who believes that smoking is disgusting because it killed his grandfather, smokes at a party with his friends: he will experience dissonance, and may later tell himself that "one cigarette at a party doesn't count". In Festinger and James Carlsmith's 1959 study, students paid only one dollar to tell a waiting student that a boring task was interesting later rated the task as more enjoyable than students paid twenty dollars; with insufficient external justification, they changed their attitude instead. Notice the family resemblance to the overjustification effect: too much external reward weakens internal interest, too little strengthens internal justification. Effort justification, the tendency to value what we have suffered for (Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills, 1959), is a special case. The theory and its research are developed in the Social Psychology book.

10.8 Conflict and FrustrationMedium yield · 5 Qs

So far we have treated motives one at a time. Real life is not so tidy. You want the job in Bengaluru and you want to stay near your parents in Mysuru; you want to pass the entrance and you dread another night of revision. Motives collide with each other and with the world, and the study of those collisions is the study of conflict and frustration. It also connects motivation to stress (Chapter 11) and to clinical psychology.

Frustration and its sources

In psychology, frustration is the blocking or thwarting of goal-directed behaviour, and also the unpleasant emotional state that results. Its sources are usually grouped into three classes. Environmental (external) sources include physical obstacles (a traffic jam, a closed office), natural events (floods, illness in the family), and social and cultural barriers (rules, prejudice, family disapproval, poverty). Personal (internal) sources include physical or mental limitations and, very commonly, a gap between aspiration and ability: the student who sets his heart on a rank his preparation cannot reach. The third source is conflict between one's own motives, taken up below. Delays, lack of resources, losses, failure and a sense of meaninglessness are the everyday forms frustration takes.

Frustration is one of several sources of stress that are usually listed together, and questions ask you to tell them apart. Conflict is the presence of two or more incompatible motives. Change is the need to adjust to new circumstances (the life-event research of Holmes and Rahe is in Chapter 11). Finally, pressure is the expectation or demand that one behave in a certain way: pressure to perform (work hard, fast and well) and pressure to conform (meet others' expectations). Ramesh, who must handle a large number of calls at high speed in a call centre, is under pressure; he is not blocked from a goal (frustration), torn between motives (conflict) or facing an unknowable future (unpredictability).

Lewin's types of conflict

Kurt Lewin, the German-American founder of field theory, described behaviour as a function of the person and the environment, B = f(P, E), played out in the person's life space. Objects in the life space have positive or negative valences that attract or repel the person, like forces in a physical field. When forces pull in incompatible directions, the result is a conflict. In A Dynamic Theory of Personality (1935) Lewin distinguished three basic types; a fourth was added by later writers (it is often credited to Carl Hovland and Robert Sears in 1938). In an approach-approach conflict the person is attracted to two goals that cannot both be reached. Such conflicts are the easiest to resolve: once you move even slightly towards one goal, its pull grows and the other's shrinks, so the conflict is unstable and resolves itself. (The medieval puzzle of Buridan's ass, which starved between two equally attractive bales of hay, is a philosopher's joke, not a psychological finding.) In an avoidance-avoidance conflict both options are unpleasant. As the person moves towards one, its repulsion grows and pushes him back towards the other, so he vacillates; if he can, he tries to leave the field altogether, by daydreaming, procrastinating or physically escaping. In an approach-avoidance conflict a single goal both attracts and repels: the cricket fan who wants to watch the final but fears what his boss will say about the day off. The person approaches until the negative features loom large, then retreats, then approaches again. In a double approach-avoidance conflict there are two or more such goals, and every option has its own pros and cons. This is the shape of most important real-life decisions: choosing a course, a job, a partner, a city.

Miller's gradients of approach and avoidance

Why does the approach-avoidance conflict produce such characteristic vacillation? Neal Miller answered with a model that turned Lewin's descriptions into testable predictions. In a 1944 chapter, "Experimental Studies of Conflict", and later in a fuller statement (1959), Miller proposed a set of postulates about approach and avoidance gradients, the way the strength of each tendency changes with distance from the goal.

  1. The tendency to approach a goal grows stronger the nearer the organism is to it (the approach gradient).
  2. The tendency to avoid a feared stimulus also grows stronger the nearer the organism is to it (the avoidance gradient).
  3. The avoidance gradient is steeper than the approach gradient: fear rises faster than desire as the goal comes closer.
  4. The strength of each tendency (the height of each gradient) depends on the strength of the drive on which it is based.
  5. When two incompatible tendencies conflict, the stronger one wins.

The evidence came from rats wearing small harnesses that measured how hard they pulled. In a 1948 study, Judson Brown found that hungry rats trained to run to food pulled only a little less hard when stopped far from the goal than when stopped near it, while rats shocked at the goal pulled away very hard near it and hardly at all far from it. The gradients really do differ in slope.

Now look at Figure 10.6. Far from the goal, approach is stronger than avoidance, so the organism moves forward. Close to the goal, avoidance is stronger, so it retreats. At the point where the gradients cross, the two are equal, and the organism hovers, moving forward and back. This is the vacillation of the bride who longs to marry and panics as the wedding day approaches, and of the student who sits down to start a feared assignment and finds himself, ten minutes later, making tea.

Clinical link

John Dollard and Neal Miller translated these gradients into therapy in Personality and Psychotherapy (1950). Suppose a client wants a relationship but fears intimacy. Increasing the approach tendency (urging him on, raising the rewards) raises the whole approach line, so the crossing point moves nearer the goal, where avoidance is steepest; he gets closer and suffers more fear and conflict. Reducing the avoidance tendency (lowering fear through reassurance, exposure and relaxation) lets him approach with less distress. Their rule: in an approach-avoidance conflict, work first on reducing fear, not on increasing desire. Modern exposure therapy follows the same logic.

Reactions to frustration

How do people respond when a goal is blocked? Some responses are constructive: trying harder, changing the approach, finding a substitute goal, or lowering the goal to a realistic level. Others are less adaptive, and four of them are classic in textbooks.

Aggression is the most studied (see the frustration-aggression hypothesis, below). It may be direct, aimed at the source of frustration, or redirected onto a safer target when the true source is too powerful or too vague. Such displaced aggression is the clerk who, scolded by his boss, goes home and shouts at his children. When a whole group is blamed for a society's frustrations, displacement becomes scapegoating. In 1940 Carl Hovland and Robert Sears reported that lynchings in the American South rose in years when the price of cotton fell; later reanalyses found the relationship weaker than first claimed, but the study remains the textbook example of displaced aggression.

Regression is a return to behaviour typical of an earlier stage of development. In a 1941 study at the University of Iowa, Roger Barker, Tamara Dembo and Kurt Lewin let young children play with ordinary toys, then showed them a room of far more attractive toys and, after a short time, put those toys behind a wire screen where the children could see but not reach them. When the children went back to the ordinary toys, their play became markedly less constructive, more like the play of much younger children.

Fixation is the rigid, stereotyped repetition of a response that no longer works. Norman Maier (1900 to 1977) gave rats insoluble discrimination problems on a jumping stand, punishing them unpredictably whatever they chose. Many rats developed a fixed habit, such as always jumping to the left, and kept it even after the problem was made soluble and the correct window was left open in front of them. In Frustration: The Study of Behavior Without a Goal (1949) Maier argued that frustration-induced behaviour is qualitatively different from goal-directed behaviour.

Withdrawal and apathy are giving up: the person stops trying, loses interest and may become passive and depressed, as in learned helplessness. Finally, people protect themselves from the anxiety of frustration and conflict with the unconscious strategies Freud called defence mechanisms. The frustration-aggression hypothesis

In 1939 a group of Yale scholars, John Dollard, Leonard Doob, Neal Miller, Orval Hobart Mowrer and Robert Sears, published Frustration and Aggression. Their frustration-aggression hypothesis made two sweeping claims: aggression is always a consequence of frustration, and frustration always leads to some form of aggression. Both claims proved too strong. Frustration can lead to regression, fixation, withdrawal or constructive problem solving, and aggression can occur without frustration (a hired fighter, a soldier obeying orders). In 1941 Miller himself revised the hypothesis: frustration produces instigations to several kinds of response, one of which is aggression. In 1989 Leonard Berkowitz (1926 to 2016) offered the influential reformulation, which he developed into his cognitive neo-associationist model: frustration produces aggression only to the extent that it produces negative affect, and aversive events of any kind (pain, heat, foul smells, insults) can do the same; cues associated with aggression, such as the weapons in Berkowitz and LePage's 1967 study, make an aggressive response more likely. Aggression as a topic belongs to the Social Psychology book.

Exam trap

The frustration-aggression hypothesis is Dollard, Doob, Miller, Mowrer and Sears (1939), not Lewin and not Freud; its revisions are Miller (1941) and Berkowitz (1989). The regression study with toys behind a wire screen is Barker, Dembo and Lewin (1941); the fixation study with rats on a jumping stand is Maier (1949). Lewin described the three basic conflict types; Miller supplied the gradients; the avoidance gradient is steeper, not shallower. "Leaving the field" is the hallmark of avoidance-avoidance conflict; "approach then retreat" is the hallmark of approach-avoidance.

10.9 Biological Motives: Hunger, Thirst, Sex and OthersMedium yield · 9 Qs

We end the survey of theories where drive theory began, in the body. The biological motives are where psychology and physiology meet, and they are where several of the most famous "facts" in introductory psychology turned out to be only half true. The detailed neuroscience of eating and drinking belongs to the Physiological Psychology book; here the aim is to understand what starts and stops these behaviours, and to know the classic findings and the traps around them.

Hunger: from the stomach to the brain

The most obvious theory of hunger is that we feel hungry because our stomachs are empty. It was tested in one of the most charming experiments in the history of physiology.

Key study: Cannon and Washburn's swallowed balloon (1912)

Question. Is hunger the feeling of an empty stomach contracting? Method. Walter Cannon had the idea; his student A. L. Washburn had the ability to swallow a balloon. Washburn swallowed an empty balloon tied to the end of a thin tube; Cannon inflated it and connected the tube to a water-filled U-tube, so that each stomach contraction pushed up the water level, which was recorded. Washburn pressed a key whenever he felt a hunger pang. Result. Large stomach contractions coincided with Washburn's reports of hunger pangs. Conclusion. Cannon and Washburn proposed that hunger is the feeling of stomach contractions and that satiety is the feeling of stomach distension. Criticism. Patients whose stomachs had been surgically removed, with the oesophagus joined directly to the small intestine, still reported hunger and satiety and kept a normal body weight by eating smaller, more frequent meals. The stomach contributes to hunger, but it is not its source.

If not the stomach, then what? In the 1940s and 1950s researchers looked for signals in the blood, assuming that eating, like temperature, is regulated around a set point. The glucostatic theory (associated with Jean Mayer, 1953) held that we become hungry when blood glucose falls below its set point and stop eating when it is restored; glucose is, after all, the brain's main fuel. The lipostatic theory (associated with Gordon Kennedy, 1953) held that each person has a set point for body fat, and that deviations from it produce compensating changes in eating. The two were seen as complementary: a short-term glucostatic system for starting and stopping meals and a long-term lipostatic system for body weight.

Pinel and Barnes, whose Biopsychology textbook is the standard source on this topic, argue that these set-point theories have several serious problems. They cannot explain the worldwide epidemic of overeating and obesity, since a set-point system should resist it. They fit poorly with the evolutionary pressures on eating: our ancestors faced an unpredictable food supply, so they needed a system that made them eat good food whenever it was available and bank the calories as fat, preventing energy deficits rather than merely responding to them. Their key predictions have not been confirmed: reductions in blood glucose or body fat large enough to trigger eating rarely occur in ordinary life. And they ignore the enormous influence of taste, learning and social factors on eating. Their alternative is the positive-incentive perspective introduced in Section 10.4: we are motivated to eat mainly by the anticipated pleasure of eating, and body weight is regulated loosely around a settling point, the weight at which the many factors that influence intake and expenditure come into balance. Their "leaky barrel" analogy makes the point: the water level in a leaky barrel stays fairly constant not because anything defends a set level, but because a fuller barrel leaks faster. Change the inflow (a more tempting food environment) and the level drifts to a new settling point. This is why dieters' weight drifts back up, and why the same person can stabilise at different weights in different environments.

The hypothalamus: two centres and their revision

Every entrance paper seems to ask about the "satiety centre". Here is the story behind the term. In 1940 Albert Hetherington and Stephen Ranson reported that large bilateral lesions of the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) made rats eat enormously (hyperphagia) and become extremely obese. The VMH syndrome has two phases: a dynamic phase of several weeks of gross overeating and rapid weight gain, and a static phase in which the rat eats just enough to maintain its new, high weight, and defends it against both starvation and forced feeding. In 1951 Bal Krishan Anand and John Brobeck at Yale reported that bilateral lesions of the lateral hypothalamus (LH) produced aphagia, a complete refusal to eat that led to death unless the animal was tube-fed, and later work showed that LH lesions also cause adipsia, the refusal to drink. Stimulating the LH made sated animals eat. In 1954 Eliot Stellar put the findings together in the dual-centre theory: the LH is a feeding (hunger) centre and the VMH a satiety centre, working like an accelerator and a brake.

Indian context

Bal Krishan Anand, the "Anand" of Anand and Brobeck, was an Indian physiologist. After his work at Yale he returned to India and built one of the country's leading neurophysiology departments at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi. With G. S. Chhina and Baldev Singh he also published, in 1961, one of the earliest electroencephalographic studies of yogis in meditation. The "feeding centre" in every psychology textbook carries an Indian name.

The dual-centre theory is still the answer examiners want, but you should know that it has been substantially revised, as Pinel describes. VMH-lesioned rats turned out to overeat because they gain weight, rather than gaining weight because they overeat: VMH lesions raise blood insulin, which speeds the conversion of nutrients into fat (lipogenesis) and slows its release (lipolysis), so the rats must keep eating to have enough usable energy. Moreover, many effects of large VMH lesions come from damage to fibres from the nearby paraventricular nucleus (PVN), and lesions of the PVN alone produce overeating. LH lesions, for their part, produce a wide range of motor and sensory deficits, not a specific loss of hunger. Modern research focuses on particular cell groups, especially in the arcuate nucleus, which receives the body's chemical signals about energy. Hunger and satiety signals

The body tells the brain about its energy state through chemical messengers. In 1950 a spontaneous mutation in a mouse colony at the Jackson Laboratory in Maine produced ob/ob mice, which eat excessively and weigh up to three times as much as normal mice. In 1994 Jeffrey Friedman and his colleagues identified the missing product of the ob gene: a hormone released by fat cells, which they named leptin (from the Greek leptos, thin). Leptin signals the size of the body's fat stores to the hypothalamus and, in normal animals, reduces eating; injecting it into ob/ob mice made them eat less and lose weight. Hopes that leptin would cure human obesity faded, however, because most obese people already have high leptin levels and seem to be resistant to it. The pancreatic hormone insulin plays a similar long-term signalling role. In the short term, ghrelin, a hormone released mainly by the empty stomach (identified in 1999), rises before meals and promotes hunger, while gut peptides such as cholecystokinin (CCK), released from the intestine as food arrives, promote satiety (Gibbs, Young and Smith showed in 1973 that injecting CCK reduced meal size in rats).

Satiety also depends on variety. In sensory-specific satiety, the pleasure of a particular food falls as you eat it, while the pleasure of other foods is barely affected; this is why there is always room for dessert, and why rats offered a varied "cafeteria diet" of tasty foods eat far more and grow obese.

External cues, dieting and eating disorders

If eating were controlled only by internal signals, the clock on the wall would not matter. Stanley Schachter (1922 to 1997) of Columbia University suspected that it does matter, and that it matters more for some people. His externality hypothesis (1968 to 1971) proposed that obese people are unusually responsive to external food cues (the sight, smell and taste of food, the time of day) and relatively insensitive to internal cues of hunger and fullness. In one study by Schachter and Larry Gross (1968), participants worked in a room with a rigged clock; obese participants ate more crackers when the clock showed that it was past dinner time than when it showed an earlier hour, while normal-weight participants were little affected. Obese participants also ate more when more food was visible and ate less when the food took effort to get. Later research, summarised by Judith Rodin in 1981, showed that responsiveness to external cues is found at all body weights and does not on its own explain obesity, but the idea that environmental cues drive eating survives in the positive-incentive view.

Peter Herman and Janet Polivy added the concept of restrained eating: chronic dieters who control their intake by willpower rather than by internal cues. In a classic 1975 study by Herman and Deborah Mack, restrained eaters who were first required to drink a milkshake then ate more ice cream in a "taste test" than restrained eaters who had not, as if, once the diet was "broken", there was no reason to hold back. Obesity (a body mass index of 30 or above), anorexia nervosa (restriction of intake leading to significantly low weight, with an intense fear of gaining weight) and bulimia nervosa (recurrent binge eating with compensatory behaviours such as vomiting) are disorders of this motivational system; their diagnosis and treatment are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

Thirst

Water makes up more than half of body weight, and two different kinds of loss produce thirst. The first, osmotic thirst, arises when the fluid outside the cells becomes too salty (after a salty meal, or when water is lost through sweating). Water then moves out of the cells by osmosis, and the cells shrink. Specialised neurons called osmoreceptors, in a region at the front of the hypothalamus (the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis), detect the change and trigger both thirst and the release of vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) from the posterior pituitary, which makes the kidneys conserve water. The second, hypovolaemic thirst, arises when the volume of blood and other extracellular fluid falls, as after bleeding, diarrhoea or vomiting, even if its saltiness is unchanged. Receptors in the heart and large blood vessels detect the drop in pressure, and the kidneys release renin, which leads to the formation of angiotensin II; angiotensin acts on the brain (the subfornical organ) to produce thirst and also an appetite for salt, which is needed to restore volume. Drinking usually stops long before the water has been absorbed, because signals from the mouth, stomach and intestines meter the intake. Cannon had proposed that thirst is simply a dry mouth, but wetting the mouth does not abolish thirst for long, and the theory failed for much the same reason as his stomach theory of hunger.

Sexual motivation

Sex is classed as a primary motive, yet it differs from hunger and thirst in a fundamental way: no tissue is depleted when it is unsatisfied, and no individual dies for lack of it. It serves the survival of the species rather than of the individual, and in human beings it is shaped heavily by learning, culture and relationships.

Scientific study of human sexual behaviour began with surveys. Alfred Charles Kinsey (1894 to 1956), a zoologist at Indiana University who had spent years classifying gall wasps, turned the same taxonomic zeal on human sexuality. Kinsey and his colleagues interviewed thousands of Americans and published Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948) and Sexual Behavior in the Human Female (1953), together known as the Kinsey reports. They shocked the public by showing that practices officially condemned, such as premarital and extramarital sex and homosexual experience, were far commoner than anyone had admitted. Kinsey described sexual orientation not as two boxes but as a continuum, the Kinsey scale, from 0 (exclusively heterosexual) to 6 (exclusively homosexual). His samples were volunteers rather than random samples, a weakness critics stressed.

Where Kinsey asked people what they did, William Howell Masters (1915 to 2001), a gynaecologist, and Virginia Eshelman Johnson (1925 to 2013) observed it directly. In their laboratory in St. Louis they recorded the physiological responses of several hundred volunteers (382 women and 312 men) during thousands of sexual response cycles and reported the results in Human Sexual Response (1966). They described a four-phase sexual response cycle: excitement (rising arousal, with engorgement of the genitals with blood, erection and vaginal lubrication), plateau (arousal levels off at a high level), orgasm (rhythmic muscular contractions and intense pleasure) and resolution (the body gradually returns to its unaroused state). Men enter a refractory period after orgasm during which another orgasm is not possible, while many women can have multiple orgasms if stimulation continues. Their second book, Human Sexual Inadequacy (1970), launched modern sex therapy.

The New York sex therapist Helen Singer Kaplan (1929 to 1995) noticed that many of her patients' problems began before excitement, with a lack of interest in sex. In Disorders of Sexual Desire (1979) she proposed a three-phase (triphasic) model: desire, excitement, orgasm. Adding desire as the first phase is the key difference between Kaplan and Masters and Johnson, and diagnostic systems later organised sexual dysfunctions by phase. Rosemary Basson later (2000) proposed a circular model of female sexual response in which desire often follows, rather than precedes, arousal and intimacy.

Hormones matter, but less in humans than in other mammals. In many female mammals sexual receptivity is confined to the oestrous period around ovulation, when oestrogen is high. Testosterone supports sexual desire in both men and women, and castration in adulthood reduces but does not always abolish it. Hormones have organisational effects before birth, shaping the developing brain and body, and activational effects from puberty onwards, switching behaviour on (the Physiological book develops this).

Exam trap

Masters and Johnson: Excitement, Plateau, Orgasm, Resolution (EPOR, four phases, 1966). Kaplan: Desire, Excitement, Orgasm (three phases, 1979). Options that put desire into a Masters and Johnson sequence, or put plateau before excitement, or end with "refractory" instead of resolution, are scrambles. The reports on the sexual behaviour of men (1948) and women (1953) are Kinsey's, not Freud's, Anna Freud's or Karen Horney's.

Maternal drive, pain avoidance and other biological motives

How strong are the biological drives, compared with each other? In the 1920s and 1930s Carl Warden and his colleagues at Columbia University built the Columbia obstruction box: a rat was placed in a start compartment separated from a goal (food, water, a mate, its pups, or a novel area) by an electrified grid, and the experimenters counted how many times in a fixed period the rat would cross the grid to reach the goal. In Warden's results (Animal Motivation, 1931) the maternal drive produced the most crossings, followed by thirst, hunger, sex and exploration: a mother rat would cross the shock grid to reach her pups more often than a hungry rat would to reach food. Maternal behaviour in rats is primed by the hormones of pregnancy and birth, such as oestrogen, prolactin and oxytocin, but it is not purely hormonal: virgin female rats left with pups for several days begin to build nests and retrieve the pups. In primates, Harlow's later studies of contact comfort showed how much the infant's own motive for attachment matters (the Developmental book covers attachment).

Pain avoidance is a powerful primary motive that, unlike hunger, has no cycle: it is aroused by tissue damage and lasts as long as the damage does. Its importance is clearest in its absence: people born with congenital insensitivity to pain suffer repeated injuries, burns and joint damage, and often die young. The need to keep body temperature, oxygen supply and elimination in balance are further primary motives, usually satisfied so automatically that they become noticeable only when blocked. Sleep behaves like a drive too, building up with time awake and discharged by sleeping; its stages, rhythms and theories are covered in Chapter 5.

10.10 Motivation in Practice: Work, School and ClinicHigh yield · 7 Qs

Motivation theories are tested hardest outside the laboratory: in offices and factories, in classrooms and in consulting rooms. This section applies the chapter's ideas to those three settings, and introduces the work-motivation theories that entrance papers ask about every year.

Motivation at work

In the late 1950s Frederick Herzberg (1923 to 2000) and his colleagues Bernard Mausner and Barbara Snyderman interviewed about two hundred engineers and accountants in the Pittsburgh area using the critical incident technique: each person described a time he had felt exceptionally good about his job and a time he had felt exceptionally bad, and what had caused it. The results, published in The Motivation to Work (1959), surprised them. The things that made people feel good were not simply the opposite of the things that made them feel bad. Herzberg's two-factor theory (also called motivator-hygiene theory) proposed two separate sets of factors, shown in Table 10.13.

His hygiene factors are features of the job environment, and their absence or poor quality produces dissatisfaction; but improving them merely removes dissatisfaction, without producing positive motivation. The word "hygiene" was chosen deliberately: like clean water, good hygiene prevents disease but does not make you healthy. The opposite of satisfaction, in this theory, is not dissatisfaction but "no satisfaction", and the opposite of dissatisfaction is "no dissatisfaction". The practical recommendation is job enrichment: building responsibility, achievement and growth into jobs, rather than relying on pay and perks. Critics point out that the critical-incident method invites self-serving answers (people credit good events to their own achievement and blame bad ones on the company), and that pay can motivate as well as satisfy. Still, Herzberg's lesson that job satisfaction depends on both the content of the work and its environment is the answer examiners expect.

Exam trap

Herzberg's motivators are recognition, achievement, advancement, responsibility, growth and the work itself. Salary, benefits, job security, working conditions, company policies and interpersonal relationships are hygiene factors, which prevent dissatisfaction but do not by themselves motivate. "Job satisfaction depends on" work motivation and job environment (both, not either, and not disposition alone). Hygiene factors are not Maslow's self-actualisation needs; if anything they correspond to Maslow's lower needs, and the motivators to his higher needs.

Herzberg asked what motivates; the next theories ask how motivation works. Victor Harold Vroom (born 1932), in Work and Motivation (1964), applied expectancy-value logic to the workplace in his expectancy theory, often called VIE theory. Motivational force depends on three beliefs. Expectancy (E) is the belief that effort will lead to successful performance ("if I work harder, I will meet my target"). Instrumentality (I) is the belief that performance will lead to outcomes ("if I meet my target, I will get the bonus"). Valence (V) is the value of those outcomes to the person ("I want the bonus"). The three multiply: Motivational force = E × I × V. Work it through for Priya, a sales executive in Bengaluru. If she is fairly sure that extra calls will meet her target (E = 0.8), confident that the target brings a bonus (I = 0.9) and keen on the bonus (V = 0.7), her motivation is 0.8 × 0.9 × 0.7, about 0.50. If her company has a history of not paying promised bonuses, instrumentality drops to 0.2 and her motivation falls to about 0.11, even though nothing about her effort or ability has changed. Motivation is highest when effort leads to performance, performance leads to rewards, and the rewards are valued.

John Stacy Adams's equity theory (1963, 1965) adds a social comparison. People compare the ratio of their outcomes (pay, recognition, promotion) to their inputs (effort, skill, experience, time) with the same ratio for a comparison person. When the ratios are equal, they feel equity. When their own ratio is lower (underpayment inequity) they feel angry and resentful; when it is higher (overpayment inequity) they feel guilty, although people tolerate this more easily. Inequity motivates its own reduction: a person may change his inputs (work less), seek higher outcomes (ask for a raise), distort his perceptions ("he deserves more; he has a harder job"), change the comparison person, or leave. If Ravi learns that Suresh, who joined at the same time and does the same work, earns more, equity theory predicts that Ravi's effort will fall, whatever his absolute salary.

The most strongly supported work-motivation theory is goal-setting theory, developed over several decades by Edwin Locke (born 1938) and Gary Latham (born 1945), beginning with Locke's 1968 paper and summarised in their book A Theory of Goal Setting and Task Performance (1990). Its central finding is that specific, difficult (but attainable) goals lead to higher performance than easy goals, vague goals, or the exhortation to "do your best". Goals work through four mechanisms: they direct attention, mobilise effort, increase persistence and encourage the search for strategies. They work best under four conditions: when the person is committed to the goal, when there is feedback on progress, when the person has the ability, and when the task is not so complex that a demanding outcome goal causes panic (for complex new tasks, learning goals work better). Organisational writers later popularised "SMART" goals; the acronym is a management mnemonic, not Locke and Latham's theory.

Two further ideas complete the picture. In The Human Side of Enterprise (1960), Douglas McGregor contrasted Theory X managers, who assume that workers dislike work and must be controlled, with Theory Y managers, who assume that workers seek responsibility and are self-motivated; the assumptions tend to be self-fulfilling. And J. Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham's job characteristics model (1976) identified five core features of motivating jobs: skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback.

Exam trap

Expectancy theory is Vroom (1964); motivation is highest when effort leads to performance, performance leads to outcomes, and the outcomes are valuable (not uncertain, difficult or negative). If rewards are guaranteed regardless of performance, instrumentality collapses and so does motivation. Goal-setting theory is Locke and Latham: goals should be specific and challenging, with feedback; vague, easy or feedback-free goals are the wrong options. Equity theory is Adams; the two-factor theory of work is Herzberg; ERG is Alderfer.

Motivation in education

Almost every idea in this chapter has a classroom application. The overjustification research warns against tangible rewards for activities students already enjoy, while cognitive evaluation theory suggests that feedback which informs ("your answer shows clear reasoning") supports intrinsic motivation and feedback which controls ("you must score above 80") undermines it. Attribution theory explains why students who attribute failure to low ability give up while those who attribute it to effort persist; in 1975 Carol Dweck showed that "helpless" children could be retrained to attribute failure to insufficient effort, after which they persisted longer. Dweck's later work distinguished students who hold an entity (fixed) theory of intelligence from those who hold an incremental (growth) theory, and showed that praising effort and strategy rather than ability encourages the latter. Achievement goal research contrasts mastery goals (to learn and improve) with performance goals (to look competent compared with others); mastery goals tend to produce deeper learning and more persistence after failure. Self-efficacy, built through mastery experiences such as graded practice papers, predicts both effort and results, and expectancy-value theory explains why a student works hardest at subjects he both expects to master and values. Finally, the Yerkes-Dodson law reminds teachers and students that a moderate level of exam arousal helps, while high anxiety harms performance, especially on difficult questions.

Motivation in the clinic

Disturbances of motivation run through psychopathology. Anhedonia, the loss of interest or pleasure, is a core symptom of depression, and avolition, a reduction in self-initiated purposeful activity, is a negative symptom of schizophrenia (both are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book). Addiction can be understood through opponent processes and incentive sensitisation (Section 10.4), and treatments such as contingency management deliberately use extrinsic reinforcers (vouchers for drug-free urine samples) to compete with the drug. Behavioural activation treats depression by scheduling rewarding activities, on the reasoning that action can come before motivation and rebuild it.

The best-known clinical method built directly on motivation is motivational interviewing (MI), developed by the American psychologist William Richard Miller (born 1947) in the early 1980s and set out with the British psychologist Stephen Rollnick in Motivational Interviewing (1991). MI is a collaborative, client-centred style of conversation for strengthening a person's own motivation to change. It assumes that people with problems such as harmful drinking are usually not "in denial" but ambivalent: they are caught in an approach-avoidance conflict about change, exactly as in Section 10.8. Confrontation tends to push them to defend the status quo. Instead, the counsellor expresses empathy, helps the client see the discrepancy between current behaviour and deeper goals and values, avoids arguing (originally phrased as "rolling with resistance"), supports self-efficacy, and listens for and reinforces the client's own change talk. Its core skills are summarised as OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries. MI is often combined with James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente's transtheoretical model of the stages of change: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance. The detailed technique belongs to the Clinical book; the motivational insight belongs here: people change most readily when change serves their own values, when they believe they can do it, and when they feel autonomous rather than controlled. That sentence summarises self-determination theory, self-efficacy theory and expectancy-value theory at once.

The case of Harlow's puzzle-solving monkeys, revisited

Go back to the rhesus monkey in Madison, pulling the pin, lifting the hook and swinging open a hasp with nothing behind it. In 1950 the most powerful theory in American psychology could not say what need was being reduced. You can now give several answers, and each comes from a different part of this chapter. The monkey was showing one of the general motives of Section 10.1, a manipulation drive that is inborn yet repairs no tissue deficit. A caged monkey with nothing to do is under-aroused, and optimal arousal theory predicts that it will seek stimulation, just as the students in Hebb's sensory-deprivation cubicles at McGill could not bear to go without it. Berlyne would point to the collative properties of the puzzle (its novelty, its complexity, the uncertainty about how it comes apart); Robert White would point to the satisfaction of having an effect on the world; Deci and Ryan would call it the need for competence. In the simplest language of all, the monkey was intrinsically motivated: the reward was the activity itself, exactly as it is for the toddler who opens an empty box or for you, finishing a crossword that nobody will ever check.

Harlow's odd side observation, that bringing food into the test tended to disrupt the monkeys' smooth performance, has a modern echo too. Two decades later Edward Deci's paid puzzle solvers and Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's rewarded preschool artists showed that an expected external reward can crowd out an internal interest, and cognitive evaluation theory explained why: a reward that feels controlling shifts the perceived cause of the behaviour from inside the person to outside. One more thread runs from that laboratory. Harlow's doctoral student Abraham Maslow left Wisconsin convinced that motivation had to be studied at its highest as well as its lowest levels, and he went on to build the hierarchy of needs. So the small wooden board in Madison stands at a crossroads of the whole chapter. It marked the limits of instinct and drive-reduction theory, and it pointed the way to curiosity, competence, growth and the self.

Chapter summary

10.1 What Motivation Is. Motivation (from the Latin movere, to move) is the set of processes that activate behaviour, direct it, set its intensity and keep it going; it is never seen directly but inferred as an intervening variable. A need is a physiological deficiency, a drive is the aroused psychological state the need produces, a motive is the broader and often learned state that directs behaviour towards a goal, an incentive is an external object that attracts or repels, and a goal is the end state pursued. Put in order, they form the motivational cycle: need, drive, instrumental behaviour, goal, relief. Primary motives (biological, unlearned and mostly homeostatic, such as hunger and thirst) are contrasted with secondary motives (psychosocial and learned, such as achievement, affiliation and gregariousness), with general motives such as curiosity and manipulation in between; Murray's viscerogenic and psychogenic needs draw the same line, and Allport's functional autonomy (1937) explains how a learned motive can outlive its origin. Intrinsic motivation is doing something for its own sake; extrinsic motivation is doing it for a separable outcome. The main families of theory are instinct and psychodynamic, drive and arousal, incentive and expectancy, humanistic, and cognitive.

10.2 Instinct Theories. An instinct is a complex, unlearned, species-specific and stereotyped pattern of behaviour. William James (1890) argued that human beings have more instincts than animals, not fewer. William McDougall (1908) made instincts, each with a cognitive, affective and conative aspect and each paired with a primary emotion, the prime movers of all behaviour in his purposive, hormic psychology, and later listed eighteen propensities. Freud's instincts (Trieb) have a source, aim, impetus and object; in 1920 he divided them into the life instincts (Eros, whose energy is the libido) and the death instinct (Thanatos, seen in war and aggression), with the id as their reservoir and cathexis as the investment of their energy. The ethologists Lorenz, Tinbergen and von Frisch (Nobel Prize 1973) described fixed action patterns released by sign stimuli. Instinct theory declined because naming a behaviour does not explain it, because the lists grew to absurd lengths (L. L. Bernard, 1924), and because culture and rearing (Kuo, 1930) shaped behaviour that was supposed to be inborn.

10.3 Drives, Homeostasis and Arousal. Claude Bernard described the constant milieu intérieur, and Walter Cannon named the body's self-correcting balance homeostasis (1926; The Wisdom of the Body, 1932), a negative-feedback system defending a set point. Woodworth introduced the word drive (1918), and Clark Hull's drive-reduction theory (1943) held that a need creates a drive and that any response which reduces the drive is reinforced; in his equation SER = SHR × D × K × V, drive energises but habit directs. Secondary drives are learned, as Neal Miller's fearful rats (1948) and Mowrer's two-factor theory of avoidance (1947) showed. Drive reduction could not explain Harlow's puzzle solvers, Butler's window-watching monkeys, Sheffield and Roby's saccharin-running rats or the misery of sensory deprivation, which led to Hebb's optimal arousal theory (1955). The Yerkes-Dodson law (1908) describes an inverted-U relation between arousal and performance, with a lower optimum for complex tasks; Zajonc's drive theory of social facilitation (1965) applies the same logic to audiences; and Zuckerman's sensation seeking shows that people differ in the level of arousal they seek.

10.4 The Pull of the Goal: Incentives, Expectancies and Opponent Processes. Incentive theory holds that behaviour is pulled by external rewards rather than pushed by internal tension; Tinklepaugh's monkeys (1928) refused lettuce when they expected banana, and Crespi's contrast effects (1942) forced Hull to add incentive motivation (K) to his equation. Berridge and Robinson separated wanting from liking in their incentive-sensitisation theory of addiction (1993). Expectancy-value theories, from Tolman, Lewin's valences and Rotter's formula (1954) to Atkinson, Vroom and Eccles, make motivation the product of the expectancy that an action will succeed and the value of its outcome, so that motivation vanishes if either is zero. Solomon and Corbit's opponent-process theory of acquired motivation (1974) proposes that every strong emotional state (the a-process) triggers a sluggish opposing b-process that grows stronger with repetition, which explains tolerance, withdrawal and the exhilaration of the veteran parachutist; it must not be confused with Hering's opponent-process theory of colour vision.

10.5 Hierarchies of Need: Maslow, Alderfer and Other Lists. Humanistic psychology, the third force, emphasises free will and an innate tendency to grow. Abraham Maslow (1943, 1954) ordered human needs from physiological through safety, belongingness and love, and esteem to self-actualisation, with the principle of prepotency: unmet lower needs dominate and must be largely satisfied before higher needs take charge. The four lower levels are deficiency (D) needs; self-actualisation is a growth or being (B) need. Self-actualisers show metamotivation towards metaneeds (B-values) whose frustration produces metapathologies, they report peak experiences, and Maslow later added cognitive and aesthetic needs and self-transcendence. Goldstein coined the term self-actualisation. The theory has been criticised for its hand-picked sample, weak evidence for the ordering (Wahba and Bridwell, 1976), counter-examples and cultural bias. Alderfer's ERG theory (1969) reduces the levels to existence, relatedness and growth, lets several needs operate at once and adds frustration-regression. Other lists include those of Murray, Fromm, McClelland, Deci and Ryan, Glasser (whose five needs include power or achievement) and Frankl, whose will to meaning is the basis of logotherapy.

10.6 Psychosocial Motives and Achievement. Henry Murray's Explorations in Personality (1938) described about twenty psychogenic needs, manifest and latent, and the press of the environment (alpha press as it is, beta press as perceived) that helps or hinders them; the Thematic Apperception Test of Morgan and Murray (1935) was built to measure them. David McClelland scored TAT stories for the need for achievement (nAch), whose holders prefer moderate risk, personal responsibility and quick feedback, and added the needs for power and affiliation, all three learned; his Kakinada training studies tested whether nAch can be taught. John Atkinson's theory (1957) makes the tendency to approach success equal to Ms × Ps × (1 − Ps), which peaks at intermediate difficulty, so success-oriented people prefer moderate tasks while failure-avoidant people choose very easy or very hard ones. Matina Horner proposed a fear of success in women (1968, 1972), later reinterpreted as a reflection of cultural stereotypes. Building on Heider's attributions and Rotter's locus of control (1966), Bernard Weiner classified the causes of success and failure by locus, stability and controllability, and showed how each dimension shapes emotion and expectancy.

10.7 Intrinsic Motivation, Competence and the Self. Harlow's manipulation drive, Butler's visual exploration and Berlyne's curiosity (1960), aroused by the collative variables of novelty, complexity, surprise and incongruity, showed that organisms seek stimulation and knowledge for their own sake. Robert White (1959) unified these findings as effectance or competence motivation. Albert Bandura's self-efficacy (1977), a task-specific belief in one's capability built mainly through mastery experiences, is the motivational core of his social cognitive theory and its triadic reciprocal causation. Deci's Soma puzzles (1971) and Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's magic markers (1973) demonstrated the overjustification effect, in which expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic interest. Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory (1985) proposes three innate needs, autonomy, competence and relatedness; its cognitive evaluation theory separates the controlling from the informational aspect of rewards, and its continuum runs from amotivation through external, introjected, identified and integrated regulation to intrinsic motivation. Csikszentmihalyi's flow is total absorption when challenge and skill are balanced, and Festinger's cognitive dissonance (1957) is a drive-like tension that motivates people to make their beliefs and actions consistent.

10.8 Conflict and Frustration. Frustration is the blocking of goal-directed behaviour, arising from environmental obstacles, personal limitations or conflict between motives; it differs from pressure, the demand to perform or conform, and from change. Kurt Lewin (1935) described approach-approach conflicts (the easiest to resolve), avoidance-avoidance conflicts (vacillation and attempts to leave the field) and approach-avoidance conflicts (ambivalence), and later writers added the double approach-avoidance conflict typical of real-life decisions. Neal Miller's gradients (1944, 1959) showed that both approach and avoidance strengthen near the goal but that avoidance is steeper, so the organism vacillates where the lines cross; Dollard and Miller's clinical rule is to reduce fear before increasing desire. Classic reactions to frustration are aggression (direct or displaced, as in scapegoating), regression (Barker, Dembo and Lewin, 1941), fixation (Maier, 1949), withdrawal and apathy, and defence mechanisms. The frustration-aggression hypothesis of Dollard, Doob, Miller, Mowrer and Sears (1939) was softened by Miller (1941) and reformulated by Berkowitz (1989) in terms of negative affect and aggressive cues.

10.9 Biological Motives: Hunger, Thirst, Sex and Others. Cannon and Washburn's swallowed balloon (1912) linked hunger pangs to stomach contractions, but people without stomachs still feel hunger. Glucostatic and lipostatic set-point theories gave way, in Pinel's account, to a positive-incentive view with a settling point for body weight. In the classic dual-centre theory (Stellar, 1954), lesions of the ventromedial hypothalamus, the satiety centre, cause hyperphagia and obesity (Hetherington and Ranson, 1940), and lesions of the lateral hypothalamus, the feeding centre, cause aphagia and adipsia (Anand and Brobeck, 1951); modern work has revised both claims and emphasises insulin, the paraventricular and arcuate nuclei, and signals such as leptin, ghrelin and cholecystokinin. Schachter's externality hypothesis and Herman's restrained eating show the power of external cues. Thirst is osmotic (cell dehydration detected by osmoreceptors) or hypovolaemic (loss of blood volume, signalled through angiotensin II). Kinsey's reports (1948, 1953) surveyed sexual behaviour, Masters and Johnson (1966) described excitement, plateau, orgasm and resolution, and Kaplan (1979) added desire. In Warden's obstruction box the maternal drive was the strongest; pain avoidance, temperature regulation and sleep complete the list of primary motives.

10.10 Motivation in Practice: Work, School and Clinic. Herzberg's two-factor theory (1959) separates motivators intrinsic to the work (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth), which create satisfaction, from hygiene factors in the job environment (policy, supervision, relationships, conditions, salary, security), which only prevent dissatisfaction; job satisfaction depends on both. Vroom's expectancy theory (1964) makes motivational force the product of expectancy, instrumentality and valence; Adams' equity theory explains reactions to unfair outcome-to-input ratios; and Locke and Latham's goal-setting theory shows that specific, challenging goals with commitment and feedback raise performance. McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y and Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics model complete the work picture. In schools, informational feedback, attribution retraining (Dweck, 1975), incremental beliefs about ability, mastery goals, self-efficacy and moderate arousal all support learning. In the clinic, anhedonia, avolition and addiction are disturbances of motivation, and William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick's motivational interviewing (1991) resolves ambivalence by evoking the client's own reasons for change, often alongside Prochaska and DiClemente's stages of change.

From the past papers

  1. A toddler goes to great lengths to open a box. She knows there is nothing in the box. This is an example of: (RML 2024)

    (a) Intrinsic motivation (b) Homeostasis (c) Extrinsic motivation (d) Secondary motivation

    Answer: (a) Intrinsic motivation. The box is empty, so the only reward is the activity of opening it, which is what doing something for its own sake means (compare Harlow's puzzle-solving monkeys). Homeostasis would need a bodily imbalance to correct, extrinsic motivation needs a separable payoff such as a sweet or praise, and secondary motivation refers to learned social motives such as achievement or approval, none of which is being served here.

  2. The phenomenon of "gregariousness" has a: (RML 2022)

    (a) Social motive (b) Biological motive (c) Psychological motive (d) Personal motive

    Answer: (a) Social motive. Gregariousness, the tendency to seek the company of others, is classed with affiliation among the social (secondary or psychosocial) motives. "Biological" tempts only because McDougall listed gregariousness among his instincts; "psychological motive" and "personal motive" are not standard class names in the classification of motives.

  3. Which model asserts that inner forces largely beyond individual control drive human behavior? (RML 2025)

    (a) Cognitive model (b) Behavioral model (c) Humanistic model (d) Psychodynamic model

    Answer: (d) Psychodynamic model. Freud's model places the causes of behaviour in unconscious instincts and conflicts that the person neither sees nor controls. The cognitive model locates the causes in conscious thoughts and beliefs, the behavioural model in conditioning and reinforcement from the environment, and the humanistic model in free will and personal growth, which is the opposite of control by hidden forces.

  4. According to Freud, human beings are motivated by powerful 'innate forces' which are known as: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Needs (b) Drives (c) Instincts (d) Id

    Answer: (c) Instincts. Freud's word Trieb is conventionally translated "instinct", and he grouped the instincts into the life instincts (Eros) and the death instinct. "Needs" is the vocabulary of Maslow and Murray; "drives" is Hull's term (and a fair literal translation of Trieb, but not the classical answer); the id is the reservoir that holds the instincts, not the forces themselves.

  5. Deviations from homeostasis can create an internal state of biological and psychological tension. What is the best term for this state? (RML 2021)

    (a) Regulation (b) Drive (c) Dissonance (d) Equilibrium

    Answer: (b) Drive. In Hull's theory a departure from homeostasis is a need, and the aroused, tense state it produces is a drive, which energises behaviour until the need is reduced. Equilibrium is the balanced state that homeostasis restores, the opposite of tension; regulation is the corrective process (negative feedback), not the tension itself; dissonance is Festinger's tension between inconsistent cognitions, not a bodily deviation.

  6. The Yerkes-Dodson law states that between performance and anxiety, there is: (RML 2024)

    (a) Inverted-U shaped relationship (b) Curvilinear relationship (c) Linear relationship (d) M-shaped relationship

    Answer: (a) Inverted-U shaped relationship. Performance rises with arousal up to a moderate level and then falls, and the peak comes at a lower level of arousal for complex tasks (Figure 10.2). "Curvilinear" is true but less specific, so the precise inverted U wins; a linear relationship would mean that more anxiety always helps or always hurts; no two-peaked (M-shaped) version of the law exists.

  7. According to Zajonc's drive theory of social facilitation, the presence of others raises arousal, which strengthens the dominant response. This means an audience will MOST improve performance on a task that is: (Amity recall)

    (a) Novel and difficult (b) Easy and well-learned (c) Creative and open-ended (d) Performed for the first time

    Answer: (b) Easy and well-learned. On an easy or well-practised task the dominant (most probable) response is the correct one, so the extra arousal from an audience strengthens it and performance improves. On novel, difficult or first-time tasks the dominant response is more often an error, and on creative, open-ended tasks it is the obvious, stereotyped idea, so an audience tends to impair performance there.

  8. In Incentive Theory, motivation is primarily driven by: (RML 2025)

    (a) Intrinsic satisfaction (b) External rewards (c) Biological drives (d) Self-efficacy beliefs

    Answer: (b) External rewards. Incentive theory says behaviour is pulled by external goals, as Tinklepaugh's lettuce-refusing monkeys and Crespi's contrast effects showed. Intrinsic satisfaction is the core of self-determination theory, biological drives push from within in Hull's theory, and self-efficacy beliefs belong to Bandura's social cognitive theory. The same paper asked what drive theory explains motivation as a process to do: reduce physiological needs, not seek external rewards (incentive theory), set long-term goals (goal-setting theory) or increase social connections (affiliation).

  9. Which stage is characterised by realising your full potential according to Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs? (RML 2021)

    (a) Safety Needs (b) Social Needs (c) Esteem Needs (d) Self-Actualisation Needs

    Answer: (d) Self-Actualisation Needs. Self-actualisation, the top of the hierarchy, is the need to become everything one is capable of becoming. Safety needs concern security and order, social needs concern belonging and love, and esteem needs concern respect and achievement; all three are deficiency needs that must be largely met before self-actualisation takes charge.

  10. According to Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs, which need must be mostly fulfilled before an individual seeks esteem needs? (RML 2025)

    (a) Self-actualization needs (b) Physiological needs (c) Social belonging needs (d) Safety needs

    Answer: (c) Social belonging needs. The printed key gives (b). The level immediately below esteem is belongingness and love, so it is the need that must be mostly fulfilled just before esteem needs take over. Physiological and safety needs must be met as well, but they come earlier in the sequence and so are not the best answer to "before esteem"; self-actualisation lies above esteem and cannot be its precondition (see the Exam trap in Section 10.5).

  11. In Reality Therapy, developed by William Glasser, which of the following is considered one of the five basic genetic needs? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Self-actualization (b) Power or Achievement (c) Collective Unconscious (d) Unconditional positive regard

    Answer: (b) Power or Achievement. Glasser's five basic needs are survival, love and belonging, power (achievement and recognition), freedom and fun. Self-actualisation is the top of Maslow's hierarchy, the collective unconscious is Jung's inherited layer of the psyche, and unconditional positive regard is one of Rogers's therapeutic conditions; none of them is a Glasser need.

  12. Viktor Frankl's concept of the "will to meaning," the fundamental motivational force in humans to find purpose in life, is central to which therapeutic school? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) (b) Logotherapy (c) Existential-Humanistic Therapy (d) Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

    Answer: (b) Logotherapy. Frankl held that the primary human motive is the will to meaning, in contrast to Freud's will to pleasure and Adler's will to power, and built logotherapy on it. Existential-humanistic therapy (Rollo May, James Bugental) shares the concern with meaning but is not Frankl's school; REBT (Albert Ellis) disputes irrational beliefs, and ACT (Steven Hayes) trains acceptance and values-based action.

  13. McClelland's theory of needs proposes three acquired needs that drive behaviour. They are the needs for: (Amity recall)

    (a) Achievement, affiliation, and power (b) Safety, esteem, and self-actualization (c) Existence, relatedness, and growth (d) Autonomy, competence, and relatedness

    Answer: (a) Achievement, affiliation, and power. McClelland scored achievement, affiliation and power imagery in TAT stories and insisted that these needs are learned. Option (b) lists three of Maslow's levels, (c) is Alderfer's ERG theory, and (d) gives the three innate needs of Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory.

  14. Which of the following is NOT one of the three fundamental psychological needs according to Self-Determination Theory? (RML 2025)

    (a) Autonomy (b) Competence (c) Affiliation (d) Relatedness

    Answer: (c) Affiliation. The three innate needs of self-determination theory are autonomy, competence and relatedness. Affiliation looks like relatedness, which is why it is the bait, but it is McClelland's learned need (nAff), not an SDT need. The same paper asked which SDT motivation is driven by internal rewards and personal growth: intrinsic motivation, not extrinsic motivation, not amotivation (the absence of motivation) and not external regulation (the least autonomous form of extrinsic motivation).

  15. Reciprocal causation model has been described under: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Social cognitive theory (b) Two-factor theory (c) Drive reduction theory (d) Constructivist theory

    Answer: (a) Social cognitive theory. Bandura's social cognitive theory (1986) rests on triadic reciprocal causation, in which personal factors, behaviour and environment influence one another. "Two-factor theory" names several other theories (Herzberg, Mowrer, Schachter and Singer, Spearman); drive-reduction theory is Hull's one-way chain from need to drive to behaviour; constructivist theory (Piaget, Vygotsky, Bruner) is a theory of learning in which learners actively build their own knowledge, not a causal model of motivation.

  16. Social Cognitive Theory highlights the importance of which factor in motivation? (RML 2025)

    (a) External rewards only (b) Biological drives (c) Self-efficacy (d) Goal difficulty

    Answer: (c) Self-efficacy. In Bandura's theory, belief in one's capability to carry out a task decides which tasks people attempt, how hard they try and how long they persist. External rewards do matter in the theory, but not "only"; biological drives belong to Hull; goal difficulty is the central variable of Locke and Latham's goal-setting theory.

  17. Who introduced the concept of "flow" in psychology? (RML 2023)

    (a) Carl Rogers (b) Abraham Maslow (c) Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (d) Viktor Frankl

    Answer: (c) Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. Csikszentmihalyi described flow (1975, 1990), the absorbed, autotelic state that arises when challenge and skill are in balance. Maslow's peak experiences are brief, ecstatic moments in self-actualisers, Rogers described the fully functioning person, and Frankl the will to meaning.

  18. Arvind is a college student who has strong negative feelings about smoking cigarettes. His grandfather died of lung cancer and he believes smoking is disgusting. One night at a party, Arvind's friends all go outside for a cigarette and he decides to smoke as well. What will he most likely experience as a result? (RML 2021)

    (a) Justification of efforts (b) Increased drive to smoke (c) Cognitive Dissonance (d) Drive Reduction

    Answer: (c) Cognitive Dissonance. Acting against a strongly held belief creates the tension Festinger (1957) called cognitive dissonance, which Arvind will be motivated to reduce, perhaps by telling himself that one cigarette at a party does not count. Justification of effort is a particular kind of dissonance reduction after suffering for a goal, drive reduction is Hull's account of satisfying a bodily need, and nothing in the story suggests an increased drive to smoke.

  19. If Ramesh works in a call centre, and has to work very hard, do a lot of work, and that too at a fast rate, he experiences: (RML 2020)

    (a) Conflict (b) Frustration (c) Unpredictability (d) Pressure

    Answer: (d) Pressure. Pressure is the demand to perform at a high level, quickly and well (or to conform to others' expectations). Frustration would mean that a goal is blocked, conflict that incompatible motives pull him in different directions, and unpredictability that he cannot foresee what will happen; Ramesh's problem is simply the demand itself.

  20. The 'satiety centre' of the brain is located in the: (RML 2024)

    (a) Lateral hypothalamus (b) Ventromedial hippocampus (c) Lateral thalamus (d) Ventromedial hypothalamus

    Answer: (d) Ventromedial hypothalamus. Bilateral VMH lesions produce hyperphagia and obesity (Hetherington and Ranson, 1940), so Stellar's dual-centre theory (1954) called the VMH the satiety centre. The lateral hypothalamus is the opposite, the feeding centre, whose destruction causes aphagia (Anand and Brobeck, 1951); "ventromedial hippocampus" and "lateral thalamus" are invented by changing one word. JEMAS 2025 asked the same question with the cerebrum and the basal ganglia as baits; neither is a hunger centre.

  21. Masters and Johnson's sexual response cycle is distinct from Kaplan's model. Which of the following correctly reflects Masters and Johnson's sequence? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Excitement → Plateau → Orgasm → Resolution (b) Plateau → Excitement → Orgasm → Resolution (c) Excitement → Desire → Plateau → Orgasm → Refractory (d) Desire → Arousal → Plateau → Orgasm → Resolution

    Answer: (a) Excitement → Plateau → Orgasm → Resolution. Masters and Johnson (1966) observed four phases in that order. Option (b) reverses excitement and plateau; options (c) and (d) insert desire, which is Helen Singer Kaplan's addition (desire, excitement, orgasm, 1979), and (c) also ends with "refractory", which is the male recovery period after orgasm, not a phase of the cycle.

  22. According to Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory, which of the following factors are considered motivators that lead to job satisfaction? (RML 2023)

    (a) Salary and benefits (b) Job security and work conditions (c) Recognition and opportunities for advancement (d) Company policies and interpersonal relationships

    Answer: (c) Recognition and opportunities for advancement. Herzberg's motivators belong to the content of the work: achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement and growth. Salary, benefits, job security, working conditions, company policies and interpersonal relationships are all hygiene factors: when they are poor they cause dissatisfaction, but improving them only removes the dissatisfaction without creating motivation.

  23. Job satisfaction depends on (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) work motivation or job environment. (b) only work motivation. (c) personal disposition only. (d) work motivation and job environment.

    Answer: (d) work motivation and job environment. Herzberg's research showed that satisfaction comes from the content of the work (motivators) while dissatisfaction comes from its context (hygiene factors), so the two together determine how a person feels about the job. "Or" and "only" make a single source sufficient, and personal disposition plays a part but is not the only determinant.

  24. In Expectancy Theory, motivation is highest when an individual believes: (1) effort leads to performance, (2) performance leads to outcomes, and (3) outcomes are: (RML 2025)

    (a) Uncertain (b) Valuable (c) Difficult (d) Negative

    Answer: (b) Valuable. Vroom's motivational force is expectancy × instrumentality × valence, and the third term, valence, is the value of the outcome to the person. Uncertain outcomes lower instrumentality, "difficult" describes the task rather than the outcome, and a negative valence repels. A second item in the same paper turned the idea round: motivation is highest when effort leads to performance and performance to desired rewards, not when rewards are guaranteed regardless of performance (instrumentality collapses), when effort is unrelated to performance (expectancy is zero) or when rewards are unimportant (valence is zero).

  25. In Goal-Setting Theory, what characteristic makes a goal most effective at motivating behavior? (RML 2025)

    (a) Being vague and flexible (b) Being specific and challenging (c) Being easy to accomplish (d) Avoiding feedback

    Answer: (b) Being specific and challenging. Locke and Latham found that specific, difficult but attainable goals, accepted by the person and accompanied by feedback, produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals or easy goals. Avoiding feedback is wrong because feedback is one of the conditions that makes goals work. A parallel item in the same paper offered "unrelated to personal values" as a bait; a goal the person does not value wins little commitment, and commitment is another of the theory's conditions.
