Unit 3 – Psychometrics & Test Construction

The science of measuring the mind: how a psychological test is built, scored and judged. This book teaches the four scales of measurement, the anatomy of a good item and how to analyse one, the full reliability and validity families with their formulae, the meaning of norms and standard scores, and the major families of real tests – intelligence, neuropsychological, personality, interest and aptitude – together with where and why each is applied (education, occupation, military, clinic). It closes with the M.Phil-style exam questions folded in. 1. Scales of measurement & attitude scaling

The one idea. Before you can measure a psychological attribute you must decide what kind of number you are assigning. S. S. Stevens' four levels of measurement – nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio – form a ladder: each rung adds one mathematical property and therefore unlocks one more legitimate statistic. Choosing the wrong scale is the commonest hidden error in test interpretation: you cannot average ranks, and you cannot say one IQ is "twice" another.

The four levels

LevelAllows categorisingAllows ranking (order)Equal intervalsTrue (absolute) zeroExample
NominalYes – – – Diagnosis codes, gender, jersey numbers
OrdinalYesYes – – Rank in class, Likert agree/disagree, race finishing order
IntervalYesYesYes – Temperature in °C, IQ scores, calendar years
RatioYesYesYesYesReaction time, weight, number of errors, age

Why each property matters. A nominal scale only labels – the numbers are names, so the only legitimate operations are counting frequencies and the mode. An ordinal scale adds order, so you can take a median and percentiles, but the gaps between ranks are unequal (the difference between 1st and 2nd is not the same "amount" as between 2nd and 3rd). An interval scale adds equal spacing, so you can finally add, subtract and compute a mean and standard deviation; but its zero is arbitrary (0°C is not "no temperature"). A ratio scale adds a true zero meaning total absence of the attribute, so ratios become meaningful: 40 errors is genuinely twice 20 errors.

High-yield

Memorise the ladder of properties: Nominal = categorise; Ordinal = + order; Interval = + equal intervals; Ratio = + true zero. Each level permits everything the levels below it permit, plus one more.

Mnemonic

NOIR (French for "black") gives the order of increasing power: Nominal → Ordinal → Interval → Ratio.

Trap

Interval vs ratio. Both have equal intervals; only ratio has a true zero. IQ and Celsius temperature are interval – an IQ of 0 does not mean "no intelligence," so you cannot say IQ 140 is "twice as smart" as IQ 70. Kelvin temperature and reaction time are ratio.

Semantic differential vs Stapel. Semantic differential uses bipolar adjective pairs with a neutral midpoint; the Stapel scale uses a single (unipolar) adjective on a −5 to +5 line with no neutral point. Guttman is cumulative (endorsing a strong item implies endorsing the weaker ones); Likert is summative.

2. Item writing & item analysis

The one idea. A test is only as good as its items. Item construction is part craft (writing clear, unambiguous questions) and part statistics (keeping the items that actually discriminate among people and dropping those that do not). Item analysis is the set of empirical methods for that pruning.

Guidelines for item writing (DeVellis, 2012)

  • Define clearly what you want to measure before writing anything.
  • Generate an item pool larger than the final test, so weak items can be discarded.
  • Avoid exceptionally long items – length adds reading load, not information.
  • Keep reading difficulty appropriate to the intended respondents.
  • Avoid "double-barrelled" items that pack two ideas into one statement ("I feel anxious and depressed") – the respondent cannot answer cleanly.
  • Mix positively and negatively worded items to counter acquiescence (yea-saying) response bias.

Item formats & correction for guessing

The dichotomous format scores an item 0 or 1 (true/false, right/wrong). The polytomous format gives each item more than two alternatives. In a multiple-choice item, the incorrect alternatives are called distractors; it is usually best to write three or four good distractors per item, and three distractors are about as effective as five in most cases.

Because a low-ability test-taker can score by guessing, scores are sometimes adjusted by a correction for guessing:

Definition

Corrected score = R − W/(n − 1), where R = number of right responses, W = number of wrong responses, and n = number of choices per item. The formula subtracts an estimate of how many "right" answers were really lucky guesses.

Mathematical treatments summarise the mean, the reliability from the binomial distribution, and a guessing threshold – the chance that a low-ability test-taker will reach each score. Personality and attitude measures, by contrast, do not score responses right or wrong; they quantify a characteristic of the response using formats such as Likert, the category scale, and the Q-sort.

Item difficulty (p)

Item difficulty is defined by the proportion of people who get the item correct – confusingly, a higher p means an easier item. The optimal difficulty level lies roughly halfway between 100% correct and the chance level.

Worked example for a four-choice item (chance = .25):

  • Half the difference between 1.00 and chance: (1.00 − 0.25)/2 = 0.75/2 = 0.375
  • Add chance back: 0.375 + 0.25 = 0.625
  • Shortcut: (chance + 1.00)/2 = (0.25 + 1.00)/2 = 0.625

A good test, however, needs items at many difficulty levels, because it must discriminate across the whole range of ability. For most tests, items in the range .30 to .70 maximise the information they give about differences between individuals. Some achievement tests deliberately load on harder items to identify high-ability candidates.

Trap

High p = EASY item, not hard. p = .90 means 90% passed it (easy); p = .20 means only 20% passed (hard). The "optimum" .625 for a 4-choice item already builds in the chance correction – for a true/false item (chance .50) the optimum is (.50 + 1.00)/2 = .75.

Item discrimination

Item discriminability asks whether the people who did well on a particular item also did well on the test as a whole. There are two classic approaches:

  • The extreme-group (discrimination index) method. Compare a high-scoring group (e.g. top third of the class) with a low-scoring group (bottom third). Find the proportion in each group who got the item correct; the difference between these proportions is the discrimination index (D). The closer D is to 1.0, the better the item. A negative D (the weak students passed it more often than the strong ones) is a red flag.
  • The point-biserial method. The correlation between a dichotomous variable (passed/failed the item) and a continuous variable (total test score) is the point-biserial correlation, used here as an index of discriminability. On a short test, correlating an item with the total score is problematic because the item itself inflates the total, so an item-remainder (item-total minus that item) score is used – e.g. on a six-item test, correlate passing item 1 with the score from items 2–6. If the value is negative or low, drop the item.

Item characteristic curves & Item Response Theory

An item characteristic curve (ICC) graphs total test score on the X-axis against the proportion of examinees passing that item on the Y-axis. A good item's curve rises steeply across the ability range. Newer approaches, collectively Item Response Theory (IRT), give each item its own ICC describing the probability of a correct response given the ability level of the test-taker. IRT models use one, two, or three parameters:

  • difficulty,
  • discriminability, and
  • a guessing parameter (the probability that the lowest-ability test-takers answer correctly).

Because a computer can identify exactly which items pin down a given ability level, IRT enables computer-adaptive testing: the test-taker is not forced through items far above or below their level, sparing time and embarrassment.

High-yield

Two pillars of item analysis: difficulty (p) = proportion correct (high = easy, optimum ~.625 for 4-choice); discrimination = does the item separate strong from weak test-takers (discrimination index D = difference in pass rates between high and low groups; point-biserial = item–total correlation). IRT generalises this to per-item curves and powers adaptive testing.

Criterion-referenced tests

A criterion-referenced test compares performance against a defined standard of learning rather than against other people. You first specify objectives precisely (what the programme should achieve), then evaluate items by giving the test to two groups – one that has had the learning unit and one that has not. The resulting frequency polygon looks like a V: low scorers (left) are the un-taught group, high scorers (right) the taught group. The bottom of the V is the antimode (least frequent score), which is taken as the cutting score – the point of decision separating those who have mastered the unit from those who have not.

Trap

Norm-referenced vs criterion-referenced. Norm-referenced compares you to others (percentile rank). Criterion-referenced compares you to a fixed standard ("80% correct = pass"); item variability is deliberately minimal, which is why traditional reliability formulae are inappropriate for them.

3. Reliability

The one idea. Reliability is consistency of measurement. A reliable test gives much the same result on repeated, equivalent measurement. Reliability is never all-or-none; it is a matter of degree, expressed as a coefficient between 0.0 and 1.0.

Classical (true-score) theory

Classical test theory says any observed score is the sum of a true score and error: X = T + e, where T comes from factors that contribute to consistency and e from factors that contribute to inconsistency.

Sources of measurement error: item selection, test administration, and test scoring. There is also systematic measurement error, where (unknown to the developer) the test consistently measures something other than the intended trait; the model becomes X = T + es + eu (systematic + unsystematic error).

Assumptions of the classical model:

  • measurement errors are random;
  • the mean error of measurement = 0;
  • true scores and errors are uncorrelated (rTe = 0);
  • errors on different tests are uncorrelated (r12 = 0).

It follows that the variance of obtained scores is the variance of true scores plus the variance of error: σ2X = σ2T + σ2e.

Definition

The reliability coefficient (rXX) is the ratio of true-score variance to total score variance: rXX = σ2T / σ2X = σ2T / (σ2T + σ2e). It ranges from 0.0 (all error) to 1.0 (all true score).

The families of reliability

Reliability splits into temporal stability (test–retest, alternate-forms) and internal consistency (split-half, coefficient alpha), plus interscorer reliability for subjectively scored tests.

Test–retest reliability

The most straightforward method: administer the identical test twice to the same heterogeneous, representative sample and correlate the scores. Practice, maturation or schooling may raise the second score, but as long as the two sets of scores are strongly correlated, such effects do not threaten test–retest reliability. Psychometricians treat test–retest as the gold-standard approach. Its error source is changes over time.

Alternate-forms (parallel-forms) reliability

Two forms of a test are independently built to the same specifications (often item-by-item), so they share statistical and normative properties. Administer both to the same group and correlate. This shares features with test–retest (two administrations with a gap) but adds item-sampling differences as an extra source of error variance; alternate forms are also expensive to construct. Immediate alternate-forms isolates item sampling; delayed alternate-forms adds change over time.

Split-half reliability

Administer the test once, split it into two equivalent halves, and correlate the half-scores. The challenge is splitting the test into truly equivalent halves; since items are often ordered by difficulty (first half easier), the standard solution is to compare odd items vs even items. Because a half-test is shorter (and shorter tests are less reliable), the half-test correlation must be stepped up to a full-test estimate with the Spearman-Brown formula:

Definition

rSB = 2rhh / (1 + rhh), where rhh is the half-test reliability and rSB the estimated full-test reliability. Example: a half-test reliability of .70 corrects to a full-test reliability of about .82.

Split-half estimates are generally similar to, but often a little higher than, test–retest estimates. Its error sources are item sampling and the particular nature of the split.

Coefficient alpha (Cronbach, 1951)

Rather than rely on one arbitrary split, Cronbach proposed averaging across all possible splits. Coefficient alpha can be thought of as the mean of all possible split-half coefficients, each corrected by Spearman-Brown.

Definition

rα = [N/(N−1)] × (1 − Σσ2j / σ2), where N = number of items, σ2j = variance of one item, Σσ2j = sum of item variances, and σ2 = variance of total scores. Like all reliability estimates, α ranges 0.00–1.00.

Alpha is traditionally read as an index of unidimensionality – how far the scale measures a single factor. But beware: alpha rises with the number of items, so a high alpha can occur even with several dimensions if there are many items. This is exactly why psychometricians still treat test–retest as essential.

Kuder-Richardson (KR-20)

Coefficient alpha is the general case of an earlier formula by Kuder and Richardson (1937): KR-20. KR-20 applies to the special case where each item is scored 0 or 1 (dichotomous). Coefficient alpha extends the Kuder-Richardson method to items that are not scored 0/1 – for example an attitude scale where examinees choose strongly agree / agree / disagree / strongly disagree.

Interscorer (inter-rater) reliability

Some tests leave much to the examiner's judgment in assigning scores – projective tests, and tests of moral development and creativity fall here. A sample of tests is scored independently by two or more examiners and the pairs of scores are correlated. Interscorer reliability supplements the other estimates but does not replace them; you would still assess test–retest or another type in a subjectively scored test. (In behavioural observation, a common convention is that two observers should agree at r ≥ 0.80, or 80% inter-rater agreement for interval recording.)

Special circumstances

  • Unstable characteristics. When the trait itself changes fast (e.g. an electrodermal measure of emotional reactivity), test and retest must be nearly instantaneous to give an accurate reliability index.
  • Speed vs power tests. A speed test has uniform, easy items where the score depends on how many you finish in the time – its reliability must come from test–retest or from two separately timed half-tests (with Spearman-Brown), not from an ordinary single-session split-half. A power test gives ample time but is hard enough that no one gets a perfect score.
  • Restriction of range. Reliability is spuriously low if estimated on a homogeneous sample (e.g. estimating an IQ test's reliability by retesting only college students).
  • Criterion-referenced tests. Their score variability is minimal by design, so traditional reliability approaches are simply inappropriate.
High-yield

Match the method to the error it isolates: test–retest = stability over time; alternate-forms = item sampling (+ time if delayed); split-half / alpha / KR-20 = internal consistency; interscorer = scorer disagreement. KR-20 = dichotomous items; alpha = its general (any-scoring) form. Speed tests must NOT use single-session split-half.

4. Validity

The one idea. Validity is whether the inferences drawn from a test are appropriate, meaningful and useful – classically, whether the test measures what it purports to measure. Modern theory treats validity as a unitary concept, with the traditional "types" being different lines of evidence rather than separate validities. The three traditional categories are content, criterion-related, and construct validity.

Trap

Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. A bathroom scale that reads 5 kg too heavy every time is perfectly reliable yet invalid. A test can be reliable and still measure the wrong thing; but a test that is not reliable cannot be valid. The validity coefficient is in fact capped by reliability (see below).

Content validity

Content validity is the degree to which the items are representative of the universe of behaviour the test was meant to sample – fundamentally a sampling question. The test's items are a sample from a larger population of possible items; if the sample is representative, the test has content validity. Developers must also specify the relevant universe of responses. Content validity is hardest to ensure for ill-defined traits, where it rests on the considered opinion of expert judges.

Quantifying content validity. Two expert judges rate each item on a 4-point relevance scale; ratings are dichotomised into weak (1–2) vs strong (3–4) relevance and entered in a 2×2 agreement table. The coefficient of content validity = D / (A + B + C + D), where D is the cell where both judges rated the item strongly relevant. Example: on a 100-item test both judges agreed 87 items were strongly relevant, giving 87/(4+4+5+87) = .87.

Definition

Face validity is whether a test merely looks valid to users, examiners and especially examinees. It is a matter of social acceptability, not a technical form of validity.

Criterion-related validity

Demonstrated when a test effectively estimates performance on an outcome measure (the criterion). The test score is useful only insofar as it predicts the criterion accurately (e.g. a college entrance exam that predicts later GPA). Two sub-types differ by timing:

  • Concurrent validity – criterion measures obtained at about the same time as the test scores. Correlations between a new test and an established test are typical evidence; the instrument must measure the same construct as the criterion test.
  • Predictive validity – criterion measures obtained in the future (months or years later, like college grades predicted from an entrance exam). When a test is used for prediction, a regression equation is developed.

A criterion must be reliable, appropriate, and free of criterion contamination (the criterion must not be influenced by the test itself). Crucially, the validity coefficient is bounded by reliability:

Definition

The theoretical upper limit of the validity coefficient is the square root of (test reliability × criterion reliability): rxy ≤ √(rxx · ryy). The standard error of estimate (SEest) is the margin of error expected in the predicted criterion score.

Trap

Concurrent vs predictive. The only difference is timing of the criterion. "Same time" = concurrent; "later" = predictive. Both are species of criterion-related validity.

Construct validity

A construct is a theoretical, intangible trait on which people differ (leadership ability, overcontrolled hostility, depression, intelligence, psychopathy). Construct validity is the appropriateness of inferences about the underlying construct. All constructs share two features: there is no single external referent that can validate them (they cannot be fully operationalised), yet a network of interlocking suppositions can be derived from theory. Many theorists regard construct validity as the unifying concept for all validity evidence.

Construct-validity studies fall into several categories: (1) test homogeneity – whether items/subtests are homogeneous and measure a single construct (select items correlating highly with the total score); (2) developmental changes – whether scores change with age as theory predicts (older children should know more vocabulary); (3) theory-consistent group differences – high-construct people score high; (4) theory-consistent intervention effects – scores move correctly after an intervention; (5) convergent and discriminant validation; (6) factor analysis; (7) classification accuracy.

  • Convergent validity – the test correlates highly with other tests sharing the same construct.
  • Discriminant validity – the test does not correlate with measures from which it should differ.
  • When several traits are each measured by several methods and all pairs are correlated, the result is a multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) matrix (Campbell & Fiske) – the classic design for showing convergent and discriminant validity simultaneously.
  • Factor analysis identifies the minimum number of factors needed to account for the intercorrelations among a battery of tests; you administer the battery to several hundred subjects and analyse the correlation matrix.
  • Classification accuracy matters for screening tests – e.g. the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), whose validity hinges on correctly classifying people as cases or non-cases.
High-yield

Three validity families: content (representative sampling of the item universe; quantified by expert-judge agreement), criterion-related (predicts an outcome – concurrent = now, predictive = later), and construct (measures the theoretical trait; the unifying concept; convergent = correlates with similar, discriminant = doesn't correlate with different; MTMM matrix; factor analysis). Face validity is "looks valid" and is NOT real validity.

5. Norms & standard scores

The one idea. A raw score (e.g. "32 correct") is meaningless on its own. Norms give a frame of reference by describing how a representative standardisation (normative) sample performed, so an individual's score can be located relative to others. The deck title for the reliability material was "Reliability, Validity and Norms"; this section teaches the norm concepts at the depth NIMHANS expects.

The normative sample

Norms are only as good as the sample they come from. A good standardisation sample is large, representative of the population for whom the test is intended, and current (norms go stale – the Flynn effect means IQ norms drift over decades). Norms can be national, local, or subgroup-specific (by age, grade, sex). A test like the Henmon-Nelson provides two sets of norms – one by age, one by grade.

Percentile ranks

A percentile rank is the percentage of the norm group scoring at or below a given raw score (a percentile rank of 70 means you did as well as or better than 70% of the sample). Percentiles are intuitive and require only an ordinal scale, but they are not equal-interval: near the middle of a normal distribution, scores pile up, so a few raw-score points span many percentile points; at the extremes, large raw differences move the percentile little. A percentile band (used by the Kuhlmann-Anderson) reports a range, like a confidence interval, to remind the user that any single score has measurement error.

Trap

Percentile rank is not a percentage-correct score. 70th percentile means "better than 70% of people," not "70% of items correct." And percentiles are ordinal, so do not average them – the gap between the 50th and 55th percentile is much smaller in raw terms than the gap between the 90th and 95th.

Standard scores

Standard scores re-express a raw score in standard-deviation units, putting different tests on a common, equal-interval metric.

In a normal distribution, about 68% of scores fall within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD, and 99.7% within ±3 SD – the rule that lets you convert any z to a percentile and back.

Mnemonic

Means and SDs to memorise: z = 0, 1 · T = 50, 10 · IQ = 100, 15 · stanine = 5, 2 (1–9) · sten = 5.5, 2 (1–10). T-score → "Ten" SD; IQ → "fifteen".

Definition

The deviation IQ (Wechsler) expresses intelligence as a standard score with mean 100, SD 15: it locates a person relative to their own age group, fixing the flaw of the old ratio IQ (mental age ÷ chronological age × 100), which broke down in adulthood because mental age plateaus.

High-yield

Raw scores are uninterpretable; norms supply meaning. Percentile rank = % scoring at or below (intuitive but ordinal, not equal-interval). Standard scores (z, T, deviation IQ, stanine, sten) are equal-interval and comparable across tests. 68–95–99.7 within ±1/2/3 SD. Wechsler IQ: mean 100, SD 15.

6. Areas of testing: intelligence & creativity

The one idea. Intelligence has never had one agreed definition – each theorist measured what they conceived. The major individual tests (Wechsler, Stanford-Binet) operationalise these ideas, and creativity tests extend the toolkit to divergent thinking.

The Wechsler tests

From the 1930s, David Wechsler at Bellevue Hospital built instruments that defined adult intelligence testing. The first, the Wechsler-Bellevue Intelligence Scale (1939), drew on the Binet scales and the Army Alpha and Beta tests. It was designed to fix flaws of earlier tests: items had no appeal for adults; too many emphasised mere word manipulation; instructions stressed speed over accuracy; and reliance on mental age was irrelevant to adults.

Wechsler's corrections: a test designed specifically for adults; performance items added to balance verbal questions; reduced emphasis on speed; and a new deviation IQ method (score relative to one's own age group) rather than the ratio IQ. He split the scale into Verbal and Performance sections, hoping large V–P differences would aid psychiatric diagnosis.

The three modern Wechsler tests are WAIS-IV (adults), WISC-IV (children), and WPPSI-IV (preschool). Common features: 13–15 subtests and an empirically based breakdown into composite/index scores plus a Full Scale IQ. Where the originals gave only Verbal IQ and Performance IQ, the WISC-IV and WAIS-IV now yield four index scores: Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory, and Processing Speed. The WPPSI-IV (ages 4:0–7:7) adds a fifth, Fluid Reasoning.

Other intelligence tests

  • Stanford-Binet, Fifth Edition (SB5) – lineage to the Binet-Simon 1905 scale; the oldest, most prestigious pedigree. Built so each of five factors is assessed in two domains (nonverbal and verbal). The five factors (from Carroll, 1993; Baddeley, 1986) are fluid reasoning, knowledge, quantitative reasoning, visual-spatial processing, and working memory; crossing 5 factors × 2 domains gives 10 subtests.
  • Detroit Tests of Learning Aptitude-4 (DTLA-4) – revision of a 1935 instrument; individually administered, ages 6–17; 10 subtests forming 16 composites (general intelligence, optimal level, and 14 ability areas).
  • Cognitive Assessment System-II (CAS-II) – ages 5–17; explicitly built on the PASS theory (Planning, Attention, Simultaneous, Successive) of intelligence.
  • Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test-2 (KBIT-2) – a brief screening test with a Verbal/Crystallised scale (Verbal Knowledge, Riddles) and a Nonverbal/Fluid scale (Matrices). Its brevity suits research and signalling the need for fuller assessment.

Creativity

Most definitions stress novelty and usefulness of the product, and treat creativity as a kind of process. Kaufman (2012) factor-analysed 94 items into five factors – Self/Everyday, Scholarly, Performance, Mechanical/Scientific, and Artistic creativity – producing the Kaufman Domains of Creativity Scale (K-DOCS).

Classic creativity tests:

  • Remote Associates Test (RAT) (Mednick, 1962) – a timed 40-minute paper-and-pencil test indexing the remoteness of verbal associations (find a word linking three cues, e.g. rat–blue–cottage → "cheese"). Critics (Osche, 1990) say it is more a measure of verbal intelligence than of general creativity.
  • Composite Creative Personality Scale (Harrington, 1975) – 42 self-checked adjectives.
  • Guilford's Structure of Intellect (SOI) model parcelled intelligence into 150 factors; from the operations dimension came divergent thinking. Guilford's experimental divergent-thinking measures (Alternate Uses, Consequences, Ideational Fluency) were standardised as the Christensen-Guilford Fluency Tests.
  • Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT) – loosely based on Guilford; two parallel forms (A, B) and two parts (Verbal, Figural). Verbal scoring uses Fluency (number of relevant ideas), Originality (inventiveness), and Flexibility (range of categories). Figural scoring adds Abstractness of Titles, Elaboration, and Resistance to Premature Closure.
High-yield

Wechsler = deviation IQ + V/P split → four indices (VC, PR, WM, PS). SB5 = 5 factors × 2 domains = 10 subtests. CAS-II = PASS theory. Creativity's signature construct is Guilford's divergent thinking; the TTCT scores Fluency, Originality, Flexibility (+ Elaboration on the figural part).

7. Areas of testing: neuropsychological assessment

The one idea. Neuropsychological tests map cognition onto brain function across discrete domains – orientation, attention, memory, language, spatial ability, and executive function. The Mental Status Examination (MSE) is the loosely structured interview that usually precedes other assessment; it describes the patient's orientation, memory, thought, feeling and judgment and is the psychological equivalent of the general physical exam.

By domain

  • Sensory input. Reitan-Klove Sensory-Perceptual Examination (unilateral/bilateral stimulation in touch, hearing, vision – especially diagnostic if errors cluster on one side of the body); Finger Localization Test (identify touched fingers without sight).
  • Attention & concentration. Test of Everyday Attention (TEA) (Robertson et al., 1994/96) measures sustained, selective, divided attention and attentional switching, with three parallel versions. The Continuous Performance Test (CPT) (Rosvold et al., 1956) measures sustained attention/vigilance – errors of omission (failing to respond to a target) and errors of commission (responding to a non-target). Digit Span and Serial Subtractions also index attention.
  • Learning & memory. Wechsler Memory Scale-IV (WMS-IV) – nine subtests (seven for the Standard Battery) yielding five indices (Immediate Memory, Delayed Memory, Auditory Memory, Visual Memory, Visual Working Memory). Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) – descended from Claparede's free-recall of a 15-word list; five learning trials, an interference list, immediate recall, then a recognition trial. Wide Range Assessment of Memory and Learning-2 (WRAML-2) – first comprehensive memory scale for children (5–17, norms now to 90); three indices (Verbal Memory, Visual Memory, Attention/Concentration).
  • Language. Standardised aphasia screening tests resemble the brief clinical exam but add objective, precise scoring; their weakness is missing subtle aphasia.
  • Spatial & manipulatory ability. Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test (BGT) – design copying; the original nine cards, BGT-II adds seven; assembly tests (Benton 3-D Block Construction, forms A and B).
  • Executive functions (logical analysis, conceptualisation, reasoning, planning, flexibility). Porteus Mazes; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) – sort 64 cards (1–4 symbols: triangle, star, cross, circle; in red, green, yellow, blue) by an unknown principle, told only "right"/"wrong" as you go (sensitive to perseveration and set-shifting); Lezak's Tinkertoy Test – build anything from 50 pieces, scored 21–112 (pieces used, mobility, symmetry, naming); promising for Alzheimer's assessment.

Screening for alcohol use disorders

  • CAGE – four questions: Cutting down, Annoyed by criticism, Guilty about drinking, Eye-opener in the morning. More effective for screening men than women.
  • TWEAK – developed specifically for women: Tolerance, Worried friends/relatives, Eye-opener, Amnesia, feeling the need to Kut down.
Mnemonic

Alcohol screens: CAGE (Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener) for general/men; TWEAK for women. WCST → executive/set-shifting; CPT → sustained attention (omission vs commission errors); RAVLT → verbal learning over 5 trials.

8. Areas of testing: personality assessment

The deck groups personality inventories by their method of construction (the next section unpacks the four strategies in full). Key instruments named:

Theory-guided inventories

  • Personality Research Form (PRF) (Jackson) – expands Murray's needs; short forms A/B and long forms AA/BB (440 true–false items); the long form gives 20 personality scores plus two validity scores (Infrequency, Desirability).
  • State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) – current Form Y; distinguishes transient state anxiety from stable trait anxiety.

Factor-analytically derived inventories

  • Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) – measures three dimensions: Psychoticism (P), Extraversion (E), Neuroticism (N), plus a Lie (L) validity scale; 90 yes/no items (age 16+); Junior EPQ has 81 items (ages 7–15).
  • Comrey Personality Scales (CPS) – 180 items, 8 trait scales (Trust, Orderliness, Social Conformity, Activity, Emotional Stability, Extraversion, Mental Toughness, Empathy) plus Validity Check (V) and Response Bias (R).
  • Also factor-derived: 16PF and the NEO-FFI (Big Five).

Criterion-keyed inventories: the MMPI

The MMPI (1943) was a 566-item true–false inventory built for psychiatric diagnosis; the MMPI-2 (1989) restandardised it. Its four classic validity scales:

  • Cannot Say (?) – total items omitted or double-marked.
  • L (Lie) – 15 items scored "false"; endorsing personal virtue rarely seen in the culture flags faking-good.
  • F (Infrequency) – 60 items endorsed by normals ≤10% of the time; reflects serious maladjustment, but very high F can also mean poor reading, random responding, faking-bad, or a "cry for help".
  • K – a subtle defensiveness scale (30 items) detecting normal-looking profiles from defensive patients.
High-yield

MMPI validity scales = ? (Cannot Say), L (Lie), F (Infrequency), K (defensiveness). EPQ measures P-E-N + Lie. STAI separates state (now) from trait (enduring) anxiety.

9. Interest & aptitude tests

The one idea. Interest inventories measure what a person likes (vocational preferences), distinct from aptitude tests which measure what a person can learn to do. Both feed career counselling and personnel selection.

History of interest inventories

The first item pool of interest activities was assembled in a 1919 seminar by Clarence S. Yoakum at Carnegie Institute of Technology – a 1,000-item pool built by rational sampling to represent the whole domain of interests. Many later inventories grew from it: the Occupational Interest Inventory (Freyd, 1922–23), Interest Report Blank (Cowdery, 1926), General Interest Survey (Kornhauser, 1927), Vocational Interest Blank (Strong, 1927), Purdue Interest Report (Remmers, 1929), Interest Analysis Blank (Hubbard, 1930), and the Minnesota Interest Inventory (Paterson et al., 1930).

The earliest inventories used the atheoretical, empirical method of contrast groups: people with similar interests cluster together and can be differentiated from dissimilar groups. This logic underlies the Strong Interest Inventory (Harmon, Hansen, Borgen & Hammer, 1994), the Career Assessment Inventory (Johansson, 1975/1986), and the Campbell Interest and Skill Survey (CISS) (Campbell, Hyne & Nilsen, 1992).

Theories of vocational interest

Anne Roe (1956) and John Holland (1959) drew on Guilford's factor analysis, which found seven interest factors: (a) mechanical, (b) scientific, (c) social welfare, (d) aesthetic expression, (e) clerical, (f) business, and (g) outdoor work.

Holland's theory of vocational personality (RIASEC). From data with the Vocational Preference Inventory (VPI) and other inventories, Holland proposed that people can be classed into six types (or combinations): Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional. The types arrange on a hexagon in R-I-A-S-E-C order: adjacent types (e.g. Realistic–Investigative, Enterprising–Conventional) are more related than opposite types (e.g. Realistic–Social, Artistic–Conventional). His theory spawned the Self-Directed Search (SDS), the ACT Interest Inventory, the Career Decision-Making System-Revised (CDM-R; Harrington & O'Shea, 1993), and the General Occupational Themes of the Strong Interest Inventory.

Mnemonic

Holland's hexagon: RIASEC – "Realistic Investigators And Social Enterprising Conventionals." Adjacent = similar; opposite = dissimilar.

Current inventories

Major current interest inventories include the Vocational Preference Inventory (Holland, 1985), the Self-Directed Search, various forms of the Kuder, the Strong Interest Inventory, the Career Assessment Inventory, the Jackson Vocational Interest Survey (JVIS) (Jackson, 1977), the unisex ACT Interest Inventory (UNIACT; Lamb & Prediger, 1981), and the Vocational Interest Inventory.

Campbell Interest and Skill Survey (CISS) profile has three scale types: 7 Orientation Scales (the major interest factors from statistical clustering), 29 Basic Scales (homogeneous content clusters such as Sales, Supervision, Adult Development, International Activities, Science, Woodworking, Military/Law Enforcement), and 60 Occupational Scales (built by E. K. Strong's empirical method of contrast groups, surveying successful, satisfied workers in 60 occupations). A distinctive CISS feature is reporting both interest and self-reported skill.

Aptitude test batteries

Aptitude tests predict future learning/performance and rely heavily on criterion-related validity (contrast with achievement tests, which look backward at what has been learned and rest on content validity). Major multiple-aptitude batteries (also covered in the applications deck) include the General Aptitude Test Battery (GATB), the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery (ASVAB), the Differential Aptitude Test (DAT), and the Employee Aptitude Survey. Specialised aptitude tests include the Bennett Mechanical Comprehension Test (mechanical aptitude via pictures of belts and flywheels; forms S and T), the Minnesota Spatial Relations Test and Revised Minnesota Paper Form Board (spatial), the Crawford Small Parts Dexterity Test and Purdue Pegboard (motor/dexterity), and the Minnesota Clerical Test (Number and Name Comparison, scored for speed with errors penalised).

High-yield

Interest inventories built by the empirical method of contrast groups (Strong, CISS, CAI). Holland's RIASEC hexagon is the most-tested vocational theory. CISS scales = 7 Orientation + 29 Basic + 60 Occupational. Aptitude predicts future ability (criterion-related validity); achievement measures past learning (content validity).

10. Applications of testing

The one idea. The same psychometric machinery serves very different settings – schools, employers, the military, and the clinic. Each setting privileges a different kind of validity evidence.

Testing in education

Achievement tests measure the effects of a specific programme of instruction and represent a terminal evaluation of training; their validity is mostly content-related. Aptitude tests reflect the cumulative influence of everyday experience and predict subsequent performance, relying on criterion-oriented evidence.

  • Group achievement tests: Stanford Achievement Test (10th ed., K–12); Metropolitan Achievement Test (MAT-8, renormed 2000, with Braille/large-print/audio versions).
  • Group mental ability tests: Kuhlmann-Anderson (KAT, 8th ed., 8 levels, nonverbal at lower levels, scores as deviation IQs or percentile bands); Henmon-Nelson (single g score, norms by age and by grade, low ceiling, caution below 80 or above 130); Cognitive Abilities Test (CogAT, verbal/quantitative/nonverbal, good for poor readers and ESL, sensitive to giftedness).
  • Graduate/professional entrance: GRE (verbal GRE-V, quantitative GRE-Q, analytical GRE-A – essay since 2002; overpredicts younger, underpredicts older students; prone to grade inflation); Miller Analogies Test (strictly verbal, 120 analogies in 60 minutes, has an age bias); LSAT (reading comprehension, logical reasoning, analytical reasoning).
  • Early childhood / readiness: Metropolitan Readiness Tests (6th ed.); Boehm Test of Basic Concepts (relational concepts). School readiness = prerequisite skills enabling the learner to profit from instruction.

Occupational / personnel selection

I/O psychology emphasises criterion-related validity for selection – tests should correlate strongly with the criterion of job performance. Guion noted the difficulty of predicting complex behaviour (partly situation-dependent) from simple tests.

  • Autobiographical data via a Biographical Information Blank, scored by empirical keying: contrast a successful and an unsuccessful group, find which biodata items discriminate, and weight them.
  • Employment interview: reliability assessed by correlating different interviewers; prone to halo bias (rating high/low on all dimensions from a global impression); panel interviews raise reliability.
  • Cognitive ability tests: general (Wonderlic Personnel Test – 50 items in 12 minutes, a speeded test, points added for those 30+; Shipley, Wesman); mechanical (Bennett); clerical (Minnesota Clerical Test); the multiple-aptitude GATB (12 tests, 9 factors, 3 composites).
  • Personality in selection: most personality–proficiency correlations are negligible, but specific criteria predict better, and the Big Five (especially Conscientiousness) show useful operational validity (corrected for sampling error, range restriction, criterion unreliability). The CPI predicts managerial potential; the Inwald Personality Inventory and MMPI screen law enforcement; the Hogan Personality Inventory (built on the Big Five) predicts performance across settings. The added unique information a test gives is its incremental validity.
  • Integrity tests: attitudes toward theft + overt admissions; overt vs personality-based types.
  • Work samples (a miniature replica of the job) and situational exercises (the white-collar equivalent, for managerial selection).

Military

The ASVAB is used across the U.S. armed services – a multiple-aptitude battery for grades 11–12 with 10 subtests (general science, arithmetic reasoning, word knowledge, paragraph comprehension, numerical operations, coding speed, auto/shop, mathematics knowledge, mechanical comprehension, electronics information) grouped into academic, occupational, and overall composites.

Clinical & counselling

The WAIS and Stanford-Binet are staples; clinicians also do profile analysis – examining the pattern of subtest scores via three procedures: evaluating scatter (V–P differences, subtest variation), interpreting features against base-rate data, and matching score patterns to clinical syndromes (Alzheimer's, learning disability, anxiety). Later research found little significance in scatter and favoured combining objective scores with qualitative clinical interpretation.

  • Specific learning disability is diagnosed when there is a severe discrepancy between intellectual ability and achievement, and the child is not achieving commensurate with age and ability despite appropriate education. Tools: Kaufman Test of Educational Achievement; Woodcock-Johnson; Stanford-Binet, K-ABC, Wechsler scales (for global index + qualitative deficits).
  • Dynamic assessment deliberately departs from standardised administration – "testing the limits" and learning-potential assessment using a test–teach–test format.

Behavioural assessment

From the behavioural movement (1960s–70s), many clinicians shifted to directly assessing the target behaviour (motor responses, physiological responses like heart rate, and verbal reports). Lang & Lazovik's (1963) classic desensitisation paper introduced three still-used strategies: the Fear Survey Schedule (FSS) (50 fears rated 1–7), the Behavioural Avoidance Test (BAT) (motor approach to a phobic object), and the Fear Thermometer (rate discomfort 1–10). Behavioural assessment is integrated with treatment and formalised as the single-case experimental design.

Four basic measurement approaches for ongoing behaviour:

MethodWhat it recordsNote
Permanent productsA lasting trace/result of behaviour (blood alcohol level, weight loss, a wet bed)Behaviour is inferred from the product; minimal observer time, high inter-rater agreement, but no guarantee the behaviour was performed appropriately.
Event recordingTally the rate of the target behaviourOnly for behaviours with clear beginning and end points; low-frequency behaviours need longer periods.
Duration recordingHow long the behaviour lasts (per occurrence, cumulative, or % of total time)Requires discrete limits.
Interval recording (time sampling)Whether the behaviour occurs in chosen time intervalsFor behaviours with unclear limits/rate; high inter-rater reliability; cannot distinguish frequency from duration within an interval.

For inter-rater reliability, behavioural assessors use two observers; a primary observer and an independent checker who rates one-third to one-half of periods. By convention, a Pearson correlation ≥ 0.80, or 80% inter-rater agreement (interval recording), is acceptable.

High-yield

Setting → favoured validity: achievement/education = content; aptitude & personnel = criterion-related. Personnel: empirical keying, halo bias, incremental and operational validity. Behavioural assessment's four recording methods: permanent products, event, duration, interval (time sampling).

11. Structured personality-test construction

The one idea. There are four strategies for building a structured (objective) personality test, grouped into deductive (logical-content, theoretical) and empirical (criterion-group, factor-analytic). Knowing which test was built by which strategy is a perennial exam target.

Deductive strategies

Logical-content strategy

Items are written whose content logically taps the trait. Examples:

  • Woodworth Personal Data Sheet – the first personality inventory; a "paper-and-pencil psychiatric interview" giving a single global score. Items endorsed by ≥25% of normals were excluded (to cut false positives); only symptoms occurring twice as often in a neurotic group as in normals were kept.
  • Bell Adjustment Inventory – multidimensional: Home, Health, Social, Vocational/Occupational, Economic, Emotional functioning.
  • Bernreuter Personality Inventory – ages 13+, six traits (introversion, confidence, sociability…).
  • Mooney Problem Checklist (1950) – a list of problems recurring in case histories and students' written statements.

Theoretical strategy

Items are derived from a personality theory.

  • Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS) – based on Murray's (1938) need system (achievement, deference, exhibition…); Edwards selected 15 needs and, to combat social-desirability response bias, paired items of comparable desirability in a forced-choice format. Choosing between two needs yields an ipsative score (relative, not absolute). A consistency scale repeats 15 item pairs.
  • Personality Research Form (PRF-III) and Jackson Personality Inventory-Revised (JPI-R) – also based on Murray's needs, with strict definitional standards; include an F-like infrequency scale and a K-like social-desirability scale. The JPI-R has 300 true–false items, 15 scales, five higher-order dimensions (analytical, extroverted, emotional, opportunistic, dependable). The PRF is mainly for research.
Definition

An ipsative score (from forced-choice formats like the EPPS) expresses the strength of one trait relative to the person's own other traits, not relative to other people – so you cannot compare ipsative scores across individuals the way you can normative scores.

Empirical strategies

Criterion-group strategy

Items are kept only if they empirically distinguish a criterion group from controls – content is irrelevant.

  • MMPI – the archetype. Eight original clinical scales were built from eight criterion groups of 50 patients each, compared with ~700 controls. Plus the masculinity-femininity (Mf) and social-introversion (Si) scales. Validity scales: L (rationally derived lie scale), K (empirically identified defensiveness), F (infrequency/deviant responding), and ? (Cannot Say / omissions). The original MMPI had 550 items + 16 repeated = 566; the MMPI-2 has 567 items and adds validity scales FB (Back F), VRIN (Variable Response Inconsistency – random responding) and TRIN (True Response Inconsistency – acquiescence), plus 15 content scales (e.g. HEA health concerns, TPA Type A, FAM family problems, WRK work interference).
  • California Psychological Inventory (CPI) – criterion-group method applied to normally adjusted people (counselling settings). 20 scales in four classes (I: poise/self-assurance; II: socialisation/maturity/responsibility; III: achievement potential/intellectual efficiency; IV: interest modes), plus 13 special-purpose scales (managerial potential, tough-mindedness, creativity).

Factor-analytic strategy

Statistical factor analysis reduces many items to a few underlying dimensions.

  • Cattell's 16PF – from a catalogue reduced to 4,504 "real" traits → 171 terms → 36 surface traits → finally 16 source traits (basic dimensions of personality). The 16 factors themselves were factor-analysed into four second-order factors. Six forms (parallel forms at three vocabulary levels); parallel versions exist for younger ages (Junior-Senior High School PQ; Children's PQ).
  • Clinical Analysis Questionnaire (CAQ) – extends the 16PF with 12 additional factors for clinical (psychopathology) assessment.
Mnemonic

Four construction strategies: Deductive = Logical-content + Theoretical (write items from logic/theory); Empirical = Criterion-group + Factor-analytic (let data decide). Woodworth = logical-content; EPPS/PRF = theoretical (Murray's needs); MMPI/CPI = criterion-group; 16PF = factor-analytic.

Trap

MMPI item counts: original MMPI = 566 (550 + 16 repeated); MMPI-2 = 567. Don't confuse the validity scales' origins: L was rationally derived, K was empirically identified.

12. M.Phil exam questions

The M.Phil question bank ranges across clinical psychology; the psychometrics-relevant items are folded into the sections above. The remaining high-yield items (history, ethics, classification, assessment-data concepts) are distilled here with their keyed answers.

History & the profession

  • Who coined the term "mental tests"? – James McKeen Cattell (not Binet, Terman, or Wechsler).
  • Clinical psychology was shaped by the psychometric and dynamic traditions.
  • The U.S. armed forces began working with clinical psychologists due to a sudden dramatic expansion in World War I (psychologists began working with adults in WWI). Before World War II clinical psychology was not a recognised profession; WWII drove its rise.
  • The Shakow Report recommended high academic and personal standards for selecting graduate students; its recommendations were adopted at the Boulder conference (the scientist-practitioner / "Boulder model"). Basic criticism: training students as both scientists and professionals is desirable in theory but hard in practice.
  • Gordon Derner introduced the term scholar-professional; the Vail report proposed the Doctor of Psychology (Psy.D.) degree.
  • Recommended curriculum focus areas: Assessment, Research, and Treatment.

Indian legal/regulatory framework

  • Eligibility for M.Phil Clinical Psychology: a Master's degree in Psychology.
  • The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) is under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment; its mandate (all of the above): regulate/monitor disability services, standardise rehabilitation/special-education syllabi, and maintain the Central Rehabilitation Register.
  • Under the Mental Health Care Act, 2017, registration with the RCI is mandatory to practise in India; the Act mandates rehabilitation professionals to register.
  • Recognised clinical-psychology qualification (RPWD Act, 2016): a PG diploma in (Clinical/Applied) Psychology + M.Phil in Clinical Psychology or Medical and Social Psychology.
  • Ethical standards in India are set by the RCI and the Indian Association of Clinical Psychologists (IACP); ethical guidelines also come from the IACP and the National Academy of Psychology (NAOP).

Assessment-data concepts

  • The most standardised and consistent assessment tool is tests; the most valid form of assessment is observation. A life record example is a medical history.
  • Clinicians conceive assessment information as samples, correlates, or signs. The approach not tied to any theoretical orientation is the correlate-oriented / psychometric approach.
  • Cognitive-function tools: Folstein's MMSE (systematic exam of higher mental functions; Memory category assesses all sub-types; Intelligence category assesses general knowledge and abstract thinking; Insight = awareness of one's illness); Glasgow Coma Scale = consciousness; Digit Span = attention; Serial Subtractions = attention. A significant disturbance of cognitive function commonly indicates an organic psychiatric disorder.

Ethics

  • The APA's Ethical Principles has five General Principles; Equity is NOT one of them. Integrity emphasises accuracy/honesty/truthfulness; Respect for People's Rights and Dignity emphasises privacy, confidentiality and self-determination.
  • Deontological ethics judges the intrinsic nature of actions (moral duties/obligations); the utilitarian view judges by consequences (cost-benefit).
  • The four pillars of principle-based ethics: beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and respect for autonomy. Beneficence = acting in the person's best interests; when principles conflict, non-maleficence ("do no harm") is often the primary obligation.
  • Confidentiality may be broken (IACP) given sufficient evidence of abuse in the family; in couple/family therapy the therapist is a relationship advocate (Margolin, 1982).

Classification & psychopathology (high-yield keyed answers)

  • The syndromal approach classifies disorders by clinical signs and symptoms (vs the historical approach by known etiology); a syndrome is a group of symptoms/signs occurring together. The two aspects judging DSM/ICD usefulness are reliability (agreement on diagnosis) and validity (distinguishing one diagnosis from another).
  • The nomothetic framework (pre-established categories) is the mainstream "psychiatric model"; the idiographic framework (singularity of each case) suits psychoanalytic/psychodynamic approaches.
  • Models of normality: Medical = absence of disorder; Utopian = optimal functioning; Subjective = absence of distress/disability/help-seeking; Social = socially acceptable behaviour; Continuum = normal and disorder at two ends of a continuum (severity above a cutoff = symptom); Process = a dynamic changing process; Statistical = within two SDs of the population's normal curve.
  • DSM-IV-TR multiaxial system: Axis I clinical diagnosis; Axis II personality disorders + mental retardation; Axis III general medical conditions; Axis IV psychosocial/environmental problems; Axis V Global Assessment of Functioning. The DSM-5 dropped the multiaxial system and added a dimensional component.
High-yield

Memorise: "mental tests" = Cattell; scientist-practitioner = Boulder model (from the Shakow Report); Psy.D = Vail; RCI under Min. of Social Justice & Empowerment; registration mandatory under MHCA 2017; four ethics pillars = beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, autonomy; DSM/ICD judged on reliability & validity.
