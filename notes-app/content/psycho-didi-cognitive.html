1 · Sensation & Perception

The perceptual sequence runs stimulation → transduction → sensation → attention → perception. Perception can be driven two ways:

  • Top-down — expectation driven (knowledge and context shape what we perceive).
  • Bottom-up — stimulus / data driven (the raw sensory input drives perception).
Top-down vs bottom-up. Top-down = expectation/knowledge driven (constructivist, e.g. Gregory). Bottom-up = stimulus/data driven (direct/ecological, e.g. Gibson). Endogenous attention is top-down; exogenous attention is bottom-up.

Gestalt psychology

Core dictum: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. Key ideas include the Phi phenomenon and the law of Prägnanz — every stimulus pattern is seen so that the resulting structure is as simple as possible.

Prägnanz in practice (the ring drawing). Prägnanz, also called the law of good figure or law of simplicity, is the master law of the Gestalt psychologists (Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka, Wolfgang Köhler): of all the ways a pattern could be organised, we see the simplest, most regular and most stable one. The page shows interlocking rings drawn two ways. In the black version (the Olympic logo) we see five complete circles lying over each other, not a puzzle of curved pieces and odd-shaped gaps. In the blue version the overlapping bits are only dashed, yet we still see five whole rings, because "five circles" is the simplest reading and the mind fills the gaps.

Prägnanz vs closure. Closure is one specific law (filling small gaps to see a whole). Prägnanz is the general principle that sits above all the laws: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity and common fate are all ways of reaching the simplest organisation. If an MCQ asks for the "fundamental" or "overall" Gestalt law, the answer is Prägnanz.
Max Wertheimer — founder of Gestalt perception; described the Phi phenomenon, a type of stroboscopic motion, best seen at a time interval of 30 to 200 milliseconds.

Perceptual Constancy

  • Colour / brightness constancy — perceiving colour or brightness as constant despite changes in lighting.
  • Size constancy — perceiving an object as the same size regardless of its distance from the observer.
  • Shape constancy — perceiving an object as maintaining its shape despite changes in orientation.

Apparent (Illusory) Motion

Perception of motion when there actually is none.

  • Autokinetic effect — a stationary point of light appears to move in a dark room. Found by Muzafer Sherif (1935).
  • Induced motion — motion of one object causes a nearby stationary object to appear to move (e.g. clouds moving past the moon make the moon appear to race through the clouds).
  • Phi phenomenon — an optical illusion in which stationary objects shown in rapid succession, past the threshold at which they can be perceived separately, appear to move. Temporal threshold: 30–200 ms.
  • Beta phenomenon — similar to the phi phenomenon but usually circular motion.
  • Critical Flicker Fusion Frequency — the frequency at which a flickering light appears steady; the threshold where separate/discontinuous images seem to fuse.
  • Motion parallax — as we move, nearby objects appear to glide rapidly past us in the direction opposite to our own movement, while more distant objects move more slowly; very distant objects such as the moon seem to stand still or even travel along with us (the moon "follows" the car). In the page's drawing the passenger keeps his eyes on the house (the fixation point) while he travels to the left: things nearer than the house, like the grass, sweep backwards against his direction of travel, while things beyond the house drift slowly forwards, the same way he is going. Strictly, motion parallax is a real difference in how fast images move across the retina, and it is used as a monocular depth cue (see Depth & Distance Cues below), not an illusion of motion.
  • Stroboscopic effect — a rapid succession of still images or lights creates the illusion of motion.
    • Worked example: a cinema film is a series of still frames shown at 24 frames per second; a flip-book, or a neon sign whose bulbs light one after another, works the same way. Each picture is still, but shown quickly enough in sequence the brain sees one smooth movement.
    • The dancer photo on the page is a stroboscopic (multi-flash) photograph: a strobe light flashed many times while the dancer moved, so one picture holds a row of frozen positions. Flash those positions one after another fast enough and you would see a single continuous movement.
    • Under a strobe light or on film, a spinning wheel can look frozen or even turn backwards (the wagon-wheel effect), another stroboscopic illusion.
Phi vs stroboscopic. The phi phenomenon (Max Wertheimer, 1912) is one particular kind of stroboscopic motion: two stationary lights flashed one after the other at the right interval (about 30 to 200 ms) look like one light jumping across. "Stroboscopic motion" is the wider term for any apparent motion produced by a series of still images.

Depth & Distance Cues

Monocular cues

CueDescription
AccommodationChange in the lens shape as the eye focuses.
Interposition (overlap / partial occlusion)A closer object cuts off the view of part or all of a more distant one.
Relative sizeThe closer of two same-size objects casts a larger image on the retina.
Texture gradientCloser objects have a coarser, more distinct texture; far objects appear more densely packed / smooth.
Aerial / atmospheric perspectiveCloser objects appear sharper than more distant, hazy objects.
Relative height / elevationObjects closest to the horizon appear farthest; the lowest objects in the field of vision seem closest.
Linear perspectiveParallel lines (e.g. edges of sidewalks) seem to converge in the distance.
Relative brightnessThe closer of two identical objects reflects more light to your eyes.
Relative clarityHazy, blurry objects are judged farther away than sharp, clear ones, because light from far objects passes through more air. Example: on a misty day distant hills look farther away than they really are. This is essentially the same cue as aerial perspective; some books (e.g. Myers) use this name.
Motion parallaxWhen you move, near objects shift across your view quickly and in the opposite direction to your movement, far objects shift slowly. Example: riding in a bus, the roadside poles race backwards past the window, distant trees crawl, and the moon seems to keep pace with you. The brain reads "fast-moving" as near and "slow-moving" as far. It needs only one eye, so it is a monocular cue (often called a motion-produced cue).
Motion parallax: depth cue, not illusion. These notes also list motion parallax under apparent motion, but in standard textbooks it is a monocular depth cue: the movement on the retina is real, and its speed tells you distance. If an MCQ asks which depth cue depends on the observer's own movement, the answer is motion parallax.

Binocular cues

  • Binocular convergence — depth cue based on the inward movement of the eyes as they focus on nearby objects.
  • Retinal disparity — the slight difference in the images seen by each eye, used by the brain to perceive depth.
    • Vertical disparity — due to the image falling on a different place in the retina.
    • Horizontal disparity — due to the difference in angles at which the eyes are placed.
  • Horopter — the imaginary line at which an object placed there produces no disparity.
Eleanor Gibson & Richard Walk — the Visual Cliff depth-perception experiment on 3–6 month-old babies; concluded that depth perception is innate.

The visual cliff apparatus (Gibson & Walk, 1960): a table-high platform covered by one sheet of thick, strong glass, with a raised centre board running down the middle where the baby is placed. On the shallow side a red-and-white checkered pattern sits directly under the glass, so it looks like solid floor. On the deep side the same checkered pattern is laid on the floor a few feet below and seen through clear glass, so it looks like a sudden drop (the "cliff"). Touch tells the baby the glass is solid on both sides; only vision says "drop". The mother calls the baby first from the shallow side, then from the deep side. In the original study, of the 27 babies who moved off the centre board, all crawled onto the shallow side at least once, but only 3 crept onto the deep side; many cried or crawled away from the mother when she called from the deep side.

Heads up on ages and "innate". Gibson and Walk's 1960 study tested crawling infants, about 6 to 14 months old (a baby must crawl to be tested), not 3 to 6 months as written above. Because these babies already had months of visual experience, the study shows depth perception is present by crawling age; it does not by itself prove it is inborn. Joseph Campos and colleagues (1970) later placed 2-month-olds (too young to crawl) over the deep side and found their heart rate slowed (interest, not fear); fear of the drop appears around the time infants start crawling.

Perceptual Set

Refers to the influence of values on perception.

Jerome Bruner & Goodman (1947) — coins were perceived as larger than same-size cardboard discs, and more so by poor children than rich children. Because coin size correlates with value, valuable coins "loomed larger" in perception even when the cardboard was the same size.

Top-Down Theories of Perception

  • Constructivist theory (Processing theory) — proponent Richard Gregory (1970). Perception is a hypothesis based on prior knowledge; we actively construct our perception of reality from the environment and stored information. Higher-order thinking is involved.
  • Template Matching theory — a template is an internal construct that, when matched by sensory stimuli, leads to recognition of an object. "Lock and key" idea of pattern recognition; the object must match the template exactly.
  • Prototype theory (Eleanor Rosch, 1975) — an alternative to template matching. A prototype for each object is stored in memory; a pattern is checked against the prototype and recognised if resemblance is found. Not as rigid as templates. Shortcoming: does not account for exceptions.

Bottom-Up Theories of Perception

J. J. Gibson — Direct (Ecological) Perception: the information in the stimulus itself is the important element; learning and cognition are unnecessary because there is enough information in the environment to make an interpretation.
  • Constraints — a barrier in the environment that helps perception.
  • Affordance — a mediator in the environment that helps perception.
  • Light feedback — from the reflection of light we decide whether to move forward or backward.

Feature Detection theory — object recognition is high-order processing preceded by a step in which complex incoming stimuli are identified according to simpler features.

Irving Biederman (1987) — Geon theory (aka recognition by components): the human information-processing system has a limited number of simple geometric shapes (geons, "geometric ions") that combine to form meaningful objects. Identifying an object from its geons = the principle of componential recovery; each geon's unique set of Non-Accidental Properties gives geons the property of discriminability.
Oliver Selfridge — Pandemonium Model ("pandemonium" = a very noisy, chaotic place). Four kinds of demons: image demons (sensation) → feature demons (feature separation) → cognitive demons (compare with memory) → decision demons (perception).

Audition

Properties of sound

  • Audible frequency: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
  • Amplitude — height of the sound wave; determines loudness.
  • Frequency — waves per second; determines pitch.
  • Timbre — quality of sound.
Hermann von Helmholtz — Place theory: we hear different pitches because different sound waves trigger activity at different places along the cochlea's basilar membrane. Best explains high pitches.
Rinne & Rutherford (1880) — Frequency theory: the brain reads pitch by monitoring the frequency of neural impulses travelling up the auditory nerve. Does not explain sound sensing above 1000 Hz. Best explains low pitches.
Key — Volley principle (Ernest Wever and Charles Bray, 1930). Supports frequency theory: neurons fire in alternating rapid succession so that, combined, they achieve a frequency above 1000 waves per second.

Auditory pathway: Pinna → Auditory canal → Tympanic membrane → Hammer (Malleus) → Anvil (Incus) → Stirrup (Stapes) → Cochlea → Auditory nerve → Cochlear nerve → Superior olivary complex → Lateral lemniscus → Inferior colliculus → Medial geniculate body → Auditory cortex.

Common traps. The stapes is the smallest bone in the body. "Auditory nerve" and "cochlear nerve" in the pathway above are the same nerve, not two steps. Damage to the outer or middle ear (eardrum, ossicles) gives conductive hearing loss; damage to hair cells or the cochlear nerve gives sensorineural (nerve) deafness.

Other senses

  • Kinesthetic / Proprioception — sense of body position and movements.
  • Vestibular — sense of head positioning (parietal lobe); semi-circular canals and cerebellum involved.

How the page groups them. The diagram puts proprioception at the top as the umbrella "sense of where the body is", with two branches: kinesthetic (receptors in muscles, tendons and joints telling you the position and movement of your limbs, e.g. touching your nose with your eyes closed) and vestibular (the inner-ear sense of balance and head position: the semicircular canals detect head rotation, the otolith organs detect tilt and straight-line acceleration, e.g. knowing you are tilting in a lift or on a swing). This wide use follows Charles Sherrington, who coined "proprioception" (1906) and counted the inner-ear balance organs among the proprioceptors.

Terminology trap. Many textbooks (e.g. Ciccarelli, Baron) use "proprioception" and "kinesthesis" almost as synonyms and list the vestibular sense separately. So "kinesthetic = body/limb position and movement" and "vestibular = balance and head orientation" is what to remember; whether proprioception includes the vestibular sense depends on the book. Motion sickness is a conflict between vestibular and visual signals.

3 · Theories of Attention

Attention is the concentration of mental activity that lets you take in a limited portion of the vast stream of information available from both your sensory world and your memory. Sequence: sensation → attention → perception.

Types of attention (source)

  • Exogenous — controlled by the stimulus; bottom-up.
  • Endogenous — the brain internally decides what is important; top-down.
Sohlberg & Mateer (1987, 1989) — Hierarchical model of attention: focused → sustained → selective → alternating → divided.
  • Focused attention — ability to focus attention on a stimulus.
  • Sustained attention (vigilance) — ability to attend to a stimulus/activity over a long period. Determinants: sensory factor, temporal certainty, spatial certainty, clarity of stimuli.
  • Selective attention — ability to attend to a specific stimulus in the presence of distracting stimuli.
  • Alternating attention — ability to shift focus between two or more stimuli.
  • Divided attention (multitasking) — ability to attend to different stimuli at the same time.

Why a pyramid, not a list. Sohlberg and Mateer drew the five types as levels: focused attention is the base and divided attention the top, and each level needs the ones below it to be working. You cannot divide attention between two tasks if you cannot first select one stimulus, sustain it, and switch between them. The model is a clinical one, built for rehabilitating people with brain injury: their Attention Process Training (APT) trains the lowest impaired level first and then moves up.

Level (bottom to top)Everyday example
1. FocusedTurning towards your name being called, or responding to a touch.
2. SustainedListening to a one-hour lecture without drifting; a radar operator watching for blips (vigilance).
3. SelectiveReading in a noisy canteen while ignoring the chatter.
4. AlternatingSwitching between reading a recipe and stirring the pot.
5. DividedDriving while holding a conversation.
Order trap. Exams ask for the sequence: focused → sustained → selective → alternating → divided. Divided is the highest (most demanding) level, focused the most basic. Alternating = switching back and forth; divided = both at the same moment.

Signal Detection Theory (SDT)

Wesley W. Peterson & Theodore G. Birdsall — Signal Detection Theory: detection of a signal depends on sensitivity and response bias. Sensitivity (d′) = number of hits − number of false alarms.

The page's second table: sorting outcomes by responder type. Response bias is where a person sets the criterion for saying "yes, I detected it".

Correct outcome they make more ofHitCorrect rejection
Error they make more ofFalse alarmMiss

Example: a radiologist scanning X-rays for a tumour. A liberal radiologist calls anything doubtful "tumour": she catches almost every real tumour (hits) but also flags many healthy patients (false alarms). A conservative one calls "tumour" only when certain: few false alarms and many correct rejections, but she misses some real tumours. Their sensitivity can be identical; only the bias differs.

Traps. (1) Both responders can make all four outcomes; bias only shifts the balance between them. (2) Changing the criterion trades hits against false alarms but does not change sensitivity (d′); d′ changes only if the signal becomes easier or the observer more skilled. (3) "Hits minus false alarms" above is shorthand: formally d′ = z(hit rate) − z(false-alarm rate).

Theories of Selective Attention

Heads up: the diagram above dates Treisman's attenuation model to 1980; the standard date is 1960/1964 (Anne Treisman's attenuation theory). 1980 is the date of a different theory, Feature Integration Theory (Treisman & Gelade, 1980).

Selective attention is tested with a shadowing / dichotic listening task.

Colin Cherry (1953) — early selection model: no overlap in dichotic listening; we do not understand a stimulus before we perceive it.
Donald Broadbent (1958) — Filter model (a bottleneck model): we filter information right after we notice it, at the sensory level.

Tracing Broadbent's diagram. (1) Two messages (stimulus 1 and stimulus 2, e.g. one in each ear) enter a sensory register (sensory buffer) together, where they are held briefly and only their physical features (ear, pitch, loudness, voice) are available. (2) A selection filter picks one channel on the basis of those physical features and blocks the other completely. (3) Only the selected message passes on to short-term memory and is analysed for meaning. The unattended message is lost before its meaning is ever processed, which is why it is called an early selection model. It fits Colin Cherry's shadowing findings: people noticed a change of voice in the unattended ear but not its content or language.

Why Broadbent needed revising. If the filter blocks the other channel completely, nothing meaningful should get through. But Neville Moray (1959) found people often noticed their own name in the unattended ear, and Gray and Wedderburn (1960) found people reported "Dear Aunt Jane" when the words were split between the two ears. Meaning was leaking through, which led to Treisman's attenuation model.
Anne Treisman (1960, 1964) — Attenuation model: explains the cocktail-party phenomenon. Instead of blocking stimuli out, the filter merely weakens the strength of stimuli other than the target.

Tracing Treisman's diagram. (1) Input from both ears enters the sensory buffer. (2) An attenuator analyses physical features and turns the unattended channel down (like a volume knob), not off; the attended channel passes at full strength. (3) Only after the attenuator is meaning extracted: both the strong attended message and the weak unattended one go through a "dictionary" of words, each with its own threshold. Important or primed words (your own name, "fire", a word that fits the story you are shadowing) have low thresholds, so even the weakened signal can trigger them and reach short-term memory. Treisman (1960) showed this when a story being shadowed switched ears: people briefly followed the meaning into the "wrong" ear.

Broadbent vs Treisman. Both put the filter early (before meaning). Broadbent's filter is all-or-none (blocks); Treisman's attenuates (weakens). Hearing your name at a party is explained by Treisman's low-threshold words, not by Broadbent.
Deutsch & Deutsch (1963) — Late Selection model: stimuli are filtered out only after being analysed for both their physical properties and their meaning.

Tracing the late selection diagram. All incoming messages, attended or not, go through (1) physical analysis and then (2) semantic analysis (their meaning is fully worked out). Only at the last stage, (3) awareness and response, is one message (the most important or relevant) selected for conscious report and action; the rest are understood but quickly forgotten. So the bottleneck is at response, not at perception. Criticism: analysing everything for meaning would be very wasteful, and people detect far fewer targets in the unattended ear than in the attended one. Nilli Lavie's perceptual load theory (below) reconciles the two camps: under low load, selection is late; under high load, it is early.

Early vs late in one line. Broadbent: filter before meaning, blocks. Treisman: filter before meaning, weakens. Deutsch & Deutsch: everything gets meaning, selection only at response.
Johnston & Heinz (1978) — Multimode attention theory: attention is a flexible system that selects a stimulus at three stages — (1) sensory representations created, (2) semantic representations created, (3) both sensory and semantic representations enter consciousness. More processing requires more mental effort.
Lavie & Tsal (1994) — Perceptual Load theory: perception is a limited-capacity process that proceeds automatically until that capacity is filled.
Anne Treisman & Garry Gelade (1980) — Feature Integration Theory (FIT): explains how an individual combines pieces of observable information about an object to form a complete perception. Two kinds of visual search: feature search (fast, pre-attentive, primitive features) and conjunction search (slower, effortful; targets defined by a conjunction of features).

Tracing the FIT diagram. FIT has two stages. (1) Pre-attentive stage: the scene is broken into separate feature maps, registered automatically and in parallel: colour maps (one for blue, red, green) and orientation maps (vertical, tilted, horizontal lines). At this stage features are "free-floating"; nothing yet says which colour goes with which line. (2) Focused-attention stage: attention is directed to one spot on the map of locations, like a spotlight. Everything found at that location (say, blue + vertical) is bound together into a temporary object representation ("object file": its properties at time t and place x). (3) This object file is compared with the recognition network, the stored descriptions of objects with their names, and the object is identified.

Illusory conjunctions. FIT predicts that if attention is overloaded or the display is flashed too briefly, features can be wrongly combined (e.g. reporting a red X when a red O and a blue X were shown). Treisman and Schmidt (1982) found exactly this, which is key evidence for FIT.
Eriksen & St James (1986); Michael Posner et al. — Spotlight theory of attention: people can attend to only one region of space at a time. Inspired by William James, attention has a focus (central area extracting high-resolution information where attention is directed), a fringe (extracts information more crudely, extending over a specified area), and a margin (the cut-off between them).

Reading the spotlight illustration. The page shows a circle of German text. Only the word at the very centre ("noch") is sharp: that is the focus, where attention gives full detail. The words around it are blurred but still recognisable as words: that is the fringe, where information is taken in only crudely. The edge of the circle is the margin, the boundary beyond which nothing is processed. Like a torch beam in a dark room, attention lights up one region of space at a time and can be moved around without moving the eyes (covert attention). Michael Posner (1980) showed this with a cueing task: people respond faster to a target that appears where a cue had pointed than elsewhere.

Spotlight vs zoom lens. In the spotlight model the beam has a fixed size. Eriksen and St James (1986) are best known for the zoom-lens version: the beam can be widened or narrowed, and the wider it is, the less detail it gives (a trade-off between area and resolution).

Shortcomings of selective attention

  • Inattentional blindness (Arien Mack and Irvin Rock, 1998) — people fail to see things that are actually there.
  • Change blindness — inability to detect changes in objects or scenes being viewed.

4 · Classical & Operant Conditioning

Key — definition. Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour as a result of experience.

Two basic kinds of learning

  • Non-associative learning (short term) — learning about a single stimulus:
    • Habituation — a decreased behavioural response to an innocuous stimulus.
    • Sensitization — an increase in a behavioural response to an intense stimulus.
  • Associative learning (long term) — learning relationships among events: classical conditioning and instrumental (operant) conditioning.

Classical Conditioning

Ivan Pavlov (1897) — first researched classical conditioning: a previously neutral stimulus becomes associated with another stimulus through repeated pairing.

Based on three broad principles:

  • Contiguity — nearness in space and time.
  • Contingency — prediction power.
  • Blocking — a CS blocks conditioning with another neutral stimulus.

Contiguity and Contingency

Robert Rescorla — conditioning involves cognitive components. The CS must be a reliable predictor of the US (contingency); mere temporal contiguity is not enough.
  • Positive contingency — CS predicts the presence of the UCS → excitatory conditioning.
  • Zero contingency — no pattern, so no learning takes place.
  • Negative contingency — CS predicts the absence of the UCS → inhibitory conditioning.

Classical conditioning & biological preparedness

Animals and humans are biologically prepared to make certain connections more easily (e.g. learned taste aversions can be learnt in a single trial).

John Garcia & Robert Koelling (1966) — rats more readily learned certain associations: food more likely to become CS for nausea; noise more likely to become CS for shock.

The "bright, noisy, tasty water" experiment. Rats drank water that was sweet (flavoured) and, at the same time, every lick set off a click and a flash of light. So each drink gave a taste cue and an audio-visual cue together. Then one group was made nauseous (by radiation or a toxin) and another group got pain (electric shock through the floor). Later each cue was tested alone:

CuePaired with nauseaPaired with pain (shock)
Sweet tasteAcquires aversion (rats avoid the sweet water)Does not
"Click" (+ light)Does notLearns defence (rats avoid the noisy water)

Within each group, the taste and the click were always paired with the consequence together, equally often, so pure contiguity predicts equal learning for both cues. Instead, taste linked only with illness, and sound/light only with external pain. Evolution has "prepared" rats to blame sickness on what they ate and pain on what they saw or heard. This challenged the behaviourist idea of equipotentiality (any CS can be linked with any US equally well) and supports Martin Seligman's concept of preparedness (1970).

Two Garcia facts examiners love. (1) Taste aversion can form in one trial. (2) It works even with a delay of hours between the taste and the sickness, breaking the usual rule that the CS and US must be close in time.
Key — classical conditioning at a glance. Totally based on reflexes; response is elicited by the stimulus (also called respondent conditioning); passive learning; reflexes can be conditioned or unconditioned; magnitude of response matters; it is an S–S type of conditioning. The amygdala (lateral nucleus) organizes emotional responses provoked by aversive stimuli.

Operant Conditioning

Edward Thorndike — learning theory leading to operant conditioning (B. F. Skinner); used a cat in a puzzle box (trial-and-error learning). His theory is called associationism / connectionism. Three laws: law of readiness, law of practice/exercise, and the law of effect (behaviour followed by pleasant consequences is repeated; behaviour followed by unpleasant consequences is stopped).

Thorndike's puzzle box (1898, Animal Intelligence). A hungry cat was shut inside a wooden slatted cage with food (fish) placed outside, in view. The way out was a simple mechanism: in the page's drawing, pulling a string (loop) that opens the trap door; other boxes used a lever or latch. At first the cat scratched, bit and clawed at random; by accident it pulled the string, the door opened and it reached the food. Over repeated trials the useless actions dropped out and the time to escape fell gradually, not suddenly. Thorndike took this smooth learning curve as evidence of trial-and-error learning strengthened by its consequences (the law of effect), not of insight.

B. F. Skinner (1937) — coined the term operant conditioning. The Skinner box delivers food via a lever/disk; the food is a reinforcer and giving it is reinforcement. Operant/instrumental conditioning = learning the relationship between responses and outcomes (an S–R association).

Puzzle box vs Skinner box. Thorndike's puzzle box used discrete trials (the cat escapes, the trial ends, the cat is put back) and measured time to escape. Skinner's box is a free-operant setup: the animal stays inside and can respond at any time, and the measure is rate of responding, drawn automatically on a cumulative recorder.

Principles of operant conditioning

  • Acquisition — the rat learns to press the bar for food.
  • Extinction — the rat unlearns the bar–food connection and stops pressing.
  • Spontaneous recovery — after extinction and rest, the rat presses the bar again.
  • Generalization — the rat presses other objects that look like the bar.
  • Discrimination — the rat learns to press only a particular bar.

Shaping and Chaining

  • Shaping — reinforces the steps used to reach the desired behaviour.
  • Chaining — animals are taught to perform a number of responses successively to get a reward.

Learning and maintaining fear (two-factor)

  1. Stage 1 — classical conditioning / escape learning: through repeated pairing of the warning light (CS) and shock (UCS), the animal learns the light predicts the shock and shows a conditioned fear response (CR) to the light alone.
  2. Stage 2 — instrumental conditioning / avoidance learning: successful reduction of the aversive stimulus reinforces and strengthens the avoidance behaviour. It gives temporary relief, but the long-run consequences of avoidance are detrimental.

What the page adds to stage 1: instrumental escape learning. Stage 1 is not only classical. While the shock is actually on, the animal also learns an escape response: jumping to the safe side ends the shock, and the ending of the shock negatively reinforces the jump. So stage 1 = light → fear (classical) plus shock → jump to escape (instrumental). In stage 2 the animal starts jumping as soon as the light comes on, before any shock: this is avoidance, reinforced by the drop in fear. This is O. Hobart Mowrer's two-factor theory (1947): fear is acquired by classical conditioning and kept going by operant avoidance.

Escape vs avoidance. Escape ends an aversive stimulus that is already present (jump while shocked). Avoidance prevents it from arriving (jump at the warning light). Because the avoider never stays to learn that the light is no longer followed by shock, the fear never extinguishes. That is why phobias persist, and why treatment uses exposure with response prevention.

5 · Cognitive & Social Learning

Learned Helplessness

Martin Seligman — cognitive approach to learning: control over the outcome of behaviour is as important for learning as contiguity and contingency.

Experiment — Maier & Seligman (1976):

  • Stage 1: some dogs learn that whether they receive a shock depends on their own behaviour; other dogs learn they have no control over the shock.
  • Stage 2: both groups placed in a shuttle box; a tone signals an imminent shock, and the dog must jump the barrier to avoid it.
  • Found: control dogs learn the response rapidly; yoked dogs do not. They had learned that shocks were uncontrollable, and this non-control made avoidance learning impossible → learned helplessness.

The shuttle box (as drawn on the page). A box with two compartments, each with a metal grid floor, separated by a low barrier the dog can easily jump. A warning signal comes first: in the drawing a light dims (the notes' text says a tone; either can be used). A few seconds later the grid floor on the dog's side is electrified, while the other side stays safe. If the dog jumps the barrier during the warning it avoids the shock completely; if it jumps after the shock starts it escapes it. Dogs given controllable shock in stage 1 (or no shock) quickly learn to jump. Dogs given inescapable (yoked) shock mostly lie down and whine, taking the shock even though escape is one easy jump away.

Dates and yoking. The original dog studies were by Overmier & Seligman (1967) and Seligman & Maier (1967); Maier & Seligman (1976) is their major review. "Yoked" means the helpless dog received exactly the same shocks as its partner, but its own responses had no effect; so the only difference between the groups is control, not the amount of shock. Seligman linked this to depression.
Robert Woodworth — S–O–R approach: to predict the response we must know not only the stimulus but also the organism stimulated; human motivations and conscious decisions play an important part in behaviour.
Wolfgang Köhler (1925) — Insight learning, studied on chimpanzees: insight occurs when one suddenly realises how to solve a problem. Learning happens in a sudden way due to insight, not gradual strengthening of the S–R connection.

Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura — Social Learning Theory (aka observational learning): learning is achieved by mentally rehearsing and then imitating the observed actions of models. An S–O–R theory.

Process of learning (sequence): attention → retention → motor reproduction → motivation.

Bobo doll experiment — conditions: model hits doll and is reinforced (vicarious reinforcement), model hits doll and is not reinforced, model hits doll and is punished (vicarious punishment).

Key. Learning occurs in all three conditions irrespective of reinforcement; however, reproduction of behaviour was dependent on reinforcement.

Tolman's Purposive Behaviourism

Edward Tolman — Purposive behaviourism (aka latent learning, expectancy, place learning, sign-stimulus theory). Experiment: Tolman & Honzik (1930).
  • Group A — reinforced on each trial; Group B — never reinforced; Group C — not reinforced on days 1 to 10, then reinforced (food in the goal box) from day 11 onwards.
  • Found: Group C had actually learnt the maze without reinforcement (latent learning) but showed it once reinforcement appeared. The rats developed a cognitive map — a mental representation of the maze.

Reading the graph (one trial per day for 17 days; y-axis = average errors, i.e. wrong turns into blind alleys). The rewarded group's errors fall steadily from day 1. The no-reward group improves only slightly (getting out of the maze was a small reward in itself). The delayed-reward group runs level with the no-reward group for 10 days; then, right after it first finds food on day 11, its errors drop sharply, catching up with the rewarded group almost at once and then making even slightly fewer errors over days 11 to 17. So the learning had already happened during the unrewarded days; the food only gave the rats a reason to show it.

Learning vs performance. Tolman's point is that reinforcement affects performance, not learning itself. A drop that sudden cannot be explained by gradual S–R strengthening from just one or two rewards. Group C was rewarded on every day from 11 on, not "only on day 11".

Components of learning: the significate (desired outcome/goal), the sign (stimulus prompting action), and means–end relations (the learner's internal thought process).

Field Theory

Kurt Lewin (1930) — Field Theory, based on Gestalt psychology, an S–O–R approach: a person's psychological state affects their social field and vice versa. "Field" = the totality of a person's experiences, needs and topography; field forces (intrinsic or extrinsic) drive behaviour.

Reading the life space diagram. Lewin summed up his theory as B = f(P, E): behaviour is a function of the person and their psychological environment. The life space (outer boundary) is everything that matters to the person at that moment, as they see it. Inside it:

  • Person: the circle on the left.
  • Goal marked +: it has positive valence (attraction). A feared object would have negative valence (−).
  • Driving force (vector): an arrow pushing the person towards the goal. A vector has direction and strength.
  • Restraining force (vector): an arrow pointing back, holding the person away from the goal.
  • Barrier: the hatched wall between person and goal, an obstacle that blocks movement.

Example: a student's goal is a seat in an M.Phil Clinical Psychology course (+ valence). Driving forces: interest in clinical work, family support. Restraining forces: fear of failure, the cost of another year. Barrier: the entrance exam and its limited seats. Behaviour (studying, applying) depends on the balance of these forces; Lewin's later force-field analysis says change happens by strengthening driving forces or weakening restraining ones.

6 · Theories of Memory

Key — definition. Memory is our capacity to register, store and recover information over time — the persistence of learning over time. It is a complex mental system involving multiple stages of information processing.

Early models

In the mid-1960s researchers proposed two structures of memory:

  • Primary memory — proposed by William James; holds temporary information currently in use.
  • Secondary memory — proposed by Waugh & Norman (1965); holds information permanently or for a very long time.

Sperling & iconic memory

George Sperling — iconic memory: a type of visual memory lasting only a fraction of a second. Sensory memory capacity is much larger than believed. In Sperling's test, partial report was more successful than whole report; partial report showed about 9 of 12 letters were briefly available in iconic memory, roughly twice what whole report suggests.

Sperling's experiment (1960), step by step (the page's figure):

Why partial report proves a bigger store. The person does not know which row will be cued until the letters are gone. If they can report 3 of 4 from any row cued, then about 75 percent of all 12 letters (about 9) must have been available at that instant. Whole report underestimates this because the image fades while the person is still naming the first few letters. When the tone was delayed, the advantage shrank, and by about 1 second (1000 ms) it had gone: the icon lasts well under a second.

Numbers to remember. Iconic memory: large capacity, about 250 ms to 1 s. Echoic memory (auditory sensory memory) lasts longer, about 2 to 4 s. Exams often ask "partial report technique" = Sperling; "whole report underestimates sensory memory".

The arrows in the diagram.

  • Maintenance rehearsal is drawn as a small loop on STM itself: repeating information over and over (like saying a phone number until you dial it) keeps it alive in STM but does little to move it into LTM. Stop repeating and it is lost in about 18 to 30 seconds.
  • Elaborative rehearsal is the arrow from STM down to LTM: linking the new item to meaning and to what you already know (e.g. connecting a new term to an example) transfers it to LTM.

Standard answer: forgetting can happen at every stage, but in different ways. Sensory memory: the trace decays within a second or so. STM: information is lost quickly by displacement and decay unless rehearsed, so STM is the store described as "fragile". LTM: storage is relatively permanent; forgetting there is mainly retrieval failure and interference, not loss.
George Miller (1956) — the magic number 7 ± 2: the average number of items held in short-term memory. Emphasized recoding / chunking of information.

Neural basis

  • Long-Term Potentiation (LTP; Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo, 1973) — persistent strengthening of a synaptic connection lasting minutes to hours; based on the Hebbian principle ("neurons that fire together, wire together"). NMDA glutamate receptors are necessary for memory formation; AMPA receptors — dendrites double their contact surface by splitting in two; terminal buttons release more neurotransmitter after potentiation; nitric oxide acts as a catalyst. In the lab, a high-frequency electric charge is applied to the pre-synaptic neuron.
  • Reading the LTP drawing (normal vs potentiated synapse). Left, a normal synapse: the presynaptic terminal releases a few vesicles of neurotransmitter (glutamate) across the synaptic cleft (only about 20 nm wide) onto receptors on a dendritic spine, letting in a little Na+ and Ca2+. Right, after high-frequency stimulation, the same synapse is "potentiated": (1) the presynaptic terminal releases more neurotransmitter; (2) extra receptors (AMPA receptors) are inserted into the spine's membrane; (3) so more Na+ and Ca2+ flow in and the postsynaptic response to the same input is bigger. That bigger response is the "stronger synapse".
    • Sequence to remember: strong, repeated input → glutamate → AMPA receptors depolarise the spine → this removes the magnesium (Mg2+) block from NMDA receptors → Ca2+ floods in → enzymes add more AMPA receptors (postsynaptic change), and a retrograde messenger such as nitric oxide travels back to make the terminal release more transmitter (presynaptic change). With lasting LTP the spine can also grow or split, adding contacts.
    • Trap: the NMDA receptor is the "coincidence detector": it opens only when glutamate is bound and the postsynaptic cell is already depolarised, which is Hebb's rule in molecular form. Bliss and Lømo first showed LTP in the rabbit hippocampus. In a brain slice it lasts hours; in a living animal it can last days or weeks.
  • Long-Term Depression (LTD) — long-term weakening of a synaptic connection; prunes unused synapses, trimming unimportant connections and leaving only the salient connections strengthened by LTP.

Theories of forgetting

  • Decay theory — forgetting is an automatic process; the engram for the information disappears.
  • Interference theory — supported by the serial-position curve.

Serial Position Curve

Represents the probability of recall of a word given its serial position in a list.

  • Primacy effect — superior recall of words at/near the beginning of a list (into LTM). Nothing came before them, so they suffer no proactive interference; they are exposed only to retroactive interference from the later words. They also get the most rehearsal.
  • Recency effect — superior recall of words at/near the end of a list (still in STM). Nothing comes after them, so they suffer no retroactive interference; they are exposed only to proactive interference from the earlier words.
  • Middle of the list: the low point of the curve. These words get both proactive interference (from words before) and retroactive interference (from words after), and little rehearsal, so they are recalled worst.

How to read the curve's labels. The diagram writes "retroactive interference" at the primacy end, "proactive interference" at the recency end, and "retroactive & proactive" at the dip. Each label names the only kind of interference that part of the list is exposed to, not the cause of the effect. That matches the standard account: proactive interference (earlier learning) hurts recall of later items; retroactive interference (later learning) hurts recall of earlier items.

How each end is wiped out. Recency disappears if recall is delayed by about 30 seconds of a distracting task (e.g. counting backwards), because the last items drop out of STM; primacy is unaffected (Murray Glanzer and Anita Cunitz, 1966). Primacy shrinks if words are presented faster, because there is less time to rehearse them into LTM. This double dissociation is classic evidence for separate STM and LTM stores.
John Bergström (1892) — Interference theory of forgetting.
Proactive vs retroactive interference. Proactive — old information learned before disrupts recall of the new item (forward-acting). Retroactive — new information disrupts retrieval of previously learned information (backward-acting). Factors: learning new information unlearns previous; response competition between similar materials.

Motivated forgetting

Sigmund Freud — motivated forgetting via repression: we unconsciously prevent traumatic events from entering awareness to avoid anxiety or blows to self-concept. The repressed material is not lost, but hidden from consciousness.

Encoding specificity

Endel Tulving — encoding specificity. Two related types:
  • State-dependent memory — information is better retrieved in similar emotional and physical states to those at encoding.
  • Context-dependent memory (Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley, 1975) — information is easier to retrieve in the place/environment where it was originally learnt.

The Zeigarnik effect

Bluma Zeigarnik — the Zeigarnik effect: unfinished, incomplete and unpleasant tasks are not forgotten as easily as completed ones.

Blocking vs transience. In blocking the memory is still there (it often pops up later); in transience it has actually faded. Misattribution is a source-monitoring error (where did I learn this?); suggestibility is memory being changed by outside information.

Distorted / manufactured memories

Three common factors that contribute to faulty reconstructions: source misattributions, exposure to misinformation, and effects of imagination.

Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues (1978) — the misinformation effect: participants watched a film of a traffic accident. Group 1 was asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" into each other (implying a severe accident); Group 2 heard "hit" (milder). Those who heard "smash" remembered higher speeds and were more likely to (falsely) report broken glass — there was none in the film.
Elizabeth Loftus, James Coan & Jacqueline Pickrell — the Lost in the Mall study: showed how easy it is to implant a fictitious memory of having been lost in a shopping mall as a child.

Mnemonic devices

Helpful for consolidation:

  • Categorical clustering — organizing a list into a set of categories.
  • Interactive images — creating images that link the isolated words in a list.
  • Pegword system — associating each new word with a word on a previously memorized list via an interactive image.
  • Method of loci — visualizing a walk around a familiar area with distinctive landmarks, linking landmarks to items.
  • Acronym — a word/expression whose letters each stand for another word or concept.
  • Acrostic — forming a sentence (rather than a single word) to help remember new words.
  • Keyword system — an interactive image linking the sound and meaning of a foreign word with a familiar word.

8 · Theories of Intelligence

Key — definition. Wechsler (1944): intelligence is the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with his environment.

The first theory of intelligence was Alfred Binet's unifactor theory; the second was Charles Spearman's two-factor theory.

Spearman's Two-Factor Theory

Charles Spearman (1904) — Two-factor theory; laid the groundwork of factor analysis. Intelligence has a general 'g' factor (an overriding intelligence, almost exclusively inherited) and specific 's' factors (competence in specific abilities such as mathematics, language, music).

Spearman used the method of tetrad difference: the tetrad difference should be zero but was not, due to a common g factor. Tetrad equation: r12 × r34 = r23 × r14.

Cattell — Fluid & Crystallized Intelligence

Raymond Cattell — general intelligence = fluid (Gf) + crystallized (Gc) intelligence (aka the investment theory); used factor analysis and second-order factors.

DevelopmentGrows rapidly in adolescence to the 20s, then declines (does not disappear).Keeps increasing until death; created by the investment of fluid intelligence into the environment.

John Horn — expanded the Gf–Gc model with additional abilities: visual processing (Gv), short-term acquisition & retrieval (SAR / Gsm), long-term storage & retrieval (TSR / Glr), processing speed (Gs), auditory processing (Ga), reaction/decision speed (Gt), quantitative ability (Gq), and broad reading–writing (Grw).

Jensen's view of intelligence

Arthur Jensen (1969) — divides intelligence into two sets of abilities (method of study: regression).
  • Level 1 abilities — memory functions and simple associative learning; equally distributed among the races.
  • Level 2 abilities — abstract reasoning and conceptual thought; White and Asian students showed advantages on Level 2 tests.

Carroll's Three-Stratum Theory (1993)

A hierarchy: Stratum 3 = general intelligence (g); Stratum 2 = broad abilities; Stratum 1 = ~70 narrow abilities. Broad abilities include: Fluid intelligence (Gf), Crystallized intelligence (Gc), General Memory and Learning (Gy), Broad visual perception (Gv), Broad auditory perception (Gu), Broad retrieval ability (Gr), Broad cognitive speediness (Gs), and Processing speed / RT decision speed (Gt).

Vernon's Hierarchical Theory

Philip Vernon — hierarchical theory.
  • Level 1: general ability (g).
  • Level 2 (major group): verbal-educational (v:ed) and spatial-mechanical (k:m).
  • Level 3 (minor group): creative abilities, verbal fluency, numerical factors, spatial factors, psychomotor factors, mechanical-information factors.
  • Level 4: specific abilities (s).

Traps. Numerical ability sits under v:ed (it is school-taught), not k:m. Vernon's model is a bridge between Spearman (keeps g at the top) and Thurstone (keeps group factors below it), and is a forerunner of Carroll's three-stratum model.

Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities (1938)

L. L. Thurstone — Primary Mental Abilities (group-factor theory); each primary ability is distinct, rejected the g factor.

The seven factors (in order): Verbal comprehension, Verbal fluency, Numerical ability, Memory, Perceptual speed, Inductive reasoning, Spatial visualization.

Thorndike's theory of intelligence (1920)

Edward Thorndike — classified intelligence into concrete / mechanical, abstract, and social intelligence. Does not believe in a g factor (Charles Spearman, 1904); no factor analysis.

Heritability & the Flynn effect

Francis Galton — began the study of heritability of intelligence, inspired by Darwin's On the Origin of Species. In Hereditary Genius he argued children inherit their intelligence from their parents.

Flynn effect — the observed rise in average IQ scores over generations.

9 · Intelligence & Creativity

Creativity is a group of mental abilities covered by divergent thinking (J. P. Guilford). Components include fluency (swift current of ideas and tendency to alter direction), flexibility (readiness to change), originality (newness), and elaboration (detail).

Graham Wallas' theory of creativity (1926)

Heads up: the flow above shows "Incubation" twice; the fourth box should read Illumination. The correct order is orientation (only in some five-step versions) → preparation → incubation → illumination → verification.
Graham Wallas (1926) — the first theory of creativity (stage model of the creative process, in his book The Art of Thought). Wallas's own model has four stages; these notes add orientation at the start, giving five steps.
  1. Orientation (only in the five-step version): becoming aware of and defining the problem.
  2. Preparation: conscious hard work, gathering information and trying solutions.
  3. Incubation — unconscious: you stop working on the problem and the mind keeps working on it in the background.
  4. Illumination — maximum creative thinking (insight): the "aha" moment when the idea suddenly appears.
  5. Verification — evaluation and revision: testing and refining the idea.

Example: a researcher reads everything on a problem for weeks (preparation), gives up and goes on holiday (incubation), suddenly sees the answer while walking on the beach (illumination), then returns to test it (verification).

What exams key. The standard answer is Wallas's four stages: preparation → incubation → illumination → verification. Wallas also described intimation (the feeling that a solution is about to arrive) just before illumination, which is why some books count five stages. Orientation is never placed after incubation.

10 · Thinking & Concepts

Key — definition. Thinking involves the mental manipulation and processing of information — generating and/or evaluating ideas, arguments, beliefs and statements. Its two major components are mental images and concepts.

Concepts

  • Natural concept — a mental representation of an object/event drawn from personal experience, used to categorize other objects/events.
  • Artificial concept — a concept defined by a specific set of characteristics.
  • Simple concept — has a single common feature.
  • Complex concept:
    • Conjunctive — simultaneous presence of two or more common characteristics.
    • Disjunctive — presence of one common characteristic or another.

Learning concepts (concept formation)

Jerome Bruner, Goodnow & Austin (1956) — the hypothesis-testing model of concept formation. Strategies participants may use:
StrategyDescription
Simultaneous scanningStart with all possible hypotheses and eliminate the untenable ones.
Successive scanningBegin with a single hypothesis, keep it if successful; if not, change it based on all previous experience.
Conservative focusingFormulate a hypothesis, pick a positive instance as focus, then reformulate changing only one feature at a time, noting each outcome.
Focus gamblingChange more than one feature at a time, gambling to determine the concept more quickly.

Two families of strategy (as the page groups them):

FamilyStrategiesHow it works
ScanningSimultaneous scanning, successive scanningYou work with hypotheses (guesses about the whole concept) and test them: all at once (simultaneous) or one at a time (successive).
FocusingConservative focusing, focus gamblingYou start from one positive instance (an example that fits the concept, the "focus") and change its features to see which ones matter: one at a time (conservative) or several at once (gambling).

Example: in Bruner, Goodnow and Austin's card task, each card varies in shape, colour, number of figures and number of borders, and the hidden concept is, say, "red circles". Told that a card with two red circles and one border is an example, a conservative focuser next picks a card that differs only in colour (two green circles, one border). If it is not an example, colour matters; and so on, one feature at a time.

Which is best? Simultaneous scanning is the most demanding on memory (you must hold every possible hypothesis). Conservative focusing is the safest and most efficient. Focus gambling can reach the answer faster but risks having to start over. Successive scanning is the easiest on memory but slow.

How concepts are organized

  • A schema is a mental construct consisting of a cluster of related concepts.
  • Concepts are organized in semantic networks — a complex web of associations.

Reasoning

  • Inductive reasoning — from specific observations to a general conclusion.
  • Deductive reasoning — from general premises to a specific conclusion.

Wertheimer's types of thinking

Max Wertheimer — productive vs reproductive thinking.
  • Productive thinking — thinking with insights that go beyond existing associations.
  • Reproductive thinking — thinking based on existing associations / what is already known.

Types of problem solving (Wertheimer): Type A — productive thinking; Type B — partially productive and partially unproductive; Type Y — blind trial and error.

Other modes of thinking

  • Autistic thinking — mental activity directed inward and subjective; inner thoughts and individual reality; narcissistic, egocentric processes such as fantasizing and daydreaming with little relation to reality.
  • Dereistic thinking (given by Sigmund Freud) — thinking not bound by logic or reality, directed away from reality, not following ordinary rules of logic; often seen in children and creative individuals; excessive daydreaming leading to negative consequences.

11 · Problem Solving, Reasoning & Decision-Making

The page names three methods of problem solving: trial and error, algorithms and heuristics.

  • Trial and error — try a number of different solutions until the correct one is reached.
  • Algorithm — sequences of operations that, in theory, guarantee a solution.

Halo effect (Edward Thorndike, 1920) — being influenced by a person's initial impression or external features (favourable). Horn effect — allowing an unfavourable first impression to influence overall judgment.

Why the page puts halo and horn under anchoring. On the page these two effects are sub-points of the anchoring and adjustment heuristic. The logic: the first impression works as an anchor, and later judgments are adjusted from it, but not enough. Example: an interview candidate who is well dressed and confident (anchor) is then rated as more competent and honest too (halo); one who arrives late and untidy is rated lower on everything else (horn), even on qualities the first impression says nothing about.

Classification trap. In standard textbooks the halo effect (Edward Thorndike, 1920, from officers rating their soldiers) is treated as a separate bias in person perception and rating, not as a "type" of the anchoring heuristic; the anchor link is this source's way of explaining it. Anchoring proper (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974) is usually shown with number estimates: after a rigged wheel of fortune stopped at 10 or at 65, people estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations at about 25 and 45 respectively.

All the heuristics in the table above are the page's informal heuristics: fast judgment shortcuts that usually work but often lead to biases, which is why the page marks them as obstacles to problem solving.

Obstacles to problem solving

  • Fallacies — a failure in reasoning that renders an argument invalid.
  • Fixed mindset — belief that you have a fixed amount of intelligence/skill and no effort can improve performance.
  • Mental set — tendency to keep trying a previously used solution even when an easier method exists; represents overactive top-down processing.
  • Functional fixedness (Karl Duncker, 1945) — the uses we assign to an object tend to remain fixed/stable.

Key. Sensitive period of language development: 0 years to 5–7 years.

Components of language

  • Morphology — units of meaning involved in word formation.
  • Syntax — rules for combining words into phrases/sentences.
  • Semantics — meaning of words and sentences.
  • Pragmatics — appropriate use of language in different contexts.

Sapir–Whorf Hypothesis (1956) — linguistic relativity: speakers of different languages have differing cognitive systems that influence how they think about the world; we cannot think about things not experienced in our culture.

Vygotsky's theory of language

Lev Vygotsky — stages of language development.
  1. Primitive stage — infant experimenting with sound production; cooing / "ga-ga".
  2. Naive stage — baby speaks words without grasping their purpose and meaning.
  3. External (egocentric) stage — child uses objects to signify meaning and words; rhyming is used in this phase.
  4. Ingrowth stage — inner speech shortens (called predication).

Nativist theory — Chomsky

Noam Chomsky — nativist theory: children have an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD). Language is modular — a set of specific linguistic abilities that do not follow the principles of other cognitive processes.
  • Deep structure — the underlying syntactic structure linking phrase structures through transformation rules.
  • Surface structure — the phrase structures resulting from applying transformational rules.
  • Transformational grammar — two sentences may have very different surface structures but similar deep structures.
  • Ambiguous sentences — two sentences may have identical surface structures but very different deep structures.

Bruner — LASS

Jerome Bruner — Language Acquisition Support System (LASS): parents, teachers and elders act as a support system that helps in the acquisition of language.
