1 Variables and scales of measurement

Statistics is a set of tools for one job: getting from what you observed in a handful of people to a defensible statement about people in general. Everything in this book is either describing what you have (descriptive statistics) or generalising beyond what you have (inferential statistics). Keep that split in your head; it is the first line of a hundred exam answers.

The rule that generates half the notation questions: Greek letters are for populations, Roman letters are for samples. μ is the population mean, M (or X̄) is the sample mean. σ is the population SD, s is the sample SD. ρ (rho) is the population correlation, r is the sample correlation. β is the population regression weight, b is the sample one.

Types of variable

  • Independent variable (IV): the one the experimenter manipulates or selects. The presumed cause. Also called the predictor (in regression) or the factor (in ANOVA). Its values are called levels.
  • Dependent variable (DV): the one you measure. The presumed effect. Also called the criterion or the outcome.
  • Extraneous variable: anything else that could affect the DV. Controlled by randomisation, matching, holding constant, or statistically (ANCOVA).
  • Confounding variable: an extraneous variable that actually varies systematically with the IV, so its effect is inseparable from the IV's. A confound is an extraneous variable that got away.
  • Mediator: the variable through which the IV acts (X → M → Y). Explains how or why. Tested by Baron and Kenny's (1986) steps or by Sobel / bootstrapped indirect effects.
  • Moderator: a variable that changes the strength or direction of the X–Y relation. Explains when or for whom. Statistically, a moderator is an interaction.
  • Discrete variable: takes only separate whole values (number of children, number of hospital admissions).
  • Continuous variable: can take any value in a range; between any two values another is possible (reaction time, IQ). Measured to a limit of precision, with real limits of ±half the smallest unit: a reported weight of 62 kg means 61.5 to 62.5.
Trap: mediator and moderator are the single most confused pair in research-methods sections. Mediator = the mechanism, sits between IV and DV in the causal chain. Moderator = the condition, sits outside and changes the size of the effect. "Stress causes depression through rumination" is mediation. "Stress causes depression more strongly in people with low social support" is moderation.

The two traps that are asked every year. (1) Temperature in Celsius is interval, not ratio, because 0°C is the freezing point of water, not the absence of heat; 40°C is not "twice as hot" as 20°C. Temperature in kelvin is ratio. (2) IQ is interval, not ratio. There is no IQ of zero meaning "no intelligence", so a person with IQ 140 is not twice as intelligent as one with 70. Strictly, IQ is arguably only ordinal, but for exam purposes it is treated as interval.
Why the scale decides everything downstream. The scale of your DV is the first branch of the which-test decision tree. Nominal DV means frequencies means chi-square. Ordinal DV means ranks means the non-parametric family. Interval or ratio DV means the mean and the SD are meaningful, which means the parametric family (t, ANOVA, Pearson, regression) becomes available. If you can name the scale you have already narrowed the answer to about four tests.

Two more scales worth knowing

  • Absolute scale: a counting scale where the unit is one and cannot be changed (number of children). Some texts treat it as a fifth, strongest scale.
  • Interval-like ordinal (quasi-interval): Likert items are ordinal per item, but a summed Likert scale of many items is conventionally treated as interval. Say this explicitly if asked; examiners like the nuance.

2 Frequency distributions and graphs

Raw data is unreadable. The first act of every analysis is to impose order on it, and the tool for that is the frequency distribution.

2.1 Building a grouped frequency distribution

  1. Find the range: R = highest score − lowest score (some texts add 1 for the "inclusive range").
  2. Choose the number of class intervals: conventionally 10 to 20. Too few hides the shape, too many defeats the purpose.
  3. Find the class interval width (i): i = R / (number of intervals), rounded to a convenient number (2, 3, 5, 10).
  4. Set the intervals so they are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, and start the lowest interval at a multiple of i.
  5. Tally and count.

3 Central tendency

A measure of central tendency is a single value that best represents the whole distribution: the point around which the scores cluster. There are three you must know cold, and two more worth naming.

3.1 The mean (arithmetic average)

Definition: the sum of all scores divided by the number of scores. The balance point of the distribution.

Formulas. Ungrouped sample mean: M = ΣX / n. Population mean: μ = ΣX / N. Grouped data: M = ΣfXᵐ / N, where Xᵐ is the midpoint of each interval and f its frequency. Assumed-mean (short) method: M = A + (Σfd / N) × i, where A is any assumed mean, d = (Xᵐ − A)/i, and i is the class interval.

Worked example

Scores: 4, 7, 7, 9, 13. ΣX = 40, n = 5, so M = 40 / 5 = 8.

The six mathematical properties of the mean (each one is an exam item)

  1. The sum of deviations about the mean is exactly zero: Σ(X − M) = 0. This is what "balance point" means, and it is why you must square deviations to get a usable measure of spread.
  2. The sum of squared deviations about the mean is a minimum: Σ(X − M)² is smaller than Σ(X − any other value)². This is the least squares property, and it is the reason regression, ANOVA and the variance are all built on the mean.
  3. Adding a constant c to every score adds c to the mean. Multiplying every score by c multiplies the mean by c.
  4. The mean is affected by every single score, so one extreme score moves it. It is not resistant.
  5. The mean of a combined group is the weighted mean: Mᵗ = (n₁M₁ + n₂M₂) / (n₁ + n₂).
  6. The mean is the most reliable measure across repeated samples, that is, it has the smallest standard error, which is why inferential statistics is built on it.
Trap: the mean cannot be computed from an open-ended distribution ("60 and above"), because the top interval has no midpoint. In that situation you must use the median.

3.2 The median

Definition: the value that divides the ordered distribution into two equal halves. The 50th percentile, the 2nd quartile, the 5th decile.

Formulas. Ungrouped, n odd: the ((n+1)/2)th score. Ungrouped, n even: the mean of the (n/2)th and (n/2 + 1)th scores. Grouped: Md = L + [(N/2 − cf) / f] × i, where L is the exact lower limit of the median class, cf the cumulative frequency below it, f the frequency of the median class and i the interval width.

Worked example

Scores 4, 7, 7, 9, 13 (n = 5, odd): the (5+1)/2 = 3rd score, so Md = 7. Add a sixth score of 40: ordered 4, 7, 7, 9, 13, 40; median = (7 + 9)/2 = 8, while the mean jumps from 8 to 13.3. That single comparison is the whole argument for the median.

Properties

  • The sum of absolute deviations from the median is a minimum: Σ|X − Md| is smaller than about any other point. (The mean minimises squared deviations; the median minimises absolute ones.)
  • It is a positional average, so it is unaffected by extreme scores: it is robust or resistant.
  • It can be computed for open-ended distributions and for ordinal data.
  • It cannot be manipulated algebraically the way the mean can, so it is not used in inferential statistics.

3.3 The mode

Definition: the most frequently occurring score. The point of maximum density.

Grouped formula (crude vs. exact). Crude mode = midpoint of the interval with the highest frequency. Exact (King's) formula: Mo = L + [(f₁ − f₀) / (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] × i. Empirical relation for a moderately skewed distribution (Karl Pearson): Mo = 3Md − 2M, equivalently M − Mo = 3(M − Md).
  • A distribution can have no mode, one mode (unimodal), two (bimodal), or many (multimodal). A bimodal distribution usually means two different populations have been pooled, and that is a substantive finding, not a nuisance.
  • It is the only measure of central tendency usable with nominal data.
  • It is the least stable across samples and the least used inferentially.
  • A rectangular or uniform distribution has no mode at all.

3.4 Choosing between them

Classic applied example: income. Income distributions are strongly positively skewed, so the mean income of a country is dragged up by a few very rich people, and the median income is the honest description of the typical person. Same logic for hospital length of stay, reaction times, and number of therapy sessions attended.

Two extra means worth naming

  • Geometric mean: the nth root of the product of n scores. Used for rates of change, growth rates and ratio data. GM ≤ AM always.
  • Harmonic mean: n divided by the sum of the reciprocals. Used for averaging rates and, in statistics, for computing the effective sample size in unequal-n ANOVA and post hoc tests. HM ≤ GM ≤ AM always.

4 Dispersion

Two distributions can have the same mean and be completely different. Central tendency without dispersion is half a description. Measures of dispersion (variability, scatter, spread) tell you how far the scores stray from the centre.

4.1 Range

R = highest score − lowest score. Simplest, crudest, and completely determined by the two most extreme scores, which are also the two least reliable ones. Unstable across samples. Useful only as a quick indication.

4.2 Quartile deviation (semi-interquartile range)

Q = (Q₃ − Q₁) / 2, where Q₁ is the 25th percentile and Q₃ the 75th. The interquartile range (IQR) is Q₃ − Q₁; the quartile deviation is half of it. It covers the middle 50% of scores, so it is unaffected by extreme values. It is the partner of the median: report Md and Q together, always.

4.3 Average deviation (mean deviation)

AD = Σ|X − M| / N. The average distance of scores from the mean, ignoring sign. Uses all scores, which is a step up from the range, but the absolute-value operator makes it algebraically useless, so it disappeared from inferential statistics. It is minimised when taken about the median, not the mean.

4.4 Variance and standard deviation

Definition: the standard deviation is the root-mean-square deviation from the mean. It is, quite literally, the typical distance a score sits from the mean, in the original units. The variance is its square.

Formulas.
Population variance: σ² = Σ(X − μ)² / N  ·  population SD: σ = √[Σ(X − μ)² / N]
Sample variance (unbiased estimate): s² = Σ(X − M)² / (n − 1)  ·  sample SD: s = √[Σ(X − M)² / (n − 1)]
Computational (raw-score) form: s² = [ΣX² − (ΣX)²/n] / (n − 1)
The numerator Σ(X − M)² has its own name: the sum of squares (SS). Remember that, because the whole of ANOVA is bookkeeping about sums of squares.

Worked example

Scores 4, 7, 7, 9, 13; M = 8.
Deviations: −4, −1, −1, +1, +5. (They sum to 0, as they must.)
Squared: 16, 1, 1, 1, 25. SS = 44.
s² = 44 / (5 − 1) = 11.0  ·  s = √11 = 3.32.
If these five were the whole population: σ² = 44/5 = 8.8, σ = 2.97. Note that dividing by n gives a smaller, biased-downward answer.

4.5 Degrees of freedom: why n − 1

The idea. Degrees of freedom is the number of values in a calculation that are free to vary once the constraints are imposed. To compute the sample variance you must first compute M from the same data. That fixes one thing: the deviations must sum to zero. So if n = 5 and you know four of the deviations, the fifth is forced. Only 4 of the 5 are free. Hence df = n − 1.

The consequence. Dividing SS by n gives a variance estimate that is systematically too small, because the sample mean is always closer to the sample's own scores than the population mean is. Dividing by n − 1 corrects the downward bias, and is called Bessel's correction. It makes s² an unbiased estimator of σ².

4.6 Coefficient of variation

CV = (s / M) × 100. A relative, unit-free measure of dispersion, so it lets you compare the variability of two things measured in different units (heights in cm vs. weights in kg), or two groups with very different means. It requires a ratio scale with a meaningful zero, so it must not be used on IQ or on Celsius temperature.

4.7 Properties of the standard deviation

  1. Adding a constant to every score leaves the SD unchanged (the whole distribution shifts, the spread does not). Multiplying every score by c multiplies the SD by |c| and the variance by c².
  2. The SD is in the same units as the original scores; the variance is in squared units. That is why you report SD and analyse variance.
  3. Variances add; standard deviations do not. This is the reason ANOVA partitions variance.
  4. The SD is the most reliable measure of dispersion across repeated samples, and it is the one used in every inferential formula.
  5. It is affected by every score, including extreme ones. On badly skewed data, report the quartile deviation with the median instead.
Recap of sections 1 to 4: Stevens's four scales (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) determine which statistics are legal. A frequency distribution and its graphs (histogram, polygon, ogive, box plot) show you the shape. Central tendency locates it: the mean is the balance point and minimises squared deviations, the median is the 50th percentile and resists outliers, the mode is the peak and the only option at nominal level. Dispersion measures how wide it is: range, quartile deviation (partner of the median), average deviation, and the standard deviation (partner of the mean), whose squared numerator SS and denominator n − 1 are the foundation of everything that follows.

5 Skewness and kurtosis

Central tendency gives the location, dispersion gives the width. The third and fourth properties of a distribution are its shape: how lopsided it is (skewness) and how peaked it is (kurtosis). Formally these are the third and fourth moments about the mean; the first moment is zero by definition and the second is the variance.

Interpretation rule of thumb: |Sk| under 0.5 is approximately symmetrical, 0.5 to 1.0 is moderately skewed, over 1.0 is substantially skewed. Another common working rule: skew is a problem if it exceeds twice its own standard error.

What causes skew, and what to do about it

  • Floor effect: the measure cannot go below a limit and most people are at it. Produces positive skew. Common with symptom counts and error counts in healthy samples.
  • Ceiling effect: the measure cannot go above a limit and most people are at it. Produces negative skew. Common with easy tests.
  • Selection: studying only the extreme end of a population.
  • Transformations to fix positive skew: square root (mild), logarithm (moderate), reciprocal (severe). For negative skew, reflect the variable first (subtract each score from the maximum + 1), then apply the same transformation.
  • Or switch to a non-parametric test, which does not care about the shape.

6 The normal curve and standard scores

The normal curve was derived by Abraham de Moivre in 1733 as the limit of the binomial, developed by Pierre-Simon Laplace, and applied to measurement error by Carl Friedrich Gauss (hence "Gaussian"). Adolphe Quetelet was the first to apply it to human characteristics ("l'homme moyen", the average man), and Sir Francis Galton brought it into psychology, along with the words "regression", "correlation" and "percentile".

6.1 Properties of the normal probability curve

  1. Bell-shaped and perfectly symmetrical about the mean. Skewness = 0.
  2. Mean = median = mode, all at the centre.
  3. Asymptotic: the tails approach the X axis but never touch it, so the curve extends from −∞ to +∞. Any score has a non-zero probability.
  4. Unimodal, with a single maximum ordinate at the mean. The maximum ordinate is 0.3989 (that is, 1/√2π) when expressed in z units.
  5. Mesokurtic: kurtosis = 3, excess kurtosis = 0.
  6. Total area under the curve = 1.00 (or 100%), which is why areas can be read as probabilities.
  7. Points of inflection (where the curve changes from concave-down to concave-up) lie exactly at ±1σ.
  8. It is completely defined by two parameters: μ and σ. Change μ and the curve slides; change σ and it widens or narrows.
  9. The standard normal curve is the special case with μ = 0 and σ = 1.
  10. The quartile deviation Q = 0.6745σ, and the average deviation AD = 0.7979σ.

6.3 z scores

Definition: a z score expresses a raw score as the number of standard deviations it lies above or below the mean.

z = (X − M) / s  (sample)  ·  z = (X − μ) / σ  (population)  ·  to go back: X = M + z·s

Worked example

A student scores 85 on a test with M = 70 and s = 10. z = (85 − 70)/10 = +1.5. From the table, the area below z = 1.5 is .9332, so the student is at the 93rd percentile and beat 93% of the group. A second student scores 60: z = −1.0, area below = .1587, so the 16th percentile.

Properties of a z distribution

  • The mean of a set of z scores is always 0 and the SD always 1. Converting to z is a linear transformation.
  • Standardising does not change the shape of the distribution. A skewed distribution converted to z scores is still exactly as skewed. This is the most frequently missed point about z scores.
  • z scores make scores from different tests comparable, which is the whole reason they exist.
  • Roughly 99.7% of z scores fall between −3 and +3.
Trap: "z transformation normalises a distribution." False. It standardises it (fixes M = 0, s = 1) but leaves the shape untouched. To actually change the shape into a normal one you need a normalised standard score, which is computed from percentile ranks via the area table, not from the linear z formula. Normalised T scores and stanines are of this second kind.

Percentile rank is not a standard score

Percentiles are ordinal, not interval. A percentile rank says what proportion of the group scored below you. Because scores pile up in the middle of a normal distribution, percentiles are squeezed together at the centre and stretched at the extremes: the difference between the 50th and 55th percentile is a tiny difference in raw score, while the difference between the 94th and 99th is large. So you must not average percentiles or do arithmetic on them. Convert to z first. This is one of the most reliably examined points in the psychometrics section.

7 Probability, sampling and the sampling distribution

7.1 Probability basics

  • Probability p(A) = number of favourable outcomes / total number of equally likely outcomes. Ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • Addition rule (for "or"): mutually exclusive events, p(A or B) = p(A) + p(B). Non-exclusive, p(A or B) = p(A) + p(B) − p(A and B).
  • Multiplication rule (for "and"): independent events, p(A and B) = p(A) × p(B). Dependent, p(A and B) = p(A) × p(B|A).
  • Conditional probability p(A|B): the probability of A given that B has occurred. The basis of Bayes's theorem, and of sensitivity, specificity and predictive value in diagnostic testing.
  • Binomial distribution: the probability distribution of the number of successes in n independent trials each with probability p. Mean = np, variance = npq. As n grows it approaches the normal curve, which is exactly de Moivre's 1733 result.

Definitions to be exact about: sampling error is the natural, unavoidable discrepancy between a sample statistic and its population parameter, caused by chance alone; it shrinks as n grows. 7.3 The sampling distribution of the mean

This is the pivot of the whole book. Everything inferential rests on one imaginary object.

The idea. Take a sample of size n and compute its mean. Put it back, take another sample of size n, compute its mean. Do this endlessly. You now have a distribution, not of raw scores, but of sample means. That is the sampling distribution of the mean. It is theoretical; you never actually build it. But you know its three properties exactly, and that is enough to say how surprising your one real sample is.
  1. Its mean equals the population mean: μM = μ. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator.
  2. Its standard deviation is the standard error of the mean: SEM = σ / √n, estimated in practice by s / √n.
  3. Its shape approaches normal as n grows, whatever the shape of the parent population. That is the central limit theorem.

The central limit theorem, stated properly

Central limit theorem (CLT). As sample size n increases, the sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n, regardless of the shape of the population distribution. If the population is already normal, the sampling distribution is normal for any n. If it is not, n ≥ 30 is the conventional threshold at which the approximation is good enough.
Why the CLT is the most important theorem you will be asked about. It is the licence to use z, t, F and every parametric test on real psychological data, which is almost never normally distributed. It says that the non-normality of the raw scores stops mattering once n is reasonable, because the tests are built on the sampling distribution of the mean, not on the raw scores.

Standard error: the two things to get right

  • SE is a standard deviation, specifically the SD of a sampling distribution. Every statistic has one: SE of the mean = s/√n; SE of a proportion = √(pq/n); SE of the difference between means = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂); SE of r ≈ (1 − r²)/√(n − 1); SE of measurement (in psychometrics) = s√(1 − rxx).
  • SE shrinks with √n, not with n. To halve the standard error you must quadruple the sample. This is the law of diminishing returns in sample-size planning, and it is a favourite exam item.
SD vs SE. The standard deviation describes how spread out the individual scores are; it is a fact about your data and it does not shrink as you collect more. The standard error describes how much a sample statistic bounces around from sample to sample; it is a fact about your estimate and it does shrink as n grows. Report SD to describe the sample; report SE (or a confidence interval) to describe the precision of your estimate.

7.4 Estimation and confidence intervals

  • Point estimate: a single value offered as the estimate of a parameter (the sample mean estimates μ). Simple, but certainly wrong by some amount, and it carries no information about precision.
  • Interval estimate: a range of values, with a stated confidence level, likely to contain the parameter. Always preferable.
  • A good estimator is unbiased (its expected value is the parameter), consistent (converges on the parameter as n grows), efficient (smallest standard error among unbiased estimators) and sufficient (uses all the information in the sample).
Confidence interval for a mean.
σ known: CI = M ± zcrit (σ/√n)  ·  σ unknown: CI = M ± tcrit (s/√n)
95% CI uses z = 1.96  ·  99% CI uses z = 2.58  ·  90% CI uses z = 1.645
The quantity added and subtracted is the margin of error.

Worked example

M = 52, s = 12, n = 36. SE = 12/√36 = 2.0. 95% CI = 52 ± 1.96(2.0) = 52 ± 3.92 = 48.08 to 55.92. Because this interval does not contain 60, a null hypothesis that μ = 60 would be rejected at .05. That equivalence between confidence intervals and significance tests is worth stating in any answer.

The interpretation trap. A 95% confidence interval does not mean "there is a 95% probability that μ lies in this interval". μ is a fixed constant; it is either in the interval or it is not. The correct statement is: if we repeated this sampling procedure many times, 95% of the intervals so constructed would contain μ. The confidence is in the procedure, not in this one interval.
The three things that widen a confidence interval: a higher confidence level (99% is wider than 95%), a larger standard deviation, and a smaller sample. There is a permanent trade-off between confidence and precision: you can be more certain, or you can be more specific, but not both at once with fixed n.
Recap of sections 5 to 7: skew is asymmetry (named for the tail; mean drags furthest into it; Mo < Md < M for positive) and kurtosis is peakedness (lepto tall, meso normal, platy flat). The normal curve is defined by μ and σ, holds 68 / 95 / 99.7 percent within 1, 2 and 3 SD, and exactly 95% within ±1.96 SD. z scores standardise without normalising, and every derived score (T, IQ, stanine, sten) is a linear rescaling of z. Probability sampling makes sampling error calculable; stratified is the most precise, cluster the least. The sampling distribution of the mean has mean μ, SD equal to σ/√n (the standard error) and, by the central limit theorem, a normal shape once n is about 30, whatever the population looked like. Confidence intervals wrap that standard error around the sample mean.

8 Hypothesis testing, errors, power and effect size

The framework you are about to learn is a hybrid of two feuding traditions. Sir Ronald Aylmer Fisher gave us the null hypothesis, the p value, significance testing, the F distribution, ANOVA, randomisation, degrees of freedom and the .05 convention. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson (son of Karl) gave us the alternative hypothesis, Type I and Type II error, α and β, power, and the decision-theoretic frame. Textbooks teach them fused; historically the two camps despised each other. Naming both in an answer is an easy extra mark.

8.1 The hypotheses

The single biggest language trap in the whole subject. You never accept the null hypothesis and you never prove anything. A non-significant result means only that you failed to find sufficient evidence against H₀; it does not establish that H₀ is true, because the failure could equally be low power, a small sample, or a poor measure. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Write "failed to reject H₀" or "retained H₀", never "accepted H₀" or "proved there is no difference".
Why test the null at all? Because you cannot compute a sampling distribution for "there is some difference of unspecified size", but you can compute one exactly for "the difference is zero". Falsification is logically clean: one contrary result can refute a universal claim, while no number of confirming results can prove it. Fisher borrowed the logic; Karl Popper made it a philosophy of science.

8.2 The steps of a significance test

  1. State H₀ and H₁, and decide whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed.
  2. Set the significance level α (conventionally .05, or .01 when a false positive is costly).
  3. Choose the test statistic appropriate to the data (see section 16) and compute its degrees of freedom.
  4. Find the critical value from the table, or equivalently define the region of rejection.
  5. Compute the obtained value of the statistic from the data.
  6. Decide: if |obtained| ≥ critical (equivalently p ≤ α), reject H₀. Otherwise retain it.
  7. State the conclusion in the language of the research question, and report the effect size and a confidence interval alongside the p value.

Fix them in memory with a clinical analogy. A screening test for a disorder: a Type I error is telling a healthy person they have the disorder (false positive, over-diagnosis). A Type II error is telling a genuinely ill person they are fine (false negative, missed case). Which is worse depends entirely on the consequences, which is exactly why α is a decision, not a law of nature.
  • α is set by the researcher before the study and equals the probability of a Type I error. Setting α = .05 means you accept a 5% chance of a false positive.
  • β is not set directly; it follows from α, n, the effect size and the variability. Convention targets β ≤ .20.
  • α and β trade off at fixed n: making α stricter (.05 → .01) reduces false positives but raises β and lowers power. The only way to reduce both at once is to increase n.
  • Some texts add Type III error (getting the right answer to the wrong question, or correctly rejecting H₀ but misidentifying the direction of the effect).

8.5 Power

Power = 1 − β, the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. In plain terms: the probability that your study will find the effect if the effect is really there. Convention: power of at least .80.

The six determinants of power (an exam favourite; learn all six)
1. Sample size (n): the biggest lever, and the only one fully under your control. Larger n shrinks the standard error, which separates the two distributions.
2. Effect size: bigger real effects are easier to detect.
3. Significance level α: a more lenient α (.05 rather than .01) increases power, at the cost of more Type I errors.
4. One-tailed vs two-tailed: one-tailed tests are more powerful when the direction is correctly predicted.
5. Error variance: reducing it through better measurement, standardised procedure, homogeneous samples, or a repeated-measures design increases power sharply.
6. Choice of test: parametric tests are more powerful than their non-parametric equivalents when their assumptions hold.
Power analysis is done a priori to determine the n needed to detect an effect of a given size, and this is what ethics committees and journals now expect. Done post hoc on an obtained effect it is largely uninformative (observed power is a direct function of the p value). Jacob Cohen wrote the standard reference (Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences, 1969/1988), and G*Power is the standard free software.

8.6 The p value, and what it is not

Definition. The p value is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. Formally, p = P(data | H₀).
Four things p is NOT, each of which appears as a distractor.
1. It is not the probability that H₀ is true. That would be P(H₀ | data), which is the reverse conditional and needs Bayes's theorem.
2. It is not the probability that the result is due to chance.
3. It is not a measure of the size or importance of the effect. With n = 10,000 a correlation of .03 is significant at .01 and means nothing.
4. It is not the probability that the finding will replicate.
Statistical significance vs practical significance. Significance is a statement about how surprising the data would be under H₀; it is heavily driven by sample size. Practical or clinical significance is a statement about whether the effect is big enough to matter, and that is what effect size measures. A drug that lowers depression scores by 0.4 points can be highly significant in a trial of 5,000 and clinically worthless. Always report both. This is the core of the American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p values and of the replication-crisis literature.

9 The t tests

William Sealy Gosset derived the t distribution in 1908 while working as a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, dealing with the very small samples of barley the brewery could afford. Guinness forbade employees from publishing, so he wrote under the pen name "Student", and the test is called Student's t to this day. Fisher later gave it its modern form and the letter t.

9.1 Why t and not z

A z test requires you to know the population standard deviation σ. You essentially never do. When you estimate it with the sample s, you introduce a second source of error (s itself varies from sample to sample), so the resulting statistic no longer follows the normal distribution. It follows a slightly flatter, fatter-tailed distribution: the t distribution, one for every value of df.

9.2 One-sample t test

Question: does my sample mean differ from a known or hypothesised population mean?

t = (M − μ) / (s / √n)  ·  df = n − 1
In words: (what I got minus what I expected) divided by the standard error.

Worked example

A clinic's 25 patients score M = 108 on a wellbeing scale with s = 15. The published population mean is 100.
SE = 15/√25 = 3.0. t = (108 − 100)/3.0 = 2.67, df = 24. Critical t at .05 two-tailed, df 24 = 2.064. Since 2.67 > 2.064, reject H₀.
Effect size: d = (108 − 100)/15 = 0.53, a medium effect.

9.3 Independent-samples t test

Question: do two separate, unrelated groups differ on the DV? (Also called unpaired, uncorrelated, or between-subjects t.)

t = (M₁ − M₂) / SEdiff  ·  df = n₁ + n₂ − 2
where SEdiff = √[s²p(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)], and the pooled variance is
s²p = (SS₁ + SS₂) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2), a weighted average of the two group variances.

Worked example

Therapy group: n₁ = 12, M₁ = 22, s₁ = 5. Control: n₂ = 12, M₂ = 17, s₂ = 6.
s²p = (11×25 + 11×36)/22 = (275 + 396)/22 = 30.5.
SEdiff = √[30.5(1/12 + 1/12)] = √5.083 = 2.25.
t = (22 − 17)/2.25 = 2.22, df = 22. Critical t = 2.074, so reject H₀.
d = (22 − 17)/√30.5 = 5/5.52 = 0.91, a large effect.

Welch's t (the unequal-variances t). When Levene's test shows the variances differ, or the group sizes are very unequal, use Welch–Satterthwaite: the standard error is computed without pooling and the df are adjusted downwards (and become fractional). SPSS prints it as the "equal variances not assumed" row. Naming Welch when asked about a violation of homogeneity is a strong answer.

9.4 Paired-samples t test

Question: do the same people (or matched pairs) differ across two conditions or two time points? (Also called dependent, correlated, related, matched-pairs, repeated-measures or within-subjects t.)

t = MD / (sD / √n)  ·  df = n − 1, where n is the number of pairs
The trick: compute the difference score D for each pair, then run a one-sample t test on those difference scores against μ = 0. That is all the paired t test is.

Worked example

Six patients, BDI before and after therapy. Differences (before − after): 8, 5, 11, 3, 7, 8. ΣD = 42, so MD = 7.0, n = 6.
Deviations from 7: +1, −2, +4, −4, 0, +1. Squared: 1, 4, 16, 16, 0, 1. SS = 38, so s²D = 38/5 = 7.6 and sD = 2.76.
SE = 2.76/√6 = 1.126. t = 7.0/1.126 = 6.22, df = 5. Critical t at .05 two-tailed = 2.571, so reject H₀: therapy produced a significant reduction. Effect size d = MD/sD = 7.0/2.76 = 2.54, very large.

Why the paired test is more powerful. Individual differences (some people are simply more depressed than others) are a large source of error variance in a between-subjects design. In a within-subjects design each person acts as their own control, so that variance is subtracted out entirely and only the change matters. The error term shrinks, t grows, and power rises. The price is the order effects (practice, fatigue, carryover, sensitisation) that within-subjects designs create, which is why you counterbalance, ideally with a Latin square.

Howard Levene (1960) gave us the standard test of homogeneity of variance: it runs a one-way ANOVA on the absolute deviations of each score from its group mean or median. Read assumption tests backwards. For Levene's, Shapiro–Wilk, Box's M, Mauchly's and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, a non-significant result (p > .05) is the good news: it means the assumption holds. Students routinely reverse this. In every one of these tests, the null hypothesis is the assumption itself.

9.6 Why not run many t tests instead of an ANOVA

The inflation of familywise error. Each t test carries a Type I error risk of α = .05. Run several and the probability that at least one is a false positive rises fast: for c independent comparisons, familywise α = 1 − (1 − .05)c. With three groups you would run three t tests, giving 1 − .95³ = .143. With five groups that is ten comparisons and a familywise error rate of .40: a two-in-five chance of a false claim. ANOVA tests all group differences in a single test at a single α, which is exactly the problem it was invented to solve. This is the standard answer to "why do we need ANOVA when we have the t test?" and it is asked constantly.
The relationship between t and F. For exactly two groups, one-way ANOVA and the independent t test give an identical decision, and F = t². Check it: t = 2.22 gives F = 4.93; the critical t of 2.074 squared is 4.30, which is the critical F for df 1, 22. They are the same test written two ways.
Recap of sections 8 and 9: hypothesis testing sets up a null of no effect, computes how surprising the data would be if it were true, and rejects when that probability falls below α. Type I error is a false positive (rate α, set by you), Type II is a false negative (rate β), and power = 1 − β is driven mainly by sample size and effect size. The p value is P(data | H₀) and says nothing about effect magnitude, so always report Cohen's d, η² or r alongside it. The t tests compare means when σ is unknown: one-sample against a known μ, independent-samples between two unrelated groups (pooled variance, df = n₁+n₂−2), and paired-samples on difference scores (df = pairs − 1). They assume independence, normality, homogeneity of variance (test with Levene, repair with Welch) and an interval DV. Running many t tests inflates familywise error, which is why ANOVA exists.

10 Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

Sir Ronald Aylmer Fisher developed ANOVA at Rothamsted Experimental Station in the 1920s, working on agricultural field trials, and published it in Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) and The Design of Experiments (1935). The same book gave psychology randomisation, replication, the factorial design, the null hypothesis and the .05 convention. George Snedecor named the ratio F in Fisher's honour.

10.1 The logic: partitioning variance

ANOVA is misnamed. It compares means, but it does so by analysing variances. Here is the whole idea in three sentences. Take all the scores together and measure how much they vary in total. Some of that variation exists because the groups were treated differently (between-groups), and the rest exists because people differ from each other within the same treatment (within-groups, which is error). If the between-groups part is much bigger than the within-groups part, the treatment must be doing something.

10.4 Factorial ANOVA: main effects and interactions

A factorial design crosses two or more IVs so that every level of one appears with every level of the other. A 2 × 3 design has two IVs, one with 2 levels and one with 3, and therefore 6 cells. A 2 × 2 × 3 design has 3 IVs and 12 cells. The number of numbers in the notation is the number of factors; the numbers themselves are the levels; their product is the number of cells or conditions.

EffectQuestion it answersHow you read it
Main effect of AIgnoring B entirely, do the levels of A differ?Compare the row (or column) marginal means

The rule that answers most interaction items: parallel lines mean no interaction. Any departure from parallel is an interaction. If the lines diverge but never cross, it is an ordinal interaction (the rank order of the groups is preserved at every level). If they cross over, it is a disordinal or crossover interaction (the effect of A reverses direction across levels of B).
The interpretation priority rule. When an interaction is significant, interpret the interaction first and treat the main effects with great caution, because a main effect averaged across a crossover interaction can be exactly zero while both simple effects are large. Follow up with simple effects analysis: the effect of A at each separate level of B. Saying this sentence in an answer marks you out as someone who actually understands factorial designs.

Sphericity: the assumption unique to repeated measures

Sphericity means the variances of the differences between every pair of conditions are equal. (Its stricter cousin, compound symmetry, requires equal variances and equal covariances.) It only becomes an issue with three or more conditions, since with two there is only one difference score.

Test: Mauchly's test of sphericity. Non-significant (p > .05) means the assumption holds.
If violated, correct the degrees of freedom by multiplying them by an epsilon (ε) estimate:
• Greenhouse–Geisser: conservative, use when the estimated ε is below .75;
• Huynh–Feldt: less conservative, use when ε is above .75;
• Lower-bound: the most extreme correction;
• or abandon the univariate approach and use MANOVA on the difference scores, which does not require sphericity at all.
Violating sphericity inflates F and therefore the Type I error rate.

Order effects and how to control them

  • Practice effect: performance improves with repetition. Fatigue effect: it declines. Carryover: the effect of one condition persists into the next. Sensitisation: exposure to several conditions makes the purpose obvious.
  • Counterbalancing is the fix: complete counterbalancing uses all k! orders (feasible only for small k); incomplete counterbalancing uses a Latin square, where each condition appears once in each ordinal position; randomised order per participant is the simplest option.
  • Where carryover cannot be undone (learning a skill, a drug with a long half-life), a between-subjects design is the only honest choice.

Mixed (split-plot) ANOVA

Combines at least one between-subjects factor and at least one within-subjects factor. The commonest design in clinical outcome research: group (treatment vs control, between) × time (pre, post, follow-up, within). It generates two different error terms, one for the between part and one for the within part, and the effect people usually care about is the group × time interaction, because that is what "the treatment worked better than the control over time" actually means.

11.2 ANCOVA (analysis of covariance)

What it is: ANOVA with a regression built into it. Before comparing group means, ANCOVA uses the covariate to predict the DV, removes that predicted portion from both the DV and the error term, and compares the resulting adjusted means.

The two things ANCOVA buys you.
1. Increased power. Variance in the DV explained by the covariate is moved out of the error term. A smaller MSerror means a larger F and a better chance of detecting the treatment effect. This is the statistically respectable reason to use it.
2. Statistical control. Groups that were not equivalent at the start on some measured variable (pre-test score, age, IQ, symptom severity) can be compared as if they had been equal on it. This is the reason it is used in quasi-experiments, and it is the one that gets abused.
The Lord's paradox warning. ANCOVA is not a substitute for random assignment. When intact groups differ on the covariate for reasons connected to the grouping itself, adjusting for the covariate can remove part of the real treatment effect or manufacture a spurious one. Frederic Lord (1967) showed that two defensible analyses of the same non-randomised data can give opposite answers. Say "ANCOVA is a statistical control, not a design control" if you are asked whether it fixes non-equivalent groups.

Assumptions of ANCOVA (all the ANOVA ones, plus three)

  1. Linearity of the relationship between the covariate and the DV, within each group. If it is curvilinear, the adjustment is wrong.
  2. Homogeneity of regression slopes: the covariate must relate to the DV in the same way in every group. Tested by checking the covariate × IV interaction: it must be non-significant. If it is significant, ANCOVA is invalid and you should examine simple slopes or use moderated regression instead. This is the assumption unique to ANCOVA and the one most often asked about.
  3. The covariate must be measured reliably (unreliability biases the adjustment) and, ideally, measured before the treatment so that it cannot itself have been affected by the IV. A covariate influenced by the treatment removes part of the effect you are trying to measure.
  4. The covariate should be correlated with the DV (otherwise it buys no power) but uncorrelated with the IV (otherwise you are removing the treatment effect).

Worked logic

A trial compares CBT and waitlist on post-treatment BDI. The CBT group happened to start more depressed (pre-test M = 30 vs 24). Comparing raw post-test means is unfair. ANCOVA uses pre-test BDI as the covariate, adjusts each group's post-test mean to what it would have been had both groups started at the grand mean pre-test score, and compares those adjusted means. It also removes the (large) share of post-test variance predicted by pre-test score from the error term, which typically increases F substantially.

11.3 MANOVA (multivariate analysis of variance)

What it is: ANOVA with two or more dependent variables analysed simultaneously. MANOVA forms an optimally weighted linear combination of the DVs (a variate or discriminant function) that maximally separates the groups, and tests whether the groups differ on that combination.

Why not just run several ANOVAs? Three reasons.
1. Type I error control. Five separate ANOVAs at α = .05 give a familywise error rate near .23. MANOVA tests them all under one omnibus test at one α.
2. It uses the correlations among the DVs. Separate ANOVAs throw that information away. MANOVA can detect a group difference on the combination of DVs that no single DV shows on its own, which happens whenever the effect is spread thinly across correlated outcomes.
3. It protects against capitalising on chance in a set of conceptually related outcomes (for example, the several subscales of one symptom inventory).

Exam shortcut: for Wilks' lambda a small value means a large effect (it is unexplained variance). For the other three, larger means larger. When the four statistics disagree, report Pillai's trace, because it is the most robust. When there are exactly two groups, all four are mathematically equivalent and reduce to Hotelling's T².

What follows a significant MANOVA

  • Separate univariate ANOVAs on each DV, with a Bonferroni-adjusted α (the "protected F" approach). Simple, common, but ignores the correlations that justified MANOVA in the first place.
  • Discriminant function analysis, which describes the actual dimensions along which the groups differ and reports the structure coefficients for each DV. Statistically the more coherent follow-up.
  • Roy–Bargmann stepdown analysis, an ordered sequence of ANCOVAs in which each DV is tested with the preceding DVs as covariates.

Assumptions of MANOVA

  1. Independence of observations.
  2. Multivariate normality: each DV is normal, and every linear combination of them is normal. Checked in practice with Mardia's test or by examining Mahalanobis distances.
  3. Homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices: tested by Box's M. Non-significant is what you want. Box's M is highly sensitive, so it is conventionally evaluated at α = .001; if it fails, switch to Pillai's trace.
  4. Linearity among all pairs of DVs.
  5. Absence of multicollinearity and singularity: the DVs should be correlated (about .30 to .70 is the sweet spot), but not so highly correlated that they are measuring the same thing. If two DVs correlate above about .80 or .90, combine them or drop one.
  6. Adequate sample size: the n in the smallest cell must exceed the number of DVs. More is much better; MANOVA is sensitive to outliers and small cells.

11.4 MANCOVA

Multiple DVs plus one or more covariates: MANOVA's power advantages combined with ANCOVA's adjustment. It carries the union of both sets of assumptions, including homogeneity of regression slopes across groups for every DV, so it is the most demanding member of the family and needs the largest n. Use it when you have several correlated outcomes and a pre-existing difference or a strong nuisance variable to control.

Two relatives to name if the question invites them. Discriminant function analysis (DFA) is the mirror image of MANOVA: MANOVA asks whether known groups differ on a set of variables, DFA asks whether a set of variables can predict group membership. Same mathematics, IV and DV swapped. Logistic regression answers the same prediction question as DFA but without requiring multivariate normality, which is why it has largely replaced DFA in clinical research.
Recap of sections 10 and 11: ANOVA partitions total variance into between-groups (treatment plus error) and within-groups (error alone) and forms F = MSb/MSw, expected to be 1.00 under the null. A significant omnibus F means only that some pair differs, so it is followed by post hoc tests (Tukey HSD as default, Scheffé most conservative, Dunnett against a control, Games–Howell for unequal variances). Factorial ANOVA adds main effects and the interaction; parallel lines mean no interaction, crossing lines mean a disordinal one, and a significant interaction is interpreted before the main effects. Repeated-measures ANOVA removes individual differences from error, which raises power, but adds the sphericity assumption tested by Mauchly and corrected by Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh–Feldt. ANCOVA adds a covariate to raise power and equate groups, and requires homogeneity of regression slopes. MANOVA handles multiple DVs through Wilks' lambda (small = large effect), Pillai's trace (most robust), Hotelling's trace and Roy's largest root, with Box's M testing the covariance matrices. MANCOVA is both at once.

12 Correlation

Sir Francis Galton invented the concept while studying heredity in the 1880s and gave us the words correlation, regression and percentile. His student Karl Pearson turned it into the product-moment formula in 1896, which is why r is Pearson's r and is sometimes written r to honour Galton's "reversion". Charles Spearman produced the rank-order version in 1904, in the same paper that launched factor analysis and the g factor.

12.1 What a correlation is

A correlation coefficient is a single number describing the direction and strength of the linear relationship between two variables. It runs from −1.00 through 0 to +1.00.

  • Sign = direction. Positive means both variables move the same way; negative means one rises as the other falls; zero means no linear relation.
  • Absolute value = strength. The sign and the strength are completely independent: r = −.80 is exactly as strong as r = +.80, and much stronger than r = +.30. That is the most commonly missed point in the correlation section.
  • Rough conventions (Cohen): .10 small, .30 medium, .50 large. In test-retest reliability the same number would be poor, so interpret against the context.

12.2 Pearson's product-moment correlation (r)

Formulas.
Definitional: r = Σ(zX zY) / n, the average product of the paired z scores. (This is where "product-moment" comes from: a moment is a deviation from the mean.)
Covariance form: r = cov(X,Y) / (sX sY), where cov = Σ(X−MX)(Y−MY)/(n−1).
Raw score: r = [nΣXY − ΣXΣY] / √{[nΣX² − (ΣX)²][nΣY² − (ΣY)²]}
Significance: t = r√(n − 2) / √(1 − r²), with df = n − 2.
Why covariance alone is not enough. Covariance already captures whether X and Y move together, but its size depends on the units the variables are measured in, so it cannot be compared across studies. Dividing by the two standard deviations standardises it onto a fixed −1 to +1 scale. A correlation is a standardised covariance. That one sentence answers a whole family of questions.

Coefficient of determination

r² is the proportion of variance in one variable that is shared with (accounted for by) the other. 1 − r² is the coefficient of non-determination or the coefficient of alienation is √(1 − r²). So r = .60 means 36% shared variance, not 60%. This non-linearity matters: doubling r from .30 to .60 multiplies the shared variance by four (from 9% to 36%).

Worked example

Five students, hours studied (X) and score (Y): (2,4), (4,6), (6,7), (8,9), (10,11). MX = 6, MY = 7.4.
x deviations: −4, −2, 0, +2, +4. y deviations: −3.4, −1.4, −0.4, +1.6, +3.6.
Σxy = 13.6 + 2.8 + 0 + 3.2 + 14.4 = 34.0. Σx² = 16+4+0+4+16 = 40. Σy² = 11.56+1.96+0.16+2.56+12.96 = 29.2.
r = 34.0 / √(40 × 29.2) = 34.0 / √1168 = 34.0/34.18 = .995. Almost perfect positive. r² = .99, so 99% of the variance in score is shared with study hours in this (artificially tidy) data.

12.3 The assumptions and limitations of Pearson's r

  1. Both variables interval or ratio. Otherwise use a rank or categorical coefficient.
  2. Linearity. r measures linear relationship only. A perfect U-shaped relationship gives r = 0. Always look at the scatterplot first.
  3. Bivariate normality, for significance testing.
  4. Homoscedasticity: the spread of Y is roughly the same at every value of X.
  5. Independence of pairs, and continuity of the underlying variables.
  6. No extreme outliers, to which r is very sensitive.

13 Regression

13.1 Simple linear regression

Correlation asks how strongly two variables go together. Regression asks a different question: given X, what is my best prediction of Y? It fits a straight line through the scatterplot and uses it to predict.

The regression equation. Ŷ = a + bX, where Ŷ (Y-hat) is the predicted score, b is the slope or regression coefficient (the change in Y per one-unit change in X), and a is the intercept (the predicted Y when X = 0).

b = r (sY / sX)  ·  a = MY − b MX
In standardised (z) form the intercept vanishes and the slope is the correlation: zŶ = r · zX.

The least squares criterion

The line is chosen so that the sum of the squared vertical distances from each point to the line, Σ(Y − Ŷ)², is as small as possible. Those vertical distances are the residuals or errors of prediction. Note that they are vertical, not perpendicular, and that this is exactly the same least-squares logic that made the mean the balance point in section 3.

Worked example

Using the study-hours data from 12.2: r = .995, sX = 3.16, sY = 2.70, MX = 6, MY = 7.4.
b = .995(2.70/3.16) = 0.85. a = 7.4 − 0.85(6) = 2.30.
So Ŷ = 2.30 + 0.85X. A student studying 7 hours is predicted to score 2.30 + 5.95 = 8.25.

Standard error of estimate. SY.X = sY√(1 − r²) is the standard deviation of the residuals: the typical size of a prediction error, in the units of Y. When r = 0 it equals sY (you predict the mean and do no better than chance); when r = ±1 it is zero (perfect prediction). It is the single most useful number for saying how good a regression actually is.

Facts about the regression line worth memorising

  • The line always passes through the point (MX, MY).
  • There are two regression lines: Y on X (for predicting Y) and X on Y (for predicting X). They coincide only when r = ±1 and are perpendicular when r = 0. r is the geometric mean of the two slopes: r = √(bYX · bXY).
  • The residuals sum to zero and are uncorrelated with X.
  • Regression towards the mean (Galton's original discovery): unless r = 1, extreme scores on X are followed by less extreme predicted scores on Y. This is a statistical necessity, not a psychological finding, and it is the reason uncontrolled pre-post designs appear to show improvement in the most severe cases. Naming regression to the mean as a threat to internal validity is worth a mark on its own.

13.4 Multicollinearity

Definition: predictors that are highly correlated with each other, so they carry largely the same information.

  • Consequences: R² stays high, but the individual b and β weights become unstable, their standard errors inflate, real predictors test non-significant, and coefficients can flip sign between samples. You lose the ability to say which predictor matters.
  • Detection: VIF (variance inflation factor) above 10 (strict: above 5); tolerance (= 1/VIF) below .10; bivariate r above .80 between predictors; a condition index above 30. Singularity is the extreme case, where one predictor is a perfect linear combination of others (for example, entering all subscales and the total score).
  • Remedies: drop one of the pair, combine them into a composite or factor score, centre the variables when the collinearity comes from interaction terms, or use ridge regression or principal-components regression.
  • Suppressor variable: a predictor that correlates near zero with Y but nevertheless increases R² by removing irrelevant variance from another predictor. Counter-intuitive, and a favourite distractor.
Sample size rules of thumb for multiple regression: Tabachnick and Fidell suggest N ≥ 50 + 8k for testing the overall model and N ≥ 104 + k for testing individual predictors, where k is the number of predictors. A cruder and very quotable rule is at least 10 to 15 cases per predictor.

Logistic regression models a categorical outcome through the logit, estimates by maximum likelihood, and reports odds ratios.

Assumptions and cautions for chi-square

  1. Frequencies, not percentages or means. Running chi-square on percentages is a common and fatal error.
  2. Independence of observations: each case contributes to exactly one cell. This is why a repeated-measures design needs McNemar, not chi-square.
  3. Expected frequency of at least 5 in every cell (a common relaxation: no cell below 1, and at most 20% of cells below 5). If violated, combine categories or use Fisher's exact test.
  4. Yates's correction for continuity subtracts 0.5 from each |O − E| before squaring, in 2×2 tables with small n. It makes the test more conservative; many statisticians now consider it over-conservative and prefer Fisher's exact test.
  5. Chi-square tells you whether there is an association, not its strength or direction. Report phi (2×2) or Cramér's V (larger tables) alongside it.
  6. Like every test, chi-square grows significant with large N, so a trivial association becomes significant at N = 10,000.

15 Psychometrics: reliability, validity, item analysis, factor analysis and norms

Entrance papers do not separate statistics from psychometrics. Reliability coefficients are correlations, item discrimination is a point-biserial, factor analysis is a correlation matrix decomposed, and norms are standard scores. This section is worth as much as ANOVA in most of these papers and is usually the least prepared.

15.1 Classical test theory, in one equation

X = T + E: every observed score (X) is the sum of a true score (T) and an error component (E). Errors are assumed to be random, to have a mean of zero across many testings, and to be uncorrelated with true scores. From this one model the whole of reliability theory follows.

Reliability rxx = σ²T / σ²X: the proportion of observed-score variance that is true-score variance. It runs from 0 to 1, and it is a correlation, not a percentage.

Two traps about alpha. (1) Alpha rises simply because a test is longer, so a high alpha on a 60-item scale is much less impressive than the same value on a 10-item scale. Standard error of measurement

SEM = s√(1 − rxx), where s is the SD of the test and rxx its reliability. It is the standard deviation of the errors of measurement, so it tells you how much an individual's observed score would bounce around on repeated testing.

It is used to build a confidence band around a person's obtained score: 68% band = X ± 1 SEM, 95% band = X ± 1.96 SEM.

Worked example: WAIS-IV, SD = 15, reliability = .96. SEM = 15√(1 − .96) = 15(0.2) = 3.0. An obtained IQ of 105 gives a 95% confidence band of 105 ± 5.88, that is 99 to 111. This is the number that stops clinicians over-interpreting a 4-point difference between two testings.

Note the inverse relation: as reliability rises, SEM falls. Perfect reliability gives SEM = 0.

15.3 Validity

Validity is the degree to which a test measures what it claims to measure, and more precisely, the degree to which the inferences drawn from the scores are justified. Modern theory (Messick) treats it as a unitary concept with different lines of evidence, but exams still want the traditional types.

The relationship between reliability and validity, which is asked constantly. Reliability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for validity. A test can be perfectly reliable and completely invalid (a bathroom scale that consistently reads 10 kg too high; measuring head circumference as an index of intelligence). But a test that is unreliable cannot be valid, because it is not consistently measuring anything at all. Quantitatively, reliability sets the ceiling on validity: the maximum possible validity coefficient is √rxx. So a test with reliability .64 can never correlate more than .80 with any criterion.
Threats to internal and external validity (Campbell and Stanley, 1963) belong to research design rather than test theory, but papers mix them in. Internal validity threats: history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, statistical regression to the mean, selection, experimental mortality (attrition), and selection-maturation interaction. External validity threats: interaction of selection and treatment, interaction of setting and treatment, interaction of testing and treatment, and multiple-treatment interference. Note that statistical regression appears on the internal list, which links straight back to section 13.

15.5 Factor analysis

Purpose: to reduce a large set of correlated variables to a small number of underlying dimensions (factors) that explain the pattern of correlations among them. It answers "how many things is this scale actually measuring?"

Charles Spearman invented factor analysis in 1904 and used it to argue for g, the general factor of intelligence (his two-factor theory: g plus specific factors s). L. L. Thurstone developed multiple factor analysis and centroid extraction, proposed the seven primary mental abilities, and introduced simple structure and rotation. Raymond Cattell gave us the scree test, and applied factor analysis to build the 16PF. Henry Kaiser gave us the eigenvalue-greater-than-one rule, varimax rotation, and (with Meyer and Olkin) the KMO sampling adequacy measure.

The steps

  1. Check that the data are factorable. Bartlett's test of sphericity must be significant (it tests whether the correlation matrix is an identity matrix, and you need it not to be). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy should be at least .60, and preferably above .80. Note that Bartlett's test of sphericity here is a different test from Bartlett's test of homogeneity of variance, and papers exploit that.
  2. Extract the factors. Principal components analysis (PCA) analyses total variance and is technically a data-reduction method, not a factor model. Principal axis factoring (PAF) and maximum likelihood analyse only the shared (common) variance and are true factor analysis. Others: centroid (Thurstone), image, alpha factoring.
  3. Decide how many factors to retain. Kaiser criterion: retain factors with eigenvalue greater than 1.0 (tends to over-extract). Cattell's scree test: retain the factors above the elbow. Horn's parallel analysis: retain factors whose eigenvalues beat those from random data of the same size, which is the most accurate method. Or retain enough to explain a target percentage of variance (often 60% or more).
  4. Rotate to achieve simple structure (Thurstone): each variable loading strongly on one factor and near zero on the others, so the solution is interpretable. Rotation does not change the total variance explained, only how it is distributed among factors.
  5. Interpret and name the factors from the variables that load highly on them (a loading of .30 or .40 is the usual cut-off).

15.6 Standardisation and norms

Standardisation has two meanings, and papers use both. (1) Uniformity of procedure: identical instructions, materials, time limits and scoring for every test-taker, so that differences in scores reflect differences in the person and not in the administration. (2) Establishing norms: administering the test to a large, representative standardisation sample so that individual scores can be interpreted against the group.

Steps in constructing and standardising a test

  1. Define the construct and plan the test, producing a table of specifications (a blueprint of content areas by cognitive level).
  2. Write the item pool, usually two to three times more items than the final test needs.
  3. Expert review for content validity, clarity, and cultural or gender bias.
  4. Pilot / try-out on a sample resembling the target population.
  5. Item analysis: compute difficulty, discrimination and item-total correlations; run distractor analysis; drop and revise items.
  6. Assemble the final form, ordering items from easy to difficult and balancing content.
  7. Standardisation: administer to a large representative sample and develop norms.
  8. Establish reliability and validity on that data.
  9. Write the manual: administration, scoring, norms tables, reliability and validity evidence, and the limits of interpretation.

The Flynn effect and why norms expire. IQ scores in the standardisation population rose by roughly 3 points per decade through the twentieth century (James Flynn). Norms must be periodically re-established, which is why tests are revised (WAIS to WAIS-R to WAIS-III to WAIS-IV to WAIS-5). Naming the Flynn effect when asked why tests are restandardised is a strong answer.

How to attack this from here

  1. Learn section 1.2 (Stevens's scales) and section 16 (the decision tree) first, and learn them perfectly. Between them they answer more marks per minute of study than anything else in this book, because "identify the scale" and "which test would you use" are the two most repeated question forms across all these papers.
  2. Memorise the fixed numbers. 68 / 95 / 99.7 percent; 1.96 and 2.58; χ² critical of 3.84 at df 1; Cohen's .20 / .50 / .80 for d and .10 / .30 / .50 for r; η² at .01 / .06 / .14; alpha of .70 acceptable, .80 good, .90 for individual decisions; item discrimination D of .20 as the reject line and the 27% split. These get asked as bare recall.
  3. Drill the parametric-to-non-parametric mapping in section 14.3 until it is reflexive. It appears as a direct matching item in nearly every paper.
  4. Drill the assumption tables. The commonest ANOVA question is not how to compute F, it is which assumptions ANOVA rests on, which test checks each one, and what to do when one fails. Remember that for Levene, Shapiro–Wilk, Box's M, Mauchly and Hosmer–Lemeshow, a non-significant result is the good news.
  5. Learn the names. Section 17.3 is pure marks: every one of these papers asks who developed which test. Fisher, Pearson, Spearman, Gosset, Tukey, Levene, Mauchly, Wilks, Cronbach and Stevens are the ten that recur most.
  6. Do not skip section 15. Reliability, validity, item analysis and factor analysis carry as many marks as the whole inferential-testing block, and most candidates prepare them last and worst. The reliability-caps-validity-at-√rxx point, the SEM confidence band, and the EFA-versus-CFA distinction are all repeat offenders.
  7. Then sit the real papers. Work the statistics items in the IHBAS 2025, IHBAS 2024, RML and PYQ Real 2025 mocks, and any item you miss will map straight back to a numbered section above.
