North of Vancouver, in Canada, a footbridge of wooden planks and wire cable hangs across the Capilano Canyon. It is about 140 metres long and barely a metre and a half wide. It tilts and sways under every step, its cable handrails are low, and the rocks and rapids of the Capilano River lie some 70 metres below. Most people who cross it grip the cables and walk faster than they meant to. In 1974 two psychologists, Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron, decided that this frightening bridge would make an excellent laboratory.

Their procedure was simple. An attractive young woman stood on the bridge and stopped men aged roughly 18 to 35 who were crossing alone. She explained that she was doing a class project on the effects of scenic places on creativity, and asked each man to fill in a short questionnaire, which included writing a brief story about a picture of a woman. When he had finished, she tore a corner off a sheet of paper, wrote down her name and telephone number, and said that he was welcome to call her if he wanted to talk about the study. Exactly the same thing was done a little further up the river, on a second bridge: a solid cedar structure only about 3 metres above a shallow stream, with high handrails, that did not sway at all.

The men on the swaying bridge wrote stories with noticeably more sexual imagery than the men on the solid bridge. And more of them picked up the telephone: nine of the men interviewed on the suspension bridge called the woman, against only two from the solid bridge. When the interviewer was a man, almost nobody called from either bridge.

Why should a frightening bridge make a stranger more attractive? The men's hearts were pounding because of the bridge, yet at least some of them seem to have read that pounding as attraction to the woman. Think about what this implies. If the same racing heart can be "fear" on one reading and "attraction" on another, where exactly is an emotion? Is it in the body, in the brain, or in the story we tell ourselves about our body? That question has driven more than a century of research, and it leads directly to the second subject of this chapter: stress, which is what happens when the body's emergency system is switched on and then left on. Keep the men on the Capilano bridge in mind; you will meet them again at the end of the chapter.

11.1 What is an emotion?Medium yield · 4 Qs

Everyone knows what an emotion is until asked to define one. You know what it is like to be furious when a scooter cuts across you at a Mysuru signal, or to feel your stomach drop when the question paper is handed out. But what, exactly, has happened in each case? Psychologists answer by taking an emotion apart.

The three components of an emotion

An emotion is a relatively brief, whole-person response to an event that matters to you, made up of three components that normally occur together. The first is physiological arousal: the bodily changes, most of them controlled by the autonomic nervous system, such as a pounding heart, fast breathing, sweating palms and a dry mouth. The second is expressive behaviour: what can be seen and heard from outside, such as the face, the voice, posture and actions like running or striking. The third is conscious experience: the private, subjective awareness of the state ("I am afraid"), together with the thoughts that go with it. Many modern theorists add two further ingredients that are really part of the same package: an appraisal (an evaluation of what the event means for you) and an action tendency (an urge to do something, such as to flee, attack or approach).

Notice how well the three components map onto the theories you are about to meet. The great theoretical quarrels about emotion are mostly quarrels about the order and the causal links among these components. Does the body react first and the feeling follow? Do they occur together? Does a thought have to come first? Keep the three components in view and the theories will sort themselves out.

Emotion, feeling, mood, affect and temperament

Five words are used loosely in everyday speech but carefully in psychology, and examiners like to test the difference. A feeling is the subjective, consciously experienced part of an emotion: the "what it is like" component. An emotion is short (seconds to minutes) and is usually about something: you are angry at a person, afraid of a dog. A mood is longer, lower in intensity and more diffuse; it often has no clear object or cause, and it colours everything else. You can wake up irritable after a bad night and stay that way all day without being angry at anyone in particular.

The word affect has two uses. In research psychology it is the umbrella term for all states that feel good or bad, so emotions, moods and feelings are all forms of affect, and "positive affect" and "negative affect" describe your current feeling state as a whole. In clinical psychology and psychiatry, affect has a narrower meaning: the observable, moment-to-moment expression of emotion, as a clinician sees it in the face, voice and gestures during an interview. Finally, temperament is the biologically based, early-appearing and fairly stable individual difference in how easily a person becomes emotionally aroused and how well he or she calms down. A baby who startles at every noise and is hard to soothe differs in temperament from one who takes the world in her stride. (The famous temperament classification of Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess, easy, difficult and slow-to-warm-up, is taught in the Developmental book; the older "four temperaments" of Hippocrates and Galen, sanguine, choleric, melancholic and phlegmatic, belong to the history of personality theory.) Clinical link

In the mental status examination, clinicians record mood and affect separately. Mood is the patient's sustained, pervasive emotional state, usually in the patient's own words ("I've been low for weeks"); its key feature is its persistence. Affect is what the clinician observes now, and it is described by its quality (anxious, sad, cheerful), its range (full, restricted, blunted or flat), its reactivity or mobility (does it change appropriately as the topic changes, or is it fixed or labile?), its intensity, and whether it is congruent with the mood and appropriate to what is being said. A common teaching image: mood is the climate, affect is the weather.

Basic emotions: is there a short list?

If emotions are products of evolution, there might be a small set of basic emotions (also called primary emotions): emotions that are innate, appear early in life, are found in all cultures, have distinctive facial expressions, and serve a clear survival function. More complex emotions such as jealousy, pride or nostalgia would then be blends of these or would require more sophisticated thought. Several theorists have proposed such lists, and the lists are a favourite source of exam questions.

Silvan Solomon Tomkins (1911 to 1991), in his Affect Imagery Consciousness (first two volumes, 1962 and 1963), proposed an affect theory in which nine innate affects, each with its own facial pattern, are the primary motivators of behaviour: interest-excitement, enjoyment-joy, surprise-startle, distress-anguish, fear-terror, shame-humiliation, anger-rage, disgust and "dissmell" (the reaction to a bad smell). Tomkins argued that the face is the main site of emotion, an idea that led directly to the facial feedback hypothesis (Section 11.3). Two younger researchers whom he mentored and inspired, Ekman and Izard, carried the programme forward.

Paul Ekman (born 1934) and his colleague Wallace V. Friesen concluded from cross-cultural research in the late 1960s and early 1970s (Section 11.5) that six emotions have universally recognised facial expressions: happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust. In 1986 they added a seventh, contempt, whose expression is a one-sided tightening and raising of the lip corner. Carroll Ellis Izard (1923 to 2017) developed differential emotions theory (1971, 1977), which names ten fundamental emotions, each with its own feeling, expression and motivational function: interest, joy, surprise, sadness (distress), anger, disgust, contempt, fear, shame (shyness) and guilt. Izard also built coding systems for reading emotion in infants' faces.

Indian context

The idea of a short list of basic emotions is very old in India. The Natyashastra, the Sanskrit treatise on drama attributed to Bharata Muni (usually dated somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE), describes eight sthayibhavas (enduring emotional states), each of which an audience savours as a rasa: rati (love, giving the erotic rasa shringara), hasa (mirth), shoka (sorrow), krodha (anger), utsaha (energy or heroism), bhaya (fear), jugupsa (disgust) and vismaya (wonder or astonishment). The philosopher Abhinavagupta (about the tenth to eleventh century) argued for a ninth, shanta (peace). Notice how closely the list overlaps with Ekman's: anger, fear, disgust, sorrow, joy and surprise are all there, nearly two thousand years earlier.

Plutchik's wheel of emotions

Robert Plutchik (1927 to 2006) offered the most elaborate basic-emotion model in his psychoevolutionary theory of emotion (1980). He proposed eight primary emotions, each tied to an adaptive behaviour that helped our ancestors survive, arranged in a circle so that each emotion sits opposite its psychological opposite: joy opposite sadness, trust opposite disgust, fear opposite anger, and surprise opposite anticipation (Figure 11.1, right). Plutchik's famous three-dimensional "cone" adds intensity: each primary emotion has a milder and a stronger form, so that annoyance intensifies into anger and then rage (Table 11.3). Emotions next to each other mix to form primary dyads: joy and trust give love; trust and fear give submission; fear and surprise give awe; surprise and sadness give disapproval; sadness and disgust give remorse; disgust and anger give contempt; anger and anticipation give aggressiveness; and anticipation and joy give optimism. Plutchik compared emotions to colours: a few primaries, mixed in different proportions and intensities, produce the whole palette.

Dimensions instead of categories: Russell's circumplex

A rival tradition says that emotions are not separate boxes at all but points in a continuous space. The idea goes back to Wilhelm Wundt (1832 to 1920), whose tridimensional theory of feeling described every feeling by its position on three dimensions: pleasure-displeasure, tension-relaxation and excitement-depression (Chapter 1). The modern version is the circumplex model of James A. Russell (1980). Russell asked people to judge how similar emotion words were and found that the words fell in a circle defined by just two dimensions: valence (how pleasant or unpleasant the state is) and arousal (how activated or calm it is). "Excited" is pleasant and high in arousal; "calm" is pleasant and low; "afraid" is unpleasant and high; "bored" and "sad" are unpleasant and low (Figure 11.1, left). Russell later called the blend of valence and arousal you are in at any moment core affect. A closely related scheme, from David Watson and Auke Tellegen (1985), rotates the same circle by 45 degrees and describes it with two independent axes, positive affect and negative affect, the basis of the widely used PANAS scale (Psychological Assessment book).

Which view is right, categories or dimensions? Probably both capture something. Basic-emotion theorists point to universal expressions and to brain circuits such as the fear system of the amygdala (Section 11.4). Dimensional theorists point out that in everyday life emotions blend and shade into one another, and that brain imaging has not found one neat "spot" for each emotion word. The most radical dimensional view is the theory of constructed emotion of Lisa Feldman Barrett (set out in her 2017 book How Emotions Are Made). Barrett argues that the brain continually produces core affect, and that we construct specific emotions such as "anger" by categorising that core affect with concepts learned from our language and culture. On this view "fear" is less like a reflex and more like a word we have learned to apply, which is why the same pounding heart could be called fear on one bridge and attraction on another.

Exam trap

Jealousy, shame, guilt, pride, love and envy are not on Ekman's list. Ekman's six are happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust; contempt was added as a seventh in 1986. Jealousy is a complex, social emotion (a blend of fear, anger and sadness about losing a relationship to a rival). Shame and guilt are on Izard's list of ten, not Ekman's. Trust and anticipation appear only in Plutchik's eight. "Dissmell" is Tomkins's.

Do not mix up the two-dimension schemes. Russell's circumplex uses valence by arousal; Watson and Tellegen use positive affect by negative affect; Wundt's older theory has three dimensions (pleasure-displeasure, tension-relaxation, excitement-depression).

11.2 The classic theories: from the body to the labelMedium yield · 6 Qs

Now return to the three components: arousal, expression and experience. Common sense has a firm view of how they are related. You see a snake on a village path, you feel afraid, and because you feel afraid your heart races and you jump back. Feeling comes first and the body follows. The first scientific theory of emotion turned this common sense on its head.

The James-Lange theory: we feel afraid because we tremble

In 1884 the American psychologist and philosopher William James (1842 to 1910) published an article in the journal Mind with the deceptively simple title "What is an emotion?". A year later, in 1885, the Danish physician Carl Georg Lange (1834 to 1900) independently published a very similar account, so the theory carries both names. James put it memorably in his Principles of Psychology (1890): common sense says that we lose our fortune, are sorry and weep; we meet a bear, are frightened and run. James argued that this order is wrong: "the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and ... our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion." In his most quoted line, "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble."

The James-Lange theory, then, says that an emotion-provoking stimulus first triggers bodily responses, autonomic changes in the viscera and actions of the skeletal muscles, and that the emotion we feel is simply our perception of those bodily changes. Take away the racing heart, the trembling and the running, James said, and nothing would be left of fear but a "cold and neutral state of intellectual perception." Lange put more weight on changes in the blood vessels; James included all bodily changes. Notice what the theory implies: if each emotion is the feeling of a particular bodily state, then different emotions must have different bodily patterns. Fear must feel like fear because its pattern of arousal differs from that of anger.

Textbooks often draw the James-Lange sequence as stimulus, then bodily arousal, then awareness or "interpretation" of the arousal, then emotion (Figure 11.2, top row). The "interpretation" in this diagram is not a reasoned judgement about the situation. It is just the brain noticing what the body is doing: "my heart is pounding and my knees are shaking." The feeling of those changes is the fear.

Is there evidence for James? Some. In 1966 the psychologist George W. Hohmann interviewed men with spinal cord injuries and reported that those with higher injuries, who received less feedback from the body, described their anger and fear as less intense than before. Later and better-controlled studies, however, found that people with severe spinal injuries still experience a full range of emotions, with at most some dampening of fear and anger. In 1983 Paul Ekman, Robert W. Levenson and Friesen found modest differences in autonomic patterns between emotions: anger and fear both raised heart rate, but anger raised finger temperature while fear lowered it. The modern verdict is that bodily feedback is not necessary for emotion but can colour and intensify it, a point you will meet again in the facial feedback hypothesis.

Cannon's critique and the Cannon-Bard theory

The most powerful attack on James came from the Harvard physiologist Walter Bradford Cannon (1871 to 1945), the man who gave us the phrase "fight or flight" (Section 11.8). In a famous paper of 1927, Cannon listed five objections to the James-Lange theory:

  1. Cutting the body off from the brain does not abolish emotion. Animals whose viscera had been surgically separated from the central nervous system (Cannon's own sympathectomised cats, and dogs studied earlier by the physiologist Charles Sherrington) still showed rage, fear and pleasure in appropriate situations.
  2. The same visceral changes occur in very different emotions and in non-emotional states. A racing heart, faster breathing and sweating accompany fear, anger and excitement alike, and also fever, cold and exercise. Such a general pattern cannot tell fear from anger.
  3. The viscera are relatively insensitive. The internal organs have few sensory nerve fibres, so the brain receives only vague information about them, too vague to support fine distinctions among emotions.
  4. Visceral changes are too slow. Autonomic responses take a second or more to develop, but we often feel an emotion almost instantly.
  5. Producing the bodily changes artificially does not produce the emotion. When the Spanish physician Gregorio Marañón injected 210 people with adrenaline in 1924, about 71 percent reported only bodily symptoms and no emotion at all; the rest described a cold, "as if" state ("I feel as if I were afraid"), but not genuine fear.

In place of James's theory Cannon proposed that an emotional stimulus is processed in the thalamus, which then sends signals simultaneously in two directions: up to the cerebral cortex, producing the conscious experience of emotion, and down to the body through the hypothalamus and autonomic nervous system, producing the physiological arousal. His student and collaborator Philip Bard (1898 to 1977) extended the theory and showed that the hypothalamus is critical for the bodily expression of rage (Section 11.4). The Cannon-Bard theory (also called the thalamic theory) therefore holds that the feeling of emotion and the bodily reaction occur at the same time and independently; neither causes the other (Figure 11.2, second row). You see the snake, and your fear and your pounding heart arise together.

The Cannon-Bard theory, too, turned out to be only partly right. Its anatomy was wrong in detail (the thalamus is a relay, not the seat of emotion), and the facial feedback and misattribution findings you are about to meet show that bodily states do influence feelings, which the theory denies. But Cannon's criticisms were valuable. They forced the next generation to ask how a single, rather general bodily arousal could give rise to many different emotions. The answer that came next was: through thinking.

Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory

In 1962 Stanley Schachter (1922 to 1997) and Jerome E. Singer (1934 to 2010) proposed a theory that took something from both sides. Like Cannon, they accepted that physiological arousal is much the same from one emotion to another. Like James, they accepted that arousal matters. Their two-factor theory of emotion (also called the cognitive labelling or cognitive arousal theory) says that an emotion requires two ingredients: physiological arousal, which determines how intense the emotion is, and a cognitive label, an explanation of the arousal drawn from the situation, which determines which emotion it is. When you notice that you are aroused, you look around for a reason; if the reason you find is a growling dog, the arousal becomes fear; if it is a person you like, the same arousal becomes attraction. Students sometimes remember it as the "jukebox" theory: arousal is the coin that makes the machine play, and the situation selects the song.

Key study: Schachter and Singer's adrenaline experiment (1962)

Question. If people are physiologically aroused but have no explanation for it, will they label the arousal according to the emotional cues around them? Method. Male university students were told the study tested the effects of a vitamin compound, "Suproxin", on vision. Most actually received an injection of adrenaline (epinephrine), which produces a racing heart, trembling hands and a flushed face; a placebo group received saline. The adrenaline groups were told different things: the informed group was correctly told what symptoms to expect; the ignorant group was told nothing; the misinformed group was told to expect irrelevant symptoms such as numb feet and itching. Each participant then waited with a stooge who was supposedly another participant. In the euphoria condition the stooge played paper basketball, flew paper aeroplanes and twirled a hula hoop; in the anger condition the stooge complained bitterly about an insulting questionnaire that both had to fill in (one item asked how many men, other than his father, the participant's mother had had extramarital affairs with) and finally tore it up and stormed out.

Results. Participants who were aroused but had no correct explanation for their arousal (the ignorant and misinformed groups) caught the stooge's mood more: they behaved more euphorically and reported feeling happier in the euphoria condition, and acted angrier in the anger condition, than the informed participants, who could put their symptoms down to the injection. Conclusion. Unexplained arousal is labelled using situational cues. Criticisms. The effects were small and some were not statistically reliable; the placebo group did not differ as much from the adrenaline groups as the theory predicted; and later attempts to repeat the study, by Gary Marshall and Philip Zimbardo (1979) and by Christina Maslach (1979), found that unexplained arousal tended to be felt as unpleasant and anxious whatever the stooge did. A review by Rainer Reisenzein (1983) concluded that arousal can intensify emotions but that there is little evidence that it is necessary for them. Table 11.4 summarises the logic of the design.

Misattribution of arousal and excitation transfer

If arousal is labelled from the situation, it can be labelled wrongly. Misattribution of arousal occurs when people mistake the true source of their physiological arousal and attribute it to something else that happens to be present. The Capilano bridge study of Donald G. Dutton and Arthur P. Aron (1974), described at the start of this chapter, is the classic demonstration: arousal produced by fear of the bridge was misattributed to attraction to the interviewer. A related experiment by Stuart Valins (1966) showed that even the belief that one is aroused can be enough. Men looked at photographs of women while hearing what they were told was their own heartbeat; when the false heartbeat sped up for certain photographs, the men later rated those women as more attractive.

Dolf Zillmann, from the early 1970s onward, developed excitation transfer theory, which explains why arousal from one situation can spill over into the next. Physiological arousal dies away slowly, more slowly than our awareness of its cause. So for a while after one arousing event, residual arousal remains, and if a new emotional event occurs in that window, the leftover arousal is added to the new emotion and intensifies it. In Zillmann's experiments, people who had just pedalled hard on an exercise bicycle and were then provoked retaliated more aggressively than rested people, especially once enough time had passed that they no longer connected their arousal with the exercise. Everyday examples are easy to find: the near miss in traffic that makes you snap at the shopkeeper a few minutes later, or the post-match crowd that turns a minor argument into a fight. Excitation transfer is also the bridge between this chapter and the study of aggression in the Social book.

Exam trap

Match the theory to its sequence. Commonsense: stimulus, emotion, then arousal. James-Lange: stimulus, arousal, then emotion (the emotion is the perceived bodily change). Cannon-Bard: stimulus, then arousal and emotion simultaneously and independently, via the thalamus. Schachter-Singer: arousal plus a cognitive label. Lazarus: appraisal first (Section 11.3). An option such as "emotions and visceral reactions are simultaneous" describes Cannon-Bard, not James-Lange; "you feel the emotion and then the body responds" is the commonsense view that James rejected.

Misattribution of arousal is explained by the two-factor theory, not by James-Lange, Cannon-Bard or facial feedback. What makes Schachter and Singer different from both earlier theories is that their theory includes cognition and arousal.

"Two-factor theory" is a name with four owners. Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory of emotion (1962); Spearman's two-factor theory of intelligence (g and s, 1904; Chapter 9); Mowrer's two-factor theory of avoidance learning (classical plus operant conditioning; Chapter 6); and Herzberg's two-factor (motivation-hygiene) theory of job satisfaction (Chapter 10). Read the stem for the subject.

Cannon-Bard named the thalamus as the relay; the hypothalamus came in through Bard's later work on sham rage. If an option says "the somatic nervous system is the seat of emotion", reject it: no classic theory says so.

11.3 Appraisal, feedback, evolution and opposing processesLower yield · not asked yet

Schachter and Singer brought thinking into the theory of emotion, but in their account thought arrives after arousal, to explain it. The next step was to ask whether thought comes first: whether we become aroused at all only because we have judged that something matters.

Cognitive appraisal: Arnold and Lazarus

The word appraisal was introduced into emotion theory by Magda B. Arnold (1903 to 2002) in her book Emotion and Personality (1960). Arnold defined appraisal as an immediate, intuitive evaluation of whether something is good or bad for me, and argued that this evaluation is what turns a perception into an emotion: the snake frightens you only once you have appraised it as dangerous. The idea was developed into the most influential cognitive theory of emotion and stress by the American psychologist Richard S. Lazarus (1922 to 2002), first in Psychological Stress and the Coping Process (1966) and later in Emotion and Adaptation (1991).

According to Lazarus's cognitive appraisal theory, emotions are produced by the way a person evaluates a situation in relation to his or her own goals and well-being. Lazarus distinguished two kinds of appraisal. In primary appraisal you ask, "Does this matter to me, and how?" The answer may be that the event is irrelevant, that it is benign or positive, or that it is stressful; and a stressful event may be appraised as harm or loss (damage already done), threat (harm expected in the future) or challenge (an opportunity to gain or grow). In secondary appraisal you ask, "What can I do about it?": you evaluate your resources and options for coping. Because situations and resources change, appraisal is continually revised; Lazarus called this reappraisal. The specific emotion depends on the pattern of appraisal. Lazarus summed up each emotion as a "core relational theme": anger is the appraisal of a demeaning offence against me and mine; anxiety is facing an uncertain, existential threat; guilt is having broken a moral rule; sadness is having suffered an irrevocable loss.

Because the appraisal stands between the stimulus and the response, Lazarus's account is often called the cognitive-mediational theory of emotion: stimulus, then appraisal, then emotion together with its bodily arousal (Figure 11.2, bottom row). The same theory reappears, with only small changes, as Lazarus and Folkman's transactional model of stress in Section 11.9, which is why the terms primary and secondary appraisal are examined under both emotion and stress.

Key study: the subincision film (Speisman, Lazarus and colleagues, 1964)

Question. Can changing how people appraise an event change their emotional and bodily response to it? Method. Joseph Speisman, Lazarus and their colleagues showed participants a distressing anthropological film of a painful initiation rite (subincision of the penis) performed on adolescent boys in an Aboriginal Australian community, while recording skin conductance and heart rate. The film was shown with one of four soundtracks: a trauma track that emphasised the pain and mutilation; a denial track that described the boys as willing and the occasion as happy; an intellectualisation track that took a detached, anthropological view; or no soundtrack. Results. Physiological arousal was highest with the trauma track and lowest with the denial and intellectualisation tracks. Conclusion. The same images produced different emotional responses depending on how viewers were led to appraise them. This is the experimental heart of appraisal theory, and it is also the ancestor of the "reappraisal" strategy of emotion regulation (Section 11.7).

Does feeling need thinking? The Lazarus-Zajonc debate

Not everyone accepted that appraisal must come first. In 1980 the social psychologist Robert B. Zajonc (1923 to 2008) published a paper with the provocative title "Feeling and thinking: Preferences need no inferences." Zajonc argued for affective primacy: affective reactions can occur before, and independently of, any cognitive processing, and are sometimes faster than it. His main evidence came from the mere exposure effect, which he had described in 1968: simply being exposed to a stimulus repeatedly makes people like it more, whether it is a nonsense word, a Chinese character or a face. In a striking version, William Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc (1980) flashed irregular octagons so briefly that participants later could not recognise them better than chance; yet when asked which of two shapes they liked, they chose the previously seen one about 60 percent of the time. Liking had formed without recognition.

Lazarus replied in 1982 and again in 1984 ("On the primacy of cognition"), and Zajonc answered in the same year ("On the primacy of affect"), both in the journal American Psychologist. Much of the disagreement turned out to be about definitions. Lazarus insisted that appraisal need not be conscious or deliberate; a fast, automatic evaluation counts as cognition. Zajonc meant by cognition something closer to conscious, deliberate processing. Joseph LeDoux's discovery of a fast subcortical route to the amygdala (Section 11.4) later gave both sides something: there is indeed a rapid emotional evaluation that bypasses the cortex, but it is itself a form of information processing. The modern consensus is that some emotional reactions are quick and automatic while others depend on slower, more elaborate thought.

The facial feedback hypothesis

Is there anything in the old advice to "put on a happy face"? Charles Darwin thought so, writing in 1872 that "the free expression by outward signs of an emotion intensifies it," while suppressing the outward signs softens it. Tomkins later made the face the centre of his theory of affect, and Izard developed the idea further. The facial feedback hypothesis states that feedback from our own facial expressions influences our emotional experience. In its strong form, the face is necessary to produce emotion; in its weak form, which is the version with evidence behind it, facial expression modulates (strengthens or weakens) emotions that are already under way. You can see that this is a modern descendant of the James-Lange theory, with the face taking the place of the viscera.

The best-known test came from Fritz Strack, Leonard Martin and Sabine Stepper (1988). To prevent participants from realising that their faces were being manipulated, they were told the study concerned how people with disabilities might write by holding a pen in the mouth. Some held the pen between their teeth, which forces the muscles of a smile; others held it with their lips, which makes smiling impossible and produces something closer to a pout. Those holding the pen in their teeth rated cartoons as funnier. Other studies found similar effects: people who are made to frown while viewing slides report more anger, and people who hold a smile report more happiness.

The story has a modern twist that examiners at postgraduate level increasingly expect you to know. In 2016 a large, pre-registered replication of the pen study, run in 17 laboratories, found no effect at all. Later work suggested that the effect may depend on details such as whether participants know they are being video-recorded, and a 2019 meta-analysis of many facial feedback studies concluded that the effect is real but small. The fair summary is that facial feedback can nudge emotions but is not a powerful cause of them.

Clinical link

If frowning feeds sadness, could stopping people from frowning help depression? Several small randomised trials have injected botulinum toxin into the frown muscles between the eyebrows of patients with depression and found improvements in mood compared with placebo injections. The results are intriguing but preliminary. The same logic underlies simpler techniques: a therapist may coach a socially anxious client to adopt an open posture and a relaxed face, on the reasoning that expression and feeling influence each other in both directions.

The evolutionary view: Darwin's legacy

The first major work on the science of emotion was not by a psychologist at all. In 1872 Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882) published The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, in which he argued that emotional expressions are products of evolution, shared across human cultures and continuous with those of other animals. A snarling dog and a sneering man both bare the teeth. To explain expressions, Darwin proposed three principles. The principle of serviceable associated habits holds that expressions began as useful actions: the raised upper lip of disgust and the narrowed nostrils once helped reject a bad smell or taste. The principle of antithesis holds that opposite emotional states are signalled by opposite postures: a dog threatening attack stands tall with ears forward, hair and tail raised, whereas a submissive dog crouches with ears back and tail down, so that the signals cannot be confused. The principle of the direct action of the nervous system holds that some expressions, such as trembling with fear, are simply overflows of nervous excitation.

The evolutionary view of emotion that grew from Darwin's book treats emotions as adaptations: packages of physiology, expression and action that solved recurring survival problems. Fear prepares escape from predators, disgust protects against contamination and disease, anger mobilises the defence of resources, and expressions communicate our state to others so that they can respond. Plutchik's psychoevolutionary theory (Table 11.3) and Ekman's work on universal expressions (Section 11.5) are both direct descendants of Darwin's approach.

Opponent-process theory: why pleasures fade and pains turn sweet

Why does a first-time parachutist feel terror before the jump and an elated high afterwards, while a veteran parachutist feels only mild tension before and a long glow of exhilaration after? Richard L. Solomon (1918 to 1995) and John D. Corbit answered with the opponent-process theory of emotion and acquired motivation (1974). They proposed that any strong emotional reaction (the a-process) automatically triggers an opposing reaction (the b-process) that restores emotional balance. The a-process is quick and closely tied to the stimulus. The b-process is sluggish: it starts late, builds slowly and outlasts the stimulus, so when the stimulus ends we are left with an after-reaction opposite to the original emotion. Crucially, the b-process grows stronger with repeated exposure, while the a-process stays the same.

This simple machinery explains a great deal. With repetition the strengthened b-process cancels more of the a-process, so the initial reaction weakens (the drug user's high fades, a sign of tolerance), while the after-reaction grows (the drug user's craving and misery when the drug wears off, a sign of withdrawal). For painful experiences the pattern is reversed: the fear of the parachute jump weakens with practice, and the pleasant after-reaction, relief and exhilaration, grows until it becomes the reason for jumping. Solomon used the same idea to explain why separation from a loved one becomes more painful as a relationship matures. Note that this is a different theory from Ewald Hering's opponent-process theory of colour vision (Chapter 3); only the name is shared. The motivational side of Solomon's theory, including addiction, is taken up in Chapter 10.

Theories of emotion make claims about the body and the brain, so it is time to look at them directly. This section gives you the essentials; the full anatomy is in the Physiological book.

The autonomic nervous system

The bodily side of emotion is run mainly by the autonomic nervous system (ANS), the part of the peripheral nervous system that controls the heart, blood vessels, glands and internal organs without our deliberate control. It has two divisions that usually work in opposition. The sympathetic nervous system prepares the body for action: it is the "fight or flight" division, most active in fear, anger and excitement. The parasympathetic nervous system conserves and restores energy: it is the "rest and digest" division, dominant when you are calm and after a meal. Table 11.5 lists the main effects.

Where are the bodily responses actually controlled? The vital, involuntary functions of heartbeat, blood pressure and breathing are paced by centres in the medulla oblongata, the lowest part of the brainstem, which belongs to the hindbrain together with the pons and cerebellum. The hypothalamus, higher up, acts as the head ganglion of the ANS: during emotion it sends commands down to these brainstem centres and to the spinal cord. So the hindbrain carries out the adjustments and the hypothalamus orchestrates them.

The hypothalamus and sham rage

In the late 1920s Philip Bard, Cannon's student, removed the cerebral cortex of cats. The decorticate cats reacted to the slightest touch with a full display of rage: arched back, erect hair, hissing, bared teeth, lashing tail and dilated pupils. But the rage was oddly empty. It was out of all proportion to the provocation, it was not directed at the source of the touch, and it stopped the moment the stimulus stopped. Bard called it sham rage. Sham rage could still be produced when the brain was removed down to, but not including, the hypothalamus; once the hypothalamus was also removed, it disappeared. Bard concluded that the hypothalamus organises the expression of rage, and that a normal function of the cortex is to inhibit and direct it.

At about the same time, in Zurich, the Swiss physiologist Walter Rudolf Hess (1881 to 1973) implanted electrodes in the hypothalamus of freely moving cats and found that stimulating certain points produced an attack-like defensive reaction, with hissing, raised hair and dilated pupils, while other points produced calm and sleepiness. Hess shared the 1949 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this mapping of the brain's control of the internal organs.

The Papez circuit and the limbic system

Where, then, does the experience of emotion arise? In 1937 the American neuroanatomist James Papez (1883 to 1958; pronounced "payps") proposed that emotion depends on a loop of interconnected structures on the inner surface of the brain. The Papez circuit runs from the hippocampus through the fornix to the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus, then by the mammillothalamic tract to the anterior nuclei of the thalamus, then to the cingulate gyrus (cingulate cortex), and back through the parahippocampal region (entorhinal cortex) to the hippocampus. Papez suggested that emotional expression depends on this circuit's action on the hypothalamus, and emotional experience on its action on the cortex.

The French surgeon Paul Broca (1824 to 1880) had already, in 1878, named the ring of cortex bordering the brainstem the "great limbic lobe" (from the Latin limbus, border). In 1949 the American physician Paul D. MacLean (1913 to 2007) called these structures the "visceral brain", and in 1952 he renamed them the limbic system, enlarging Papez's circuit by adding the amygdala, septum, hypothalamus and parts of the prefrontal cortex. MacLean's limbic system theory, which made the limbic system "the seat of emotion", became the textbook view for half a century. MacLean later placed it within his triune brain theory, in which the human brain contains three layers laid down in evolution: a reptilian complex (basal ganglia and brainstem) for instinctive routines, a paleomammalian brain (the limbic system) for emotion, and a neomammalian brain (the neocortex) for reason.

Two corrections are needed. First, the Papez circuit is now known to matter more for memory than for emotion: damage to the hippocampus, fornix or mammillary bodies (as in Korsakoff's syndrome) produces amnesia rather than emotional change. Second, the triune brain is an outdated evolutionary story; brains did not evolve by adding new layers on top of old ones, and the limbic system is not a sharply bounded unit. But the core point survives: structures such as the amygdala, hypothalamus and cingulate cortex are central to emotion, motivation and emotional memory.

The amygdala: Klüver-Bucy syndrome and emotional memory

In 1937, and in full in 1939, the psychologist Heinrich Klüver (1897 to 1979) and the neurosurgeon Paul Bucy (1904 to 1992) described a remarkable change in rhesus monkeys after they removed both anterior temporal lobes. (Klüver had been studying the hallucinogen mescaline, and the surgery began as a test of whether the temporal lobes were involved in its effects.) Monkeys that had been wild and aggressive became tame and placid. They showed no fear of humans or of snakes, which normal monkeys find terrifying. They examined every object by putting it in their mouths, ate things they would normally reject, including meat and inedible objects, attended compulsively to every object in view, and directed sexual behaviour at inappropriate partners and objects. Although they could see, they seemed not to recognise what objects were or meant.

This pattern is now called Klüver-Bucy syndrome. Its features are best remembered as a list: psychic blindness or visual agnosia (seeing without recognising); hyperorality (examining things by mouth); hypermetamorphosis (a compulsion to attend to and touch every visual stimulus); dietary change and hyperphagia (overeating, eating inedible things); hypersexuality; and placidity, that is, loss of fear and anger. Later lesion studies traced the emotional part of the syndrome, the taming and loss of fear, to bilateral damage to the amygdala, an almond-shaped cluster of nuclei deep in each temporal lobe. In humans the syndrome is rare; it follows bilateral temporal damage from herpes simplex encephalitis, head injury or some dementias, and because the surrounding temporal cortex is usually damaged too, human patients often also show amnesia and aphasia (difficulty understanding or producing language).

Human cases make the amygdala's role in fear vivid. A woman known as S.M., whose amygdalae were destroyed on both sides by a rare genetic disorder (Urbach-Wiethe disease), cannot recognise fear in other people's faces, and when researchers at the University of Iowa took her to an exotic pet shop and a "haunted house" she showed curiosity but no fear at all. The amygdala is also the brain's tool for making emotional events memorable. When something arousing happens, stress hormones (adrenaline and cortisol) and the amygdala's own activity strengthen the consolidation of the memory by the hippocampus. In one well-known experiment by Larry Cahill, James L. McGaugh and colleagues (1994), people heard either an emotional or a neutral version of a story; the emotional version was remembered better, unless participants had taken propranolol, a drug that blocks noradrenaline receptors, in which case the memory advantage disappeared. This emotional modulation of memory explains why you remember the day of a frightening accident in vivid detail (and why "flashbulb memories", Chapter 7, feel so clear).

Key study: a double dissociation of emotional and factual memory (Bechara and colleagues, 1995)

Antoine Bechara, Antonio Damasio and their colleagues gave a fear-conditioning task to three patients: S.M., with bilateral amygdala damage; a patient with bilateral hippocampal damage; and a patient with damage to both. A coloured slide (the conditioned stimulus) was paired with a loud, startling boat horn, and the skin conductance response was recorded. S.M. never developed a conditioned skin conductance response to the slide, yet she could tell the researchers exactly which colour had been followed by the horn. The hippocampal patient showed the opposite: a normal conditioned skin conductance response, but no conscious memory of which colour went with the horn. The patient with both lesions showed neither. The amygdala is needed for emotional (conditioned) memory, the hippocampus for declarative memory of the facts.

LeDoux's two roads to fear

How does the amygdala get its information so quickly? Joseph LeDoux (born 1949), working with rats in auditory fear conditioning (a tone paired with a mild shock), traced the pathways in detail and described them for a general audience in The Emotional Brain (1996). Sounds reach the auditory part of the thalamus (the medial geniculate nucleus). LeDoux found that lesions of the auditory thalamus or of the amygdala blocked fear conditioning to a tone, but lesions of the auditory cortex did not. The reason is that information travels from the thalamus to the amygdala by two routes. The low road runs directly from the sensory thalamus to the amygdala. It is fast (in rats, a matter of milliseconds) but crude, "quick and dirty" in LeDoux's phrase: it can tell that something is sudden or snake-like, but not what it is. The high road runs from the thalamus to the sensory cortex and only then to the amygdala. It is slower but detailed, able to identify the object and its context (Figure 11.3). The amygdala then triggers the fear response through its outputs: to the periaqueductal grey of the midbrain for freezing, to the lateral hypothalamus for sympathetic arousal, and to the paraventricular hypothalamus for the stress hormone response.

The two-road model explains a familiar experience. Walking through a forest you freeze at a long, curved shape on the path, and only a moment later realise it is a stick. The freezing came from the low road; the correction came from the high road. It also explains why phobias and traumatic fears can be so resistant to reasoning: the subcortical route can trigger fear without consulting the cortex, which "knows" that the spider is harmless. A striking related phenomenon is affective blindsight. Beatrice de Gelder and colleagues (1999) studied a patient, G.Y., whose primary visual cortex was damaged on one side, leaving him consciously blind in half of his visual field. When happy, sad, angry or fearful faces were presented in the blind half, he could still guess their emotional expression well above chance, presumably through subcortical routes to the amygdala, although he reported seeing nothing.

The prefrontal cortex and the two hemispheres

The amygdala raises the alarm; the prefrontal cortex helps decide what to do about it. The classic case is that of Phineas Gage (1823 to 1860), a responsible and well-liked railway construction foreman in Vermont who, in 1848, was tamping explosive into a rock with an iron rod about a metre long when the charge exploded and drove the rod up through his left cheek and out of the top of his skull. Gage survived and kept his intelligence, but he became impulsive, profane and unreliable, so much so that his friends said he was "no longer Gage," as his physician John Martyn Harlow reported in 1868. A computer reconstruction of his skull in 1994 by Hanna Damasio and colleagues placed the damage in the medial (ventromedial) prefrontal cortex of both hemispheres.

Antonio Damasio (born 1944) studied modern patients with similar damage, such as a man he called Elliot, whose reasoning and IQ were intact but whose personal and financial decisions became disastrous. In Descartes' Error (1994), Damasio proposed the somatic marker hypothesis: through experience, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex attaches bodily feelings ("somatic markers", a gut feeling of unease) to the likely outcomes of choices, and these markers steer decisions quickly, often before we can say why. In the Iowa Gambling Task, normal participants begin to show skin conductance responses before choosing from risky card decks, and soon avoid them; patients with ventromedial damage show no such anticipatory responses and keep choosing the risky decks. Emotion, on this view, is not the enemy of good decisions but a necessary part of them.

The two cerebral hemispheres also differ in emotion. The older right-hemisphere hypothesis held that the right hemisphere is specialised for all emotion; the valence hypothesis holds that the right hemisphere is specialised for negative emotions and the left for positive ones. Richard Davidson's EEG studies support a related approach-withdrawal model: greater left frontal activity accompanies approach-related, mostly positive emotion, and greater right frontal activity accompanies withdrawal-related, mostly negative emotion, and people prone to depression tend to show relatively low left frontal activity. Facial expressions are usually stronger on the left side of the face, which is controlled mainly by the right hemisphere.

Clinical link

Emotional expression and emotional experience can come apart after brain damage. In pseudobulbar affect (pathological laughing and crying), patients with stroke, multiple sclerosis, motor neurone disease (ALS) or traumatic brain injury have sudden, brief, uncontrollable outbursts of crying or laughing that do not match how they actually feel. Damage to the pathways by which the cortex normally controls the brainstem's emotional-motor centres "releases" the expression from the feeling, much as in Bard's decorticate cats. It is not depression, and it should not be mistaken for it.

Lie detection: the polygraph and the guilty knowledge test

If lying makes people anxious, and anxiety shows in the body, then measuring the body should reveal lies. That is the logic of the polygraph (literally "many writings"), commonly called the lie detector, which records several measures of autonomic arousal at once: heart rate, blood pressure, breathing and skin conductance. The usual procedure is the control question technique. The examiner compares the suspect's responses to relevant questions ("Did you take the money from the office?") with responses to control questions designed to make almost anyone slightly uncomfortable ("Before the age of 25, did you ever take something that did not belong to you?"). A bigger response to the relevant questions is taken as a sign of deception.

The problem is that the polygraph detects arousal, not lies. An innocent suspect accused of a crime is likely to react strongly to the relevant question simply out of fear of being disbelieved, so innocent people are often wrongly judged deceptive. Guilty people can learn countermeasures, such as biting the tongue or pressing the toes to the floor during control questions, which raise their arousal and mask the difference. Reviews of real criminal cases estimate the accuracy of the control question technique at not much better than chance, and a major review by the US National Research Council in 2003 concluded that polygraph testing is too inaccurate to be relied on for security screening.

A better method changes the question. The guilty knowledge test (also called the concealed information test) does not try to catch a lie. Instead it presents details of the crime that only the guilty person would know, mixed with equally plausible false details, and looks for a consistent response to the true ones. In the classic study by David T. Lykken (1959), volunteers carried out a mock theft of a purse from an office. They were then asked, for example, "Where did we find the purse? In the washroom? In a locker? Hanging on a coat rack?" Using skin conductance alone, 88 percent of the mock criminals were correctly identified, and none of the innocent participants was judged guilty. Innocent people react to all the alternatives alike, because to them all are equally meaningless.

Indian context

Indian investigators have used polygraph tests, narco-analysis (questioning under a sedative drug) and brain-based tests such as the brain electrical activation profile. In Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010), the Supreme Court of India held that administering these tests to a person without consent violates the right against self-incrimination under Article 20(3) of the Constitution and personal liberty under Article 21. They may be given only with the person's consent, and even then the results themselves are not admissible as evidence, although facts later discovered with their help may be. The judgement drew directly on the scientific doubts about such tests summarised above.

Exam trap

The limbic system is the classic "seat of emotion", so when the options are "limbic system" versus one of its parts (hippocampus, thalamus, hypothalamus), choose the limbic system for a general question about emotional behaviour. Reject the reticular formation (arousal, wakefulness and attention), the pituitary (the "master" endocrine gland), the cerebellum (coordination and balance) and the corpus callosum (connects the hemispheres). Technically the limbic system is part of the forebrain, but "forebrain" is too broad to be the keyed answer.

Amygdala, not hippocampus, for fear, aggression, threat detection, fear conditioning and the emotional enhancement of memory. The hippocampus stores the facts; the amygdala adds the emotional boost. The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the circadian clock, not an emotion structure.

Lesion effects that are easy to swap. Bilateral amygdala (temporal lobe) removal produces Klüver-Bucy tameness, hyperorality and hypersexuality, not fear. Intense rage, attack posture and raised hair come from removing the cortex (sham rage) or stimulating the hypothalamus. Overeating and obesity come from damage to the ventromedial hypothalamus (Chapter 10). Severe anterograde amnesia "as in H.M." comes from the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe (Chapter 7). Cerebellar damage disturbs balance and coordination, and caudate nucleus (basal ganglia) damage disturbs movement and habit learning; neither produces Klüver-Bucy syndrome. A Klüver-Bucy option that includes "hyperalertness" or "avolition and alogia" is wrong: placidity, not alertness, and the "A" symptoms are schizophrenia's.

Sympathetic arousal dilates the pupils. In an "odd one out" list of sympathetic effects (raised heart rate, lowered skin resistance, faster breathing, pupil constriction), the odd one is pupil constriction, which is parasympathetic.

Heartbeat and breathing are paced by the hindbrain (medulla); the hypothalamus regulates them indirectly. If the question asks which division "modulates" involuntary heartbeat and respiration and offers hindbrain, that is the keyed answer.

Papez circuit order: hippocampus, fornix, mammillary bodies, (mammillothalamic tract), anterior thalamus, cingulate gyrus, entorhinal cortex, back to hippocampus. The amygdala is not in Papez's original loop (some textbooks, Pinel and Barnes among them, draw it with the neighbouring structures); MacLean added it to the limbic system.

Limbic system versus hypothalamus. When a question asks for the function of the limbic system, the keyed answer is the regulation of emotion (with motivation and memory as runners-up). Hunger, thirst and temperature are the classic functions of the hypothalamus, so an option such as "both emotion and hunger and thirst" was not keyed (Amity 2023), even though MacLean counted the hypothalamus as part of the limbic system.

11.5 Expressing and reading emotionLower yield · not asked yet

Darwin claimed in 1872 that emotional expressions are inherited and universal. For most of the twentieth century, however, the dominant view in the social sciences was the opposite. Anthropologists such as Margaret Mead and Ray Birdwhistell, the founder of kinesics (the study of body movement as communication), argued that expressions are learned like a language and vary from culture to culture. A smile in one society, they suggested, might signal something quite different in another. Who was right?

Are facial expressions universal?

The universality hypothesis states that certain basic emotions are expressed by the same facial movements, and recognised from them, in all human cultures. Ekman's first test, published with E. Richard Sorenson and Friesen in 1969, showed photographs of posed expressions to people in the United States, Brazil, Japan, New Guinea and Borneo, and found strong agreement about which emotion each face showed, especially among the literate groups. But literate people might simply have learned Western expressions from films and magazines. The decisive test needed people who had not.

Key study: Ekman and Friesen among the Fore of New Guinea (1971)

Question. Would people with almost no contact with outsiders recognise the same facial expressions as Westerners? Method. In the late 1960s Ekman and Friesen worked with the South Fore, a preliterate people in the highlands of Papua New Guinea who had seen no films or magazines and had little contact with Westerners. Through a translator, a participant heard a short story ("her friends have come, and she is happy"; "her child has died, and she feels very sad"; "she is angry and about to fight") and then chose which of three photographed faces fitted the story. In a second part of the study, Fore people were asked to show the face they would make in each story; their expressions were video-recorded and later shown to American students. Results. The Fore chose the expected face far above chance for happiness, sadness, anger, disgust and surprise. The one consistent confusion was between fear and surprise, which the Fore often mixed up. American students also recognised the Fore's posed expressions. Conclusion. At least six expressions are universal, as Darwin had argued. Criticisms. The forced-choice format can exaggerate agreement, the photographs were posed and exaggerated, and translation may have helped. More recent fieldwork in other isolated societies (for example, Carlos Crivelli and colleagues' 2016 study in the Trobriand Islands, where a wide-eyed "gasping" face was read as a threat rather than as fear) suggests that agreement is weaker than once believed.

Further support for an inborn basis comes from people who could never have learned expressions by watching. Children born blind smile, cry and frown much like sighted children. In a neat natural experiment, David Matsumoto and Bob Willingham (2009) compared the faces of judo athletes at the moment of winning or losing a medal: congenitally blind athletes at the 2004 Paralympic Games produced the same expressions as sighted athletes at the 2004 Olympic Games.

Display rules and cultural differences

If expressions are universal, why do people from different cultures so often seem to express emotion differently? Ekman and Friesen's answer was display rules: culturally learned norms about which emotions may be shown, by whom, to whom and when. Display rules can lead people to intensify an expression (looking delighted with a gift you do not like), de-intensify it (looking only mildly pleased when you have beaten a friend), neutralise it (keeping a poker face) or mask it with a different expression (smiling to hide anger). At an Indian wedding, the bride's tears at her bidaai are expected and appropriate; at a funeral, laughter must be hidden; and many young people learn to mask anger in front of elders.

The classic demonstration was Friesen's study of 1972. Japanese and American students watched unpleasant film clips of surgery and accidents while their faces were secretly recorded. When they watched alone, both groups showed very similar expressions of disgust and distress. When a higher-status experimenter sat with them, the Japanese students more often masked their negative expressions with polite smiles. The underlying expression was universal; its public display was governed by culture. The sociologist Arlie Hochschild extended the idea to work in The Managed Heart (1983): flight attendants, receptionists and call-centre staff must follow workplace display rules, smiling whatever they feel, and this "emotional labour" is a known contributor to burnout (Section 11.9).

Culture also shapes how expressions are read. A meta-analysis by Hillary Elfenbein and Nalini Ambady (2002) found that emotions are recognised across cultures well above chance, but more accurately within one's own culture, an in-group advantage rather like understanding your own regional accent best. Rachael Jack and colleagues (2012) showed that East Asian observers rely more on the eyes, and Western observers more on the mouth, when reading faces; tellingly, a Japanese-style emoticon puts the emotion in the eyes (^_^) whereas the Western one puts it in the mouth :). Cultures also differ in which emotions they value. Jeanne Tsai has shown that North Americans tend to prize high-arousal positive states such as excitement, whereas East Asians tend to prize low-arousal positive states such as calm, a difference visible even in the smiles shown in children's storybooks.

Measuring the face: FACS, the Duchenne smile and microexpressions

To study expressions precisely, Ekman and Friesen published the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) in 1978. FACS describes any facial movement as a combination of numbered action units, each corresponding to the action of one or more facial muscles; for example, action unit 12 is the lip corner puller (zygomaticus major) and action unit 6 is the cheek raiser (the outer part of orbicularis oculi). Trained coders can describe any expression, real or faked, in these anatomical terms, without guessing at the emotion behind it. FACS is used in research on depression, pain, deception, infant development and animation.

FACS made it possible to settle an old question: how can you tell a real smile from a polite one? In 1862 the French neurologist Guillaume-Benjamin-Amand Duchenne de Boulogne (1806 to 1875) stimulated the facial muscles of volunteers electrically and photographed the results. He noticed that a smile of genuine enjoyment involves two muscles: the zygomaticus major, which pulls the lip corners up, and the orbicularis oculi, which encircles the eye, raising the cheeks and producing "crow's feet" wrinkles at the outer corners. The zygomaticus is easy to control voluntarily; the outer part of the orbicularis oculi is not. A genuine smile, which Ekman named the Duchenne smile, therefore involves the eyes; a polite or forced smile (sometimes called a social smile) involves only the mouth. Look at the eyes of anyone posing for a photograph.

When people try to hide an emotion, the true feeling may leak out in a microexpression, a full facial expression that flashes across the face for only a fraction of a second (roughly between a twenty-fifth and a fifth of a second) before it is suppressed. Microexpressions were first described by Ernest Haggard and Kenneth Isaacs in 1966 and popularised by Ekman's work on deception. A caution is in order, however. When people are tested on whether they can tell liars from truth-tellers, accuracy is disappointing: a large review by Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo (2006) found an average of about 54 percent, barely above the 50 percent expected by guessing, and professionals such as police officers do little better.

Beyond the face: body, voice and gesture

Faces are not the whole story. Posture, gesture, gaze, touch and the tone of the voice (prosody) all carry emotion. Pride, for example, has a recognisable display involving the whole body: a small smile, the head tilted slightly back, an expanded chest and the arms raised or the hands on the hips; Jessica Tracy and Matsumoto found the same display in congenitally blind athletes after victories. Indeed, when emotions are very intense, the body can be more informative than the face. Hillel Aviezer and colleagues (2012) showed people photographs of professional tennis players at the moment of winning or losing a crucial point. From the faces alone, viewers could not reliably tell winners from losers; from the bodies, they could. Some body language is culture-specific: a sideways head-wobble that signals agreement in much of South India can confuse a visitor who reads it as "no".

Exam trap

The "7-38-55 rule" is a misquotation. Albert Mehrabian's experiments (1967, 1971) found that when a speaker's words, tone of voice and facial expression conflict about feelings or attitudes (saying "I like you" in a hostile tone, for example), listeners rely more on the face (55 percent) and tone (38 percent) than on the words (7 percent). Mehrabian himself warned that this applies only to such inconsistent messages about feelings, not to communication in general.

Gender differences in emotion

Do women and men differ in emotion? The answer depends on which component you measure. On expression, the differences are reliable: women are, on average, more facially expressive and smile more than men. On recognition, women are on average more accurate at decoding nonverbal cues, a difference documented in Judith Hall's review of 1978 and many later studies. On experience and physiology, the differences are much smaller and less consistent; Ann Kring and Albert Gordon (1998), for instance, found that women were more expressive while watching emotional films but did not report experiencing more emotion than men. Most researchers attribute the expressive differences largely to socialisation and display rules ("boys don't cry"; girls should not show anger) layered on top of a shared emotional system, and they note that the stereotype of the "emotional woman" exaggerates differences that are modest and vary by emotion; anger, for example, is not more often expressed by women.

Exam trap

Ekman's universality research used the Fore of Papua New Guinea, not the Trobriand Islanders (Crivelli's later challenge) and not Mead's Samoans. The usual confusion in the Fore data was fear with surprise. Display rules govern showing emotions; they do not change the emotion felt. The Duchenne smile is identified by the eye muscle (orbicularis oculi); the zygomaticus alone gives a social smile. FACS codes muscle action units, not emotions.

11.6 Four emotions up closeLower yield · 3 Qs

General theories are only half of the story of emotion. Each emotion also has its own causes, functions and disorders. Four have attracted the most research and the most exam questions: fear, anger, happiness and disgust.

Fear and anxiety

Fear is the emotional response to a present, identifiable danger: the dog that is snarling at you now. It is intense, rises quickly, prepares the body for fight or flight, and fades when the danger passes. Anxiety is a more diffuse, future-oriented state of apprehension about possible threats: the worry, muscle tension and vigilance of the night before an exam. When anxiety has no particular object at all, it is described as free-floating. A useful distinction for the clinic, made by Charles Spielberger in his State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (1970), separates state anxiety (how anxious you are at this moment) from trait anxiety (a stable tendency to respond to many situations with anxiety).

Both fear and anxiety are adaptive in moderate doses: they keep us away from cliffs and make us prepare for examinations. They become clinical problems when they are out of proportion to the real danger, persistent and disabling. A phobia is a persistent, irrational fear of a specific object or situation that leads to avoidance; generalised anxiety disorder involves continued, uncontrollable apprehension about many unspecified things; and panic disorder involves sudden surges of intense fear and a "fear of fear" (these disorders are covered in the Disorders book). Why are phobias of snakes, heights and spiders so much more common than phobias of electric sockets or cars, which are far more dangerous today? Martin Seligman's idea of preparedness (Chapter 6) proposes that evolution has prepared us to learn fears of the dangers our ancestors faced, and Arne Öhman's research shows that such fear-relevant stimuli are detected and conditioned especially quickly, partly through LeDoux's low road. The link between arousal and performance, the inverted U of the Yerkes-Dodson law, is discussed in Chapter 10.

Anger, aggression and the myth of catharsis

Three terms need separating. Anger is an emotion, a feeling of displeasure and antagonism, usually in response to a perceived wrong. Aggression is behaviour intended to harm someone. Hostility is an attitude, a lasting, cynical mistrust of and ill will towards others. You can be angry without being aggressive, and aggressive without being angry (a hired thug). The distinction matters later in this chapter, because it is hostility, rather than anger or the broader Type A pattern, that has turned out to be most dangerous for the heart.

What should an angry person do with the anger? A long tradition says: let it out. The idea of catharsis goes back to Aristotle, who wrote that tragedy purges the audience of pity and fear; Sigmund Freud and Josef Breuer used a "cathartic method" in which patients relived and discharged buried feelings; and the ethologist Konrad Lorenz compared aggressive energy to water building up behind a dam. The catharsis hypothesis holds that expressing anger or aggression in a safe way, by hitting a pillow, shouting, playing violent games or watching violence, drains it and so reduces later aggression. It is one of the most popular ideas in pop psychology, and one of the most thoroughly disconfirmed.

Key study: does venting feed the flame? (Bushman, 2002)

Method. Brad Bushman first made participants angry: each wrote an essay and received a harsh evaluation from a supposed fellow student ("This is one of the worst essays I have read!"). Participants were then assigned to one of three groups. The rumination group hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who had insulted them (a photograph of the "evaluator" was shown). The distraction group hit the punching bag while thinking about becoming physically fit (a photograph of an athlete was shown). The control group simply sat quietly for two minutes. All then had the chance to blast the evaluator with unpleasant noise. Results. The rumination group was the angriest and the most aggressive; the control group, who did nothing, was the least angry and least aggressive; the distraction group fell in between. Conclusion. Venting while thinking about the provocation fed anger rather than draining it. Doing nothing at all worked better than "letting it out".

Why does catharsis fail? Venting keeps the provocation in mind (rumination), rehearses aggressive responses so that they become more available, and keeps arousal high, which, as excitation transfer predicts, can be added to the next provocation. What does work is reducing arousal (relaxation, slow breathing, a time-out), reappraising the provocation (perhaps the other person was under pressure), distraction, humour, and assertive rather than aggressive communication. The theories of aggression themselves, including the frustration-aggression hypothesis, are covered in Chapter 10 and in the Social book.

Happiness and subjective well-being

For most of its history psychology studied misery far more than happiness. That changed in the 1980s, largely through the work of Ed Diener (1946 to 2021), whose landmark review of 1984 defined subjective well-being (SWB), the scientific term for what people call happiness. Diener's tripartite model says that subjective well-being has three components: a cognitive one, life satisfaction, a person's overall judgement of how his or her life is going; and two affective ones, the frequent presence of positive affect and the relative absence of negative affect. A happy person, in this sense, judges life to be satisfying and experiences frequent pleasant and infrequent unpleasant emotions. Diener and his colleagues measured the cognitive component with the five-item Satisfaction With Life Scale (1985).

What makes people happy? Research has produced some surprises. Close relationships are among the strongest correlates of happiness; income matters a good deal when it lifts people out of poverty but much less once basic needs are met. One reason for the weak effect of good fortune is adaptation. According to the adaptation-level theory of Harry Helson (1898 to 1977), set out in 1964, we judge every new experience against a neutral point set by our recent experience, and that neutral point moves as experience changes. Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell (1971) applied the idea to happiness and called the result the hedonic treadmill: as our circumstances improve, our expectations rise to match, so that we keep running without getting any happier. In a famous study, Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman (1978) found that lottery winners were not significantly happier than a comparison group a year or so later and took less pleasure in everyday events, while people paralysed in accidents were less happy than the comparison group but not nearly as unhappy as most people would predict. Later research has softened the treadmill: some events, such as long unemployment, widowhood or severe disability, can lower well-being for years, and people differ in how fully they adapt.

A second reason is comparison. Relative deprivation is the feeling that one is worse off than the people one compares oneself with, whatever one's absolute position. The term comes from the wartime research of Samuel Stouffer and colleagues, published as The American Soldier (1949): military policemen, who had poor chances of promotion, were more satisfied with their promotion prospects than men in the Army Air Corps, who were promoted far more often, because the airmen compared themselves with the many colleagues who had been promoted faster. If you receive a ten percent salary rise at your Mysuru office, you are pleased, until you discover that your colleague received fifteen. Comparison even works backwards in time and possibility: Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich (1995) found that Olympic bronze medallists looked happier than silver medallists, because silver medallists compare themselves with the gold they just missed, whereas bronze medallists compare themselves with finishing without any medal.

What are positive emotions for? Negative emotions have obvious survival value: fear narrows attention and prepares escape. Barbara Fredrickson proposed in 1998, and developed in 2001, the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions. Its first claim (the broaden part) is that positive emotions such as joy, interest, contentment and love widen our momentary "thought-action repertoire": they make attention and thinking broader, more flexible and more creative, and make us more open to people and ideas. Its second claim (the build part) is that, over time, these broadened moments build lasting personal resources, physical (health), intellectual (knowledge), social (friendships) and psychological (resilience, optimism), which help us cope with later adversity. Fredrickson and Robert Levenson (1998) also found an undo effect: after a fear-inducing film, participants who then watched a film evoking contentment or amusement recovered their resting heart rate and blood pressure faster than those who watched a neutral or sad film. (A later claim that flourishing requires a precise ratio of about three positive emotions to every negative one turned out to rest on faulty mathematics, and that part of the work was formally withdrawn.)

Positive psychology, the movement launched by Martin Seligman in 1998, also studies how people make the most of good experiences. Savoring, a concept developed by Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff (their book appeared in 2007), is the capacity to attend to, appreciate and enhance positive experiences. It is the mirror image of coping: coping manages bad experiences, savoring amplifies good ones. We can savour the past (reminiscing), the present (savouring the moment) and the future (anticipation). Bryant and Veroff described savouring processes such as basking (in praise or pride), thanksgiving (gratitude), marvelling (awe) and luxuriating (physical pleasure), and strategies such as sharing the experience with others, building memories, sharpening the senses and absorbing oneself fully. Lingering over the first sip of filter coffee on a cold morning, rather than gulping it while scrolling your phone, is savouring.

Disgust

Disgust is the emotion of rejection: in the words of Paul Rozin and April Fallon (1987), revulsion at the prospect of taking an offensive object into the body. Its facial expression, a wrinkled nose, raised upper lip and sometimes a protruding tongue, is literally the face of spitting something out, exactly as Darwin's principle of serviceable habits predicts, and its function is to protect us from contamination and disease. Disgust obeys what Rozin calls the laws of "sympathetic magic". By the law of contagion, anything that has touched a disgusting thing becomes disgusting itself: most people refuse a glass of juice into which a completely sterilised cockroach has been dipped. By the law of similarity, things that look like disgusting things are treated as disgusting: people avoid chocolate fudge shaped like dog faeces even though they know exactly what it is.

Disgust develops late. Toddlers will put almost anything in their mouths, and a full adult sense of disgust appears only during the school years, learned largely from the reactions of others. Over development, disgust spreads outward from core disgust (spoiled food, body products, vermin) to animal-reminder disgust (death, wounds and anything that reminds us we are animals), interpersonal disgust (contact with strangers or the "unclean") and finally moral disgust at cheating or cruelty. Brain imaging shows that both feeling disgust and seeing disgust on another person's face activate the anterior insula. Clinically, heightened disgust sensitivity plays a part in contamination-type obsessive-compulsive disorder and in blood-injection-injury phobia.

Exam trap

Diener's tripartite model of subjective well-being is life satisfaction (cognitive) plus high positive affect plus low negative affect. Options that replace life satisfaction with "physical wellness", "spiritual wellness" or "social wellness" are distractors. Do not confuse it with Seligman's PERMA model (positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, accomplishment) or Carol Ryff's six-part model of psychological well-being.

Barbara Fredrickson = broaden-and-build (and the undo effect). Flow theory is Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's; cognitive distortions are Aaron Beck's (and David Burns's popular list); psychodynamic theory is Freud's.

Savoring (Bryant and Veroff) means enhancing and prolonging the enjoyment of positive experiences. It is not "avoiding negative experiences", "minimising stress through relaxation" or "reflecting on past traumas".

Hedonic treadmill is Brickman and Campbell (1971), building on Helson's adaptation-level theory (1964). Relative deprivation began with Stouffer's The American Soldier (1949). The catharsis hypothesis predicts that venting reduces aggression; the evidence shows it usually increases it.

11.7 Emotional development and regulationMedium yield · 6 Qs

Emotions are not fully formed at birth, and they do not run their course untouched once they begin. This section looks at how emotions emerge in infancy, and at how people of every age manage the emotions they have.

How emotions develop

John B. Watson, as you saw in Table 11.2, believed that newborns have only three innate emotional reactions: fear (to loud noises and loss of support), rage (to restriction of movement) and love (to stroking and cuddling). A more careful observational account came from the psychologist Katharine M. Banham Bridges, who, from observations of infants in Montreal, in 1932 described emotional development as a process of differentiation, like a branching tree. At birth, she reported, the infant shows only a diffuse general excitement. Within the first three months this splits into two broad states, distress and delight. Distress then branches into anger, disgust and fear in the following months, and delight into elation and affection; by about two years the child shows a wide range of emotions, including jealousy and joy.

Modern research, using methods such as Izard's coding of infant faces, finds that differentiation happens faster than Bridges thought. By about six months infants show most of the primary emotions: joy, interest, surprise, sadness, anger, disgust and fear. The social smile, a smile directed at a human face, appears at about six to eight weeks, and wariness of strangers (stranger anxiety) at around six to eight months. A second group, the self-conscious emotions (embarrassment, pride, shame, guilt and envy), requires something more: a sense of oneself as a separate person who can be evaluated. Michael Lewis and Jeanne Brooks-Gunn (1979) showed that infants first recognise themselves in a mirror (touching a spot of rouge secretly placed on their own nose) between about 15 and 24 months, and the self-conscious emotions appear only after this, from about 18 months onwards; pride, shame and guilt, which require knowing the rules by which one is judged, become clear between about two and three years. Self-conscious emotions therefore appear well after 12 months.

Infants also learn to use other people's emotions as information. In social referencing, a child faced with an uncertain situation looks to a caregiver's face to decide how to feel and act. In a classic study by James Sorce, Joseph Campos and colleagues (1985), one-year-olds crawled towards a "visual cliff" (a glass-covered drop) of uncertain depth while their mothers, on the far side, posed either a happy or a fearful face. About three-quarters of the infants crossed when the mother looked happy; none crossed when she looked afraid. Infants also expect emotional responsiveness from caregivers. In Edward Tronick's "still-face" procedure (first described in 1978), a mother interacts normally with her infant and then suddenly holds her face still and unresponsive; within moments the infant tries harder and harder to re-engage her, then becomes distressed and turns away. Even very young infants actively expect emotional give-and-take. (Temperament, attachment and the rest of emotional development are covered in the Developmental book.)

Emotion regulation: Gross's process model

From the first months, caregivers help infants manage their emotions by soothing, distracting and feeding them; gradually children take over the job themselves. Emotion regulation refers to the processes by which we influence which emotions we have, when we have them, and how we experience and express them. The most influential framework is the process model of emotion regulation of James J. Gross (1998). Gross pointed out that an emotion unfolds over time: we enter a situation, attend to some aspect of it, appraise its meaning, and then respond. We can intervene at each of these points, which gives five families of strategies (Figure 11.4). Consider a student on the night before an entrance examination.

  1. Situation selection: choosing which situations to enter. The student decides not to join the anxious group chat where everyone is comparing marks on mock tests.
  2. Situation modification: changing the situation one is in. When a friend keeps quizzing her on topics she has not revised, she asks him to stop.
  3. Attentional deployment: directing attention within the situation. She distracts herself with a walk, or concentrates on the topics she knows well. Rumination, dwelling on the feeling and its causes, is also a form of attentional deployment, an unhelpful one.
  4. Cognitive change: changing how one appraises the situation. The central strategy here is cognitive reappraisal: "This exam is a chance to show what I have learned, and there are other colleges if it goes badly"; or "My racing heart is my body getting ready to perform."
  5. Response modulation: acting on the emotional response once it has arisen. Examples are slow breathing, exercise, drinking, and expressive suppression, keeping a calm face while feeling panic inside.

The first four families act before the emotional response is fully formed, so Gross called them antecedent-focused; the fifth acts on the response after it has formed, so it is response-focused.

Are all strategies equally good? Gross's research compared the two most studied ones, reappraisal and suppression. In experiments by Gross and Robert Levenson in the 1990s, participants watched a disgusting film; those told to suppress their expressions showed less disgust on their faces but felt just as disgusted, and their sympathetic arousal actually increased. Suppression is effortful: later studies found that it also impairs memory for what happened during the suppression and makes conversation partners feel less connected. Reappraisal, by contrast, reduces both the feeling and the expression at little cost. Using the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, Gross and Oliver John (2003) found that habitual reappraisers report more positive emotion, closer relationships and greater well-being, and habitual suppressors the reverse. Reappraisal can even improve performance under pressure: Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues (2010) told some students preparing for a graduate admission test that feeling anxious and aroused could help them perform; those students scored higher on the mathematics section than students given no such message.

Clinical link

Difficulties in emotion regulation run through many disorders: depression (rumination), anxiety disorders (avoidance, a form of situation selection taken too far), substance use (response modulation with a drug) and borderline personality disorder. Dialectical behaviour therapy, developed by Marsha Linehan, teaches four skills modules, mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness, and cognitive behaviour therapy is in large part systematic training in reappraisal (Clinical book).

Alexithymia: no words for feelings

Some people find it hard to know what they are feeling at all. The psychiatrist Peter E. Sifneos (1920 to 2008) coined the term alexithymia in 1973, from the Greek a (lack), lexis (word) and thymos (emotion): literally, "no words for emotions". He had noticed that many of his patients with psychosomatic illnesses could describe their physical symptoms in detail but could not describe their feelings. Alexithymia is a personality trait rather than a diagnosis, and it has four features: difficulty identifying feelings and telling them apart from bodily sensations ("I feel sick" rather than "I feel anxious"); difficulty describing feelings to others; an externally oriented, concrete style of thinking focused on practical details rather than inner experience; and a limited fantasy life. It is usually measured with the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), and it is more common in people with psychosomatic complaints, depression, eating disorders, substance use, post-traumatic stress and autism spectrum conditions. For the therapist, it means that "How did that make you feel?" may draw a blank, and that helping the client label emotions is often the first task.

Emotional intelligence

If people differ in recognising and managing emotions, perhaps this is a kind of intelligence. Peter Salovey and John Mayer introduced the scientific concept of emotional intelligence in 1990, and Daniel Goleman's best-selling Emotional Intelligence (1995) made it famous. Goleman's popular model has five domains: knowing one's own emotions (self-awareness), managing one's emotions, motivating oneself, recognising emotions in others (empathy), and handling relationships; later he relabelled them self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills.

Goleman's empathy domain deserves a closer look, because empathy is itself a part of emotion. Social psychologists such as Robert Baron and Nyla Branscombe describe it as having three distinct components. Emotional empathy is sharing the feelings of another person, feeling distressed when a friend is distressed. The cognitive component, sometimes called empathic accuracy or perspective taking, is perceiving accurately what another person is thinking and feeling. Empathic concern is feeling concern and compassion for the other person's welfare, the component most closely tied to helping (Social book). A clinician needs all three: accuracy to understand the client, concern to want to help, and enough control over emotional empathy not to be swamped by the client's distress. The ability model of Mayer and Salovey, the trait models, their measurement and the debate over whether emotional intelligence is really an intelligence are covered in Chapter 9.

Exam trap

Gross's five families, in order: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, response modulation. Reappraisal is cognitive change, not response modulation; suppression is response modulation. Distraction and rumination are attentional deployment.

Expressive suppression (Gross) is not Freud's suppression or repression. In Gross's sense it is hiding the outward expression of a feeling. In defence-mechanism language, repression is the unconscious, involuntary banishing of a thought or feeling from awareness, while suppression is the conscious, deliberate pushing of it out of mind.

First emotions in infancy. In Bridges's (1932) scheme, the first differentiation of general excitement is into distress and delight. Watson's three innate emotions were fear, rage and love. Self-conscious emotions (embarrassment, pride, shame, guilt) appear after 12 months, from about 18 months, because they need self-recognition.

Alexithymia (Sifneos, 1973) is difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings; it is not the absence of emotion, and it is not anhedonia (the inability to feel pleasure, a symptom of depression).

Goleman's five domains come in two wordings. The 1995 book lists knowing one's emotions, managing emotions, motivating oneself, recognising emotions in others and handling relationships; the later relabelling is self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills. A practice mock keyed the 1995 wording, so prefer it when both appear. Empathy's three components are emotional empathy, a cognitive component (empathic accuracy) and empathic concern; "behavioural aspect" and "positivity" are not among them.

11.8 Stress and the body's emergency systemsMedium yield · 6 Qs

Think back to the men on the Capilano bridge. For the minute or two it took to cross, their hearts pounded, their palms sweated and their blood carried a surge of adrenaline. Then they reached the far side and it was over. Now imagine a bridge that never ends: a threat or a demand that goes on for weeks, months or years. That is the territory of stress. Everything you have learned about the bodily side of emotion, the sympathetic nervous system, the hypothalamus and the amygdala, is also the machinery of stress. What changes is time. A system built for emergencies lasting minutes is asked to run for months, and the price is paid in health.

What is stress? Three ways to define it

The word is used so loosely ("I'm so stressed about Monday") that psychologists have had to decide what it should mean. Three answers have been given, and each one is tied to a research tradition you will meet in this chapter.

The stimulus definition treats stress as something out in the environment: a demand or event that puts pressure on a person, as a load puts pressure on a bridge. A death in the family, an examination or a flood is the stress, and the research question is how much of it a person has faced. To avoid confusion, the event itself is now called a stressor: any event or circumstance that threatens, challenges or harms a person's well-being and so calls for adjustment. Holmes and Rahe's counting of life events (Section 11.9) is the classic stimulus approach.

The response definition treats stress as what happens inside the person: the physiological and psychological reaction to a stressor. Hans Selye, whom you are about to meet, defined stress as "the nonspecific response of the body to any demand made upon it." Cannon's and Selye's work on the body's emergency reactions is the classic response approach, and some writers borrow the engineer's word strain for this reaction.

The transactional definition, associated with Richard Lazarus, says that stress lies neither in the event alone nor in the response alone but in the relationship between person and environment. A situation becomes stressful when a person appraises its demands as taxing or exceeding his or her resources. The same viva voce examination is a crushing threat to one candidate and an exciting challenge to another. This is the definition most psychologists use today, and Section 11.9 develops it. Putting the three together, stress can be defined as the process by which we perceive and respond to events, called stressors, that we appraise as threatening or challenging. Stressors can be physical (cold, injury, infection, noise) or psychological (an insult, a deadline, a loss). One of the boldest claims of early stress research was that the body responds to both kinds in much the same way.

Cannon: fight or flight and homeostasis

Walter Cannon, whose attack on the James-Lange theory you met in Section 11.2, began his career at Harvard watching digestion with the newly discovered X-rays. He noticed that the rhythmic movements of a cat's stomach stopped dead whenever the animal was frightened or enraged. Over the following years he traced the reason. In Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage (1915) he described how pain, fear and rage activate the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal glands, which together prepare the animal for violent action. He called this the fight-or-flight response: an immediate, coordinated mobilisation of the body either to attack a threat or to escape from it. The heart beats faster and harder, blood pressure rises, breathing quickens and the airways widen, blood is diverted from the skin and gut to the skeletal muscles and brain, the liver releases glucose for fuel, the pupils dilate, digestion stops, and the blood clots more readily, which limits bleeding from a wound. Every item is a preparation for running or fighting (compare Table 11.5).

The machinery behind this response is now called the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary axis (SAM axis). The hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system. Sympathetic nerves act directly on the organs within a second or two, and they also stimulate the adrenal medulla, the inner core of the adrenal glands that sit on top of each kidney, to release adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine) into the bloodstream. Because these hormones circulate, they spread and prolong the sympathetic effects throughout the body. The SAM axis is fast: it acts in seconds.

Cannon also gave physiology one of its central ideas. He coined the term homeostasis in 1926 and popularised it in The Wisdom of the Body (1932): the tendency of the body to keep its internal environment, such as temperature, blood sugar and the balance of salt and water, stable within narrow limits (the motivational side of homeostasis, drive reduction, is in Chapter 10). Fight or flight is a temporary, deliberate departure from resting balance in the service of survival; when the emergency passes, the parasympathetic division restores it. Cannon even argued, in a famous essay on "voodoo death" (1942), that a person convinced of having been fatally cursed could die from a prolonged, unrelieved emergency reaction. Modern researchers add a third option that often comes before fighting or fleeing: freezing, the motionless watchfulness of a deer caught in headlights, organised by the periaqueductal grey of the midbrain (Section 11.4). Hence the modern phrase "fight, flight or freeze".

Selye's general adaptation syndrome

Cannon studied the emergency. The scientist who asked what happens when the emergency does not end was an endocrinologist working in Montreal, and he came to the question by accident.

Key study: Selye's rats and "a syndrome produced by diverse nocuous agents" (1936)

Question. In the mid-1930s Hans Selye (1907 to 1982), a young Vienna-born researcher at McGill University, was hunting for a new sex hormone. Method. He injected rats with extracts of ovary and placenta and examined their organs. Result. The rats showed a striking triad of changes: the adrenal cortex was enlarged; the thymus, spleen and lymph nodes had shrunk; and the lining of the stomach and intestine was covered in bleeding ulcers. Selye was thrilled, until he found that extracts of other organs produced the same triad, and so did a plain toxic chemical, formalin. His hormone had vanished. Then came the insight: perhaps he had found not a hormone but the body's general response to any damaging agent. He exposed rats to cold, heat, surgical injury, forced exercise and restraint, and the same triad appeared every time. Conclusion. In a short letter to Nature in 1936, "A syndrome produced by diverse nocuous agents," Selye proposed that the body reacts to all such agents with one nonspecific pattern. He later named it the general adaptation syndrome and brought it to a wide audience in The Stress of Life (1956). Criticism. The stressors were physical and often extreme, and the subjects were rats; whether psychological stressors in people work the same way had still to be shown.

The general adaptation syndrome (GAS) is Selye's description of how the body responds to prolonged stress, in three stages that always occur in the same order: alarm, resistance, exhaustion (Figure 11.5).

  1. Stage 1. In the alarm reaction the body first recognises the stressor and mobilises against it. Selye described a brief shock phase in which resistance actually dips below normal (body temperature and blood pressure may fall), followed by a countershock phase in which the sympathetic system and the adrenal glands spring into action. The alarm reaction is, in effect, Cannon's fight-or-flight response. An overwhelming stressor can kill during this stage.
  2. Stage 2. In the stage of resistance, if the stressor continues, the body settles into sustained adaptation. Stress hormones, especially cortisol, stay high; resistance to this stressor rises above normal, and outwardly the person seems to be coping. But resources are being spent all the time, and resistance to other, new stressors falls. Selye's rats that had adapted to cold, for example, became unusually vulnerable to other stressors.
  3. Stage 3. In the stage of exhaustion, if the stress goes on long enough, the body's capacity to adapt, which Selye pictured as a limited store of "adaptation energy", is used up. Resistance collapses, signs of the alarm reaction return, and the organism becomes vulnerable to what Selye called diseases of adaptation: high blood pressure, heart disease, ulcers, kidney disease, arthritis and lowered resistance to infection. In the extreme, the outcome is death.

Selye also insisted that stress is not always bad. In Stress Without Distress (1974) he distinguished eustress (from the Greek eu, good), stress that is pleasant, motivating or beneficial, from distress, stress that is unpleasant and harmful. A wedding, a promotion, a hard-fought cricket match or a well-prepared examination can all be eustress; a bereavement or a long illness is distress. Whether a stressor is experienced as eustress depends on its intensity, on how long it lasts and, above all, on how it is appraised. Eustress is not the absence of stress; it is the optimal amount, the moderate arousal that the Yerkes-Dodson principle (Chapter 10) says helps performance. Pinel and Barnes note a biological echo: brief stressors actually strengthen some parts of the immune system (Section 11.10).

How well has the theory lasted? Its central claims, that psychological and physical stressors can trigger the same bodily response and that long-lasting stress can make us ill, have stood. Three of its details have not. First, the response is less nonspecific than Selye thought. The American physiologist John W. Mason showed in the early 1970s that different stressors produce different patterns of hormones, and that when the psychological upset is removed (monkeys deprived of food but given flavoured, non-nutritive pellets, so that they were not upset by the deprivation), the usual hormonal stress response largely fails to appear. Emotional appraisal, in other words, is a key ingredient, which is exactly Lazarus's point. Second, Selye largely ignored the sympathetic nervous system; modern accounts give the SAM axis and the HPA axis equal billing. Third, exhaustion is now thought to come less from running out of hormones than from the damage done by prolonged exposure to them, as the next subsection explains.

The HPA axis: the slower stress system

The body's second stress system, the one whose effects Selye saw in his rats' enlarged adrenal glands, is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA axis). It works in three hormonal steps. First, the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH, also called corticotropin-releasing factor). Second, CRH travels a short distance through a private blood supply to the anterior pituitary gland, which responds by releasing adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into the general circulation. Third, ACTH reaches the adrenal cortex, the outer layer of the adrenal glands, and stimulates it to secrete glucocorticoids, the steroid hormones that help regulate glucose metabolism. The main glucocorticoid in humans is cortisol (in rats it is corticosterone). The level of circulating glucocorticoids is the most commonly used physiological measure of stress.

The HPA axis is slower than the SAM axis: cortisol levels peak roughly 20 to 40 minutes after a stressor begins, and its effects last for hours. Cortisol raises blood sugar by making glucose from protein and fat, which fuels sustained effort; it damps down inflammation and immune activity; and it slows digestion, growth and reproduction, which can wait until the crisis is over. It also feeds back on the brain. Cortisol acts on receptors in the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary (and the hippocampus) to switch off further release of CRH and ACTH, a negative feedback loop that works like a thermostat. In many patients with severe (melancholic) depression this feedback is weakened: given dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid that normally suppresses the body's own cortisol, their cortisol stays high (the dexamethasone suppression test is discussed in the Disorders book).

The trouble is that the HPA axis was built for the zebra's problem, not ours. The neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky made the point in the title of his book Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers (1994): a zebra's stress is acute, a desperate sprint from a lion that is over in minutes, one way or the other. Human beings switch on the same response for loans, traffic, deadlines and family quarrels, and leave it switched on. Prolonged high cortisol suppresses immune function, raises blood pressure and blood sugar, encourages fat storage around the abdomen and damages the hippocampus, which is packed with glucocorticoid receptors: chronic stress reduces dendritic branching and the birth of new neurons there and impairs hippocampus-dependent memory. Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer (1988) called the body's active adjustment to demands allostasis ("stability through change"), and Bruce S. McEwen (1938 to 2020) named its cost allostatic load: the cumulative wear and tear on the body produced by repeated or chronic activation of the stress systems. Allostatic load is the modern replacement for Selye's stage of exhaustion. Table 11.7 sets the two stress systems side by side.

Tend and befriend

Fight or flight was mapped almost entirely on males. Shelley E. Taylor (born 1946) and her colleagues at the University of California, Los Angeles, pointed out in 2000 that until the mid-1990s women had made up only about 17 percent, fewer than one in five, of the participants in laboratory studies of the physiological stress response. Looking at the evidence from animals and humans with both sexes in mind, they proposed that females, in particular, often respond to stress with a pattern they called tend-and-befriend. Tending means protecting and calming offspring; befriending means seeking out and maintaining social networks that offer protection and support. The evolutionary logic is that a pregnant or nursing female who fights or flees puts her young at risk, whereas one who shields them and gathers allies protects both. The proposed biology centres on the hormone oxytocin, whose effects are enhanced by oestrogen, together with the brain's own opioids, which promote affiliation and calm. Taylor did not claim that women never fight or flee, or that men never seek company; tend-and-befriend is a difference of emphasis that sits on top of the shared fight-or-flight machinery. It also explains part of the protective power of social support, discussed in Section 11.10.

Exam trap

The order of the GAS is alarm, resistance, exhaustion. "Illness" is not a stage (illness is what exhaustion leads to), and resistance never comes first. Alarm, resistance and exhaustion belong to Selye's general adaptation syndrome, not to life events theory, the health belief model or protection motivation theory (Section 11.10).

Match the person to the idea. Cannon: fight or flight (1915) and homeostasis (1926). Selye: general adaptation syndrome (1936; The Stress of Life, 1956) and eustress versus distress (1974). Taylor: tend-and-befriend (2000). McEwen: allostatic load.

Cortex versus medulla. Cortisol comes from the adrenal cortex (HPA axis); adrenaline and noradrenaline come from the adrenal medulla (SAM axis). Asked which gland is primarily responsible for the stress response, answer the adrenal glands. The pituitary is the "master gland" and releases ACTH, but it is not the stress gland. Distractor hormones: insulin (pancreas, lowers blood sugar), thyroxine (thyroid, sets metabolic rate), melatonin (pineal, signals darkness and sleep).

The HPA sequence is hypothalamus (CRH), then anterior pituitary (ACTH), then adrenal cortex (cortisol); cortisol's negative feedback acts mainly on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary. The posterior pituitary releases oxytocin and vasopressin and is not part of the HPA axis.

Eustress is good stress, not zero stress.

11.9 Sources of stress and the transactional modelHigh yield · 8 Qs

What kinds of events cause stress? Researchers have tried three ways of answering: counting the big events in a person's life, counting the small ones, and sorting stressors by how long they last and how many people they strike. Each approach found something real, and each ran into the same awkward fact: the same event affects different people very differently. That fact leads to the transactional model at the end of this section.

Life events: Holmes and Rahe's Social Readjustment Rating Scale

In the 1950s and 1960s the psychiatrists Thomas H. Holmes and Richard H. Rahe, at the University of Washington School of Medicine in Seattle, noticed in their patients' case histories that illnesses seemed to follow clusters of major changes in their lives. (The idea of charting a patient's life events alongside the course of illness went back to the psychiatrist Adolf Meyer's "life chart".) In 1967 they published a way of measuring such change. They gave 394 people a list of 43 life events and asked them to rate how much readjustment each one required, compared with marriage, which was arbitrarily given a value of 500. The average ratings, divided by 10, became life change units (LCUs), ranging from 100 for the death of a spouse to 11 for minor violations of the law. The resulting instrument is the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS). A respondent ticks the events experienced in the past year and adds up their life change units (Table 11.8).

Look down the list and something stands out. Marriage, pregnancy, outstanding personal achievement, a vacation and Christmas are all there, alongside death and divorce. For Holmes and Rahe the stressor was change itself, whether welcome or not, because any change requires readjustment. This is the stimulus definition of stress in its purest form. In their research, people whose scores totalled more than about 300 in a year were classed as facing a major life crisis and were considerably more likely to fall ill in the following year; scores of 150 to 299 carried a moderate risk. In prospective studies, Rahe gave the scale to US Navy sailors before long tours of duty and found that those with higher life-change scores reported somewhat more illness while at sea.

The SRRS was a landmark because it made stress countable, and thousands of studies used it. But it has serious weaknesses, and examiners like them:

  1. It lumps good and bad events together. Later research found that it is undesirable, uncontrollable change that predicts illness. The Life Experiences Survey of Irwin Sarason, James Johnson and Judith Siegel (1978) let respondents rate each event as positive or negative and how much it affected them; negative change predicted problems, positive change did not.
  2. It ignores meaning and appraisal. A divorce is a catastrophe for one person and a release for another, yet both score 73.
  3. It ignores resources and coping, such as social support, which change the impact of the same event.
  4. It relies on memory. Recall of events over a year is unreliable, and people who are ill may search their memories harder for causes.
  5. Some items are confounded with illness. "Personal injury or illness", "change in sleeping habits" and "change in eating habits" may be early signs or results of illness rather than causes of it.
  6. It is culture-bound. Christmas and mortgages of over 10,000 dollars reflect American life in the 1960s.
  7. The correlations are small. Most people with high scores do not become ill, so the scale is a weak predictor for any individual.
Indian context

An American list of life events fits Indian lives imperfectly: few Indian respondents would rate Christmas or a large mortgage as typical stressors, while events such as arranging a daughter's marriage, a family dispute over property or a failed examination may weigh more heavily. In 1984 Gurmeet Singh, Dalbir Kaur and Harsharan Kaur published the Presumptive Stressful Life Events Scale (PSLES), a 51-item life-events scale developed with Indian respondents, in the Indian Journal of Psychiatry. It remains one of the most widely used life-events measures in Indian clinical research.

Daily hassles and uplifts

Richard Lazarus thought that major life events were the wrong unit of stress. On most days nobody dies, marries or is sacked. But the bus is late, the phone battery dies in the middle of a call, the neighbour's dog barks all night and there are three assignments due on Friday. In 1981 Allen D. Kanner, James C. Coyne, Catherine Schaefer and Lazarus defined daily hassles as the irritating, frustrating and distressing demands that to some degree characterise everyday transactions with the environment, and uplifts as the positive experiences that can offset them, such as relating well with friends, completing a task, getting enough sleep or receiving good news. They built a 117-item Hassles Scale and a 135-item Uplifts Scale and had a group of 100 middle-aged adults complete them every month for most of a year. The most frequent hassles were concerns about weight, the health of a family member, rising prices of common goods, home maintenance, having too many things to do, and misplacing or losing things.

The key finding was that hassles predicted psychological symptoms, such as anxiety and depression, better than life-event scores did, and they went on predicting symptoms even when life events were statistically controlled. Later work by Anita DeLongis, Coyne, Lazarus and their colleagues (1982) found that hassles also predicted physical health better than major events. Part of the reason is that big events act partly through small ones: a divorce brings a hundred daily hassles about money, housing and children. Uplifts had weaker and less consistent effects than hassles; the bad, it seems, is stronger than the good.

Acute, chronic and cataclysmic stressors

Stressors can also be sorted by how long they last. An acute stressor is short-lived, with a clear beginning and end: a surprise test (a "pop quiz"), a job interview, a near miss on the highway. A chronic stressor persists for months or years, often with no foreseeable end: living with HIV disease or cancer, a long-running marital conflict, caring for a parent with dementia, poverty, unemployment or discrimination. The body handles acute stressors well; as Section 11.8 showed, it is chronic stress that produces allostatic load and Selye's exhaustion, and Section 11.10 shows that the two kinds even have opposite effects on immunity.

Stressors can be sorted, too, by how many people they strike. Lazarus and Cohen (1977) distinguished three categories. Cataclysmic events are sudden, powerful catastrophes that affect many people at once: earthquakes, floods, wars, terrorist attacks and pandemics, such as the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami on the Tamil Nadu coast or the Kedarnath floods of 2013. Because the suffering is shared, survivors can support one another, and for many people the stress subsides over time, although some develop lasting problems. Personal stressors are major life events, such as bereavement, divorce or job loss, that strike one person or family. Background stressors are the daily hassles of the previous subsection.

When a stressor is extreme, involving actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, some of those exposed develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). ICD-11 places PTSD in a group of disorders specifically associated with stress, together with complex PTSD, prolonged grief disorder and adjustment disorder, whose diagnosis requires an identifiable stressor; generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder and obsessive-compulsive disorder do not. (The disorders themselves are covered in the Disorders book.)

Frustration, conflict, pressure and change

Many textbooks, following Wayne Weiten, group the psychological sources of stress into four types: frustration, conflict, change and pressure. You studied the first and second in Chapter 10 (Section 10.8), so a short reminder is enough here. Frustration is the blocking of goal-directed behaviour. Conflict is the simultaneous presence of incompatible motives. Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947) described three basic types in 1935: approach-approach (two attractive goals, such as two good colleges; the mildest and quickest to resolve), avoidance-avoidance (two unattractive options, such as a painful dental visit or a worsening toothache; the person vacillates and tries to "leave the field") and approach-avoidance (one goal with both attractive and repellent features, such as a well-paid job far from home). Later writers added the double approach-avoidance conflict, two goals each with good and bad features, which is the shape of most real-life decisions. Neal E. Miller (1909 to 2002) showed that the avoidance gradient is steeper than the approach gradient, which is why people in approach-avoidance conflicts move towards the goal and then retreat as they near it (Figure 10.6). Approach-avoidance and avoidance-avoidance conflicts are the most stressful; approach-approach conflicts the least. Change is the life-event stress measured by Holmes and Rahe. Pressure is the expectation or demand that one behave in a certain way: pressure to perform (a call-centre worker who must handle a quota of calls at high speed) and pressure to conform (a young adult expected to choose the career or marriage the family has in mind).

Stress at work and burnout

For most adults the workplace is a major source of stress, and organisational psychologists have catalogued its sources. A widely used scheme from Stephen Robbins's textbook of organisational behaviour sorts potential stressors into three groups. Environmental factors lie outside the organisation: economic uncertainty, political uncertainty and technological change. Organisational factors lie within the job and the organisation: task demands (workload, pace, working conditions, the design of the job), role demands, interpersonal demands (poor relationships with colleagues, lack of support), the structure of the organisation and its style of leadership. Individual factors are those the employee brings from personal life: family problems, economic problems such as debt, and personality.

Role demands deserve a closer look. In Organizational Stress (1964), Robert Kahn and his colleagues described role conflict, the presence of incompatible expectations (a supervisor told to cut costs and also to improve service, or a working mother pulled between office and home), and role ambiguity, uncertainty about what one is expected to do, how one's work will be judged or where one's authority ends. To these is added role overload, simply having too much to do. In 1979 Robert A. Karasek proposed the job demand-control model: strain is greatest not in jobs with the heaviest demands but in jobs that combine high demands with low control (little decision latitude). The assembly-line worker, the call-centre agent with a scripted screen and the waiter at a busy restaurant are in "high-strain" jobs; the busy senior executive, who can decide how and when to work, often is not. Jeffrey Johnson and Ellen Hall (1988) later added social support as a third dimension: isolated high-strain jobs are the worst of all.

When work stress becomes chronic, the result may be burnout. The term was introduced in 1974 by the psychologist Herbert J. Freudenberger (1926 to 1999), who saw it in himself and in fellow volunteers at a free clinic in New York. Christina Maslach (born 1946), whose research on human-service workers made the concept scientific, and Susan Jackson published the Maslach Burnout Inventory in 1981. It measures three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (feeling drained and used up), depersonalisation (a cynical, detached, even callous attitude towards the people one serves) and reduced personal accomplishment (feeling ineffective). Burnout is most common in the helping professions: nursing, medicine, teaching, social work, policing and counselling, where the emotional labour of Section 11.5 is part of the job. In ICD-11 (2019) the World Health Organisation describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, and characterised by exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about one's job, and reduced professional efficacy.

Clinical link

Clinical psychologists are themselves at high risk of burnout. They spend their working days absorbing other people's distress, often with heavy caseloads and little control over the system they work in, which is Karasek's high-strain recipe. The protections are those this chapter describes: regular supervision and peer support (social support), realistic caseloads and some control over one's schedule (control), and deliberate attention to sleep, exercise and life outside work. Training programmes increasingly treat self-care as a professional skill rather than a luxury.

Control and predictability

Why do some stressors do so much more damage than others of the same physical size? A series of animal experiments gave an answer that has run through stress research ever since.

Key study: executive monkeys and helpless rats (Brady, 1958; Weiss, 1971)

In 1958 Joseph V. Brady reported a famous experiment on "executive" monkeys. Pairs of monkeys received electric shocks; the "executive" in each pair could prevent shocks for both of them by pressing a lever at regular intervals, while its yoked partner could do nothing. The executives developed stomach ulcers, and some died; the yoked monkeys did not. The study was taken as proof that the burden of responsibility causes ulcers. But the executives had not been chosen at random: they were the monkeys that had learned the avoidance task fastest in a pretest, and these were probably the most emotionally reactive animals.

In 1971 Jay M. Weiss repeated the logic with proper controls, using rats in triads. One rat could avoid or turn off a shock by turning a wheel; a yoked rat received exactly the same shocks but had no control; a third rat received no shocks. The results reversed Brady's: the rats with control developed far fewer stomach ulcers than the helpless yoked rats. Weiss also found that rats that heard a warning tone before each shock developed fewer ulcers than rats shocked without warning. Conclusion. The same physical stressor does less damage when it is controllable and predictable. This is the same lesson as Seligman's learned helplessness experiments (Chapter 6).

Human research tells the same story. In the experiments David Glass and Jerome Singer (the same Singer as the two-factor theory) reported in Urban Stress (1972), people exposed to loud, unpredictable bursts of noise afterwards gave up sooner on insoluble puzzles and made more proofreading errors than people exposed to the same noise at predictable intervals. When participants were told they could stop the noise by pressing a button, the after-effects shrank, even though hardly anyone pressed it. Simply believing that one has control helped. In a field study, Ellen Langer and Judith Rodin (1976) gave residents on one floor of a nursing home small choices about their daily lives and a plant to look after; compared with residents on another floor whose lives were managed for them, they became more active and happier, and 18 months later fewer of them had died. Perceived control is a thread you will see again in secondary appraisal, hardiness and self-efficacy.

Lazarus and Folkman's transactional model

Every finding in this section points the same way. Life events matter less than how they are taken; hassles matter because of how they accumulate in a particular life; control and predictability change the impact of identical shocks. Richard Lazarus drew the conclusion in his appraisal theory of emotion (Section 11.3) and, with Susan Folkman, built it into a full theory of stress in Stress, Appraisal, and Coping (1984). Their transactional model of stress defines psychological stress as "a particular relationship between the person and the environment that is appraised by the person as taxing or exceeding his or her resources and endangering his or her well-being." Stress is a transaction: it exists only in the meeting of a particular person with a particular situation.

The model runs through a sequence of appraisals (Figure 11.6). In primary appraisal the person asks, "Is this relevant to my well-being, and how?" The event may be judged irrelevant, benign-positive or stressful, and a stressful event may be appraised as harm or loss (damage already done), threat (harm anticipated) or challenge (an opportunity for gain or growth, which brings eagerness rather than dread). In secondary appraisal the person asks, "What can I do about it?", evaluating his or her coping options and resources: physical (health and energy), social (people who can help), psychological (skills, beliefs, self-efficacy) and material (money, time). Stress is greatest when the demands appraised in primary appraisal seem to outweigh the resources appraised in secondary appraisal. The person then copes (Section 11.10), and the results of coping, together with any new information, feed back into reappraisal, so that the whole transaction keeps changing.

Push two people through the model and its power becomes clear. On the morning of the entrance examination, Ananya and Kiran sit in the same Mysuru hall with the same question paper. Ananya's primary appraisal is threat: "If I fail, my parents will be humiliated and my year is wasted." Her secondary appraisal is bleak: "I never finished revising developmental psychology, and nobody can help me now." Demands far exceed resources, and she is shaking before the first question. Kiran's primary appraisal is challenge: "This matters, but it is my chance to show what I know." His secondary appraisal is reassuring: "I have practised twenty past papers, I know my weak areas, and there is another attempt next year if I need it." He feels keyed up but focused, which is Selye's eustress. Then Ananya reads the first five questions and finds she knows every answer. Her reappraisal ("Maybe I am better prepared than I feared") lowers her stress, and she settles. Same stressor, different transactions, different stress. Later research by Jim Blascovich and his colleagues has shown that challenge and threat appraisals even produce different cardiovascular patterns: in challenge the heart pumps more blood and the blood vessels relax, in threat the vessels constrict.

Two features of the model are often misunderstood. First, "primary" and "secondary" do not mean first and second in time, or more and less important; the two appraisals interact continuously, and a good secondary appraisal ("I can handle this") can turn a threat into a challenge. Second, appraisals need not be slow or conscious. As Lazarus argued in his debate with Zajonc (Section 11.3), an appraisal can be an instant, automatic evaluation.

Stress meets vulnerability: the diathesis-stress model

The transactional model explains why the same event is stressful for one person and not another. A related idea explains why the same amount of stress makes one person ill and leaves another well. The diathesis-stress model holds that psychological disorders develop when a diathesis (from the Greek for "disposition"), a pre-existing vulnerability, is triggered by stress. The diathesis may be genetic or biological (a family history of depression, an overreactive HPA axis), psychological (a negative way of thinking about oneself) or the result of early experience (childhood maltreatment). Neither ingredient is usually enough on its own: a person with a strong vulnerability may stay well in a protected life, and a person with little vulnerability may weather severe stress. The two trade off: the larger the diathesis, the less stress is needed to trigger a disorder. The idea was set out for schizophrenia by Paul E. Meehl (1920 to 2003) in 1962 and developed by Joseph Zubin and Bonnie Spring as the vulnerability-stress model in 1977; it is now applied to depression, anxiety disorders, PTSD and much else.

The diathesis-stress model is often set against single-cause models of abnormality, which examiners use as distractors. The biological model locates disorders in genes and brain chemistry alone; the psychodynamic model in unconscious conflicts; the behavioural model in learned experiences; the cognitive model in maladaptive thinking (Aaron Beck, Albert Ellis); the humanistic-existential model in blocked growth, lack of self-acceptance or failure to find meaning (Carl Rogers, Abraham Maslow, Rollo May); and the sociocultural model in social and cultural forces such as poverty, family structure and discrimination. The oldest view of all, the supernatural (demonological) model, blamed possession by spirits or divine punishment, and no modern model accepts it. The diathesis-stress model is the one that explicitly says a biological or psychological predisposition is set off by a stressful situation. Its broader cousin, the biopsychosocial model, appears in Section 11.10. (The models of abnormality are covered fully in the Disorders book.)

Exam trap

The SRRS measures life events. Holmes and Rahe (1967) counted major life changes in life change units; death of a spouse scores 100 and marriage, the anchor, 50. It does not measure daily hassles (Kanner, Coyne, Schaefer and Lazarus, 1981), physiological reactions (Selye's tradition) or cognitive appraisal (Lazarus). Positive events such as marriage, vacations and Christmas count, because the scale measures change. Some revision notes credit Holmes and Rahe with "catastrophes and daily hassles" as types of stressor; that threefold scheme (cataclysmic, personal, background) is Lazarus and Cohen's (1977).

Hassles beat life events as predictors of symptoms and health. Acute or chronic? A pop quiz, an interview or a near miss is acute; HIV disease, cancer, marital discord, caregiving and poverty are chronic.

Secondary appraisal is the assessment of whether one has the resources to cope. Primary appraisal judges relevance and whether the event is harm-loss, threat or challenge. Phrases such as "initial threat evaluation", "coping resource assessment" or "situational capacity evaluation" sound plausible but are not Lazarus and Folkman's terms.

Organisational stressors are task demands, role demands, interpersonal demands, structure and leadership. Family and economic problems and the number of dependants are individual factors; political and economic uncertainty are environmental factors. Role conflict means incompatible expectations; role ambiguity means unclear ones.

Control reduces damage. Brady's "executive monkeys" (1958) seemed to show the opposite, but the executives were not randomly assigned; Weiss's properly controlled rats (1971) with control got fewer ulcers. Karasek: high demands plus low control produce the most strain.

11.10 Coping, personality and healthHigh yield · 15 Qs

Two people meet the same stressor and appraise it in much the same way, yet one stays well and the other falls ill. Part of the difference lies in what they do about it (coping), part in the people around them (social support), part in stable features of their personalities, and part in what stress does to the heart and the immune system. This final section follows that path, from the first attempt to cope to the cells of the immune system, and ends with what can be done to manage stress.

What coping is: problem-focused and emotion-focused

Lazarus and Folkman (1984) defined coping as "constantly changing cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage specific external and/or internal demands that are appraised as taxing or exceeding the resources of the person." Notice three things about the definition. Coping is a process: it is what a person actually thinks and does in a particular encounter, and it changes as the encounter unfolds, rather than being a fixed trait. Coping is defined by effort, not by success: a strategy that fails is still coping. And coping may be aimed at the outside demand or at the inner turmoil it causes, which gives the most important distinction in the field.

Problem-focused coping is directed at the stressor itself: defining the problem, gathering information, generating and weighing solutions, making a plan, managing time, confronting the person responsible, or learning a new skill. Emotion-focused coping is directed at the emotional response to the stressor: reappraising the situation in a more positive light, seeking sympathy and emotional support, accepting what cannot be changed, distancing oneself, humour, prayer, relaxation and distraction, but also venting, wishful thinking, denial and drinking. In a study of 100 middle-aged adults who described more than 1,300 stressful episodes, Folkman and Lazarus (1980) found that people used both kinds of coping in 98 percent of them. Problem-focused coping rose when people saw something that could be done, such as a difficulty at work, and emotion-focused coping rose when they saw the situation as one that had to be accepted, such as a health problem.

Which is better? Neither, in general. The best predictor of good outcomes is the goodness of fit between the strategy and the controllability of the stressor: change what can be changed, and manage your feelings about what cannot. A candidate who learns three weeks before the entrance examination that she is weak in statistics should use problem-focused coping (a revision plan, extra practice papers). The same candidate, the evening after the results are declared, can no longer change the outcome; emotion-focused coping (talking it over with a friend, reappraising the result as one attempt among several) is now what helps. Studying caregivers of partners dying of AIDS in the 1990s, Folkman found that even in the midst of chronic grief they reported positive emotions, which they drew from finding meaning in caregiving, revising their goals and noticing small good moments. She later called this meaning-focused coping: drawing on beliefs, values and goals to find benefit and purpose in a stressful situation that cannot be changed.

A second distinction cuts across the first. Approach coping turns towards the stressor and one's reactions to it (seeking information, planning, expressing feelings, reappraisal); avoidance coping turns away (denial, distraction, wishful thinking, withdrawal, substance use). Susan Roth and Lawrence Cohen (1986) reviewed the evidence, and a meta-analysis by Jerry Suls and Barbara Fletcher (1985) gave the most useful summary: avoidance can help in the short term, for example in the hours before an operation, when worrying about details only raises anxiety, but approach coping does better in the long run, and chronic avoidance is linked to poorer mental and physical health. Researchers measure these strategies with questionnaires such as Folkman and Lazarus's Ways of Coping Questionnaire and the COPE Inventory of Charles Carver, Michael Scheier and Jagdish Weintraub (1989). Where do defence mechanisms fit? Sigmund Freud introduced the idea, and his daughter Anna Freud systematised it in her book The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936), describing defence mechanisms as unconscious, automatic strategies that protect the ego from anxiety by distorting or denying reality. Coping, in Lazarus's sense, is conscious, deliberate and flexible. The two overlap at the edges, and George Vaillant, in Adaptation to Life (1977), arranged defences in a hierarchy from immature (such as projection and acting out) to mature (sublimation, humour, altruism, anticipation and suppression), and found that men who relied on mature defences enjoyed better physical and mental health over decades. The defences examined most often are easy to tell apart by their key words: displacement redirects a feeling from its true target to a safer one (a woman angry with her husband shouts at her child); sublimation channels an unacceptable impulse into a socially valued activity (an angry young man takes up kickboxing); repression is the involuntary banishing of a painful memory or feeling from awareness, whereas suppression is deliberate; projection attributes one's own unacceptable feelings to someone else; isolation of affect keeps the idea but strips away its feeling (a patient calmly describes a gruesome fantasy); rationalisation offers a plausible excuse; denial refuses to acknowledge a painful reality at all (insisting that a frightening diagnosis must be a mistake); and reaction formation turns a feeling into its opposite. Notice the difference between denial and isolation: the patient who calmly describes a gruesome fantasy is not denying the idea, which he reports in full, but has cut it off from its feeling. Social support

Of all the resources that people bring to stress, the most consistently protective is other people. Social support is the perception or experience that one is loved and cared for, valued and esteemed, and part of a network of mutual help. James House (1981) distinguished four kinds: emotional support (empathy, love, trust and caring), instrumental or tangible support (money, a lift to the hospital, help with the cooking), informational support (advice, suggestions and information) and appraisal support (feedback that helps a person evaluate himself or herself and the situation). An important finding is that perceived support, the belief that help would be there if needed, predicts well-being more consistently than received support, the help actually given. Highly visible help can even backfire, by making the receiver feel indebted or incompetent.

How does support protect? Sheldon Cohen and Thomas Wills (1985) reviewed the evidence for two answers. The main-effect (or direct-effect) hypothesis says that support is good for people whether or not they are under stress, because belonging, a sense of being valued and encouragement towards healthy habits are beneficial in themselves. The stress-buffering hypothesis says that support protects mainly when stress is high, by improving secondary appraisal ("I am not alone with this") and by supplying coping resources. Both turned out to be true: measures of how integrated a person is in a social network tend to show main effects, and measures of perceived support that matches the person's needs tend to show buffering.

The health effects are large. In the Alameda County study in California, Lisa Berkman and Leonard Syme (1979) followed nearly 7,000 adults for nine years. People with the fewest social ties (marriage, friends and relatives, membership of religious and other groups) had roughly two to three times the death rate of those with the most, even after allowance was made for their initial health and health habits. A meta-analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and her colleagues (2010), combining 148 studies of more than 300,000 people, found that people with stronger social relationships had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the periods studied, an effect comparable in size to that of giving up smoking. In Sheldon Cohen's laboratory, people with more diverse social networks were less likely to develop colds after being deliberately exposed to a cold virus (1997). Support has a darker twin, too: relationships full of conflict are themselves a major source of stress, a point made clear in the Indian joint family, which can provide abundant support and abundant daily hassles at the same time.

The absence of support is loneliness, and the sociologist Robert S. Weiss, in Loneliness: The Experience of Emotional and Social Isolation (1973), showed that it comes in two forms. Emotional isolation (emotional loneliness) is the absence of a close, intimate attachment to one specific person, such as a partner or a confidant; it produces a sense of utter aloneness that no number of acquaintances can cure. Social isolation (social loneliness) is the absence of a wider network of friends, colleagues and community, a place to belong; it produces boredom and a feeling of being on the margins, and a loving partner alone does not cure it. A newly married woman who moves to a new city with her husband may have no emotional isolation but severe social isolation; a widower surrounded by kind relatives may have the reverse. Neither is the same as feeling incompetent or having low self-esteem, which are problems of self-evaluation rather than of attachment.

Hardiness, optimism, resilience and sense of coherence

Some people seem to stay healthy under stress that flattens others. Four related ideas try to say what they have.

The first is hardiness, introduced by Suzanne C. Kobasa in 1979. Kobasa studied middle- and upper-level executives at a large public utility in Chicago (Illinois Bell Telephone) and picked out two groups who had all experienced many stressful life events in the previous three years: those who had fallen ill and those who had stayed well. The healthy executives scored higher on three attitudes, often remembered as the three Cs. Commitment is a sense of involvement and purpose in work, family and life, rather than alienation. Control is the belief that one can influence events, rather than being powerless. Challenge is the tendency to see change as a normal part of life and an opportunity for growth, rather than a threat. A later prospective study by Kobasa, Salvatore Maddi and Stephen Kahn (1982) found that hardiness predicted who would stay healthy in the years that followed. In transactional terms, hardy people appraise stressors as challenges (primary appraisal) and believe they can act (secondary appraisal), and so they tend to cope actively. The main criticism is that hardiness scales overlap heavily with low neuroticism, so part of what they measure may simply be a lack of negative emotionality.

The second idea is optimism, studied in two traditions. Michael F. Scheier and Charles S. Carver (1985) defined dispositional optimism as a generalised expectancy that good things rather than bad will happen, and measured it with the Life Orientation Test. Optimists use more problem-focused coping and positive reappraisal and less denial, and in one study by Scheier and colleagues (1989), optimistic men recovered faster after coronary bypass surgery and returned sooner to normal life. The second tradition began with helplessness. Martin E. P. Seligman (born 1942) and Steven F. Maier showed in 1967 that dogs exposed to inescapable shock later failed to escape shock they could have avoided, the phenomenon of learned helplessness (Chapter 6). When the theory was reformulated in 1978 in terms of how people explain bad events, it led to the idea of explanatory style, a person's habitual way of explaining the causes of events. A pessimistic explanatory style explains bad events as internal, stable and global ("It's me, it will always be like this, and it ruins everything"); an optimistic style explains them as external, unstable (temporary) and specific ("That paper was unusually hard, it was one bad day, and it is only one subject"). Christopher Peterson, Seligman and George Vaillant (1988) found that the explanatory style of Harvard graduates at about age 25 predicted their physical health between 45 and 60. In Learned Optimism (1990) Seligman argued that pessimism can be unlearned, by catching and disputing pessimistic explanations, a technique he adapted from Albert Ellis; he called the result learned optimism. One caution: unrealistic optimism, the belief that bad things happen to other people, can lead people to ignore genuine health risks.

The third idea is resilience: the process of adapting well, or bouncing back, in the face of serious adversity, trauma or risk. Its classic evidence comes from the Kauai Longitudinal Study of Emmy E. Werner (1929 to 2017) and Ruth Smith, who followed all 698 children born in 1955 on the Hawaiian island of Kauai from before birth into middle age. About a third of the children were at high risk, born into poverty, family discord, parental alcoholism or mental illness, or after a difficult birth. Yet about one in three of these high-risk children grew into competent, confident and caring adults. What protected them was ordinary: an easy-going temperament, at least one close bond with a caring adult (often a grandparent, aunt or teacher rather than a parent), competence in some valued activity, faith, and a supportive community. Ann Masten (2001) summed up such findings by calling resilience "ordinary magic": it comes from ordinary human adaptive systems, not from rare gifts. George Bonanno (2004) showed that after bereavement or trauma, a resilient course, with a short period of disturbance and a steady return to normal functioning, is the most common outcome, not the exception. Resilience is not invulnerability, and it can be strengthened.

The fourth idea came from an unusual question. In a 1970 study of the menopause among women in Israel, the medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky (1923 to 1994) noticed that some of the women had survived Nazi concentration camps, and that, astonishingly, a substantial minority of these survivors (about 29 percent, against about 51 percent of a comparison group who had not been in the camps) were in good emotional health. Medicine, he argued, had always asked what makes people ill (pathogenesis); he proposed instead to ask what keeps people healthy, which he called salutogenesis (from the Latin salus, health). His answer, in Health, Stress and Coping (1979) and Unraveling the Mystery of Health (1987), was the sense of coherence (SOC): a pervasive, enduring yet dynamic feeling of confidence that life is comprehensible (events are structured, predictable and explicable), manageable (resources are available to meet its demands) and meaningful (its demands are challenges worth investing in). Meaningfulness, Antonovsky thought, is the motivational heart of the three. People draw on generalised resistance resources, such as money, knowledge, social support and a stable culture, and a strong sense of coherence lets them use these resources well. Two further personal resources, self-efficacy (Albert Bandura, 1977) and an internal locus of control (Julian Rotter, 1966), work in much the same way and are covered in Chapter 10.

Personality and heart disease: Types A, B, C and D

In the mid-1950s two San Francisco cardiologists, Meyer Friedman (1910 to 2001) and Ray H. Rosenman (1920 to 2013), had the chairs in their waiting room reupholstered. The upholsterer remarked that only the front edges of the seats were worn out, as if their patients spent their waiting time perched on the edge, ready to spring up. Friedman later said that he did not grasp the significance of the remark until years afterwards, but he and Rosenman did come to notice that many of their coronary patients shared a distinctive style: hurried, impatient, intensely competitive and quick to anger. In 1959, in the Journal of the American Medical Association, they reported that men showing this pattern had higher blood cholesterol, faster blood clotting and more coronary heart disease than men who did not.

They called it the Type A behaviour pattern, and they described three core components. The first is competitive achievement striving: a relentless drive to accomplish more and more, and to win. The second is an exaggerated sense of time urgency ("hurry sickness"): doing two things at once, finishing other people's sentences, fuming in queues and traffic. The third is aggressiveness and hostility, easily aroused. The opposite pattern, Type B, is relaxed, patient and easy-going, unhurried and less driven by competition, although not necessarily less ambitious or less successful. Friedman and Rosenman assessed the pattern mainly with the Structured Interview, in which the interviewer deliberately provokes impatience (by speaking slowly, pausing or interrupting) and codes the person's speech and behaviour as much as the content of the answers; a questionnaire, the Jenkins Activity Survey, was developed later. Their popular book was Type A Behavior and Your Heart (1974).

Key study: the Western Collaborative Group Study (1960 to 1969)

Question. Does Type A behaviour predict heart disease in men who are healthy at the start? Method. In 1960 and 1961 Rosenman, Friedman and their colleagues classified 3,154 healthy employed men, aged 39 to 59 and working in ten Californian companies, as Type A or Type B using the Structured Interview, and followed them for eight and a half years. Results. Type A men developed coronary heart disease about twice as often as Type B men, and the difference remained after allowing for the standard risk factors of cholesterol, blood pressure and smoking. In 1981 a review panel of the US National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute accepted Type A behaviour as an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease. The twist. Later large studies, notably the Multiple Risk Factor Intervention Trial (1985), found no link between global Type A behaviour and heart disease, and meta-analyses showed that the association had weakened or vanished in later research. The question became: is one ingredient of Type A doing the damage?

The answer that emerged in the 1980s was hostility, in particular cynical hostility: a mistrustful, cynical view of other people combined with a readiness to become angry. When the Western Collaborative Group interviews were reanalysed component by component, first by Karen Matthews and her colleagues (1977) and later by Michael Hecker and his colleagues (1988), "potential for hostility" stood out as the strongest predictor of heart disease; in the 1988 analysis it still predicted heart disease after the standard risk factors and the other Type A components were taken into account, and it accounted for much of the link between global Type A and heart disease. John Barefoot, Grant Dahlstrom and Redford B. Williams (1983) traced 255 doctors who had taken the MMPI as medical students 25 years earlier; those with high scores on its Cook-Medley Hostility scale had developed coronary heart disease several times more often, and had a higher death rate from all causes. Williams popularised the finding in Anger Kills (1993), and a meta-analysis by Yoichi Chida and Andrew Steptoe (2009) confirmed that anger and hostility predict coronary heart disease in both healthy and already ill people. How might hostility harm the heart? Hostile people show larger and more frequent surges of blood pressure and stress hormones in everyday encounters; they tend to have poorer health habits; and they drive away the social support that would buffer their stress. The good news is that the pattern can change: in Friedman's Recurrent Coronary Prevention Project (1986), heart attack survivors who received counselling to reduce their Type A behaviour had significantly fewer second heart attacks over the next four and a half years than patients who received only standard cardiological advice.

Two later "types" extended the idea to other illnesses. A Type C (cancer-prone) pattern was described in the 1980s, most influentially by the psychologist Lydia Temoshok from her work with patients with melanoma: cooperative, appeasing, unassertive and patient, compliant with authority and, above all, inclined to suppress negative emotions, especially anger. (Some revision notes describe Type C people as introverted, detail-minded perfectionists; the research construct is the emotionally suppressed, unassertive style.) The evidence that personality causes cancer is weak: large prospective studies have not found a reliable link, and the most striking supporting data, from Hans Eysenck and Ronald Grossarth-Maticek, were judged unsafe by an enquiry at King's College London in 2019, after which a number of the papers were retracted. Coping style may, however, affect quality of life after a diagnosis.

The most important recent addition is the Type D personality ("D" for distressed), introduced in the mid-1990s by the Belgian psychologist Johan Denollet. Type D is the joint presence of two traits. Negative affectivity is the tendency to experience negative emotions, such as worry, gloom, irritability and tension, across time and situations. Social inhibition is the tendency to inhibit the expression of those emotions and behaviours in social interaction, for fear of disapproval. A Type D person, in other words, feels bad a great deal and keeps it to himself or herself. In Denollet's best-known study, published in The Lancet in 1996, 303 patients with coronary heart disease were followed for six to ten years: 27 percent of the Type D patients died, against 7 percent of the others, and Type D predicted death independently of the severity of the heart disease. Type D is measured with the 14-item DS14 scale. Later and larger studies have found weaker effects than the early ones, and the debate about how much Type D adds to depression and anxiety continues, but it remains the personality type most often examined in cardiac psychology today. Psychoneuroimmunology: how stress gets under the skin

For most of the twentieth century medicine worked with a biomedical model: disease is a biological malfunction, to be explained by germs, genes and chemistry. In 1977, in the journal Science, the American psychiatrist George L. Engel (1913 to 1999) argued that this model was too narrow and proposed the biopsychosocial model: health and illness arise from the interaction of biological factors (genes, physiology, infection), psychological factors (emotions, beliefs, coping, behaviour) and social factors (family, relationships, social class, culture, access to care). The model is now the framework of health psychology and of clinical psychology alike. Within it, disorders in which psychological factors play a causal role are often called psychosomatic disorders. Pinel and Barnes point out that so many illnesses, from heart disease and asthma to skin disorders, have turned out to be affected by stress that it is more sensible to think of most medical disorders as psychosomatic to some degree. Gastric ulcers, the classic example, show the point: most are associated with infection by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, but many infected people never develop ulcers, and stress appears to increase the stomach's vulnerability.

The most surprising link between mind and body runs through the immune system. Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) is the study of interactions among psychological factors, the nervous and endocrine systems, and the immune system. A brief reminder of the immune system helps. The innate immune system reacts quickly and in the same way to all invaders, through inflammation and cells such as phagocytes and natural killer cells. The adaptive immune system is slower but specific and has a memory: its lymphocytes, the T cells and B cells, target particular antigens, and B cells produce antibodies. Until the 1970s the immune system was thought to work on its own, untouched by the brain. The experiment that changed this began as a study of taste aversion.

Key study: conditioned immunosuppression (Ader and Cohen, 1975)

Background. The psychologist Robert Ader (1932 to 2011), at the University of Rochester, was studying conditioned taste aversion (Chapter 6). Rats drank saccharin-flavoured water and were then injected with cyclophosphamide, a drug that causes nausea, so that they learned to avoid the sweet taste. When he went on giving the rats saccharin water without the drug, to see how long the aversion would last, some of them unexpectedly sickened and died, and the more saccharin they had drunk during conditioning, the more likely they were to die. Ader knew that cyclophosphamide also suppresses the immune system. Could the taste alone have become a conditioned stimulus for immune suppression? Method. With the immunologist Nicholas Cohen, he paired saccharin with cyclophosphamide, then injected the rats with an antigen (sheep red blood cells) and gave some of them saccharin alone. Results. Rats re-exposed to the saccharin produced fewer antibodies than control rats. Conclusion. The immune system can be classically conditioned, so the brain must be able to influence it. Ader and Cohen's paper of 1975 is usually taken as the founding study of psychoneuroimmunology, a name Ader helped establish with his edited book Psychoneuroimmunology (1981).

Evidence in people soon followed. Janice K. Kiecolt-Glaser and her husband, the immunologist Ronald Glaser, at Ohio State University, found that the activity of medical students' natural killer cells fell during examinations (1984). In a study published in 1995, they made a small, standard wound on the arm of women who were caring for a relative with dementia and of matched women who were not; the caregivers' wounds took about nine days, or 24 percent, longer to heal. Later they found that a hostile discussion between married partners was enough to slow the healing of such wounds. Sheldon Cohen, David Tyrrell and Andrew Smith (1991) measured psychological stress in 394 healthy volunteers, then gave them nasal drops containing one of five common cold viruses (a further 26 volunteers received plain saline drops) and kept them in quarantine. The higher the stress score, the more likely a person was to develop a cold, in a smooth dose-response pattern. Elissa Epel and her colleagues (2004) found that mothers with the highest levels of perceived stress, many of them caring for a chronically ill child, had telomeres (the protective caps on chromosomes that shorten with age) as short as those of women about a decade older.

Does stress, then, simply weaken immunity? Not quite. A meta-analysis of about 300 studies by Suzanne Segerstrom and Gregory Miller (2004) found that the answer depends on the kind of stress. Acute stressors lasting minutes, such as public speaking or a mental arithmetic task, actually boosted parts of the innate immune system, as if preparing the body for injury; this is the biological face of Selye's eustress. Chronic stressors, such as caregiving, unemployment, disability or trauma, suppressed immune function, above all the adaptive (cellular and antibody) system, and the longest-lasting stressors suppressed almost every measure of immunity. Stress acts through two kinds of pathway. The direct pathway is physiological: immune cells carry receptors for cortisol, adrenaline and noradrenaline, so the HPA and SAM axes speak to them directly, and chronic stress also promotes a long-lasting, low-grade inflammation, driven by chemical messengers called cytokines, that is now linked to heart disease, diabetes and depression. The indirect pathway is behavioural: stressed people sleep less, smoke and drink more, exercise less, eat worse and delay seeing a doctor. A caution runs through this literature. Most human studies are correlational, and people under stress may simply notice and report more symptoms, which is why experiments such as Cohen's cold-virus study carry so much weight.

Health beliefs and health behaviour

Because so much of stress's effect on health runs through behaviour, health psychologists have built models to predict whether people will act to protect their health: get screened, take medicine, stop smoking, exercise. Three are examined most often, and they are frequently offered as wrong options in questions about Selye or life events (Table 11.11).

The health belief model was developed in the 1950s by social psychologists in the United States Public Health Service, among them Godfrey Hochbaum and Irwin M. Rosenstock, to explain why so few people took up free screening for tuberculosis. It says that people act to protect their health when they believe they are susceptible to a condition (perceived susceptibility), that it would be serious (perceived severity), that the recommended action would help (perceived benefits) and that its costs are acceptable (perceived barriers), and when something prompts them (cues to action, such as a symptom or a reminder); self-efficacy was added in 1988. Protection motivation theory, proposed by Ronald W. Rogers in 1975 to explain how fear appeals work and revised in 1983, says that motivation to protect oneself depends on a threat appraisal (how severe the danger is and how vulnerable one is) and a coping appraisal (whether the recommended action works, which is response efficacy, and whether one can carry it out, which is self-efficacy). The theory of planned behaviour of Icek Ajzen (1985, 1991) says that behaviour follows from intention, and intention from three factors: the person's attitude towards the behaviour, the subjective norm (perceived social pressure from important others) and perceived behavioural control, a concept very close to self-efficacy (the theory is taught with attitudes in the Social book). The stages-of-change, or transtheoretical, model of Prochaska and DiClemente is in Chapter 10.

Managing stress

If stress is a transaction between demands, appraisals and the body, it can be managed at each point: by changing the stressor (problem solving, time management, assertiveness), by changing the appraisal (cognitive restructuring) and by calming the body (relaxation, meditation, exercise). The techniques below are the ones most often named in examinations; their use in therapy is covered in the Clinical book.

Relaxation training. The American physician Edmund Jacobson (1888 to 1983) noticed in the 1920s that anxious patients carried chronic tension in their muscles, and he reasoned that a relaxed body cannot also be an anxious one. His progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), set out in Progressive Relaxation (1929), teaches people to tense and then release one muscle group after another (hands, arms, face, neck, shoulders, chest, stomach, legs) while attending to the contrast between tension and relaxation, until they can relax deeply at will. Joseph Wolpe (1958) used a shortened version as the response incompatible with anxiety in systematic desensitisation, in which clients rate their anxiety on a 0 to 100 Subjective Units of Distress (SUD) scale as they work up a graded fear hierarchy. The cardiologist Herbert Benson (1935 to 2022), after studying practitioners of Transcendental Meditation, described the relaxation response in a 1975 book of that name: an integrated physiological state that is the opposite of fight or flight, with lower heart rate, breathing rate, oxygen consumption and blood pressure. It can be produced, Benson said, by four simple elements: a quiet environment, a mental device (a word or sound repeated silently), a passive attitude and a comfortable position.

Biofeedback. In biofeedback a person is given continuous information, as a tone, a light or a moving line on a screen, about a bodily process that is normally outside awareness, such as muscle tension (recorded by electromyography), skin temperature, skin conductance, heart rate, blood pressure or brain waves (neurofeedback), so that he or she can learn to control it. The idea drew on Neal Miller's reports in the 1960s that autonomic responses such as heart rate could be operantly conditioned in rats, results that later proved hard to repeat. Biofeedback helps with tension headache, migraine (by learning to warm the hands) and some other stress-related conditions, although it is often no more effective than relaxation training alone.

Meditation and mindfulness. Concentrative meditation focuses attention on a single object, such as a mantra or the breath; Transcendental Meditation, brought to the West by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, is the best-known example. Mindfulness is the practice of paying attention, on purpose and without judgement, to experience in the present moment, an idea drawn from Buddhist vipassana meditation. In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn (born 1944) founded an eight-week Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction programme at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale, 2002) later adapted it to prevent relapse in depression. Meta-analyses find moderate benefits for anxiety, depression and pain. A caution about evidence applies here, too: a survey showing that regular meditators report less stress than non-meditators cannot show that meditation reduces stress, because calmer people may be the ones who choose to meditate; only experiments with random assignment can establish cause (Research Methods book).

Indian context

Many of the techniques in this subsection have Indian roots. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras describe an eightfold path whose limbs include asana (posture), pranayama (regulation of the breath), dharana (concentration) and dhyana (meditation); slow, controlled breathing is one of the quickest ways to shift the autonomic balance from sympathetic towards parasympathetic activity. Transcendental Meditation came from an Indian teacher, and the vipassana tradition that inspired mindfulness was revived and taught worldwide from India by S. N. Goenka. Indian research centres, including NIMHANS in Bengaluru, have run clinical trials of yoga-based breathing for depression and anxiety, and yoga is now part of many hospital and workplace stress-management programmes.

Exercise. Regular aerobic exercise lowers resting heart rate and blood pressure, reduces the physiological reaction to later stressors, improves sleep and mood, and is an effective treatment for mild to moderate depression. It also offers distraction and a sense of mastery.

Cognitive restructuring and stress inoculation. Because stress depends on appraisal, changing appraisals is one of the most powerful tools. Cognitive restructuring means identifying distorted, stress-producing thoughts and replacing them with more realistic ones; it is the core of Albert Ellis's rational emotive behaviour therapy (with its ABC model: an activating event leads to emotional consequences only through the beliefs held about it) and of Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, and it is reappraisal (Section 11.7) made systematic. Donald Meichenbaum (born 1940) combined it with skills training in stress inoculation training, developed in the 1970s and set out in his book Stress Inoculation Training (1985). The name comes from the logic of vaccination: people are exposed to manageable doses of stress while they build their defences. It has three phases. In the conceptualisation (education) phase, the client learns how stress works and how his or her own appraisals and self-statements feed it. In the skill acquisition and rehearsal phase, the client learns and practises coping skills: relaxation, problem solving and coping self-statements such as "One step at a time; I can handle this." In the application and follow-through phase, the client uses the skills in imagined, role-played and then real, gradually harder situations, with planning to prevent relapse.

Clinical link

A clinical psychologist assessing a stressed client works through the same transactional map. What are the stressors, acute and chronic? How does the client appraise them, and with what explanatory style? What resources are available: support, money, skills, self-efficacy? Which coping strategies are being used, and do they fit the controllability of the problem? Is there a vulnerability, such as a history of depression, that makes this stress more dangerous? Intervention then targets the weakest link: problem-solving training for the controllable stressors, cognitive restructuring for the threatening appraisals, relaxation or mindfulness for the over-aroused body, and help in mobilising social support. Asking "What do you do when you are under stress?" is one of the most informative questions in any intake interview.

Exam trap

Who gave which technique? Progressive muscle relaxation: Edmund Jacobson (1920s; Progressive Relaxation, 1929). Systematic desensitisation and the SUD scale: Joseph Wolpe, who borrowed Jacobson's relaxation. Relaxation response: Herbert Benson (1975). Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction: Jon Kabat-Zinn (1979). Stress inoculation training: Donald Meichenbaum, with three phases (conceptualisation, skill acquisition and rehearsal, application and follow-through). Biofeedback gives information about one's own body; it is not a drug or a form of hypnosis.

The biopsychosocial model (George Engel, 1977) integrates biological, psychological and social factors. "Biological, psychological and cultural" is the trap; culture is part of the social.

Psychoneuroimmunology began with Ader and Cohen's conditioned immunosuppression in rats (1975), using saccharin and cyclophosphamide. Acute stress tends to boost innate immunity; chronic stress suppresses immunity (Segerstrom and Miller, 2004).

Health behaviour models. The theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen) has attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control (an option listing "attitude, subjective norms and self-efficacy" is the keyed answer, because perceived control and self-efficacy are close). The health belief model is about perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers; protection motivation theory about threat and coping appraisal. None of them describes stages of alarm, resistance and exhaustion.

The case of the Capilano bridge, revisited

Go back to the men swaying above the Capilano River in 1974, and you can now say a great deal about what was happening to them. As each man stepped onto the bridge, his low road from thalamus to amygdala flagged the drop as dangerous before he had thought about it, and his sympathetic nervous system and adrenal medulla, Cannon's fight-or-flight machinery, set his heart pounding and his palms sweating. James would have said that his fear simply was the feeling of those changes; Cannon would have said that feeling and arousal rose together. Then a smiling woman appeared with a questionnaire. Here Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory does the work: a man aroused for one reason found a second, attractive explanation standing in front of him, and some of the arousal was labelled as attraction. Dutton and Aron did not rest on one field study, which had an obvious weakness (perhaps the men who choose a frightening bridge are simply bolder). In a laboratory experiment reported in the same paper, men who expected to receive strong electric shocks found a female confederate more attractive than men who expected only mild ones. The misattribution of arousal, and Zillmann's excitation transfer, have been part of emotion theory ever since.

The bridge also illustrates the second half of the chapter. The crossing was an acute stressor, a few minutes of alarm reaction followed by recovery, and for many of the men it was probably eustress, the kind of thrill that tourists pay to experience. Whether a man felt threat or challenge depended on his appraisal and on his secondary appraisal of whether he could cope with the sway, exactly as Lazarus and Folkman would predict. Solomon's opponent-process theory would add that the relief on reaching solid ground was the b-process, and that a man who crossed every day would come to feel less fear and more exhilaration. And if the bridge had been a permanent condition of life, with no far side to reach, the HPA axis, allostatic load and the stage of exhaustion would have taken over from the brief, harmless surge that made a stranger's telephone number so appealing.

Chapter summary

11.1 What is an emotion? An emotion is a brief, whole-person response to a significant event, with three components: physiological arousal, expressive behaviour and conscious experience (modern theorists add appraisal and action tendency). A feeling is the subjective component; a mood is longer, weaker and often objectless; affect is the umbrella term for good or bad states and, clinically, the observable expression of emotion (rated for quality, range and reactivity in the mental status examination); temperament is the inborn style of emotional reactivity. Basic-emotion lists include Watson's three (fear, rage, love), Tomkins's nine affects (1962), Izard's ten (differential emotions theory), Ekman's six (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust; contempt added in 1986), Plutchik's eight primaries arranged as opposites on a wheel with dyads such as love and awe (1980) and Panksepp's seven brain systems. Dimensional models include Wundt's tridimensional theory, Russell's circumplex of valence by arousal (1980) and Barrett's theory of constructed emotion.

11.2 The classic theories: from the body to the label. The James-Lange theory (1884, 1885) says that bodily changes come first and that the feeling of them is the emotion ("we feel afraid because we tremble"). Cannon (1927) objected that emotion survives separation of viscera from brain, that visceral changes are similar across emotions, insensitive and slow, and that artificial arousal (Marañón's adrenaline injections) produces bodily symptoms and at most an "as if" emotion; the Cannon-Bard theory holds that the thalamus triggers feeling and arousal simultaneously and independently. Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory (1962) says that emotion requires arousal plus a cognitive label; in their adrenaline experiment, aroused participants without an explanation caught the euphoria or anger of a stooge. Misattribution of arousal (Dutton and Aron's bridge, 1974; Valins's false heartbeats) and Zillmann's excitation transfer follow from the theory.

11.3 Appraisal, feedback, evolution and opposing processes. Arnold (1960) introduced appraisal, and Lazarus built the cognitive appraisal (cognitive-mediational) theory: primary appraisal asks whether an event matters and whether it is harm, threat or challenge, secondary appraisal asks what can be done, and reappraisal revises both; the subincision film study showed that changing appraisals changes arousal. Zajonc argued for affective primacy from the mere exposure effect (1980, 1984), and Lazarus replied that appraisal can be automatic. The facial feedback hypothesis (Tomkins, Izard; Strack, Martin and Stepper's pen study, 1988) holds that expressions modulate feelings, although the effect is small. Darwin (1872) explained expressions by serviceable habits, antithesis and direct nervous action and founded the evolutionary view. Solomon and Corbit's opponent-process theory (1974) explains tolerance, withdrawal and acquired pleasures through a slow, strengthening b-process.

11.4 The emotional brain and body. The sympathetic division of the autonomic nervous system arouses (dilating the pupils, lowering skin resistance) and the parasympathetic division calms; the medulla of the hindbrain paces heartbeat and breathing, and the hypothalamus orchestrates. Bard's decorticate cats showed sham rage, organised by the hypothalamus; Hess elicited attack by hypothalamic stimulation. Papez (1937) proposed a circuit from hippocampus through fornix, mammillary bodies, anterior thalamus and cingulate gyrus; MacLean named the limbic system (1952), the classic "seat of emotion", and the triune brain. Bilateral temporal lobe removal produces Klüver-Bucy syndrome (tameness, psychic blindness, hyperorality, hypersexuality); the amygdala detects threat, supports fear conditioning and enhances emotional memory. LeDoux described a fast low road and a slower high road to the amygdala; affective blindsight shows emotional processing without awareness. Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis gives the ventromedial prefrontal cortex a role in decisions. The polygraph detects arousal, not lies; the guilty knowledge test (Lykken, 1959) is more accurate.

11.5 Expressing and reading emotion. Ekman and Friesen's studies of the Fore of New Guinea supported the universality of six facial expressions, with fear and surprise most often confused; blind athletes produce the same expressions as sighted ones. Display rules, cultural norms for showing emotion, explain cultural differences (Friesen's Japanese and American students, 1972), and emotional labour at work contributes to burnout. Recognition shows an in-group advantage. FACS (1978) codes expressions as muscle action units; the Duchenne smile involves the orbicularis oculi around the eyes; microexpressions may betray concealed feelings, but people detect lies at little better than chance. Body posture can be more informative than the face at high intensity. Women are on average more facially expressive and better at decoding nonverbal cues, differences attributed mainly to socialisation.

11.6 Four emotions up close. Fear is a response to present danger; anxiety is diffuse and future-oriented, and Spielberger separated state from trait anxiety. Anger is an emotion, aggression a behaviour and hostility an attitude; the catharsis hypothesis fails, because venting while ruminating increases aggression (Bushman, 2002). Diener's subjective well-being has three components: life satisfaction, positive affect and low negative affect. Helson's adaptation-level theory and Brickman and Campbell's hedonic treadmill explain why good fortune fades (lottery winners), and relative deprivation (Stouffer, 1949) explains why comparisons matter. Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory says positive emotions widen thought and build resources, and savoring (Bryant and Veroff) prolongs positive experience. Disgust, studied by Rozin, protects against contamination and follows the laws of contagion and similarity.

11.7 Emotional development and regulation. Bridges (1932) described differentiation from general excitement into distress and delight and then into specific emotions; primary emotions appear in the first six months, while self-conscious emotions such as embarrassment, pride, shame and guilt appear after 12 months, once self-recognition develops. Infants use social referencing (the visual cliff) and expect emotional reciprocity (Tronick's still face). Gross's process model (1998) lists five families of regulation strategies: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change (including reappraisal) and response modulation (including suppression); reappraisal is generally healthier than suppression. Alexithymia (Sifneos, 1973) is difficulty identifying and describing one's feelings. Goleman's five domains of emotional intelligence are pointed to Chapter 9.

11.8 Stress and the body's emergency systems. Stress has been defined as a stimulus (the stressor), as a response (Selye: the nonspecific response of the body to any demand) and, most usefully, as a transaction in which demands are appraised as exceeding resources. Cannon described the fight-or-flight response (1915), run by the fast sympathetic-adrenal-medullary axis with adrenaline and noradrenaline, and coined homeostasis. Selye's general adaptation syndrome (1936; The Stress of Life, 1956) has three stages: alarm, resistance and exhaustion, the last bringing diseases of adaptation; he also distinguished eustress from distress (1974). The slower HPA axis runs from hypothalamic CRH to anterior pituitary ACTH to cortisol from the adrenal cortex, with negative feedback; chronic activation produces allostatic load (McEwen). Taylor's tend-and-befriend (2000) describes an affiliative stress response, especially in females, involving oxytocin.

11.9 Sources of stress and the transactional model. Holmes and Rahe's Social Readjustment Rating Scale (1967) scores 43 life events in life change units (death of a spouse 100, marriage 50), counting positive as well as negative change, but it ignores appraisal and predicts illness only weakly; India has its own Presumptive Stressful Life Events Scale. Daily hassles (Kanner, Coyne, Schaefer and Lazarus, 1981) predict symptoms better than major events; uplifts matter less. Stressors may be acute or chronic, and cataclysmic, personal or background (Lazarus and Cohen, 1977). Frustration, conflict (Lewin's types and Miller's gradients), pressure and change are further sources. Work stress arises from environmental, organisational (task and role demands) and individual factors; role conflict, role ambiguity and Karasek's high-demand, low-control jobs are especially harmful, and chronic work stress can lead to burnout (Freudenberger, 1974; Maslach's three dimensions). Control and predictability reduce the damage of stressors (Weiss's rats). Lazarus and Folkman's transactional model (1984) explains stress through primary appraisal, secondary appraisal, coping and reappraisal, and the diathesis-stress model explains disorder as vulnerability triggered by stress.

11.10 Coping, personality and health. Coping is the changing effort to manage demands appraised as taxing. Problem-focused coping acts on the stressor and emotion-focused coping on the feelings; people use both, and the best strategy fits the controllability of the situation. Approach coping wins in the long run over avoidance coping; defence mechanisms are unconscious, coping is conscious. Social support (emotional, instrumental, informational and appraisal) protects health directly and by buffering stress; Weiss separated emotional from social isolation. Hardiness (Kobasa, 1979: commitment, control, challenge), dispositional optimism (Scheier and Carver), optimistic explanatory style and learned optimism (Seligman, building on learned helplessness), resilience (Werner's Kauai study) and Antonovsky's sense of coherence (comprehensibility, manageability, meaningfulness) protect against stress. Friedman and Rosenman's Type A pattern (competitiveness, time urgency, hostility) predicted heart disease in the Western Collaborative Group Study, but hostility is the toxic component; Type B is relaxed, Type C (cancer-prone) has weak support, and Denollet's Type D combines negative affectivity with social inhibition. Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977) frames health psychology; Ader and Cohen's conditioned immunosuppression (1975) founded psychoneuroimmunology, and chronic stress suppresses immunity and slows healing, while acute stress can boost innate immunity. Health behaviour is predicted by the health belief model, protection motivation theory and the theory of planned behaviour. Stress management uses progressive muscle relaxation (Jacobson), the relaxation response (Benson), biofeedback, meditation and mindfulness (Kabat-Zinn), exercise, cognitive restructuring and stress inoculation training (Meichenbaum).

From the past papers

  1. Which of the following is NOT one of the six universally recognized basic emotions described by Ekman? (RML 2025)

    (a) Anger (b) Fear (c) Jealousy (d) Disgust

    Answer: (c) Jealousy. Ekman's six are happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust (contempt was added in 1986). Jealousy is a complex social emotion, a blend of fear, anger and sadness about losing a relationship to a rival, and has no universal facial expression.

  2. Our current mood and feelings are best described by the term: (RML 2024)

    (a) Emotion (b) Mood (c) Affect (d) Feeling

    Answer: (c) Affect. Affect is the umbrella term for one's present feeling state and, clinically, its moment-to-moment expression. A mood is a sustained, pervasive state lasting hours or days; an emotion is a brief response to a specific event; a feeling is only the subjective part of an emotion.

  3. According to the James-Lange theory of emotion, what is the correct order of events? (RML 2025)

    (a) Event → Emotion → Interpretation → Arousal (b) Event → Arousal → Emotion (c) Event → Arousal → Interpretation → Emotion (d) Interpretation → Arousal → Emotion

    Answer: (c) Event, arousal, interpretation, emotion. In James-Lange the body reacts first and the felt emotion is the awareness (the "interpretation") of the bodily change. Option (b) is not false but leaves out that step, so the fuller textbook sequence (c) is keyed. Options (a) and (d) put emotion or interpretation before arousal, which reverses the theory.

  4. Schachter and Singer's theory of emotion differs from the theories of James-Lange and Cannon-Bard. How does it differ? (RML 2020)

    (a) It includes development and arousal (b) It includes the limbic system (c) It includes cognition and arousal (d) It has never been criticised

    Answer: (c) It includes cognition and arousal. The two-factor theory requires both physiological arousal and a cognitive label taken from the situation. It says nothing about development or the limbic system, and it has been criticised heavily, notably by the failed replications of Marshall and Zimbardo and of Maslach (1979).

  5. Which theory of emotion explains the phenomenon of misattribution of arousal (for example, feeling romantic attraction when actually scared)? (RML 2025)

    (a) James-Lange theory (b) Two-factor theory (c) Cannon-Bard theory (d) Facial feedback hypothesis

    Answer: (b) Two-factor theory. If emotion is arousal plus a label, the label can be wrong, as on the Capilano bridge. James-Lange and Cannon-Bard have no labelling step, and the facial feedback hypothesis concerns the effect of one's own expressions on feelings.

  6. According to experts on socioemotional development like Campos (2009) and Kagan (2010), self-conscious emotions appear at which age? (RML 2022)

    (a) 6 to 9 months (b) Before 9 months (c) After 12 months (d) 3 months

    Answer: (c) After 12 months. Embarrassment, pride, shame and guilt require a sense of self, which appears with mirror self-recognition at about 15 to 24 months; these emotions emerge from about 18 months. The earlier ages fit the primary emotions, which are present by about six months.

  7. The first emotions expressed by infants are: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Pain and anger (b) Rage and fear (c) Fear and euphoria (d) Delight and distress

    Answer: (d) Delight and distress. In Bridges's differentiation scheme (1932) the newborn's general excitement splits first into distress and delight, and only later into anger, fear, elation and the rest. Rage and fear (with love) are Watson's three innate emotions, a different and older claim.

  8. In James Gross's process model of emotion regulation, cognitive reappraisal belongs to which family of strategies? (Recall set)

    (a) Response modulation (b) Cognitive change (c) Situation selection (d) Attentional deployment

    Answer: (b) Cognitive change. Reappraisal changes the meaning of the situation before the emotional response is fully formed. Response modulation includes expressive suppression; situation selection is choosing which situations to enter; attentional deployment includes distraction and rumination.

  9. Savoring is a concept in Positive Psychology that involves: (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Avoiding negative experiences (b) Enhancing the enjoyment of positive experiences (c) Minimizing stress through relaxation (d) Reflecting on past traumas

    Answer: (b) Enhancing the enjoyment of positive experiences. Bryant and Veroff defined savoring as attending to, appreciating and prolonging good experiences, past, present or future. Avoiding bad experiences and relaxing away stress are forms of coping, not savoring, and dwelling on trauma is rumination.

  10. Barbara Fredrickson is known for her work on: (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Cognitive distortions (b) Flow theory (c) Broaden-and-build theory (d) Psychodynamic theory

    Answer: (c) Broaden-and-build theory. Fredrickson proposed that positive emotions broaden thought-action repertoires and build lasting resources. Flow is Csikszentmihalyi's, cognitive distortions are Beck's (and Burns's), and psychodynamic theory is Freud's.

  11. Choose the odd one out regarding the pattern of sympathetic activation during emotion: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Increased heart rate (b) Pupil constriction (c) Lowered skin resistance (d) Increased breathing rate

    Answer: (b) Pupil constriction. Sympathetic arousal dilates the pupils; constriction is parasympathetic. Faster heart rate and breathing, and lowered skin resistance from sweating (the galvanic skin response), are all sympathetic effects.

  12. Non-voluntary coping responses like heart-beat, respiration are modulated by: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Hypothalamus (b) Cerebellum (c) Hind brain (d) Amygdala

    Answer: (c) Hind brain. The cardiovascular and respiratory centres lie in the medulla oblongata, part of the hindbrain. The hypothalamus orchestrates them only indirectly, the amygdala triggers fear responses through the hypothalamus and brainstem, and the cerebellum coordinates movement.

  13. The seat of emotion is to be found in: (RML 2024)

    (a) Reticular formation (b) Hind brain (c) Limbic system (d) Forebrain

    Answer: (c) Limbic system. MacLean's limbic system (1952), including the amygdala, hypothalamus, hippocampus and cingulate gyrus, is the classic seat of emotion. The reticular formation governs arousal and wakefulness, the hindbrain vital functions, and "forebrain" is too broad an answer, although the limbic system lies within it.

  14. Which cluster of symptoms is seen in Klüver-Bucy syndrome? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Agnosia, aphasia, hyperorality, hyperphagia, hypersexuality (b) Agnosia, avolition, alogia, asexuality (c) Hypersexuality, altered emotional response, hyperorality, hyperalertness (d) Hypersexuality, hypo-sensitivity, delayed reaction

    Answer: (a). Bilateral temporal lobe (amygdala) damage produces psychic blindness (agnosia), hyperorality, hyperphagia and hypersexuality, and in humans often aphasia because the nearby temporal cortex is damaged. Option (c) is tempting, but the monkeys became placid, not hyperalert; avolition and alogia are negative symptoms of schizophrenia, and "asexuality" is the opposite of what happens.

  15. 'Psychological disorders develop when a biological predisposition is set off by a stressful situation', is proposed by: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Humanistic existential models (b) Socio-cultural model (c) Diathesis-stress model (d) Cognitive model

    Answer: (c) Diathesis-stress model. A diathesis (vulnerability) plus a stressful trigger is the definition of the model (Meehl, 1962; Zubin and Spring, 1977). Humanistic-existential models stress blocked growth and meaning, sociocultural models social forces, and cognitive models maladaptive thinking; none of them is defined by predisposition plus stress.

  16. In order, starting from the first stage, what are the stages of the general adaptation syndrome? (RML 2024)

    (a) Resistance, alarm, exhaustion (b) Alarm, resistance, exhaustion (c) Resistance, illness, alarm (d) Alarm, exhaustion, illness

    Answer: (b) Alarm, resistance, exhaustion. Selye's body first mobilises (alarm), then adapts (resistance), then, if stress continues, runs down (exhaustion). Illness is a consequence of exhaustion, not a stage, and resistance cannot come before the alarm that triggers it.

  17. Which endocrine gland is primarily responsible for controlling the body's stress response and stress-related metabolic changes? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Thyroid gland (b) Adrenal glands (c) Pituitary gland (d) Pancreas

    Answer: (b) Adrenal glands. The adrenal medulla releases adrenaline and noradrenaline (SAM axis) and the adrenal cortex releases cortisol (HPA axis). The pituitary releases ACTH but is not itself the stress gland; the thyroid sets metabolic rate and the pancreas controls blood sugar through insulin and glucagon.

  18. Which of the following would be considered an acute stressor? (RML 2024)

    (a) A pop quiz (b) HIV disease (c) Marital discord (d) Colon cancer

    Answer: (a) A pop quiz. An acute stressor is brief, with a clear beginning and end. HIV disease, cancer and a troubled marriage last for months or years with no clear end, so they are chronic stressors.

  19. Social Readjustment Rating Scale of Holmes and Rahe focuses upon: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Life events (b) Physiological reactions (c) Cognitive changes (d) Daily hassles

    Answer: (a) Life events. The SRRS (1967) scores 43 major life changes in life change units, from death of a spouse (100) to minor violations of the law (11). Daily hassles were measured by Kanner and colleagues (1981); physiological reactions belong to Selye's tradition and cognitive appraisal to Lazarus's.

  20. According to the Transactional Model of Stress, which term refers to the individual's assessment of whether they have the resources to cope with a stressor? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Initial threat evaluation (b) Secondary appraisal (c) Coping resource assessment (d) Situational capacity evaluation

    Answer: (b) Secondary appraisal. In Lazarus and Folkman's model (1984), primary appraisal judges whether an event is relevant and whether it is harm, threat or challenge; secondary appraisal judges the options and resources for coping. The other three phrases sound plausible but are not terms of the model.

  21. Which factor is an organizational stressor leading to job related stress? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Family and economic problems (b) Political uncertainty (c) Task and role demands (d) Number of dependents in family

    Answer: (c) Task and role demands. Organisational stressors arise within the job: task demands, role demands (role conflict, ambiguity, overload), interpersonal demands, structure and leadership. Family and economic problems and the number of dependants are individual factors; political uncertainty is an environmental factor.

  22. Type A behavior pattern is characterized by: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Non-competitiveness, impatience and hostility (b) Impatience, non-competitiveness, and aggression (c) Competitiveness, impatience and hostility (d) Hostility, lack of aggression and competitiveness

    Answer: (c) Competitiveness, impatience and hostility. Friedman and Rosenman's Type A combines competitive achievement striving, time urgency (impatience) and hostility. Every other option contains a "non-" or "lack of" that contradicts the pattern.

  23. Which of the following factor is most directly linked to a higher risk for coronary heart disease? (RML 2024)

    (a) Type A personality (b) Type B personality (c) Hostility (d) Self-efficacy

    Answer (as keyed): (a) Type A personality. Type A was the classic coronary-prone pattern of the Western Collaborative Group Study, and it is the keyed answer. Be aware, though, that later research identifies hostility, one component of Type A, as the part that carries most of the risk, so a stem asking for the "toxic component" of Type A should be answered with hostility. Type B is the low-risk pattern, and self-efficacy is a protective belief.

  24. The "D-type" personality, identified as a risk factor for poor prognosis in cardiovascular disease, is characterized by: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Hostility and competitiveness (b) The joint tendency to experience negative emotions and to inhibit self-expression in social situations (social inhibition) (c) An optimistic and resilient outlook on life (d) Perfectionism and rigidity

    Answer: (b). Denollet's Type D (distressed) personality is negative affectivity combined with social inhibition. Hostility and competitiveness describe Type A; optimism and resilience are protective factors; perfectionism and rigidity belong to obsessive-compulsive personality descriptions.

  25. Learned helplessness phenomenon is offered by: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Aaron T. Beck (b) Martin Seligman and Steven F. Maier (c) Harry Stack Sullivan (d) Daniel Kahneman

    Answer: (b) Martin Seligman and Steven F. Maier. Their dogs (1967), given inescapable shocks, later failed to escape avoidable ones; the idea became a model of depression and, through explanatory style, of learned optimism. Beck is known for cognitive therapy, Sullivan for the interpersonal theory of psychiatry and Kahneman for judgement heuristics and prospect theory.
