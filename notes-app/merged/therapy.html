Therapy, Counselling & Ethics

One consolidated chapter for everything the MET / NIMHANS paper tests on treatment: the learning principles that behaviour therapy is built on, the major schools of psychotherapy & counselling (Sharf's frame, school by school, with the third-wave and integrative models folded in), the common therapeutic factors that cut across schools, and professional ethics. Each concept lives in exactly one place. The exam tests four moves: name the theory from its claim; pin the theory to its founder; pin the named technique to its theory; and tell rival theories apart, because that is where the distractor always hides. Learn every technique in its contrast pair.

High-yield

The single most-tested skill is telling near-identical pairs apart: REBT (Ellis) vs Cognitive Therapy (Beck), systematic desensitisation (Wolpe) vs flooding, ERP (OCD) vs EMDR (trauma), cognitive triad (Beck) vs ABC (Ellis), DBT modules (4) vs ACT hexaflex (6), and the four family-therapy founders (Bowen / Minuchin / Haley / Satir). And in learning: shaping vs chaining, fading vs extinction, VR vs VI, latent vs insight learning. Master these pairs and most of the section answers itself.

1. Learning & conditioning: the foundation of behaviour therapy

Behaviour therapy is applied learning theory, so the vocabulary below is tested both as 1-mark definition traps and as the mechanism behind clinical techniques. Read this orientation once: classical (Pavlovian) conditioning is about stimulus pairings; operant (Skinnerian) conditioning is about consequences shaping voluntary behaviour; priming is about one stimulus making a later one easier to process; and habituation / sensitization are non-associative (a single stimulus, no pairing). When a question mixes the families, the answer is almost always the term whose family matches the stem.

1.1 Operant shaping tools — shaping, chaining, fading, prompting

Shaping (Skinner): reinforcing successive approximations of a target behaviour the organism does not yet emit. Train a rat to press a lever by reinforcing facing it, then approaching, then touching, then pressing. The defining feature is that the reinforcement criterion changes across trials. Clinically: skill acquisition, autism intervention.

Chaining: linking a sequence of already-learned behaviours into a longer chain, where each behaviour is the discriminative stimulus (SD) for the next and only the final link is reinforced (brushing teeth: pick up brush → wet → paste → brush → rinse → put away). Forward chaining teaches step 1 first; backward chaining teaches the last step first, so the learner always reaches the reinforcer at the natural endpoint (often preferred clinically). Used heavily in ABA / ADL skills training.

Prompting: an added antecedent stimulus (verbal, gestural, physical, modelled, visual) that helps the learner emit the right response in the presence of the natural cue. Prompt hierarchy, most → least intrusive: physical → modelling → gestural → verbal → visual/positional. "Least-to-most" vs "most-to-least" prompting are classic vignette distractors.

Fading: gradually removing the prompt while transferring stimulus control to the natural cue (teach "ball": full model "say ball" → partial "say buh…" → just holding up the ball).

Trap

Shaping vs chaining: shaping builds a new behaviour; chaining links existing behaviours. Fading vs extinction: fading removes the prompt (an antecedent) and transfers stimulus control; extinction removes the reinforcer / UCS and eliminates the behaviour. Prompts are antecedents; reinforcement is a consequence — that distinction has been tested directly.

1.2 Classical conditioning phenomena

Extinction: reduction and disappearance of a conditioned response when reinforcement (operant) or the UCS (classical) is no longer presented — Pavlov's dog stops salivating to the unpaired bell; the ignored tantrum fades. Watch for the extinction burst: a temporary increase in frequency/intensity right after reinforcement is withdrawn, before decline. Extinction does not erase learning.

Spontaneous recovery: reappearance of a previously extinguished response after a rest period, with no new conditioning trials — proving extinction is suppression, not unlearning. The recovered response is typically weaker, and re-extinction is faster the second time (savings effect). Distinguish from reinstatement (re-emergence after UCS-alone presentation) and renewal (re-emergence in a new context).

Generalization vs discrimination. Generalization = responding similarly to stimuli that resemble the CS / SD (Little Albert, conditioned by Watson & Rayner to fear a white rat, also feared a white rabbit, cotton wool, a Santa beard). The generalization gradient: response strength drops with distance from the original CS. Discrimination = responding only to the specific stimulus, taught by differential reinforcement (reinforce in presence of SD, withhold for SΔ). One-liner: generalization is the default; discrimination is trained. Failure to discriminate underlies many phobias. Note: stimulus generalization (similar CS) ≠ response generalization (different responses to the same CS).

Ratio = paid per response → high rates. Interval = paid per time → moderate rates. Variable = unpredictable → most resistant to extinction. Fixed = predictable → pause/scallop after reinforcement. Resistance to extinction: VR > VI > FR > FI > continuous (CRF). Continuous reinforcement learns fastest but extinguishes fastest (partial-reinforcement extinction effect).

1.4 Cognitive & social learning

Latent learning (Tolman): learning that occurs without reinforcement and without immediate behavioural expression, showing up later once reinforcement appears — proving learning ≠ performance. Tolman & Honzik (1930) rats: a group unreinforced for 10 days, then reinforced from day 11, immediately outperformed the always-reinforced group on day 12. Buzzword: cognitive map (Tolman, 1948, "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men"); the first big challenge to strict behaviourism.

Insight learning (Köhler, Gestalt): sudden understanding of a solution without trial-and-error. Köhler's chimp Sultan (1925) joined two short sticks to reach a banana after a pause — the "aha!" moment. Cognitive, not behaviourist; contrast with Thorndike's trial-and-error / law of effect (puzzle-box cats). Stem says "aha / sudden / no gradual improvement" → insight.

Observational learning (Bandura): learning by watching a model; vicarious reinforcement of the model is enough, no direct reinforcement of the learner required. Bobo doll (1961): children who watched an adult beat the doll did the same. Four processes (tested): Attention → Retention → Reproduction → Motivation (ARRM). Vicarious reinforcement/punishment modulate motivation. Bandura's framework = Social Learning Theory (1977), renamed Social Cognitive Theory (1986); home of self-efficacy and reciprocal determinism.

1.5 Priming, habituation & sensitization

Priming: exposure to one stimulus (the prime) unconsciously speeds the response to a later one (the target) by activating related memory representations — a form of implicit memory. Read NURSE → identify DOCTOR faster than BREAD. Subtypes: semantic (NURSE→DOCTOR), perceptual (briefly flashed word → faster identification), affective (smiling face → positive judgement). It is not classical conditioning (no UCS, reflex or pairing) and not recall (no conscious memory of the prime).

Habituation vs sensitization — the simplest, non-associative learning (Kandel's Nobel work on Aplysia). Habituation = a decrease in response to a repeated harmless stimulus (you stop hearing the wall clock; Aplysia's gill-withdrawal weakens with repeated mild touch); mechanism = reduced neurotransmitter release at the sensory-motor synapse. Sensitization = an increase in response after an intense/noxious stimulus (after a loud bang you flinch at every creak); mechanism = heterosynaptic facilitation via serotonergic interneurons → increased NT release. Habituation is stimulus-specific (a new stimulus restores the response → dishabituation).

Mnemonic

Habituation says "this is safe, ignore it"; sensitization says "the world is dangerous, react more."

Trap

Habituation vs sensory adaptation: habituation is in the CNS (learning); sensory adaptation is receptor fatigue (peripheral). Habituation vs extinction: habituation needs only one stimulus with no learning history; extinction requires a previously conditioned association. Latent vs insight: latent = hidden until reinforced (Tolman, rats); insight = sudden "aha" (Köhler, chimps). Observational learning ≠ imitation: imitation is one component; observational learning is the whole 4-stage ARRM process.

2. How to read any theory — the master frame

Sharf describes every theory on the same recurring template, and you can predict almost any "which approach" answer from where the theory sits on five axes. The book's chapter frame: (1) history of the originator · (2) theory of personality / view of human nature (how problems develop) · (3) goals of therapy · (4) techniques · (5) the therapeutic relationship · (6) uses / evidence · (7) criticisms.

Definition

A theory of psychotherapy = a coherent set of assumptions giving the clinician a map of personality, a set of goals, and a set of techniques that follow from the goals. A good theory (Sharf) is precise/clear, comprehensive, parsimonious, useful/applicable, and verifiable/testable.

The five predictive axes:

  • View of human nature — determined/pessimistic (psychoanalysis) vs free/optimistic/growth-oriented (person-centred, existential, Adlerian). Behavioural = neutral (a product of learning).
  • Role of the past — past-focused (psychoanalysis, Jungian) vs present/future-focused (behavioural, cognitive, REBT, reality, Gestalt, solution-focused, narrative).
  • Directiveness — highly directive (behaviour therapy, REBT, reality, strategic family) vs non-directive (person-centred, the extreme), with collaborative in between (Beck, Adlerian, solution-focused).
  • The relationship — the cure itself (person-centred) vs the site of the work via transference (psychoanalysis) vs a collaboration (cognitive, Adlerian, postmodern) vs a means/working alliance (behaviour therapy, REBT).
  • Insight vs action — insight-based (psychoanalysis, Jungian) vs action/doing-based (behavioural, reality, solution-focused, strategic); REBT and Beck use insight into thinking then behavioural action.
High-yield

The four "forces" / waves (NIMHANS): 1st = psychodynamic; 2nd = behavioural / cognitive-behavioural; 3rd = humanistic-existential; 4th (sometimes-cited) = multicultural / social-justice (and/or transpersonal). Useful for "which wave / force" stems.

3. Psychodynamic approaches

3.1 Psychoanalysis — Sigmund Freud

Originator. Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), Viennese neurologist. Working with hysteria (and Breuer's Anna O., source of the "talking cure") he concluded symptoms have hidden unconscious causes. The first systematic theory of psychotherapy, the tradition every later theory reacts to.

Personality / how problems develop. Behaviour is driven by instincts (life/Eros, energy = libido; death/Thanatos) and by an unconscious. The mind has a topographic layout (conscious / preconscious / unconscious) and a structural one: id (pleasure principle, primary process), ego (reality principle, secondary process, mediator), superego (conscience + ego ideal). Personality forms through five psychosexual stages — oral (0–1.5), anal (1.5–3), phallic (3–6: Oedipus/Electra), latency (6–puberty), genital (puberty–adulthood); over/under-gratification → fixation → adult character traits. Problems arise from unconscious conflict and unresolved early childhood; the ego manages anxiety with defence mechanisms (repression the master defence, plus projection, displacement, reaction formation, sublimation, denial, regression, rationalisation — catalogued by Anna Freud).

Definition

Vaillant's defence hierarchy: psychotic (denial, distortion) → immature (projection, acting out, regression, somatization) → neurotic (intellectualisation, displacement, reaction formation, repression, isolation of affect) → mature (humour, sublimation, suppression, altruism, anticipation).

Goals. Make the unconscious conscious; bring repressed material into awareness so the ego can deal with it; strengthen the ego; resolve childhood conflicts re-enacted in the present. Insight is the engine of change.

Techniques. Free association (the fundamental rule, censorship off); dream analysis (the "royal road to the unconscious"; separate manifest from latent content); analysis and interpretation of resistance and transference; attention to parapraxes (Freudian slips); and working through — repeated re-examination of a conflict across shifting contexts until insight is integrated and behaviour changes (this distinguishes intellectual from emotional/behavioural insight).

Relationship. Centres on transference: the client transfers feelings about early figures onto the analyst, and analysing that is the work. The analyst stays relatively neutral (blank-screen); countertransference (the analyst's own reactions) must be monitored.

Frequency. Classical psychoanalysis: 3–5×/week, couch, years. Psychoanalytic / psychodynamic therapy: 1–2×/week, face-to-face, months–years.

Uses / evidence. Originally neurosis/hysteria; now personality and relational problems. Built on rich but unrepeatable case studies; modern brief psychodynamic therapy gives more empirical footing.

Criticisms. Long, expensive, largely unfalsifiable; overweighted on sex and the deterministic past; biased sample; little support for the psychosexual stages.

Trap

Free association + dream analysis + transference = Freud. The defining technique is free association; the defining relationship mechanism is the transference. Working through is NOT free association and NOT a single interpretation — it is the repeated re-encounter with the same conflict in shifting contexts until consolidated. Classic MET/NIMHANS distractor.

3.3 Jungian analysis — analytical psychology

Originator. Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961), Swiss psychiatrist, Freud's "crown prince" until their 1913 break over the sexual nature of libido.

Personality. Jung kept the unconscious but enlarged it and de-sexualised libido into general psychic energy. The psyche = ego (consciousness) + personal unconscious (your own forgotten/repressed material, organised into emotionally charged complexes) + collective unconscious (an inherited layer shared by all humanity, holding the archetypes: persona, shadow, anima/animus, the self). Coined introversion / extraversion, added four functions (thinking, feeling, sensing, intuiting) → psychological types. Problems reflect one-sided development and failure of individuation, especially at midlife.

Goals. Individuation: integrate conscious and unconscious, persona and shadow, into a balanced self.

Techniques. Dream analysis in series with amplification (linking symbols to myth and culture) rather than free association; active imagination (dialoguing with inner images); the word-association test (reveals complexes). Four therapy stages: confession, elucidation, education, transformation.

Relationship. A collaborative mutual encounter; both parties are changed by the work.

Criticisms. Mystical, obscure, hard to test; collective unconscious and archetypes resist verification (the MBTI descends from his types but has weak psychometrics).

Trap

Collective unconscious + archetypes + individuation + amplification = Jung, NOT Freud (Freud = personal unconscious only). Dreams: amplification (Jung) vs free association (Freud).

3.4 Adlerian therapy — individual psychology

Originator. Alfred Adler (1870–1937), Viennese; broke from Freud in 1911 over the sexual emphasis. A major forerunner of humanistic and cognitive therapy.

Personality. The one dynamic force is striving for superiority (really, striving for competence/completion). Everyone starts with inferiority; healthy growth compensates, derailment yields an inferiority complex or over-compensating superiority complex. Each person builds a unique style of life by about age 5; we are pulled by future fictional goals (fictional finalism); we are not passive (creative power of the self); the mark of health is social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl). Birth order shapes the style of life. Problems = a mistaken style of life + discouragement / low social interest.

Goals. Foster social interest, correct the mistaken style of life and faulty basic mistakes, replace discouragement with encouragement.

Techniques. Lifestyle assessment, early recollections (earliest memory reveals the style of life), birth-order analysis, family constellation, encouragement, "the question" ("how would your life be different if you were well?"), acting as if, catching oneself, spitting in the client's soup, and paradoxical intention.

Relationship. Egalitarian, collaborative, encouraging.

Trap

Inferiority/superiority complex · style of life · social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) · birth order · early recollections · encouragement = Adler. Match the three "wills": will to pleasure = Freud, will to power/superiority = Adler, will to meaning = Frankl.

4. Humanistic & existential approaches

4.1 Existential therapy — May, Yalom, Frankl

Originators. A movement, not one founder: Rollo May (brought existentialism to US psychology; existential vs neurotic anxiety, the daimonic, freedom and destiny), Irvin Yalom (the ultimate concerns), Viktor Frankl (logotherapy, forged in the camps). Roots in Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, Binswanger, Boss.

Human nature. No fixed human nature; we are free, responsible, and create ourselves through choices (existence precedes essence). Distress comes from facing, or fleeing, the ultimate concerns / givens of existence. Yalom's four givens: death, freedom (and its responsibility), isolation, meaninglessness. Avoiding these through inauthenticity ("bad faith") produces existential anxiety/guilt. Three modes of being-in-the-world: Umwelt (physical), Mitwelt (social), Eigenwelt (self).

Goals. Live authentically, accept freedom and responsibility, confront the givens, find or create meaning. Not symptom removal.

Techniques. Deliberately light on technique; the relationship is the method. Frankl's logotherapy is the named exception: paradoxical intention (deliberately wish for the feared thing to break anticipatory anxiety), dereflection (shift attention away from the self/symptom), Socratic dialogue toward meaning. Frankl's three sources of meaning: creative (work/contribution), experiential (love/beauty), attitudinal (stance toward unavoidable suffering).

Relationship. A real, authentic I–Thou (Buber) encounter; presence over technique.

Uses / criticisms. Grief, terminal illness, life transitions, "existential vacuum." Vague, hard to operationalise; demands an articulate client.

Trap

Logotherapy · will to meaning · existential vacuum · paradoxical intention · dereflection = Frankl. The four givens = Yalom. Rollo May brought existentialism to American therapy. Note: paradoxical intention appears in Frankl AND in Adlerian/strategic work — as a named logotherapy technique it's Frankl; in family work the context decides.

4.2 Person-centred therapy — Carl Rogers

Originator. Carl Rogers (1902–1987), American; created client-centred (later person-centred) therapy and pioneered recorded-session research.

Human nature. Deeply optimistic: every organism has an innate actualising tendency toward growth. Trouble starts when the child, needing love, takes on conditions of worth ("I'm valued only if…") and distorts/denies experience that doesn't fit the self-concept. The gap between organismic experience and self-concept is incongruence, the source of anxiety.

Goals. Reduce incongruence; drop conditions of worth; reconnect with organismic valuing; become a fully functioning person (open to experience, present-living, self-trusting, free, creative).

Techniques. Famously non-directive — no diagnosis, no advice, no interpretation. Change comes from the relationship itself. The core triad the therapist provides: congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard (UPR), and empathic understanding. These sit within six necessary and sufficient conditions (1957): two persons in psychological contact; the client is incongruent/anxious; the therapist is congruent; the therapist feels UPR; the therapist feels empathy; and the client perceives the empathy and regard. Skills: reflection of feeling, active listening. Outcome marker: self vs ideal-self congruence (the Q-sort).

Mnemonic

"EUC" — Empathy, UPR, Congruence. Lock the count: six conditions total, of which the therapist supplies three (the core triad), and they are necessary AND sufficient.

Trap

The core triad (congruence, UPR, empathy) is Rogers; so are "six necessary and sufficient conditions," "fully functioning person," and "actualising tendency." There is no "interpretation" in Rogers (that's Freud). And note the stem flip: "the relationship is necessary and sufficient" = Rogers; "necessary but not sufficient" = Beck / CBT.

4.3 Gestalt therapy — Fritz Perls

Originator. Fritz (Frederick) Perls (1893–1970), with Laura Perls and Paul Goodman. Roots in Gestalt perceptual psychology (figure/ground), psychoanalysis, existentialism.

Human nature. A whole person in constant contact with the environment, organising experience into figure (salient now) and ground. Health = staying aware in the here-and-now and completing the cycle of need → contact → satisfaction → withdrawal. Trouble = unfinished business (unexpressed past feelings intruding now) and contact-boundary disturbances: introjection (swallowing others' values whole), projection, retroflection (turning back on the self what you'd do to others), confluence (no boundary), deflection. Perls described five layers of neurosis (phony, phobic, impasse, implosive, explosive).

Goals. Restore present awareness; re-own disowned parts; finish unfinished business; move from environmental support to self-support and integration of polarities.

Techniques / experiments. The empty-chair technique (dialogue with an absent person or a part of the self); the two-chair / top-dog vs under-dog dialogue (the moralising "shoulds" tyrant vs the evasive resister within); staying with feeling; exaggeration; the "I take responsibility" exercise; dream work (every dream element = a projected part of the self, acted out rather than interpreted); attention to body language and language ("it" → "I").

Relationship. Active, present-centred, I–Thou dialogic; the therapist may confront and frustrate avoidance.

Trap

Empty chair · top-dog vs under-dog · here-and-now awareness · unfinished business · figure/ground · layers of neurosis = Gestalt / Perls. Don't assume "chair → couch → Freud"; the empty chair is Gestalt, not psychoanalysis, and not the "miracle question" (SFBT).

5. Behavioural & cognitive approaches

5.1 Behaviour therapy

Originators. No single founder. Roots in classical conditioning (Pavlov; Watson & Rayner's Little Albert), operant conditioning (Skinner), social learning (Bandura), and clinical pioneers Joseph Wolpe (systematic desensitisation), Hans Eysenck, Arnold Lazarus (later, multimodal therapy).

Personality / how problems develop. No deep "personality" structure; behaviour (including symptoms) is learned and maintained by antecedents and consequences. A phobia is a conditioned fear; a habit is a reinforced operant. Strong focus on observable behaviour, the present, and measurement. Begins with a behavioural assessment / functional analysis (A–B–C: antecedent, behaviour, consequence).

Goals. Concrete, specific, measurable behavioural change agreed with the client.

Relationship. Important but not curative; the therapist is an active teacher / trainer / consultant who forms a working alliance and reinforces progress.

Uses / evidence. Phobias, anxiety, OCD, habit disorders, skills deficits, institutional/developmental settings. The strongest empirical evidence base of the classical theories.

Criticisms. Can ignore feelings, insight, the relationship and the underlying cause; risk of symptom focus.

Trap

Systematic desensitisation (Wolpe) vs flooding: desensitisation = graded hierarchy + relaxation (reciprocal inhibition), gentle. Flooding = maximal exposure, no hierarchy, no relaxation. Same idea (exposure), opposite intensity. ERP is the first-line behavioural treatment for OCD (SSRIs = first-line pharmacotherapy); systematic desensitisation is for phobias, not OCD. Token economy = operant/Skinnerian; modeling = Bandura; aversion = pairing with an unpleasant stimulus.

5.2 Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) — Albert Ellis

Originator. Albert Ellis (1913–2007), American; created REBT in the mid-1950s — the first of the cognitive-behavioural therapies (predates Beck).

Personality / how problems develop. It is not events that disturb us but our beliefs about them. Disturbance comes from irrational beliefs — rigid absolutist demands phrased as musts, shoulds, oughts (musturbation) — plus their derivatives: awfulising/catastrophising, low frustration tolerance ("I-can't-stand-it-itis"), and global self/other-rating / damnation. The three core irrational demands: "I must do well; others must treat me well; the world must be easy/fair."

Definition

The A-B-C-D-E model. A = activating event; B = belief (rational or irrational); C = emotional/behavioural consequence. The insight: it is B, not A, that causes C. Therapy adds D = disputing the irrational belief (logical, empirical, pragmatic), leading to E = a new effective philosophy (sometimes A-B-C-D-E-F, F = new feeling).

Goals. Identify and dispute irrational beliefs, replace them with flexible preferences, reach deep philosophical change and unconditional self-acceptance.

Techniques. Active-directive disputing; cognitive (cost-benefit analysis); emotive (rational-emotive imagery, shame-attacking exercises, humour, forceful self-statements); behavioural (homework, risk-taking, in-vivo desensitisation, reinforcement). Heavy homework and psychoeducation.

Relationship. Warm but secondary; not "necessary" in the Rogerian sense. The therapist is a forceful, active, sometimes blunt teacher/debater who models unconditional acceptance while attacking the beliefs.

Criticisms. Confrontational style can clash with some clients/cultures; "rational vs irrational" can be a value judgement.

5.3 Cognitive therapy — Aaron Beck

Originator. Aaron T. Beck (1921–2021), American psychiatrist; developed cognitive therapy in the 1960s out of his research on depression (daughter Judith Beck carried it forward).

Personality / how problems develop. Emotion and behaviour are shaped by how we interpret events. Early experience lays down schemas (core beliefs) → which generate, in the moment, automatic thoughts (rapid, plausible appraisals just below awareness) → filtered through cognitive distortions: all-or-nothing/dichotomous thinking, overgeneralisation, magnification/minimisation (catastrophising), selective abstraction/mental filter, disqualifying the positive, arbitrary inference, personalisation, labelling, "should" statements, mind-reading/fortune-telling (jumping to conclusions), emotional reasoning. Depression is marked by the cognitive triad: negative view of the self, the world, and the future.

Goals. Identify, test and correct distorted thinking; change automatic thoughts and underlying schemas; teach the client to become their own therapist (cognitive restructuring).

Techniques. Socratic questioning / guided discovery; identifying and testing automatic thoughts (thought records, dysfunctional-thought log); collaborative empiricism (client + therapist treat beliefs as hypotheses and gather evidence); behavioural experiments; activity scheduling and mastery-and-pleasure ratings; graded task assignment; decatastrophising; downward-arrow to reach core schemas; homework.

Relationship. Genuine, warm, collaborative (Rogers' conditions are necessary but not sufficient); the hallmark is collaborative empiricism — not arguing, but jointly investigating.

Uses / evidence. The strongest evidence base of any single approach for depression and anxiety; foundation of modern CBT; extended to OCD, PTSD, eating disorders, psychosis (CBTp).

Trap

REBT (Ellis) vs Cognitive Therapy (Beck) — the most-tested pair. Ellis = A-B-C(DE), irrational beliefs, musturbation, disputing; directive, confrontational, philosophical — the therapist argues the belief is illogical. Beck = automatic thoughts, cognitive distortions, schemas, cognitive triad, collaborative empiricism; Socratic, gentler — the client tests the thought as a hypothesis against evidence. "Dispute the irrational belief" = Ellis; "test the automatic thought against the evidence" = Beck. The cognitive triad is Beck; the ABC is Ellis — examiners swap these constantly. REBT predates Beck.

5.4 Reality therapy / Choice theory — William Glasser

Originator. William Glasser (1925–2013), American psychiatrist; reality therapy in the 1960s, later grounded in choice theory (originally "control theory").

Personality / how problems develop. Behaviour is our best attempt to meet five innate basic needs: survival, love/belonging, power/achievement, freedom, fun. We act to close the gap between what we want (our "quality world") and what we perceive we're getting. Choice theory: we choose nearly all our behaviour and are responsible for it. Total behavior has four inseparable components — acting, thinking, feeling, physiology — and we have the most direct control over acting and thinking (Glasser uses verbs like "depressing," "anxietying"). Problems = irresponsible choices and unsatisfying relationships, not "mental illness."

Definition

The WDEP system (formalised by Robert Wubbolding): W = explore Wants (needs / quality world); D = examine Doing / current behaviour; E = Evaluation (the client self-evaluates: "is what you're doing getting you what you want?"); P = make a Plan. Plans should be SAMIC (simple, attainable, measurable, immediate, controlled by the client). No excuses, no punishment, no giving up.

Relationship. Warm and involved first (build involvement), then focus on present behaviour and choices; firm but caring, refuses excuses.

Trap

WDEP · choice theory · total behavior · the five needs = Glasser (WDEP formalised by Wubbolding). Don't confuse Glasser's five needs with Maslow's hierarchy, or WDEP with MI's PACE/OARS. Reality therapy is present-focused, NOT past-focused.

6. Constructivist, feminist & systemic approaches

6.1 Solution-Focused Brief Therapy & Narrative therapy

These constructivist / postmodern approaches share a premise: reality is socially constructed through language and story, so therapy works on meaning, not a fixed pathology. Both are brief, collaborative, resolutely non-pathologising, and treat the client as the expert.

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) — Steve de Shazer & Insoo Kim Berg (Brief Family Therapy Center, Milwaukee; building on MRI brief therapy and Milton Erickson). Core idea: don't analyse the problem, build the solution; focus on what the client wants and on times the problem is already absent. Signature techniques: the miracle question ("suppose a miracle solved the problem overnight — what would you notice tomorrow?"), exception questions ("when is the problem not happening?"), scaling questions ("on 0–10, where are you, and what is +1?"), the formula first-session task, coping questions, compliments. Rule of thumb: if it works, do more; if not, do something different. 4–8 sessions typical.

Narrative therapy — Michael White (Australia) & David Epston (New Zealand). We live by stories we tell about ourselves; a problem-saturated "dominant story" traps a person. "The problem is the problem; the person is not the problem." Techniques: externalising the problem (talk about "the Anger" or "the OCD" as separate, often by naming it), mapping its influence, finding unique outcomes / sparkling moments (times the person resisted the problem), re-authoring, therapeutic documents/letters, recruiting outsider witnesses / a definitional ceremony.

Trap

Miracle / exception / scaling questions = SFBT (de Shazer & Berg). Externalising · re-authoring · unique outcomes = Narrative (White & Epston). Both are postmodern and "client-as-expert," but SFBT builds the solution/future while narrative re-writes the story. The "miracle question" is the most-tested single item — it's de Shazer, NOT Gestalt's empty chair.

6.2 Feminist therapy

Originators. A movement from the 1960s–70s women's movement (Jean Baker Miller, Carolyn Enns, Laura Brown); often integrated with other approaches.

How problems develop. Distress cannot be separated from the social/political context, especially gender-role socialisation and the unequal distribution of power. Much "pathology" in women is a reasonable response to oppression, sexism and trauma. Slogan: "the personal is political."

Goals. Symptom relief plus empowerment, self-definition, awareness of how gender-role/power dynamics shaped the problem, and (often) social action.

Techniques. Gender-role analysis and intervention; power analysis; assertiveness training; reframing/relabelling (move blame from the individual to the social cause); bibliotherapy; therapist self-disclosure; demystifying therapy.

Relationship. Deliberately egalitarian — minimise the power differential, treat the client as an equal collaborator.

Trap

"The personal is political" · gender-role analysis · power analysis · egalitarian relationship = feminist therapy.

6.3 Family systems therapies

The unit of treatment shifts from the individual to the system. Drawing on general systems theory and cybernetics, a symptom in one member (the "identified patient") expresses a dysfunction in the whole family's patterns. Shared concepts: homeostasis (the system resists change), circular causality (a mutual loop, not A→B), feedback loops, boundaries, subsystems.

7. Third-wave, integrative & specialised models

These are recognition-item favourites ("which of these is a component of ___?"). The trap is always a real concept from a different therapy, so learn the named parts of each.

7.1 Transactional Analysis (TA) — Eric Berne

Originator. Eric Berne (1910–1970); popularised in Games People Play. Personality has three ego states: Parent (taught — nurturing or critical), Adult (rational here-and-now processor), Child (felt — spontaneous or adapted). Transactions can be complementary (smooth), crossed (conflict), or ulterior (hidden agenda). Early decisions form a life script; we exchange strokes (units of recognition); we play repetitive games ("Why Don't You — Yes But") paying off in bad feelings. Four life positions, healthy = "I'm OK–You're OK." Goals: autonomy (awareness, spontaneity, intimacy), strengthen the Adult, rewrite the script via structural / game / script analysis; often contract-based and in groups.

7.2 DBT — Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (Linehan, 1993)

Synthesis of behavioural change + Zen acceptance ("dialectics"). Primary target: BPD with chronic suicidality. Four skill modules: mindfulness · distress tolerance · emotion regulation · interpersonal effectiveness. Four treatment modes: individual therapy + group skills training + phone/between-session coaching + therapist consultation team (~1 year). Target hierarchy: life-threatening → therapy-interfering → quality-of-life-interfering behaviours.

Mnemonic

DBT modules — "My Dog Eats Iguanas": Mindfulness, Distress tolerance, Emotion regulation, Interpersonal effectiveness.

7.3 ACT — Acceptance & Commitment Therapy (Hayes)

Goal = psychological flexibility, not symptom reduction. The Hexaflex (6 processes): Acceptance · Cognitive Defusion · Self-as-Context · Present-Moment Contact · Values · Committed Action. Each opposed by an inflexibility process (experiential avoidance, fusion, attachment to the conceptualised self, etc.). Underpinned by Relational Frame Theory (RFT). Techniques: defusion ("I'm having the thought that…"), values clarification, metaphors (passengers on the bus, tug-of-war with a monster).

7.4 Mindfulness-based programmes

MBSR — Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (Kabat-Zinn, 1979): 8-week group; body scan, sitting meditation, mindful movement (yoga); chronic pain, stress, anxiety. MBCT — Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (Segal, Williams, Teasdale): 8-week group combining MBSR + cognitive therapy; indication = relapse prevention in recurrent depression (≥3 episodes).

Trap

MBCT is for preventing relapse, not treating an acute episode. MBSR came first (1979); MBCT added the cognitive layer.

7.5 IPT — Interpersonal Therapy (Klerman & Weissman, 1984)

Time-limited (12–16 sessions), originally for MDD; evidence extends to bulimia, postpartum and adolescent depression. Four problem areas (pick one focus): grief · role disputes · role transitions · interpersonal deficits. Deliberately stays in the here-and-now of relationships, not the past.

Trap

IPT's four areas are NOT "thoughts / feelings / behaviours / body" and NOT "transference." If you see "role dispute / role transition" → IPT.

7.6 Motivational Interviewing (Miller & Rollnick, 1991)

A client-centred but directive method to resolve ambivalence and elicit change talk. Spirit — PACE: Partnership, Acceptance, Compassion, Evocation. Processes: Engaging → Focusing → Evoking → Planning. Core skills — OARS: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries. Change talk — DARN-CAT: Desire, Ability, Reason, Need → Commitment, Activation, Taking steps. "Roll with resistance," develop discrepancy, support self-efficacy. Indication: ambivalence, especially SUDs; also weight loss, medication adherence. Pairs naturally with the stages of change.

7.7 Trauma-focused therapies

  • EMDR (Francine Shapiro, 1987): 8-phase protocol pairing brief recall of the trauma memory with bilateral stimulation (eye movements / taps / tones). PTSD (APA, NICE, WHO endorsed). Mechanism debated (working-memory taxation, REM-like processing, dual attention); comparable to trauma-focused CBT.
  • CPT — Cognitive Processing Therapy (Resick & Schnicke): 12-session manualised TF-CBT for PTSD; stuck points → impact statement → Socratic dialogue + worksheets.
  • Prolonged Exposure (Foa): imaginal + in-vivo exposure to trauma memories and avoided stimuli; manualised; equally evidence-based for PTSD.
Trap

ERP vs EMDR: ERP (exposure + response prevention) = OCD; EMDR (8 phases + bilateral eye movements) = trauma. Different conditions, easily confused.

Definition

Trauma therapist lineage (high-yield): Pierre Janet (dissociation, traumatic memory, predates Freud) · Judith Herman (Complex PTSD; 3-stage recovery: safety → remembrance/mourning → reconnection) · Bessel van der Kolk ("the body keeps the score," somatics, polyvagal) · Edna Foa (Prolonged Exposure; ERP for OCD; emotional processing theory) · Patricia Resick (CPT) · Francine Shapiro (EMDR) · Peter Levine (Somatic Experiencing) · Marsha Linehan (DBT).

7.8 Specialised models for BPD & integration

TFP — Transference-Focused Psychotherapy (Kernberg): twice-weekly psychodynamic, here-and-now transference interpretation, "object relations dyads." MBT — Mentalization-Based Treatment (Bateman & Fonagy): builds the capacity to understand one's own and others' mental states; day-hospital and outpatient versions.

Integrative / eclectic routes (Sharf): technical eclecticism (pick whatever techniques work, regardless of theory — Lazarus's multimodal therapy, the BASIC ID: Behaviour, Affect, Sensation, Imagery, Cognition, Interpersonal, Drugs/biology); theoretical integration (blend theories into a new whole — Wachtel's cyclical psychodynamics); the common-factors route; and assimilative integration (work from one home theory, importing techniques). Fit the therapy to the client.

Trap

Ego states (Parent–Adult–Child) · games · scripts · strokes · life positions = TA / Berne. Multimodal therapy / BASIC ID / technical eclecticism = Arnold Lazarus. Don't decide DBT vs ACT on the word "mindfulness" alone — both use it; DBT has 4 modules, ACT has 6 hexaflex processes.

8. Group therapy — Yalom

The group is a social microcosm: relationship patterns from outside life replay in the group, and the therapist's job is to surface the here-and-now process. Format: 6–10 members + therapist(s). Bion's basic-assumption groups (dependency, fight-flight, pairing) are influential background.

Definition

Yalom's 11 therapeutic factors: (1) instillation of hope · (2) universality ("I'm not the only one") · (3) imparting information · (4) altruism · (5) corrective recapitulation of the primary family group · (6) development of socialising techniques · (7) imitative behaviour · (8) interpersonal learning (the engine, paired with here-and-now) · (9) group cohesiveness (the group analogue of the therapeutic alliance) · (10) catharsis · (11) existential factors (responsibility, isolation, meaning, mortality).

Trap

Tag words social microcosm + here-and-now + interpersonal learning → Yalom. NOT a Yalom factor: "transference interpretation," "free association," "the miracle question."

9. Common factors & the therapeutic frame

Beyond Sharf's schools, NIMHANS leans on the research finding that the schools share more than they differ.

High-yield

Common factors & the Dodo-bird verdict. Across bona-fide therapies, much outcome is explained by common factors — the therapeutic alliance, empathy, expectancy/hope, a believable rationale + ritual, goal consensus — rather than specific techniques. The "Dodo-bird verdict" (Rosenzweig; championed by Luborsky and Bruce Wampold's contextual model): "everyone has won and all must have prizes," i.e. all therapies achieve broadly comparable outcomes. The working alliance (Bordin: bond + goals + tasks) is the most robust predictor of outcome.

Outcome variance (Lambert, Wampold) roughly splits: ~40% client + extra-therapeutic factors · ~30% relationship (alliance) · ~15% expectancy/placebo · ~15% specific technique. Implication: most schools work, mostly via common factors. Specific techniques matter most for specific disorders with strong protocols (ERP for OCD, exposure for PTSD, behavioural activation for depression).

Definition

Stages of change — the Transtheoretical Model (Prochaska & DiClemente): precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance (relapse possible). Match the intervention to the stage — "the client isn't resistant, they're precontemplative."

Definition

Multicultural counselling competencies (Sue & Sue, the "fourth force"): a competent counsellor has awareness (of own biases/worldview), knowledge (of the client's culture), and skills (culturally appropriate interventions). Watch for cultural encapsulation and etic vs emic.

11. Professional ethics

Tested two ways: straight definition ("non-maleficence means ___") or a vignette ("a clinician does X — which principle is violated?"). The four big ones are Beauchamp & Childress's principles; the rest are the day-to-day rules that fall out of them.

Mnemonic

The four core principles — "A-B-N-J" / "Auto Brings No Justice": Autonomy · Beneficence · Non-maleficence · Justice.

PrincipleOne lineIn practice
AutonomyRespect the client's right to decide for themselvesInformed consent · right to refuse · no coercion
BeneficenceAct for the client's goodChoosing the treatment most likely to help; promoting wellbeing
Non-maleficence"First, do no harm" (primum non nocere)Not using unproven/harmful methods; staying within competence
JusticeFairness — treat like cases alike, distribute resources fairlyNo discrimination; fair access; equitable waitlists

11.1 Autonomy cluster

Informed consent: the client understands the nature, risks, benefits and alternatives, and agrees voluntarily and with capacity. Three pillars: disclosure · capacity · voluntariness. Assent: agreement from someone who can't legally consent (a minor) — you get their assent alongside the parent's consent. Right to refuse / withdraw at any time without penalty.

Trap

Paternalism = overriding a competent person's choice "for their own good" — the violation of autonomy. Only justified when the person genuinely lacks capacity. Consent vs assent: adults with capacity give consent; minors give assent (parent consents).

11.2 Confidentiality and its limits

Privacy = the client's right; confidentiality = your duty to protect it; privileged communication = the legal protection in court. Limits / when you must break it: risk of serious harm to self or others · suspected child/elder abuse (mandatory reporting) · court order.

High-yield

Duty to warn / protect (Tarasoff): if a client makes a credible threat against an identifiable person, the therapist must act to protect that person — non-maleficence/beneficence to a third party overrides confidentiality. Very common vignette. Confidentiality is not absolute.

11.4 Indian & international context

RCI (Rehabilitation Council of India) licenses clinical psychologists and has its own code of ethics. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 codifies advance directives, nominated representative, right to confidentiality, and consent for persons with mental illness, and decriminalises suicide attempt. The APA Ethics Code is the international reference; its five general principles: beneficence/non-maleficence, fidelity/responsibility, integrity, justice, respect for rights & dignity. Multicultural competence (Sue & Sue): awareness, knowledge, skills.

12.2 The traps that catch everyone

  1. Triad vs ABC. Cognitive triad = Beck; ABC = Ellis. Both CBT-family, easy to swap.
  2. REBT (Ellis) vs CT (Beck). Ellis disputes irrational beliefs (directive, confrontational); Beck tests automatic thoughts as hypotheses (collaborative, Socratic). REBT predates Beck.
  3. Systematic desensitisation vs flooding. Graded + relaxation (Wolpe) vs intense, no hierarchy, no relaxation.
  4. ERP vs EMDR. ERP = OCD; EMDR (8 phases + eye movements) = trauma.
  5. DBT (4 modules) vs ACT (6 hexaflex processes). Both use "mindfulness" — don't decide on that word alone.
  6. Working through = repeated re-encounter with a conflict until integrated, NOT free association and NOT one interpretation.
  7. Empty chair = Gestalt, not psychoanalysis ("chair → couch → Freud" is wrong).
  8. Rogers' three core conditions = empathy, UPR, congruence — no "interpretation." Relationship "necessary AND sufficient" = Rogers; "necessary but not sufficient" = Beck/CBT.
  9. IPT four areas = grief, role disputes, role transitions, interpersonal deficits (not "thoughts/feelings/behaviours/body").
  10. MI spirit = PACE; skills = OARS. MI is the opposite of confronting/arguing the client out of it.
  11. Family swaps: genogram → Bowen; enactment/boundaries → Minuchin; directives/paradox → Haley; sculpting/stances → Satir.
  12. Paradoxical intention appears in BOTH Frankl (logotherapy) and strategic family therapy — read the stem for context.
  13. The three wills: pleasure = Freud, power/superiority = Adler, meaning = Frankl.
  14. Glasser's five needs ≠ Maslow's hierarchy.
