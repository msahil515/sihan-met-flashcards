In the published report he is simply "Albert B.", the son of a wet nurse at the Harriet Lane Home for Invalid Children, a hospital attached to Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore. When Albert was about nine months old, two psychologists, John B. Albert reached for some of them and stared at others. He was afraid of none of them.

Only one thing reliably upset him. When a long steel bar was struck sharply with a hammer just behind his head, Albert startled violently; at the second blow his lips puckered and trembled; at the third he broke into a sudden fit of crying. Watson and Rayner had found what they were looking for: a stimulus that produced fear without any learning at all.

When Albert was eleven months old the experiment proper began. The white rat was taken from its basket and offered to him, and just as his hand touched it the bar was struck behind his head. Albert jumped violently and fell forward, burying his face in the mattress. The pairing was repeated once more that day, and he began to whimper. A week later the rat and the noise were paired five more times, seven pairings in all. Then the rat was shown alone, in silence. The instant he saw it, Albert began to cry, toppled over, raised himself on all fours and crawled away so fast that he was caught only just before he reached the edge of the table.

Five days later he was tested again. Now he whimpered and withdrew from things that had never been paired with the noise: a rabbit, a dog, a sealskin coat, cotton wool, a Santa Claus mask. His wooden blocks, offered between the tests, he played with happily. Soon afterwards Albert left the hospital, and the fears that had been built into him were never removed. A healthy, placid baby had been taught to be afraid, and the fear had spread well beyond the animal that started it. How could this happen in a week? Why did the fear spread to the rabbit and the fur coat but not to the blocks? Could the same process be run backwards to cure a fear? Keep Albert in mind. These questions run through the whole chapter, and we will return to him at the end.

6.1 What Learning Is, and What It Is NotMedium yield · 6 Qs

Almost everything that makes you a particular person rather than a generic human being was learned: your language, your taste in chai, your fear of a certain teacher, your skill at a cricket cover drive, the way your stomach tightens when you walk into an exam hall. Psychologists have studied learning more intensively than almost any other topic, and for the first half of the twentieth century the study of learning very nearly was experimental psychology. Before we look at how learning happens, we need to be precise about what it is.

A working definition

The definition most textbooks use descends from Gregory Kimble, who in 1961 revised a classic text on conditioning. In modern form: learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour, or in the potential for behaviour, that results from experience. Each phrase does work.

  • Relatively permanent excludes passing states. If you perform badly because you are sleepy, nothing has been learned; when you wake up, the change is gone. "Relatively" is there because learned things can be forgotten or extinguished; learning is durable, not eternal.
  • Behaviour or the potential for behaviour acknowledges that learning can be silent. You may learn the route to a new friend's house in Kuvempunagar while riding pillion and show no sign of it until the day you have to drive there yourself.
  • Results from experience excludes changes caused by growth, injury, drugs or disease. Kimble's own wording said "reinforced practice"; later writers dropped the word "reinforced" because, as you will see in Sections 6.7 and 6.8, a great deal is learned without any reward at all.

The second point leads to one of the most important distinctions in this chapter. Performance is what an organism actually does on a given occasion; learning is the change in knowledge or capacity that we infer from performance. The two can come apart. A student who knows the answer but freezes in the exam has learned but not performed. As you will see, Edward Tolman's rats (Section 6.7) and Albert Bandura's children (Section 6.8) both showed that learning can occur without being displayed until there is a reason to display it.

Changes that are not learning

Several kinds of behavioural change look like learning but are not, and examiners like to use them as distractors.

Maturation is change that unfolds according to a biological timetable, largely independent of specific experience. A baby does not learn to walk in the way she learns to ride a bicycle; she walks when her muscles, bones and nervous system are ready, and practice before that point achieves little. Fatigue, illness, injury and drugs also change behaviour, but temporarily or without experience being the cause. So do motivational states: a hungry rat runs faster than a satiated one, but it has not learned anything new.

Finally, much behaviour is innate. Ethologists, biologists who study animal behaviour in natural settings, describe the fixed action pattern: an unlearned, stereotyped sequence of behaviour that is the same in every member of a species and, once triggered, runs to completion. The trigger is a sign stimulus (also called a releaser), a simple feature of the environment to which the animal is tuned from birth. Nikolaas (Niko) Tinbergen (1907 to 1988) found that a male three-spined stickleback in breeding season attacks crude models of fish as long as they have a red underside, the sign stimulus of a rival male, while ignoring a realistic model with no red. A greylag goose that sees an egg outside its nest stretches out and rolls it back with its bill; if the egg is removed halfway, the goose completes the rolling movement anyway. Fixed action patterns are instinctive; they are the result of evolution, not of the individual's experience.

Exam trap

"Fixed action patterns result from ..." The answer is innate (unlearned) behaviour, not classical or operant conditioning. Also watch the word "permanent": learning is relatively permanent. And do not confuse habituation (below) with sensory adaptation, the reduced firing of receptors under constant stimulation (Chapter 3). Adaptation happens in the sense organ and is not reversed by an unrelated stimulus; habituation happens in the nervous system and is.

The simplest learning: habituation and sensitisation

The simplest forms of learning involve only one stimulus. Because nothing is being associated with anything else, they are called non-associative learning. There are two.

Habituation is a decrease in response to a stimulus that is presented repeatedly and turns out to be harmless. The first night in a hostel room near a railway line, every passing train wakes you; by the end of the week you sleep through them. Habituation is adaptive: an organism that kept reacting to every irrelevant event would have no attention left for the ones that matter. It has three diagnostic features. It is stimulus-specific (you still wake if your phone rings). It shows spontaneous recovery (after a long gap without trains, the first train wakes you again). And it shows dishabituation: if a new, strong stimulus occurs, the habituated response returns to the old stimulus. Dishabituation is the clearest proof that habituation is a form of learning and not simple fatigue of the muscles or receptors.

Sensitisation is nearly the opposite: an increase in response, usually to a wide range of stimuli, after exposure to a strong or noxious stimulus. After a near-miss with a bus on the Mysuru ring road, every horn makes you jump for the rest of the day. Unlike habituation, sensitisation is not specific to the stimulus that caused it. The dual-process theory proposed by Philip Groves and Richard F. Thompson in 1970 holds that every stimulus sets off both processes at once, and what you observe is their sum.

These two simple forms of learning were the first to be traced to the level of individual neurons. Eric Kandel (born 1929) chose the sea slug Aplysia, whose nervous system has only about 20,000 neurons, many of them large enough to record from one at a time. Touching the slug's siphon makes it withdraw its gill. With repeated gentle touches the withdrawal habituates, because the sensory neurons release less transmitter onto the motor neurons. A shock to the tail sensitises the reflex, because interneurons release serotonin onto the sensory neurons' terminals and increase their transmitter release. This work earned Kandel a share of the Nobel Prize in 2000 (the cellular details are in the Physiological Psychology book).

Habituation is also one of developmental psychology's most useful tools. Infants cannot tell you what they can distinguish, but they can get bored. Robert Fantz showed in 1964 that babies look less and less at a pattern shown repeatedly and look longer at a new one. If an infant habituated to one picture recovers its looking when a second picture appears, the infant must be able to tell the two apart. The habituation-dishabituation paradigm is therefore used to study discrimination and categorisation in babies long before they can speak.

Imprinting: learning on a biological timetable

Between instinct and ordinary learning lies imprinting, the rapid formation of a lasting attachment to the first suitable moving object a young animal encounters. The phenomenon was described in chicks by Douglas Spalding in 1873, but it is linked forever with the Austrian ethologist Konrad Lorenz (1903 to 1989). In a 1935 paper Lorenz reported that greylag goslings hatched in an incubator, whose first sight was Lorenz himself, followed him about as if he were their mother, and showed no interest in their real mother. Photographs of a line of goslings marching behind the bearded scientist made the finding famous.

Imprinting differs from ordinary learning in four ways. It happens fast, often within minutes of exposure. It needs no reward: the gosling is not fed for following. It is relatively irreversible: Lorenz's geese continued to prefer humans, and some later directed courtship at them (sexual imprinting). And it can occur only during a critical period, a limited window of development in which a particular kind of learning is possible. Eckhard Hess found the window peaked about 13 to 16 hours after hatching in mallard ducklings. Later research showed the window is less rigid than Lorenz believed, and many researchers now prefer the term sensitive period. Lorenz shared the 1973 Nobel Prize with Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch, and John Bowlby borrowed the idea of imprinting for his theory of human attachment (Developmental Psychology book).

Exam trap

Imprinting is rapid, largely irreversible, confined to a critical period and independent of reward. Options describing it as "slow and reversible across the lifespan", "operant conditioning with food reward" or "a purely visual recognition system unrelated to attachment" are all wrong. The classic species is the greylag goose (Lorenz); the 13 to 16 hour peak comes from Hess's mallard ducklings.

The map of the chapter

Habituation and sensitisation concern a single stimulus. Most learning, by contrast, is associative learning: learning that two events go together. Two broad kinds dominate. In classical conditioning the organism learns that one stimulus predicts another (the rat predicts the noise). In operant conditioning it learns that a response produces a consequence (pressing a lever produces food). Most of this research was done on animals, within comparative psychology, the branch of psychology that studies animal behaviour and compares species (its history is in Chapter 2). Later, cognitive psychologists showed that animals and people also learn maps, insights and concepts (Section 6.7), and Albert Bandura showed that people learn a great deal simply by watching others (Section 6.8). We begin, as psychology did, with a Russian physiologist and his dogs.

6.2 Pavlov and the Language of Classical ConditioningMedium yield · 7 Qs

A physiologist and his "psychic secretions"

Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (1849 to 1936) did not set out to study learning. He was a physiologist, and in 1904 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his research on digestion. To measure digestive secretions in living animals he developed surgical techniques of great elegance; for the salivary work, the duct of a dog's salivary gland was redirected through a small opening (a fistula) in the cheek so that every drop of saliva could be collected through a tube and counted.

In the late 1890s Pavlov and his co-workers noticed something that spoiled their measurements. Dogs that had been in the laboratory for a while began to salivate before food reached their mouths: at the sight of the food, at the sight of the dish, even at the sound of the attendant's footsteps. Pavlov at first called these "psychic secretions". Instead of treating them as a nuisance, he realised that they offered an objective way to study how the brain forms new connections, and from about 1897 he and his students studied them systematically. He described the work publicly at a congress in Madrid in 1903, and his lectures appeared in English in 1927 as Conditioned Reflexes.

The procedure was simple. A hungry dog stood in a harness in a quiet room. A neutral stimulus, often the ticking of a metronome, a buzzer or a bell, was presented, and a few seconds later meat powder was delivered to the dog's mouth. After a number of such pairings the metronome alone made the dog salivate. Pavlov called the new reflex "conditional", because it depended on the conditions of the dog's experience; the English translation "conditioned" stuck.

Pavlov was not quite the only discoverer. In 1902 the American graduate student Edwin Burket Twitmyer, studying the knee-jerk reflex, noticed that his participants' legs began to jerk when they heard the bell that warned of the hammer tap, even without the tap. He reported it to the American Psychological Association in 1904, where it was ignored. Discovery in science belongs partly to the person who sees what a finding means.

The five terms

Classical conditioning (also called Pavlovian or respondent conditioning) is learning in which a neutral stimulus, through repeated pairing with a stimulus that already produces a response, comes to produce a similar response itself. Its vocabulary has five terms, and exam questions test them constantly.

  • The unconditioned stimulus (UCS, or US) is a stimulus that produces a response without any prior learning: food in the mouth.
  • The unconditioned response (UCR, or UR) is the unlearned, reflexive response to the UCS: salivation to food.
  • The neutral stimulus (NS) is a stimulus that, before conditioning, does not produce the response of interest. It is not completely without effect: the first time the metronome ticks, the dog pricks up its ears and turns towards it, the orienting response that Pavlov called the "what-is-it?" reflex. But it does not produce salivation.
  • The conditioned stimulus (CS) is the formerly neutral stimulus after it has been paired with the UCS and has come to produce a response.
  • The conditioned response (CR) is the learned response to the CS: salivation to the metronome.

Notice two things. First, the same physical stimulus changes its name: the metronome is a neutral stimulus before conditioning and a conditioned stimulus after it. Second, the same physical response also changes its name according to what produces it. Salivation to food is a UCR; salivation to the metronome is a CR. The CR is usually similar to the UCR but not identical: Pavlov's dogs produced less saliva to the metronome than to food, and with a different chemical composition. What makes the whole thing work is association: the pairing of two stimuli close together in time. Figure 6.1 shows the three stages.

The best way to master the five terms is to push real cases through four questions. What produced the response before any learning took place? That is the UCS. What was that unlearned response? The UCR. What stimulus was present, neutral at first, and paired with the UCS? The CS. What does that stimulus now produce on its own? The CR. Exam trap

"In Pavlov's experiment the bell was ..." The answer is: a neutral stimulus before it was associated with food and a conditioned stimulus after. It is never an unconditioned stimulus, and never an "operant stimulus". Likewise, a neutral stimulus becomes a CS "when it is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus", not when it is "reinforced with a reward" (that is operant language) or "occurs randomly". If a question asks for the "fundamental element" of classical conditioning, the answer is association. Some question writers loosely say the pairing brings out an "unconditioned behaviour"; read past the slip and answer classical conditioning.

Timing: which arrangement conditions best?

Pavlov and his successors quickly found that the order and spacing of the CS and UCS matter enormously. Four arrangements are distinguished (Figure 6.2).

In delay conditioning, the CS comes on first and stays on until the UCS arrives, so the two overlap. The metronome starts, keeps ticking, and the food arrives while it is still ticking. This is the most effective arrangement and the standard laboratory procedure. The word "delay" refers to the delay between CS onset and UCS onset, not to any weakness. For fast reflexes such as the eyeblink, conditioning is best when the CS leads by about half a second; for slower responses, longer intervals work.

In trace conditioning, the CS comes on and goes off before the UCS begins, leaving a gap. The organism must keep a memory "trace" of the CS across the gap. Trace conditioning works if the gap is short, but it is weaker than delay conditioning and gets weaker as the gap lengthens. Because the CS precedes the UCS in both delay and trace conditioning, both are sometimes grouped together as forward conditioning.

In simultaneous conditioning, the CS and UCS begin and end together. Conditioning is weak, because the CS gives no advance warning; the UCS is already there.

In backward conditioning, the UCS comes first and the CS follows. This produces little or no excitatory conditioning. If anything, the CS comes to signal that the UCS is over, so it may become a signal for safety (a conditioned inhibitor).

Why should the order matter so much? Because, as Section 6.4 will show, classical conditioning is not really about two things happening together; it is about one thing predicting another. A stimulus that comes before the UCS can serve as a warning. A stimulus that comes with it or after it predicts nothing.

Exam trap

"Which is most effective in conditioning?" The answer is delayed (delay) conditioning. Students who think "delay" means "slow" pick trace conditioning instead; trace is the one with the gap. Backward conditioning is the least effective. The one famous exception to the short-interval rule is taste aversion, which forms even when illness follows the taste by hours (Section 6.4).

6.3 The Phenomena of Classical ConditioningMedium yield · 6 Qs

Once Pavlov had a reliable way to create a conditioned reflex, he and his students spent three decades finding out what could be done to it. They discovered that a conditioned response can be strengthened, weakened, revived, spread to new stimuli, narrowed to one stimulus and even used to build further conditioned responses. These phenomena are the grammar of classical conditioning, and most of them reappear, under the same names, in operant conditioning.

Acquisition, extinction and the return of the response

Acquisition is the stage in which the CS-UCS pairing is repeated and the CR grows stronger. Plotted against trials, it rises steeply at first and then levels off: each pairing adds less than the one before (Figure 6.3, left). Acquisition is faster when the UCS is intense, when the CS is noticeable, when the timing is right (delay conditioning with a short interval) and, as Section 6.4 will show, when the CS reliably predicts the UCS.

What happens when the UCS stops coming? If the metronome is sounded again and again with no food, the dog salivates less and less until it stops. This is extinction: the gradual weakening and disappearance of a conditioned response when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS. Pavlov called the UCS the "reinforcement" of the conditioned reflex, so in his language extinction occurs when reinforcement is withdrawn. Do not let that word mislead you into thinking of operant conditioning; in a classical-conditioning question, "withdrawing reinforcement" simply means omitting the UCS.

It would be natural to assume that extinction erases what was learned. Pavlov showed that it does not, and his evidence is still the core of every modern account.

  • Spontaneous recovery. If the extinguished animal is given a rest, say a day, and the CS is presented again, the CR reappears, weaker than before, with no further pairings (Figure 6.3, right). Something was suppressing the response, and the suppression faded with time.
  • Disinhibition. If a novel, unrelated stimulus (a sudden noise) occurs during extinction, the extinguished CR temporarily comes back. Pavlov reasoned that extinction is an active inhibitory process and that the distraction disrupted the inhibition.
  • Rapid reacquisition (savings). If CS-UCS pairings are resumed after extinction, the CR returns much faster than it was first acquired. This is the classical equivalent of finding that you relearn a forgotten poem in fewer readings than it took the first time.
  • Reinstatement. After extinction, a few presentations of the UCS on its own, with no CS, are enough to bring the CR back to the CS. Robert Rescorla and Donald Heth demonstrated this in 1975 (you will meet Rescorla again in Section 6.4).
  • Renewal. Extinction is tied to the context in which it happens. If fear is conditioned in one room (context A) and extinguished in another (context B), the fear returns when the animal is put back in room A, or into any new room. Mark Bouton has shown in many experiments that what extinction produces is a new, context-dependent memory ("the CS is safe here") that competes with the old one.
Clinical link

Spontaneous recovery, reinstatement and renewal are the laboratory faces of relapse. A client whose fear of lifts was extinguished in the therapist's building may find the fear back when she steps into the lift at her own office (renewal), or after an unrelated frightening event (reinstatement). This is why modern exposure therapy is carried out in several settings and why therapists warn clients that a brief return of fear is expected and is not failure.

Exam trap

Keep the four "comeback" terms apart. Spontaneous recovery needs only the passage of time. Disinhibition needs a novel distracting stimulus. Reinstatement needs a presentation of the UCS alone. Reconditioning (rapid reacquisition) needs new CS-UCS pairings. Sensitisation is not a comeback at all; it is the non-associative increase in responding from Section 6.1. "If the UCS is subsequently omitted, the CR will undergo ..." Extinction.

Generalisation and discrimination

Pavlov's dogs, conditioned to one tone, also salivated to other tones, the more so the more similar they were to the original. This is stimulus generalisation: the tendency for a CR to be produced by stimuli similar to the CS. If you plot the strength of the response against the similarity of the test stimulus to the original CS, you get a hump-shaped generalisation gradient, highest at the original CS and falling off on either side. In 1937 Carl Hovland demonstrated such a gradient in humans: participants conditioned to show a skin-conductance response to one tone responded less and less to tones further away from it in pitch. Albert's fear of the rabbit, the dog and the sealskin coat was generalisation from the white rat; things that were furry and rat-like produced fear, while wooden blocks, which shared nothing with the rat, did not.

Generalisation is adaptive. A gazelle that has been chased by one lion does well to be wary of a lion of slightly different size or colour. But generalisation that goes too far is costly, and so organisms also learn stimulus discrimination: the learned ability to respond to the CS but not to similar stimuli that have never been followed by the UCS. Pavlov trained it by differential conditioning. One tone (called CS+) was always followed by food; a similar tone (CS-) never was. At first the dog salivated to both; gradually it salivated only to CS+. A child who was frightened by a large black dog but still plays happily with her grandmother's small tabby cat, or a child who avoids one furry black cat but cuddles another, is showing discrimination.

Key study: Experimental neurosis in Pavlov's laboratory (1921)

Question. What happens when a discrimination becomes impossible? Method. In Pavlov's laboratory, Shenger-Krestovnikova projected a circle and an ellipse onto a screen in front of a dog. The circle was always followed by food; the ellipse never was. Once the dog discriminated them, the ellipse was made rounder step by step, until the ratio of its two axes was 9 to 8, almost a circle. Result. The dog's discrimination broke down; worse, the formerly placid animal began to squeal and wriggle in its stand, bit through the tubes and barked violently whenever it was brought to the room, and even easy discriminations it had previously mastered were lost. Conclusion. Pavlov called this state experimental neurosis: a lasting emotional disturbance produced by an unresolvable conflict between excitation and inhibition. Significance. It was the first laboratory model of anxiety, and it pointed toward the idea, developed later by Joseph Wolpe, that neurotic anxiety is learned and can therefore be unlearned.

A second, accidental "experiment" convinced Pavlov that his dogs could be traumatised. In September 1924 the River Neva flooded Leningrad, and the dogs in the laboratory's kennels were trapped as the water rose almost to the tops of their cages before they were rescued. Afterwards several of the dogs had lost their carefully trained conditioned reflexes, and some showed lasting fear: a trickle of water allowed to run under the door of the room was enough to throw them back into agitation. Pavlov devoted much of his final decade to these "neuroses" and to applying conditioning ideas to psychiatry.

Exam trap

Generalisation is responding to similar stimuli; discrimination is responding differently to similar stimuli. A child who avoids a furry black cat but plays with a short-haired tabby is showing discrimination, not extinction (the fear of the black cat has not gone), not successive approximation (that is shaping, Section 6.5), and not negative transfer (old learning interfering with new learning, Section 6.10).

Building on conditioning: higher-order conditioning and sensory preconditioning

Food, pain and loud noises are rare in daily life, yet adults have strong emotional reactions to words, logos, places and faces that have never been directly paired with any of them. How? Pavlov's laboratory found one answer. Once a metronome has become a strong CS for salivation, it can itself be used in place of food to condition a second stimulus. Pair a black square with the metronome, with no food anywhere, and after some trials the black square alone produces salivation. This is higher-order conditioning (second-order conditioning when there are two links): an established CS serves as the UCS for conditioning a new stimulus. Third-order conditioning is very weak or absent, partly because during second-order training the first CS is repeatedly presented without food and so begins to extinguish.

Higher-order conditioning helps explain why a word like "exam" can make a student's heart race. The word was never itself painful, but it was paired, over years, with the anxious waiting and the stern invigilator that had themselves become conditioned stimuli for fear.

A related procedure reverses the order of training. In sensory preconditioning, two neutral stimuli are first paired with each other when neither means anything. Only afterwards is one of them paired with a UCS. The other then produces the CR too, even though it was never paired with the UCS or with a CS. W. J. Brogden demonstrated this in dogs in 1939: a light and a bell were presented together many times; then the bell alone was paired with a shock to the leg until it produced leg flexion; finally the light alone, never paired with shock, also produced some flexion. The finding mattered because it showed that animals learn associations between neutral stimuli that have no consequences at the time; the learning stays silent until later events reveal it, a point Edward Tolman would make about rats in mazes (Section 6.7). "Using an already-established CS to condition a new neutral stimulus" is higher-order (second-order) conditioning. It is not backward conditioning (UCS before CS), not stimulus discrimination, and not counterconditioning (replacing one response to a stimulus with an incompatible response, Section 6.4). If the two neutral stimuli were paired before any UCS appeared, it is sensory preconditioning.

6.4 Fear, Prediction and PreparednessHigh yield · 29 Qs

For its first half-century, classical conditioning was thought of as a simple, mechanical process: pair two stimuli often enough, close enough together in time, and the first will come to produce the response to the second. This section tells how that picture was enriched in three directions. Conditioning was applied to human emotion, first to create fears and then to remove them. It was shown to depend on prediction, not mere pairing. And it was shown to be shaped by evolution, so that some associations are learned in a single trial and others hardly at all.

Little Albert and conditioned emotional responses

John Broadus Watson (1878 to 1958) had launched behaviourism in 1913 with the claim that psychology should study only observable behaviour (Chapter 2). He believed that most adult emotional life consisted of conditioned versions of a few innate emotional reactions, and the study you read about at the opening of this chapter, published with Rosalie Rayner in 1920 under the title "Conditioned emotional reactions", was meant to prove it. In the terms of Table 6.1, the loud clang was the UCS, the startle and fear it produced was the UCR, the white rat became the CS, and Albert's fear of the rat was the CR. His fear of the rabbit, the dog, the coat and the mask was stimulus generalisation.

A fear or other emotional reaction acquired through classical conditioning is called a conditioned emotional response. In animal laboratories it is usually measured indirectly. In a procedure introduced by William Estes and B. F. Skinner in 1941, rats press a lever for food; a tone is then paired with a mild shock; afterwards, whenever the tone sounds, the rats stop pressing. The degree of this conditioned suppression measures how much fear the tone produces, and you will meet the procedure again in Kamin's experiments below. Fear conditioning depends critically on the amygdala, a structure in the temporal lobe that receives information about the CS and organises the bodily fear response (Joseph LeDoux's work on these circuits is covered in the Physiological Psychology book).

The Albert study made Watson's case vividly, but it is a weak experiment by modern standards. There was one participant, no control condition, and the fear was measured by informal description; the fear also faded between sessions and had to be "freshened" with extra pairings. Ethically it could not be done today: the researchers deliberately frightened an infant and made no attempt to remove the fear. As you will see in the revisit at the end of the chapter, even Albert's identity is disputed.

Peter, counterconditioning and the conditioning therapies

If fear can be conditioned, can it be unconditioned? Four years after Albert, Mary Cover Jones (1897 to 1987), working with Watson's advice, answered yes. Her 1924 report "A laboratory study of fear: The case of Peter" described a boy of almost three who was intensely afraid of rabbits, and also, by generalisation, of a fur coat, feathers and cotton wool, much as Albert had been. Jones used two methods. In one, Peter played alongside other children who were not afraid of the rabbit. In the other, while Peter sat eating a favourite food, the rabbit in its cage was brought a little closer each day, never so close that he became upset. Over many sessions Peter came to tolerate the rabbit, then to touch it, and finally to let it nibble his fingers. His fears of the related objects declined as well.

The method is counterconditioning: replacing a conditioned response to a stimulus with a new response that is incompatible with it. Eating and contentment cannot easily coexist with terror, so the rabbit, paired with eating, gradually came to signal something that was no longer threatening. Jones is often called the mother of behaviour therapy.

The idea was developed into a systematic treatment three decades later by the South African psychiatrist Joseph Wolpe (1915 to 1997). Wolpe first made cats anxious by giving them mild shocks in an experimental cage; afterwards the cats were too anxious to eat in it. He then fed them first in a room quite unlike the cage, where their anxiety was weak enough for them to eat, and then in rooms that resembled the cage more and more closely, until eating, a response incompatible with anxiety, had overcome the anxiety even in the cage itself. He borrowed the physiologist Charles Scott Sherrington's term for the principle: reciprocal inhibition, the idea that if a response incompatible with anxiety can be made to occur in the presence of an anxiety-provoking stimulus, it will weaken the bond between that stimulus and anxiety. In Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition (1958) he described the treatment that grew out of it, systematic desensitisation, which has three steps.

  1. Relaxation training. The client learns deep muscle relaxation, usually a shortened form of the progressive relaxation developed by the American physician Edmund Jacobson (1888 to 1983), in which muscle groups are tensed and then released one by one. Relaxation is the response that will compete with anxiety.
  2. Constructing an anxiety hierarchy. Client and therapist list situations involving the feared object, from the mildest to the most frightening, and the client rates each on the Subjective Units of Disturbance Scale (SUDS), a 0 to 100 rating of felt anxiety that Wolpe introduced in The Practice of Behavior Therapy (1969).
  3. Desensitisation proper. While deeply relaxed, the client imagines (or, in vivo, actually encounters) the lowest item. Only when it no longer produces anxiety does the therapist move up to the next item, and so on to the top.

Systematic desensitisation is one of several treatments that grew directly out of classical conditioning. In flooding, the client is exposed to the most feared situation at full intensity, without gradual steps or relaxation, and is kept there until the anxiety subsides on its own; a person afraid of heights stands on a high balcony and does not leave. Flooding works by extinction: the CS (height) is presented without any UCS (falling), and escape, which would otherwise prevent extinction, is blocked. Implosive therapy, developed by Thomas Stampfl in the 1960s, is flooding carried out in imagination with deliberately exaggerated scenes. In aversion therapy the logic is reversed: a stimulus linked to an unwanted behaviour (the taste of alcohol) is paired with an aversive UCS (a nausea-producing drug) so that the stimulus becomes unpleasant. In covert sensitisation, introduced by Joseph Cautela in 1967, both the behaviour and the unpleasant consequence are only imagined: the client pictures reaching for a cigarette and then vividly imagines being violently sick. And in exposure and response prevention, the standard treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder developed by Victor Meyer in 1966, the client is exposed to the obsessional cue (touching a "contaminated" doorknob) and prevented from performing the compulsion (washing). The anxiety rises, peaks and falls; the treatment works through habituation and extinction. The full clinical details of all these treatments are in the Clinical Psychology book; Table 6.10 in Section 6.6 sorts them by the learning principle on which each rests.

Exam trap

Systematic desensitisation is Wolpe's, and its principle is reciprocal inhibition (counterconditioning), not operant extinction, not habituation, not the law of effect and not catharsis. Relaxation comes from Jacobson. The anxiety ratings are SUDS. Graded steps plus relaxation means desensitisation; full intensity with no escape and no relaxation means flooding; imagined unpleasant consequences of an imagined behaviour means covert sensitisation (Cautela). Exposure and response prevention rests on habituation and extinction. And do not confuse Wolpe's reciprocal inhibition with Bandura's reciprocal determinism (Section 6.8). Symptom substitution, sometimes offered as an option, is not a technique at all: it is the psychoanalytic prediction that removing a symptom without resolving its supposed underlying cause will simply produce a new symptom, a criticism of behaviour therapy that outcome research on desensitisation has generally not supported.

Contiguity is not enough

From Pavlov to the 1960s, almost everyone assumed that the key to conditioning was contiguity, the occurrence of the CS and UCS close together in time. Four findings from the late 1950s and 1960s, summarised in Table 6.3, showed that contiguity is not sufficient. What matters is whether the CS gives the animal new information about the UCS.

The first came from Robert Rescorla (1940 to 2020) in 1968. He gave groups of rats the same number of tone-shock pairings, but varied how often shocks also occurred when the tone was absent. When shocks came only with the tone, the rats developed strong fear of the tone. When shocks were just as likely without the tone as with it, the rats showed no fear of the tone at all, even though they had experienced exactly as many tone-shock pairings. Rescorla concluded that conditioning depends on contingency: the degree to which the CS predicts the UCS, that is, how much more likely the UCS is in the presence of the CS than in its absence. A positive contingency produces excitatory conditioning; zero contingency produces none; a negative contingency (UCS less likely when the CS is present) produces conditioned inhibition.

The second finding, blocking, was reported by Leon Kamin (1927 to 2017) in 1969. Kamin first conditioned rats to fear a noise by pairing it with shock. Then he presented the noise together with a light, followed by the same shock. Finally he tested the light alone. Rats that had had the first phase showed almost no fear of the light; control rats that had experienced only the noise-plus-light pairings feared the light strongly. Prior conditioning of the noise had blocked conditioning of the light. The light was perfectly contiguous with the shock, but it was redundant: the noise already predicted the shock, so the light added no information.

The third, overshadowing, had been noted by Pavlov himself. When two stimuli are presented together as a compound CS, the more salient one (the louder, brighter or more noticeable) acquires most of the conditioning and the weaker one acquires little, even though either would have been conditioned well if presented alone.

The fourth, latent inhibition, was described by Robert Lubow and A. U. Moore in 1959. If a stimulus is presented many times on its own before conditioning begins, it is later much harder to condition. The animal has learned that the stimulus is irrelevant. Latent inhibition explains a useful clinical fact: a child who has had many uneventful visits to the dentist is less likely to develop a dental phobia after one painful visit than a child whose first visit is the painful one.

The Rescorla-Wagner model: learning from surprise

In 1972 Rescorla and Allan Wagner (1934 to 2018) proposed a simple mathematical model that captures most of these findings. The Rescorla-Wagner model says that on every trial the associative strength of a CS changes in proportion to how surprising the outcome is. In words: the animal learns only when what happens differs from what it expected. In symbols:

ΔV = αβ(λ − ΣV)

Here V is the associative strength of the CS (roughly, how strongly it predicts the UCS); ΔV is the change in that strength on this trial; α is the salience of the CS (how noticeable it is); β is a learning-rate value set by the UCS; λ (lambda) is the maximum associative strength the UCS can support, which is set to zero on trials when the UCS is absent; and ΣV is the sum of the associative strengths of all the stimuli present on the trial, which is the animal's total expectation. The term in brackets, (λ − ΣV), is the surprise, or prediction error.

Push some numbers through it. Let αβ = 0.3 and λ = 100. On trial 1 the CS has no strength, so ΔV = 0.3 × (100 − 0) = 30, and V becomes 30. On trial 2, ΔV = 0.3 × (100 − 30) = 21, so V becomes 51. On trial 3, ΔV = 0.3 × 49 = 14.7, so V becomes 65.7. Each increment is smaller than the last because the UCS is becoming less surprising; that is why acquisition curves are negatively accelerated (Figure 6.3). Now blocking: after many noise-shock trials the noise has V close to 100. When the light is added, ΣV = 100 (noise) + 0 (light) = 100, so the prediction error is 100 − 100 = 0, and the light gains nothing. Extinction follows too: on a CS-alone trial λ = 0, so ΔV = 0.3 × (0 − 65.7), a negative change.

The model is not perfect. It treats extinction as unlearning, which cannot explain spontaneous recovery or renewal, and it predicts no effect of presenting a CS alone before conditioning, so it cannot explain latent inhibition. Later models added attention to deal with these problems. But its central idea proved extraordinarily fruitful. In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded from dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra of monkeys. The neurons fired in a burst to an unexpected reward; once a light reliably predicted the reward, they fired to the light and not to the reward; and if the predicted reward failed to arrive, their firing dipped below baseline at the moment it should have come. The neurons were signalling prediction error, the quantity in the Rescorla-Wagner brackets. The same idea now drives the reinforcement-learning algorithms used in artificial intelligence.

Exam trap

The brain's reward "prediction error" signal is carried by dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area (and substantia nigra), not by serotonin release, GABA inhibition or acetylcholine release. Blocking is Kamin's; contingency is Rescorla's; the model is Rescorla and Wagner's (1972); latent inhibition is Lubow and Moore's. Latent inhibition (pre-exposure slows conditioning) is a different thing from latent learning (Tolman, Section 6.7).

What is learned: a substitute stimulus or a signal?

Pavlov held a stimulus substitution theory: through pairing, the CS comes to activate the same brain centre as the UCS, so it becomes a substitute for the UCS and produces the same response. The theory predicts that the CR should be a copy of the UCR, and often it is not. Rats shocked through the floor jump and run (the UCR), but to a tone that predicts shock they freeze (the CR). The most striking evidence comes from drug tolerance. Shepard Siegel showed that the cues that regularly accompany a drug (the room, the syringe, the ritual) come to produce a compensatory response, opposite to the drug's effect, that prepares the body for it. This is part of why tolerance develops. It also explains a grim finding: rats given a large dose of heroin in a familiar drug context survived more often than rats given the same dose in an unfamiliar context, where the protective compensatory response was not triggered. Many human "overdoses" occur at doses the person usually tolerates, but in an unusual setting.

The modern view, which Rescorla championed, is an information or expectancy view: in classical conditioning the organism learns that one event predicts another, and the CR is whatever response best prepares it for the predicted event. On this view the CS is a signal, not a substitute. The idea that conditioning depends on prediction and surprise fits the evidence from contingency, blocking and the dopamine neurons.

Biological constraints: Garcia's rats and the idea of preparedness

Behind classical learning theory lay an assumption that was rarely stated because it seemed obvious. It was called equipotentiality: the view that any stimulus can be associated with any response or UCS with equal ease, so that the laws of learning are the same whatever stimuli and responses are used. Pavlov's dogs and the metronome were assumed to be a convenient example of a universal process. In the 1960s an American psychologist overturned this assumption.

Key study: Garcia and Koelling's "bright, noisy, tasty water" (1966)

Question. Are all stimuli equally easy to associate with all consequences? Method. John Garcia (1917 to 2012) and Robert Koelling let thirsty rats drink from a spout. The water was flavoured with saccharin (taste), and every lick also produced a flash of light and a click (bright, noisy). Some rats were then made ill (by X-irradiation or a toxic drug); others received electric shocks to the feet. Later each rat was tested with tasty water (flavour, no light or click) and with bright-noisy water (plain water with light and click). Result. Rats made ill avoided the tasty water but drank the bright-noisy water freely; rats shocked avoided the bright-noisy water but drank the tasty water freely. Conclusion. Rats readily associate taste with illness and sights and sounds with external pain, but not the reverse. The cue and the consequence must "belong" together. Criticism and fate. The findings so contradicted accepted theory that Garcia had great difficulty getting them published; they are now among the most cited in the field.

Garcia's broader work on conditioned taste aversion, the avoidance of a taste that has been followed by illness, broke three accepted principles at once. It showed one-trial learning: a single pairing of a new taste with illness is enough, whereas a metronome needs many pairings with food. It showed long-delay learning: the aversion forms even when the illness follows the taste by several hours, which flatly contradicts the rule that the CS and UCS must be close in time. And it refuted equipotentiality, as the key study shows. The adaptive logic is obvious once stated: in the wild, poisoned food makes an animal ill hours after eating, and an animal that could learn from a single such experience would survive to reproduce.

Martin Seligman (born 1942) generalised the point in 1970 as the principle of preparedness: through evolution, each species is prepared to learn some associations easily, unprepared for others (they can be learned with effort), and contraprepared for still others (they are learned with great difficulty or not at all). In 1971 he applied the idea to human phobias. People commonly develop phobias of snakes, spiders, heights, the dark and enclosed spaces, which were dangers to our ancestors, but rarely of guns, electric sockets or cars, which are far more dangerous today. Preparedness may also explain why Albert's fear attached so readily to a furry animal: when Elsie Bregman tried, in 1934, to condition infants to fear wooden blocks and cloth objects, she largely failed.

Taste-aversion learning has practical uses. Ilene Bernstein showed in 1978 that children receiving nausea-producing chemotherapy developed aversions to an unusual ice cream eaten shortly before treatment; later work found that giving patients a novel "scapegoat" food before chemotherapy can protect their usual diet from becoming aversive. Ranchers in the western United States have reduced coyote attacks on sheep by leaving out lamb meat laced with lithium chloride, which makes the coyotes ill and gives them an aversion to lamb. Aversion therapy for alcohol use, described above, uses the same principle.

Advertisers apply classical conditioning constantly. A brand (the CS) is shown again and again with a beloved film star, a cricket hero, attractive people or pleasant music (the UCS), in the hope that the warm feelings these produce will transfer to the brand. The transfer of liking or disliking through pairing is called evaluative conditioning. In a well-known 1982 experiment, the marketing researcher Gerald Gorn found that students who saw a pen of one colour while hearing music they liked were more likely to choose a pen of that colour afterwards.

Exam trap

Taste aversion is an example of classical conditioning (taste = CS, illness = UCS, nausea = UCR, aversion = CR), not operant conditioning, not second-order conditioning, and not a "spurious association" (a statistical term for a coincidental link). The Garcia effect best illustrates biological preparedness (constraints on learning). It contradicts two textbook rules: that all stimuli are equally easy to associate (equipotentiality) and that conditioning needs near-simultaneous events (contiguity).

6.5 Thorndike, Skinner and the Consequences of BehaviourHigh yield · 41 Qs

Classical conditioning explains how stimuli come to trigger reflexive and emotional responses. But most of what you do is not triggered in that way. You choose to study, to message a friend, to order masala dosa rather than idli. These actions are not elicited by a stimulus; they are emitted, and whether you repeat them depends on what happened the last time you did them. The study of how consequences shape behaviour began, a few years before Pavlov's first reports, with an American graduate student and a houseful of cats.

Thorndike's puzzle boxes and the law of effect

Edward Lee Thorndike (1874 to 1949) began his animal work as a student of William James at Harvard, training chicks to run through mazes; when his landlady objected, James let him keep the chicks in the cellar of his own house. Thorndike then moved to Columbia University, and his doctoral dissertation, Animal Intelligence (1898), became one of the founding documents of comparative psychology.

His best-known apparatus was the puzzle box: a slatted wooden crate from which a hungry cat could escape, to reach a piece of fish placed outside, only by performing a particular action such as pulling a loop of string, pressing a lever or stepping on a platform. Placed in the box, the cat clawed at the bars, squeezed at the gaps, bit and scrambled, until by accident it operated the release and got out. On the next trial it went through much the same frantic routine, but it hit the release a little sooner. Over many trials the useless movements dropped out and the escape time fell, but gradually and irregularly, not suddenly. When Thorndike plotted escape time against trials he produced some of the first learning curves in psychology, and their gradual slope persuaded him that the cats were not reasoning their way out. He called the process trial-and-error learning (he also called it learning by "trial and accidental success").

To explain it, Thorndike proposed the law of effect, stated in its classic form in 1911: of several responses made to the same situation, those that are accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal will, other things being equal, be more firmly connected with the situation, so that when the situation recurs they will be more likely to recur; those followed by discomfort will have their connections with the situation weakened. Thorndike defined "satisfaction" behaviourally, as a state of affairs the animal does nothing to avoid and often acts to attain. In plain words: behaviour followed by pleasant consequences is more likely to be repeated, and behaviour followed by unpleasant consequences is less likely to be repeated.

Thorndike thought of learning as the strengthening and weakening of connections (bonds) between situations and responses, and his theory is therefore called connectionism. (It should not be confused with the modern connectionist, or neural-network, models of cognition described in Chapter 8.) He added two further primary laws. The law of exercise (also called the law of use and disuse) held that connections are strengthened by being used and weakened by disuse. The law of readiness held that when an organism is prepared to act, acting is satisfying and being prevented from acting is annoying, while being forced to act when unprepared is annoying. He also proposed five subsidiary laws, summarised in Table 6.4.

Thorndike was an honest scientist and revised his laws when his own data demanded it. In the early 1930s he reported that repetition alone does not improve performance: people who drew line after line, blindfolded, trying to make each one three inches long, did not get better if they were never told how close they had come. He therefore abandoned the law of exercise. He also concluded that punishment does not weaken connections as strongly as reward strengthens them, so that only the "satisfaction" half of the law of effect survived. This revised version is often called the truncated law of effect.

Exam trap

The law of effect is Thorndike's, not Skinner's, Pavlov's or Watson's. It states that behaviours followed by pleasant consequences are more likely to be repeated. Do not confuse it with learning by observing models (Bandura), sudden restructuring of a problem (Köhler's insight) or unconscious drives (Freud). Thorndike's process is trial-and-error learning; if a question asks for the general name of "a process in which a response is made more frequent by reinforcement", the answer is operant conditioning.

Skinner and operant behaviour

Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990) set out to be a novelist, gave it up, and arrived at Harvard as a psychology graduate student in 1928 with Watson's behaviourism as his inspiration. In The Behavior of Organisms (1938) he set out the system that dominated learning research for the next thirty years. He also wrote a utopian novel, Walden Two (1948), and the books Science and Human Behavior (1953), Verbal Behavior (1957) and Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971).

Skinner drew a sharp distinction between two kinds of behaviour. Respondent behaviour is elicited by a known stimulus: salivation to food, a blink to a puff of air. It is the behaviour Pavlov studied, and Skinner called its conditioning Type S. Operant behaviour is emitted by the organism, without any obvious eliciting stimulus, and it operates on the environment to produce consequences: a rat presses a lever, a child raises her hand, you unlock your phone. Skinner coined the term "operant" in 1937 and called its conditioning Type R. Operant conditioning is learning in which the probability of a response is changed by its consequences.

To study operant behaviour Skinner built the apparatus now known as the Skinner box (he preferred "operant chamber"): a small enclosure with a lever for a rat, or a lit plastic disc (a key) for a pigeon to peck, a mechanism that delivers food pellets or grain, and often lights, a loudspeaker and a floor that can deliver mild shock. Unlike Thorndike's cat, which had to be put back in the box after every escape, Skinner's rat could respond whenever it liked, as often as it liked. Skinner therefore measured the rate of responding, and he recorded it with an elegant device, the cumulative recorder. A roll of paper moves steadily under a pen; each response moves the pen one small step upward, and the pen never comes down. The result is a line whose slope shows the rate: a steep line means rapid responding, a flat line means none, and small diagonal ticks mark the moments of reinforcement. You will see four such records in Figure 6.4.

Skinner's philosophy is called radical behaviourism. It holds that private events such as thoughts and feelings are themselves behaviour, governed by the same laws as public behaviour, and that explanations in terms of mental causes ("he studied because he wanted to pass") are incomplete until we identify the environmental history that produced the wanting. Thorndike's procedure, with separate trials, is often called instrumental conditioning, and Skinner's free-responding procedure operant conditioning, but the two terms are used almost interchangeably today.

Operant behaviour does not occur in a vacuum. A rat whose lever presses produce food only when a light is on soon presses mainly when the light is on. A stimulus in whose presence a response is reinforced is a discriminative stimulus (written SD); one in whose presence it is not reinforced is an S-delta (SΔ). The discriminative stimulus does not elicit the response, as a CS does; it "sets the occasion" for it. The green signal at a crossing, the "Open" board outside a chai stall and the presence of a strict teacher are all discriminative stimuli. Skinner described operant learning as a three-term contingency: discriminative stimulus, response, consequence, which clinicians call the A-B-C (antecedent, behaviour, consequence). When behaviour reliably occurs in the presence of a discriminative stimulus and not in its absence, it is said to be under stimulus control. Operant responses show generalisation and discrimination just as conditioned reflexes do.

Clinical link

A functional analysis (or functional behavioural assessment) is the operant method of finding out why a problem behaviour persists. The clinician records each episode with its antecedents and consequences, asking what the behaviour gets the person (attention, an object, sensory stimulation) or what it lets the person escape (a demand, a feared situation). Applied behaviour analysis (ABA), the use of operant principles to change socially important behaviour, is built on this method, so "behaviour analysis is based on" operant conditioning. A practical wrinkle: when clients start recording their own behaviour (counting cigarettes, logging snacks), the behaviour often changes before any treatment begins. This is called reactivity.

Four kinds of consequence

The heart of operant conditioning is a simple classification that confuses more students than any other idea in psychology. It rests on two separate questions about any consequence.

First: does the behaviour become more likely or less likely in future? If more likely, the consequence is reinforcement; if less likely, it is punishment. Reinforcement and punishment are defined entirely by their effect on behaviour, not by anyone's intentions or by whether the consequence seems pleasant.

Second: was a stimulus added or removed? If added, the consequence is called positive; if removed, negative. "Positive" and "negative" here mean plus and minus, exactly as in arithmetic. They do not mean good and bad.

Crossing the two questions gives four combinations (Table 6.5). In positive reinforcement, a stimulus is added and the behaviour increases (praise after a correct answer). In negative reinforcement, a stimulus is removed and the behaviour increases; the removed stimulus is usually aversive (a headache that goes away after you take a pill). In positive punishment, a stimulus is added and the behaviour decreases (a scolding after you are rude). In negative punishment, a stimulus is removed and the behaviour decreases; the removed stimulus is usually something the person values (a mobile phone confiscated for a week).

Exam trap

Negative reinforcement is not punishment. All reinforcement, positive or negative, increases behaviour; all punishment decreases it. A rat pressing a lever to turn off a shock is negative reinforcement (escape conditioning). Time-out and response cost are negative punishment. One real paper asked "which consequence increases the likelihood of a behaviour being repeated?" and offered both positive reinforcement and negative reinforcement; the keyed answer was positive reinforcement, but negative reinforcement increases behaviour too. If you meet such an item, choose positive reinforcement (the intended "reward" answer) and remember that the item, not the principle, is at fault. Operant conditioning motivates behaviour through reinforcement and punishment, not through modelling (Bandura), classical conditioning or cognitive dissonance (Festinger).

Primary, secondary and generalised reinforcers

Why does a rat work for food, a pigeon for grain, and you for a pay slip? Food is a primary reinforcer (or unconditioned reinforcer): it is reinforcing without any learning, because it satisfies a biological need. Water, warmth, sexual contact and relief from pain are other examples. Money is different. A five-hundred-rupee note is just printed paper; it is reinforcing only because it has been repeatedly associated with things that are reinforcing in themselves. A stimulus that acquires its reinforcing power through association with a primary reinforcer is a secondary reinforcer (or conditioned reinforcer). Grades, praise, a smile, the "click" a dog trainer sounds just before giving a treat, and the ping of a notification are all secondary reinforcers. Notice that the process by which a neutral stimulus becomes a secondary reinforcer is itself classical conditioning: the click is paired with food until it acquires some of food's value.

The power of secondary reinforcers was shown dramatically by John B. Wolfe in 1936. Chimpanzees learned to insert poker chips into a vending machine (nicknamed the "Chimp-O-Mat") that dispensed grapes, and then to work at a heavy lever-pulling task to earn the chips. They would work for chips even when the machine was not immediately available, and some hoarded them. A secondary reinforcer that has been paired with many different primary reinforcers, so that it works whatever the person currently needs, is a generalised reinforcer. Money is the obvious example; Skinner also listed attention, approval and affection.

These ideas were turned into a treatment method in the early 1960s by Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin at Anna State Hospital in Illinois. On a ward of long-stay psychiatric patients, many of whom had been diagnosed with schizophrenia and had become withdrawn and inactive, patients earned tokens for clearly defined behaviours: self-care, making their beds, working on ward jobs. The tokens could be exchanged for backup reinforcers such as a private room, walks in the grounds, extra time with staff, snacks and cigarettes. Participation in the target behaviours rose sharply; when tokens were given regardless of behaviour, participation fell; when the contingency was restored, it rose again. Their book The Token Economy (1968) gave the method its name. A token economy is a behaviour-modification system in which tokens (secondary reinforcers) are earned for target behaviours and exchanged for backup reinforcers. It is an application of operant conditioning, and it has since been used in classrooms, homes for children, prisons and rehabilitation units.

Exam trap

Who developed the token economy? The historically correct answer is Ayllon and Azrin (Anna State Hospital; book 1968). One real paper (Amity 2023) keyed Skinner, presumably because the method rests on Skinner's operant principles. If both appear, Ayllon and Azrin is the accurate answer; Skinner supplied the principles, not the ward programme. Token economy is based on operant conditioning (secondary reinforcement), never on classical conditioning, observational learning, insight or free association. A teacher's stickers exchangeable for a homework exemption are best called tokens; they are secondary, not primary, reinforcers. Tokens can also be generalised reinforcers when they buy many different backups, but "token" is the specific answer. A "prepotent response" is simply a dominant, highly probable response (the word echoes Thorndike's law of prepotency of elements), not a kind of reinforcer.

Remember

Harry Harlow's famous monkeys (1958) were a test of the secondary-reinforcer idea. Many learning theorists held that infants love their mothers because mothers are paired with feeding, so that the mother is a secondary reinforcer (the "cupboard love" or secondary-drive theory). Harlow's infant rhesus monkeys clung to a soft cloth surrogate that gave no milk in preference to a wire surrogate that fed them, and ran to the cloth mother when frightened. Attachment rests on contact comfort, not on conditioned reinforcement from feeding. (The attachment research belongs to the Developmental Psychology book; Harlow's learning-set research is in Section 6.7.)

Escape, avoidance and two-factor theory

Negative reinforcement comes in two forms. In escape learning, a response terminates an aversive stimulus that is already present: the rat presses a lever to turn off a shock that is on; you step out of the midday sun into the shade. In avoidance learning, a response prevents an aversive stimulus from occurring at all: you leave home early to avoid the traffic jam; you carry an umbrella in the monsoon.

Avoidance is usually studied in a shuttle box, a two-compartment box with a low barrier between the halves. A warning signal, such as a light, comes on, and a few seconds later the floor on that side is electrified. At first the animal jumps the barrier only after the shock begins (escape). Soon it jumps as soon as the light comes on, before any shock (avoidance). Avoidance responses are remarkably durable. In 1953 Richard Solomon and Lyman Wynne found that dogs which had learned to avoid a strong shock went on jumping, quickly and calmly, for hundreds of trials after the shock had been disconnected.

Avoidance posed a puzzle. In escape, the reinforcer is obvious: the shock goes off. But in successful avoidance nothing happens; the shock never arrives. How can the absence of an event reinforce anything? Orval Hobart Mowrer (1907 to 1982) answered in 1947 with his two-factor theory (two-process theory) of avoidance. In the first factor, classical conditioning, the warning signal is paired with shock and comes to elicit fear. In the second factor, operant conditioning, jumping the barrier takes the animal away from the warning signal, and the reduction in fear negatively reinforces the jump. The animal is not avoiding the shock; it is escaping the fear. The theory also explains why avoidance resists extinction: because the animal always leaves before the shock would have come, it never discovers that the shock has stopped.

Two-factor theory became the standard behavioural account of anxiety disorders. A woman who was once trapped in a lift learns to fear lifts (classical); taking the stairs reduces the fear and is negatively reinforced (operant); and because she never rides a lift again, the fear never extinguishes. Obsessive-compulsive disorder fits the same mould. An intrusive thought of contamination produces anxiety; washing the hands reduces the anxiety; the washing is therefore negatively reinforced, and from the standpoint of learning theory compulsions are escape and avoidance behaviours (often called safety behaviours). This is exactly why exposure and response prevention (Section 6.4) works: by blocking the escape response, it allows the anxiety to extinguish and habituate.

Exam trap

Escape terminates an aversive stimulus that is already present; avoidance prevents its onset. Both are negative reinforcement, not positive reinforcement. Compulsive acts in OCD are best described as escape (avoidance) behaviours maintained by anxiety reduction; one real paper keyed "escape", and "conditioning" is too general to be the best answer. Hand-washing in OCD is an avoidance response or safety behaviour, not a conditioned stimulus, not an unconditioned response, and not behaviour maintained by positive reinforcement. "Two-factor theory" in a learning question means Mowrer's theory of avoidance (do not confuse it with Schachter's two-factor theory of emotion, Chapter 11).

Punishment and its problems

Punishment is the most widely used and most poorly understood way of changing behaviour. Two forms are used deliberately in clinics and classrooms. Time-out (short for time-out from positive reinforcement) removes the person for a short period from a setting where reinforcement is available: the child who hits is taken out of the game for a few minutes. Response cost removes a specific amount of a reinforcer the person already has, after each instance of the unwanted behaviour: a fine, the loss of tokens, the loss of an hour of television. Both are negative punishment. A third method, overcorrection, requires the person to restore the environment to a better state than before and to practise the correct behaviour repeatedly.

Does punishment work? William Kaye Estes (1919 to 2011) gave an early and influential answer in 1944. Rats that had learned to press a lever for food were put on extinction, and some were also given a brief shock whenever they pressed. The punished rats stopped pressing quickly. But when the punishment ended they started pressing again, and by the end of extinction they had made about as many responses in total as unpunished rats. Punishment had suppressed the response, not erased it. Later work by Azrin and others showed that punishment can produce lasting suppression, but only under demanding conditions: it must be immediate, it must follow every instance of the behaviour, it must be intense enough from the start (animals and children adapt to punishment that is gradually increased), and it works best when an alternative behaviour is available and reinforced.

Even when it works, punishment has well-documented side effects. It tells the person what not to do but not what to do. It produces fear, and by classical conditioning the fear spreads to the punisher and the setting, so that the child learns to avoid the parent or the school. It can provoke aggression, and physical punishment models aggression for the child to imitate (Section 6.8). It often suppresses the behaviour only while the punisher is present, so that the punisher becomes a discriminative stimulus for not misbehaving. And it is negatively reinforcing for the punisher, because it stops the annoying behaviour at once, so punishment tends to escalate. Skinner argued throughout his career that society should rely on reinforcement instead.

Indian context

Section 17 of India's Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 prohibits physical punishment and mental harassment of children in schools. The behavioural evidence summarised above, that punishment suppresses rather than teaches and carries fear, avoidance and aggression with it, is part of the scientific case for such laws. Clinical psychologists working with families routinely teach parents to replace smacking with time-out, response cost and, above all, reinforcement of the behaviour they want.

Exam trap

Removing a desirable stimulus after an undesirable behaviour to reduce it is response cost (a form of negative punishment). It is not negative reinforcement (removal that increases behaviour), not positive punishment (something unpleasant added), and not extinction (simply no longer delivering the reinforcer that maintained the behaviour). Time-out is technically negative punishment.

Shaping, chaining, prompting and fading

Reinforcement can strengthen only a behaviour that occurs. How, then, do you teach a rat to press a lever it has never touched, or a child with autism to say a word he has never said? Skinner's answer was shaping, also called the method of successive approximations: reinforcing behaviours that are closer and closer to the target behaviour. To shape lever pressing, the trainer first reinforces the rat for facing the lever, then only for moving towards it, then only for rearing near it, then only for touching it, and finally only for pressing it. At each stage the previous approximation is no longer reinforced (it is put on extinction) while the closer one is. Skinner could shape a pigeon to turn a full circle in a few minutes this way.

Shaping can work on any dimension of a response: its form, its speed or rate, its force or intensity, its duration, or how quickly it begins. And because it relies only on reinforcing what the learner does, it needs no verbal instructions at all, which is why it is used to train animals, infants and people with severe intellectual disability or autism. A speech therapist who first reinforces any sound, then a sound beginning with "b", then "ba", and finally "ball" is shaping.

Many skills are sequences rather than single responses. A chaining procedure teaches such a sequence by linking responses so that each one produces the discriminative stimulus for the next, with reinforcement coming at the end. Each link's stimulus is therefore both a conditioned reinforcer for the response before it and a signal for the response after it. Chains are often taught backwards (backward chaining): when teaching a child to put on a shirt, the therapist first teaches the last step (pulling it down) so that the child always ends with success, then the step before, and so on. A rat named Barnabus at Brown University in the early 1960s was trained by backward chaining to climb a spiral staircase, cross a drawbridge, climb a ladder, pull a toy car to him, ride it through a tunnel and finally press a lever for food.

Two further techniques help get behaviour started. A prompt is an extra stimulus that makes the correct response more likely: a verbal hint, a gesture, a demonstration or physical guidance of the learner's hand. Fading is the gradual removal of the prompt so that, in the end, the natural discriminative stimulus alone controls the behaviour.

Exam trap

Reinforcing successive approximations is shaping. Linking a sequence of responses is chaining; adding an extra cue is prompting; gradually withdrawing the cue is fading. Shaping is not "removing a reinforcer to weaken behaviour", not "pairing two neutral stimuli", not "punishing every incorrect response", not "pigeons pressing a button for food" (that describes operant conditioning in general) and not "any behaviour with pleasant consequences is repeated" (the law of effect). One unusual real item (RML 2024) asked on which "principles" shaping is based and keyed speed, power and non-verbal, excluding "performance". The logic, as far as it can be reconstructed, is that shaping works on the speed and force of responses and needs no verbal instruction; learn the definition first, and treat this item as a one-off.

Operant extinction

When a response that has been reinforced stops being reinforced, it declines. This is operant extinction, and it follows a characteristic course. At first, responding often gets worse before it gets better: the rate rises, the responses become more forceful and varied, and emotional behaviour such as aggression may appear. This temporary surge is the extinction burst. You have shown one yourself if you have ever pressed a lift button again and again, harder and harder, when the lift did not come. Then responding declines until it returns to its operant level, the baseline rate at which the response occurred before it was ever reinforced. (Because many responses occur occasionally even without reinforcement, extinction brings the rate back to this earlier level, which is not necessarily zero.) After a rest, the extinguished response shows spontaneous recovery, just as in classical conditioning.

Key study: Extinguishing a toddler's bedtime tantrums (Williams, 1959)

Problem. A 21-month-old boy, recovering from a long illness during which he had received constant attention, screamed and fussed whenever he was put to bed unless a parent stayed in the room until he fell asleep. Method. Carl Williams reasoned that the tantrums were maintained by parental attention, so he advised the parents to put the boy to bed in a relaxed way, leave the room, and not go back in however much he cried. Result. The first night the boy screamed for 45 minutes; the second night he did not cry at all; within ten occasions he was smiling as his parents left. About a week later, when an aunt put him to bed, he cried and she went back in; the tantrums returned (spontaneous recovery, reinforced), and had to be extinguished a second time, after which none occurred for the following two years. Conclusion. Ignoring an attention-maintained behaviour is extinction, and it works if it is consistent. A single "giving in" during the extinction burst puts the behaviour on intermittent reinforcement, which, as Section 6.6 will show, makes it even harder to extinguish.

Exam trap

"A parent deliberately ignores a child's temper tantrums to discourage them." The correct answer is extinction. The source key for this real item (Amity 2023, repeated in JSS practice sets) marks "operant escape", which is wrong: escape is a response that terminates an aversive stimulus already present (negative reinforcement), and nothing in the parent's ignoring fits that description. Learn the item as extinction. When reinforcement of a previously reinforced behaviour stops and the behaviour fades, the term is extinction, not generalisation, discrimination or shaping. "Habit weakening through the elimination of any reward" is likewise extinction, not negative reinforcement, reciprocal inhibition or flooding.

6.6 Schedules of Reinforcement and Other Operant PhenomenaHigh yield · 25 Qs

In the 1930s, Skinner later recalled, he was running short of food pellets, which he made by hand, and he began reinforcing his rats only once a minute instead of after every press to make his supply last. To his surprise the rats kept pressing, and at steady rates. That economy launched one of the most productive research programmes in psychology. With Charles Ferster he spent years mapping how different rules for delivering reinforcement shape the pattern of responding, and their book Schedules of Reinforcement (1957) contains hundreds of cumulative records. The basic findings are few, highly examined and easy to remember once you see the records.

Continuous and partial reinforcement

A schedule of reinforcement is the rule that determines which responses are reinforced. Under continuous reinforcement (CRF), every correct response is reinforced. Under partial reinforcement (also called intermittent reinforcement), only some responses are. Continuous reinforcement produces the fastest learning, so it is the best way to establish a new behaviour. But behaviour that has been continuously reinforced also extinguishes fastest. When the vending machine that always works suddenly swallows your coin, you stop putting coins in almost at once; when the slot machine that rarely pays fails again, you keep playing.

This greater persistence after partial reinforcement is the partial reinforcement extinction effect. It was demonstrated in humans by Lloyd Humphreys in 1939, using conditioned eyeblinks: people for whom the puff of air had followed the signal on only half the trials kept blinking to the signal far longer in extinction than people for whom it had followed every time. The simplest explanation is that it is harder to tell that reinforcement has stopped when it was intermittent to begin with. The practical rule follows: use continuous reinforcement to teach a behaviour, then shift to partial reinforcement to maintain it.

The four basic schedules

Partial schedules are built from two choices. Reinforcement can depend on the number of responses (a ratio schedule) or on the time that has passed (an interval schedule, in which the first response after the interval is reinforced). And the requirement can be the same every time (fixed) or can vary unpredictably around an average (variable). Crossing the two choices gives four schedules, and each produces its own signature on the cumulative record (Figure 6.4); Table 6.7 summarises them, with continuous reinforcement for comparison.

  • On a fixed-ratio schedule (FR), reinforcement follows a fixed number of responses: FR-10 means every tenth response. Responding is high and steady, because the faster the animal responds, the sooner it is paid, but each reinforcer is followed by a short break, the post-reinforcement pause. The pause lengthens as the ratio grows, and if the ratio is raised too fast responding can break down (ratio strain). Piece-rate work, such as a tailor paid for every ten blouses stitched, and "buy nine coffees, get the tenth free" cards are fixed-ratio schedules.
  • On a variable-ratio schedule (VR), reinforcement follows an unpredictable number of responses that averages a set value. The very next response might pay off, so there is no reason to pause. VR produces the highest and steadiest rate of responding and the greatest resistance to extinction of all the schedules. Slot machines, lottery tickets and a salesperson's cold calls are the classic examples, and they explain why gambling is so hard to stop.
  • On a fixed-interval schedule (FI), the first response after a fixed period is reinforced: FI-60 seconds. Responses during the interval earn nothing, so animals learn to pause after each reinforcer and then respond faster and faster as the end of the interval approaches. The cumulative record shows a curve called the FI scallop. Overall rates are relatively low. You are on something like an FI schedule when a dish is due out of the oven in thirty minutes: you hardly check at first and check more and more often as the time approaches. Students who study little early in term and frantically before a fixed examination date show the same scallop.
  • On a variable-interval schedule (VI), the first response after an unpredictable period, averaging a set value, is reinforced. Because reinforcement could become available at any moment, responding is moderate but very steady, with no scallop, and it is quite resistant to extinction. Checking your phone for a reply to a message, fishing, and preparing for a teacher who sets surprise quizzes are VI schedules.

The Premack principle

What can serve as a reinforcer? The traditional answer was a list of stimuli: food, water, praise, money. In 1959 David Premack (1925 to 2015) proposed something more radical. Reinforcers, he argued, are not stimuli but activities, and what makes an activity reinforcing is its probability relative to other activities. The Premack principle states that a more probable (preferred) behaviour can be used to reinforce a less probable (less preferred) behaviour. You find out how probable each behaviour is by watching how much time the person spends on it when free to choose.

Premack's experiments showed that reinforcement is relative, not fixed. Some children preferred eating sweets to playing a pinball machine, and for them sweets reinforced pinball playing; for children who preferred pinball, the opportunity to play reinforced eating sweets. In rats, access to water reinforced running in a wheel when the rats were thirsty, but when the rats had free water and had been prevented from running, the opportunity to run reinforced drinking. The everyday version is called grandma's rule: "first eat your vegetables, then you can have dessert"; "first finish your homework, then you can play video games." For a child who rarely does homework, letting him play video games only after he finishes it is more effective than vague praise, and it costs nothing.

A later refinement, the response-deprivation hypothesis of William Timberlake and James Allison (1974), showed that even a low-probability activity can serve as a reinforcer if the person has been restricted to less of it than they would normally choose. (Premack is also remembered for a quite different idea: in 1978, with Guy Woodruff, he asked whether a chimpanzee has a "theory of mind", a question covered in the Developmental Psychology book.)

Exam trap

The Premack principle says high-probability behaviour reinforces low-probability behaviour, never the reverse. It does not say that reinforcement works best when intermittent (that is the partial reinforcement effect), that behaviour followed by satisfaction recurs (the law of effect), that complex behaviour is built from successive approximations (shaping), that punishment beats reinforcement, or that continuous reinforcement is most resistant to extinction (it is the least). Some question banks date the principle 1965, the year of Premack's longer statement of it; his first statement was in 1959.

Superstitious behaviour

Reinforcement strengthens whatever response happens to precede it, whether or not the response produced it. In a 1948 paper titled "'Superstition' in the pigeon", Skinner gave hungry pigeons food every 15 seconds, whatever they were doing. Six of his eight birds developed odd, repetitive rituals: one turned anticlockwise two or three times between feedings, another kept thrusting its head into an upper corner of the cage, two swung their heads from side to side like a pendulum. Each bird, Skinner argued, had happened to be doing something when food arrived; that act was reinforced, became more likely, was therefore more likely to be under way when the next food came, and so on. Superstitious behaviour is behaviour strengthened by accidental (adventitious) reinforcement, with no real relationship between the act and the reinforcer. A batsman who will not change his "lucky" pads during a run of good scores, or a student who takes the same pen to every exam, is showing it.

The interpretation has been challenged. When John Staddon and Virginia Simmelhag repeated the experiment in 1971 and watched more closely, they found that the behaviours just before food were mostly the same species-typical, food-related acts in every bird (pecking near the food hopper), which suggests that much of what Skinner saw reflected Pavlovian anticipation of food rather than accidental operant reinforcement.

Exam trap

Strictly, Skinner's pigeons were on a fixed-time schedule (food every 15 seconds, regardless of behaviour), but many textbooks describe it as fixed-interval reinforcement. Either way, the answer to "what did the superstition experiment demonstrate?" is accidental (adventitious) reinforcement.

Learned helplessness

What happens to an animal that learns that nothing it does makes any difference? The answer came from a discovery that its investigators did not expect, and it became one of the most influential models in clinical psychology.

Key study: Learned helplessness in dogs (Overmier and Seligman, 1967; Seligman and Maier, 1967)

Question. Does experience with uncontrollable aversive events change later learning? Method. In the "triadic" design used by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier, dogs were first placed in harnesses in three groups. Dogs in the escape group received shocks that they could switch off by pressing a panel with their heads. Each dog in the yoked group was wired to an escape dog and received exactly the same shocks, at the same times and of the same duration, but nothing it did affected them. A third group received no shocks. A day later all the dogs were placed in a shuttle box where they could escape or avoid shock simply by jumping a low barrier. Result. The escape dogs and the unshocked dogs quickly learned to jump. Most of the yoked dogs did not: after a brief struggle they lay down and whined, taking shock after shock without trying to escape, and even when one did jump by chance it seldom repeated the response. Conclusion. The yoked dogs had learned that their responses and the shocks were independent. This expectation produced three deficits: motivational (they stopped trying), cognitive (they had difficulty learning that a response now worked) and emotional (they appeared depressed). Seligman called this learned helplessness: passive acceptance of aversive events, even avoidable ones, that follows experience with uncontrollable aversive events. Significance. Because the shocks were identical for escape and yoked dogs, the damage was caused not by the shocks but by the lack of control.

The first published report was by J. Bruce Overmier and Seligman in 1967, with Seligman and Maier's follow-up in the same year. Later experiments by Donald Hiroto (1974) produced the same pattern in people: university students exposed to loud noise they could not turn off later failed to learn a simple hand movement that would have turned it off.

Seligman proposed learned helplessness as a model of depression, because depressed people, like the yoked dogs, often stop trying, find it hard to see that their actions can work, and feel low. The model had a problem: people do not become depressed after every uncontrollable event. In 1978 Lyn Abramson, Seligman and John Teasdale reformulated it in cognitive terms: what matters is how people explain uncontrollable events to themselves. Depression is most likely in people who attribute bad events to causes that are internal ("it's me"), stable ("it will always be like this") and global ("it affects everything I do"). This attributional reformulation, and the hopelessness theory that followed it, are covered with depression in the Psychiatric Disorders book. Fifty years after the original studies, Maier and Seligman (2016) revised their own account using neuroscience: passivity, they now argue, is the brain's default reaction to prolonged aversive events, and what animals learn in the escape condition is that they have control, a discovery that recruits the prefrontal cortex to damp down the stress response.

Exam trap

Learned helplessness was offered by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier (with Bruce Overmier), not by Aaron Beck (cognitive triad), Harry Stack Sullivan (interpersonal theory) or Daniel Kahneman (heuristics). It is passive acceptance of later aversive events after repeated unavoidable ones. It is not escaping an aversive stimulus, not watching a model rewarded for aggression, and not extinction from loss of reinforcement. One real stem places it "in social learning theory"; the concept actually comes from animal learning research and was later reformulated cognitively, but the answer is unchanged.

Instinctive drift and other limits on operant conditioning

Just as Garcia showed that classical conditioning is constrained by biology, two of Skinner's own students showed the same for operant conditioning. Keller Breland and Marian Breland set up a business training animals for zoos, fairs and television advertisements, and by the late 1950s they had trained thousands of animals of dozens of species. In 1961 they published a paper wittily titled "The misbehavior of organisms", a play on the title of Skinner's 1938 book. They had trained a raccoon to pick up a coin and drop it into a metal box for food. With one coin all went well. Given two coins, the raccoon began rubbing them together, dipping them into the box and pulling them out again, for minutes at a time, exactly as raccoons rub and wash food, and it never stopped doing so despite losing reinforcement. Pigs trained to carry large wooden coins to a "piggy bank" began, after some weeks, to drop them, root them along the ground with their snouts and toss them in the air, as pigs root for food. The Brelands called this instinctive drift: the tendency of a learned behaviour to drift towards the instinctive, species-typical behaviour associated with the reinforcer. Their conclusion was blunt: operant conditioning cannot be understood without understanding the animal's instinctive repertoire.

A related finding is autoshaping. In 1968 Paul Brown and Herbert Jenkins found that if a pigeon's key is simply lit for a few seconds before food is delivered, the pigeon begins pecking the key even though pecking has no effect on the food. A Pavlovian pairing of light and food produced what looks like an operant response. Classical and operant conditioning are separable in principle, but in living animals they constantly interact.

Choice: the matching law

In everyday life you are rarely choosing whether to respond; you are choosing which of several reinforced activities to do. Richard Herrnstein (1930 to 1994) studied this in 1961 by giving pigeons two keys, each reinforced on its own variable-interval schedule. He found that the proportion of pecks a pigeon made on each key matched the proportion of reinforcers it obtained from that key. This is the matching law: B1/(B1 + B2) = R1/(R1 + R2), where B is the rate of behaviour on each alternative and R is the rate of reinforcement it produces. If the left key yields 40 reinforcers an hour and the right key 20, the pigeon makes about two-thirds of its pecks on the left. People behave similarly: the time we spend with different friends, or on different activities, tends to be proportional to the rewards each provides.

Contrast effects: the Crespi effect

Does a bigger reward produce better learning, or just more effort? In 1942 Leo Crespi trained rats to run down a runway for food, some for a large amount and some for a small amount, and then shifted all of them to the same intermediate amount. The rats shifted down from a large reward immediately ran slower than rats that had received the intermediate amount all along. The rats shifted up from a small reward immediately ran faster than those rats. Crespi called these the depression and elation effects; in 1949 David Zeaman renamed them negative contrast and positive contrast, and today they are known together as contrast effects, or the Crespi effect. Two conclusions followed. First, the value of a reinforcer is relative to what the organism is used to: a mark of 85 delights a student who usually gets 70 and dismays one who usually gets 95. Second, the changes in running speed were too abrupt to be changes in learning; they were changes in motivation. Reward size affects performance through incentive, a point that forced Clark Hull to add incentive motivation to his equations (Section 6.9).

Exam trap

The Crespi effect refers to a change in motivation (performance) when the magnitude of reinforcement changes: an increase in reward produces a disproportionate increase in performance (positive contrast) and a decrease produces a disproportionate decrease (negative contrast). The keyed answer in IHBAS 2025 described the positive half. It is not retroactive inhibition (forgetting caused by later learning, Chapter 7), not extinction and not insight.

Biofeedback: operant control of the body

For decades it was assumed that only skeletal muscles could be operantly conditioned, while heart rate, blood pressure and other autonomic responses could only be classically conditioned. In the late 1960s Neal Miller (1909 to 2002) and his student Leo DiCara reported that rats paralysed with curare, so that they could not use their muscles to influence their hearts indirectly, could learn to raise or lower their heart rates when rewarded with electrical stimulation of the brain's reward areas. Later attempts, including Miller's own, failed to replicate the strongest results, but the idea that autonomic functions can be brought under voluntary control with feedback had already launched a clinical field.

Biofeedback is a technique in which a bodily process that is normally outside awareness is measured by an instrument and displayed back to the person, as a tone, a moving line or a number, so that he or she can learn to control it. Each change in the desired direction is reinforced by the feedback itself, so biofeedback is operant conditioning combined with knowledge of results (Section 6.10). Electromyographic (EMG) biofeedback of muscle tension in the forehead and neck is used for tension-type headache and chronic pain; thermal biofeedback of finger temperature is used for migraine; electroencephalographic biofeedback (neurofeedback) has been used for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and epilepsy; and feedback of skin conductance or heart-rate variability is used in anxiety and stress management. The idea is older than the name. In 1948 the gynaecologist Arnold Kegel taught women pelvic-floor exercises with a perineometer, a pressure gauge that let a patient watch her own muscle squeeze on a dial, an early form of biofeedback.

Exam trap

"The technique based on the idea that the autonomic nervous system can come under voluntary control through operant conditioning" is biofeedback, not hypnosis, sublimation (a Freudian defence mechanism) or systematic desensitisation (Wolpe's counterconditioning). EMG biofeedback is most useful for tension-type headache. Kegel's perineometer was an early form of biofeedback, not aversion therapy, a token economy or desensitisation.

6.7 Cognitive LearningMedium yield · 9 Qs

By the 1930s the dominant view in American psychology was that learning consists of stimulus-response connections stamped in by reinforcement, with nothing in between worth talking about. Three lines of research, in rats, apes and monkeys, showed that this view left out something essential: what animals learn is often knowledge, a representation of how the world is arranged, which they use flexibly when the occasion demands. This section describes those three lines of research and ends with a brief account of how concepts are formed.

Tolman: latent learning and cognitive maps

Edward Chace Tolman (1886 to 1959) was a behaviourist in method: he studied rats in mazes and measured only what they did. But he insisted that behaviour is purposive, directed towards goals, and that it should be described at the level of whole, meaningful acts ("running to the food box") rather than muscle twitches. His approach, set out in Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men (1932), is called purposive behaviourism. Tolman argued that between the stimulus and the response there are intervening variables, such as expectancies and knowledge of the environment, that cannot be seen but can be inferred from behaviour. (A similar point had been made by Robert Sessions Woodworth, who proposed replacing the S-R formula with S-O-R, stimulus-organism-response, to show that what the organism brings to the situation shapes its response.)

Key study: Tolman and Honzik's latent learning experiment (1930)

Question. Do rats learn a maze only when they are reinforced for running it? Method. Tolman and Charles Honzik ran three groups of hungry rats through a complex maze with fourteen choice points, once a day for seventeen days. One group always found food in the goal box. A second group never found food; they were simply removed from the goal box when they reached it. A third group found no food for the first ten days, but from day 11 onwards found food in the goal box. Result. The always-rewarded rats steadily reduced their errors. The never-rewarded rats improved only slightly. The third group made as many errors as the never-rewarded rats for ten days, but the day after food was introduced their errors dropped dramatically, to the level of the always-rewarded group and even slightly below it (Figure 6.5). Conclusion. The third group must have been learning the layout of the maze all along, during the unrewarded days, without showing it. The reward did not create the learning; it gave the rats a reason to display it. Significance. This is the clearest demonstration of the difference between learning and performance. (Hugh Blodgett had reported a similar result in 1929.)

Latent learning is learning that occurs without obvious reinforcement and remains hidden until there is an incentive to demonstrate it. What had the rats learned? Tolman's answer, developed in a famous 1948 paper, "Cognitive maps in rats and men", was that they had formed a cognitive map: a mental representation of the spatial layout of the environment. Evidence came from experiments on place learning. In 1946 Tolman and his colleagues trained one group of rats always to find food in the same place in a cross-shaped maze, whichever arm they started from, so that sometimes they had to turn left and sometimes right; another group always had to make the same turn, so the food's location changed. The rats that had to learn a place learned faster than those that had to learn a response, which a pure S-R theory cannot explain. When rats that had learned a roundabout route to food were later offered many straight paths radiating in different directions, many chose the path that pointed most directly towards where the food had been, as if they knew where it was, not just how to get there.

Tolman also argued that animals learn expectancies: that a particular goal will follow a particular action. A vivid demonstration had come from Otto Tinklepaugh in 1928. A monkey watched a banana being hidden under a cup; when it lifted the cup and found that the banana had been secretly replaced by lettuce, a food it normally ate happily, it refused the lettuce, searched around and shrieked at the observers. It had expected a banana. Because what is learned in Tolman's view is knowledge of "what leads to what", his position is variously called sign learning theory, expectancy theory and place learning theory.

Exam trap

Latent learning and the cognitive map belong to Tolman, not Bandura, Skinner, Köhler or Harlow. "A sudden increase in performance upon reinforcement, despite never being reinforced on previous trials" is latent learning, not observational learning, discrimination learning or vicarious reinforcement. Tolman's rats do not show shaping, pure S-R learning or spontaneous recovery. An option naming "Gould" refers to the biologist James Gould, who studied honeybee navigation and asked whether insects might have map-like knowledge; the concept of the cognitive map is Tolman's (1948).

Köhler: insight

On the other side of the Atlantic, a different challenge to trial-and-error learning was taking shape. Wolfgang Köhler (1887 to 1967) was, with Max Wertheimer and Kurt Koffka, one of the three founders of Gestalt psychology, which holds that experience is organised into wholes that are more than the sum of their parts (Chapters 2 and 4). From 1913 to 1920 Köhler directed a research station for apes on Tenerife in the Canary Islands, where the First World War kept him far longer than planned. There he set chimpanzees problems that could not be solved by any single learned response.

In the simplest problems a banana lay on the ground outside the cage, out of reach, and a stick lay inside; the chimpanzees soon used the stick to rake the banana in. In the famous two-stick problem, the banana was farther away, and neither of two sticks was long enough on its own; one stick was hollow, so the other could be fitted into it. The most gifted chimpanzee, Sultan, tried with one stick and then the other, failed, and gave up. Later, while playing idly with the two sticks, he happened to hold them in a line, pushed the thinner one into the hollow end of the thicker, and at once ran to the bars and raked in the banana. In the box problems a banana was hung from the roof of the enclosure, too high to reach by jumping. The chimpanzees jumped in vain, paced about, and then, often quite suddenly, dragged a box under the banana and climbed on it; some later learned to stack two or three boxes. Köhler described this work in a book published in German in 1917 and in English in 1925 as The Mentality of Apes.

Insight learning is the sudden grasp of a problem's solution through perceiving the relationships among its elements, a reorganisation of the perceptual field. Köhler identified its features. The change from no solution to solution is sudden, not gradual like Thorndike's curves. It is often preceded by a period of apparent inactivity in which the animal surveys the situation. Once found, the solution is carried out smoothly and without errors. It is remembered well. And it transfers readily to similar problems. It also depends on all the elements of the problem being in view at once: the chimpanzees rarely used a stick that was behind them. The subjective feeling of sudden understanding that accompanies insight is called the aha experience (the German "Aha-Erlebnis", a term usually credited to the psychologist Karl Bühler).

Insight was a powerful argument against the view that all learning is the gradual strengthening of S-R connections. But it has a qualification. In 1945 Herbert Birch found that young chimpanzees who had never handled sticks did not solve stick problems insightfully; after a few days of playing with sticks, they did. Insight, it seems, recombines things that were learned earlier. Harlow's work, described next, explains how.

Exam trap

Sultan, sticks and stacked boxes mean insight learning (Köhler), not trial-and-error, classical conditioning or latent learning. The sudden realisation of a solution is the "aha" experience, not the "light bulb", "momentary" or "learning". Do not confuse Köhler's insight with insight in psychiatry, the patient's awareness of his own illness, which is rated in the mental status examination on a six-grade scale (with intellectual insight usually placed at grade 5; Clinical Psychology book), or with insight as a goal of psychoanalysis.

Harlow: learning to learn

Harry Harlow (1905 to 1981), working with rhesus monkeys at the University of Wisconsin, asked what happens when an animal is given not one problem but hundreds of similar ones. In each discrimination problem a monkey was shown two objects it had never seen before, such as a cup and a wooden block, one of which always covered a raisin or a piece of food. The monkey had a few trials to discover which object paid. Then a new pair of objects was introduced, and so on through several hundred problems.

On the first few problems the monkeys learned slowly and gradually, in true trial-and-error fashion, and on the second trial of each new problem they were right only about half the time, no better than chance. But each problem was solved a little faster than the last. By the end, the monkeys were right on the second trial of a new problem more than 95 percent of the time: one trial was enough to tell them which object to choose. They had learned a general strategy, which can be described as "if the object you picked paid, stay with it; if not, switch" (win-stay, lose-shift). Harlow, in a 1949 paper, called this a learning set: an improvement in the ability to learn new problems of a given type that results from experience with many problems of that type, or, more simply, learning to learn.

Learning sets reconcile Thorndike and Köhler. A naive animal facing a new kind of problem learns by trial and error; an experienced one, armed with learning sets, appears to solve problems by insight. What looks like sudden understanding in an adult may rest on a long history of earlier, gradual learning. Students do the same: the first algebra problem of a new type takes an hour, the twentieth takes a minute.

Concept formation

A concept is a mental category that groups objects, events or ideas sharing common properties: "dog", "fruit", "democracy". Concepts let us respond to new instances without learning about each one separately. The full cognitive treatment of concepts, including the strategies that Jerome Bruner, Jacqueline Goodnow and George Austin described in 1956 and the later prototype view, is in Chapter 8. Here we need only the stage account of concept formation that is taught in general and educational psychology, in which a concept is built up in four steps.

  1. Observation. The learner encounters many instances. A two-year-old in Mysuru sees the neighbour's Labrador, the street dogs outside the temple, a Pomeranian on television.
  2. Discrimination. The learner notices how instances differ from non-instances: cows and cats, though they also have four legs and fur, are not dogs.
  3. Generalisation. The learner extends the response (the word "dog") to all the instances that share the essential features, however different their size and colour.
  4. Abstraction. The learner extracts the common defining properties from the particular instances, so that the concept exists independently of any one dog and can be applied to breeds never seen before. The concept is then named and can be defined.

Notice that the stages parallel the conditioning processes of Section 6.3: generalisation and discrimination, which Pavlov studied in dogs, are the raw materials from which human concepts are built.

Exam trap

The keyed order (JEMAS 2025, repeated in JSS practice sets) is Observation, Discrimination, Generalisation, Abstraction. Options that start with abstraction, or that put generalisation before discrimination, are wrong; so is any option containing assimilation, which is Piaget's term for fitting new experience into existing schemas (Developmental Psychology book), not a stage of concept formation. Some educational psychology texts list the steps as observation, comparison, abstraction and generalisation, followed by naming; if you see that version, accept it too, because "comparison" is discrimination under another name.

6.8 Observational LearningHigh yield · 17 Qs

Imagine learning to drive purely by operant conditioning: you would press pedals and turn the wheel at random, and be reinforced whenever the car happened to move safely forward. You would not survive the first lesson. Most complex human skills, from tying a turban to rolling a round chapati to performing a cover drive, are learned largely by watching someone else do them first. So are many attitudes, fears, habits of speech and ways of handling anger. Observational learning (also called social learning or modelling) is learning that occurs by watching the behaviour of others, called models, and its consequences.

Before Bandura: imitation as learned behaviour

The first learning theorists to take imitation seriously were Neal Miller and John Dollard (1900 to 1980) at Yale. In Social Learning and Imitation (1941) they argued that imitation is itself an operant response that is learned because it is reinforced. A younger child who follows an older sibling to where the father hands out sweets is rewarded for copying, so copying becomes a habit; they called this matched-dependent behaviour. In Personality and Psychotherapy (1950) they went further and translated Freud's ideas into the language of Clark Hull's learning theory, describing behaviour in terms of drive, cue, response and reward. This work is usually called early social learning theory. Its limitation was that, on their account, imitation still required reinforcement of the observer.

The Bobo doll studies

Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021), born in a small town in Alberta, Canada, and for most of his career a professor at Stanford University, showed that observers can learn new behaviour without performing it and without any reinforcement at all.

Key study: Bandura, Ross and Ross's Bobo doll experiment (1961)

Question. Will children imitate aggression they have merely watched, without being reinforced? Method. Bandura, Dorothea Ross and Sheila Ross studied 72 children, aged about three to six, from the Stanford University nursery school. In one condition each child, busy making pictures with potato prints and stickers in a corner of a room, watched an adult model attack a large inflatable "Bobo" clown doll in unusual ways: sitting on it and punching it on the nose, striking it with a mallet, tossing it in the air, kicking it about the room, while saying things like "Sock him in the nose!" and "Pow!". In a second condition the model played quietly with tinker toys and ignored Bobo; a third group saw no model. Each child was then mildly frustrated (shown some very attractive toys and told they were being kept for other children) and left in a room containing various toys, including Bobo and a mallet. Result. Children who had watched the aggressive model reproduced many of the model's specific, novel acts and phrases, far more than children in the other two groups. Boys showed more physical aggression than girls, and the male model was imitated more for physical aggression. Conclusion. New behaviour can be acquired simply by observation, with no reinforcement to either the model or the child. Criticism. A Bobo doll is designed to be hit, so the study may show play rather than aggression towards people; the children may have sensed what the adults expected; and only short-term effects were measured.

Two follow-up studies extended the finding. In 1963 Bandura and the Rosses showed that a filmed model, and even a cartoon-like model dressed as a cat, produced as much imitative aggression as a live model, a result that made Bandura a central figure in the debate about violence on television (Social Psychology book). And in 1965 Bandura showed children a film in which the model's aggression was rewarded, punished, or had no consequences. Children who saw the model punished imitated less aggression than the others. But when the experimenter then offered every child an attractive incentive (stickers and fruit juice) for showing what the model had done, the differences disappeared: children in all three groups could reproduce the aggressive acts equally well. The consequences to the model had affected performance, not learning. This is the same learning-performance distinction that Tolman's rats had shown.

The 1965 study also illustrates two key terms. Vicarious reinforcement is an increase in an observer's tendency to perform a behaviour after seeing a model rewarded for it; vicarious punishment is a decrease after seeing a model punished. A younger brother who watches his elder sister get scolded for answering back learns, without being scolded himself, not to answer back, at least when the parents are watching.

The four processes of observational learning

Bandura analysed observational learning into four component processes, all of which must operate for an observed behaviour to be reproduced (Figure 6.6, left).

  • Attention. The observer must notice the model and the relevant features of what the model does. We attend more to models who are attractive, competent, high in status, similar to ourselves, and whose behaviour is distinctive or rewarded.
  • Retention. The observer must store what was seen, in images and in words ("first fold, then tuck"), and rehearse it, because the behaviour may not be performed until much later.
  • Motor reproduction (or production). The observer must be physically able to turn the memory into action, and usually refines the performance with practice and feedback. You can watch Virat Kohli for years and remember every detail of his cover drive without being able to play it.
  • Motivation. Finally, the observer must have a reason to perform the behaviour: direct reinforcement, vicarious reinforcement, or self-reinforcement (the satisfaction of meeting one's own standards).

The first three processes govern learning; the fourth governs performance. That is why direct reinforcement is not a necessary condition of observational learning, although it strongly affects whether the learned behaviour is ever shown. It also explains why observational learning helps someone acquire adaptive behaviour only when the model actually demonstrates the target behaviour: a model who acts angrily, playfully or randomly gives the observer nothing useful to attend to and retain.

Exam trap

The four processes are attention, retention, motor reproduction (production) and motivation. They are not "sensory, perceptual, cognitive, affective"; not "encoding, storage, retrieval, evaluation" (the stages of memory, Chapter 7); and not "stimulus, response, reinforcement, extinction" (operant conditioning). "Which is NOT necessary for observational learning?" Reinforcement. The view that reinforcement is not required to learn a behaviour by observation is Bandura's, not Skinner's, Tolman's or Miller and Dollard's (who held that imitation must be reinforced). The Bobo doll studies demonstrate observational learning of aggression through modelling, not operant shaping, classical conditioning of fear, negative reinforcement or the effects of sensory deprivation.

From social learning to social cognitive theory

Bandura's early theory, set out with his student Richard Walters in Social Learning and Personality Development (1963), is often called later social learning theory, to distinguish it from Dollard and Miller's. Over the following decades Bandura gave cognition an ever larger role, and in Social Foundations of Thought and Action (1986) he renamed his position social cognitive theory. Three ideas from this period are heavily examined.

The first is reciprocal determinism (Bandura, 1978), which Bandura later called triadic reciprocal causation. Strict behaviourists such as Skinner held a one-way view: the environment determines behaviour. Bandura argued that three sets of factors, the person (beliefs, expectations, self-efficacy), behaviour and the environment, continuously influence one another in both directions (Figure 6.6, right). A student who believes she can master statistics (person) attends classes and practises (behaviour); her practice earns praise and a seat in the advanced group (environment); the new environment strengthens her belief, and so on round the triangle.

The second is self-regulation. Bandura noted that most human behaviour occurs in the absence of any immediate external reward or punishment. People set their own standards, observe their own behaviour (self-observation), compare it with the standards (the judgemental process) and reward or criticise themselves accordingly (self-reaction). Much of what keeps a student at her desk at eleven at night is self-regulation, not anyone else's reinforcement.

The third is self-efficacy, introduced in a 1977 paper: a person's belief in his or her capability to organise and carry out the actions needed to achieve a particular goal or deal with a particular situation. Self-efficacy is specific to a task: you may have high self-efficacy for public speaking and low self-efficacy for mathematics. It is also different from an outcome expectation, the belief that a behaviour will lead to a result: you may be sure that regular exercise would make you fitter (outcome expectation) and doubt that you could keep it up (efficacy expectation). Bandura identified four sources of self-efficacy: performance attainments (past successes, the most powerful source), vicarious experiences (seeing similar others succeed), verbal persuasion (being told you can do it) and physiological and emotional states (reading a calm body as a sign of competence). Within social cognitive theory, self-efficacy is the most important motivational construct: people with high self-efficacy choose harder tasks, try harder and persist longer.

Clinical link

Modelling is a therapy technique in its own right. In 1969 Bandura and his colleagues treated people with snake phobia by participant modelling: the therapist first handled the snake while the client watched, then guided the client through closer and closer contact. It worked better than watching a film of models or than systematic desensitisation, and Bandura argued that every successful treatment works by raising the client's self-efficacy. Social skills training, assertiveness training and much of cognitive behaviour therapy use modelling and rehearsal (Clinical Psychology book).

Remember: Rotter's social learning theory

Julian Rotter (1916 to 2014) developed a separate social learning theory in Social Learning and Clinical Psychology (1954). For Rotter, the behaviour potential (BP) is the likelihood of a particular behaviour occurring in a given situation, calculated in relation to a particular reinforcement or set of reinforcements. It depends on the expectancy (E), the person's subjective probability that the behaviour will lead to the reinforcement, and on the reinforcement value (RV), how much the person wants that reinforcement: BP = f(E and RV), within a given psychological situation. Generalised expectancies about whether outcomes depend on one's own actions became his concept of locus of control (1966), internal or external. Social learning frameworks in general explain behaviour by cognitive variables such as expectancies, situational demands and incentives, rather than by reinforcement history alone. (Rotter's theory is treated fully in the Personality book.)

Exam trap

Reciprocal determinism (Bandura): cognition, behaviour and environment influence one another bidirectionally; the "reciprocal causation model" belongs to social cognitive theory, not to two-factor theory (Mowrer), drive-reduction theory (Hull) or constructivist theory (Piaget, Vygotsky). Do not confuse it with reciprocal inhibition (Wolpe) or reciprocal altruism (the evolutionary biologist Robert Trivers). Self-efficacy is belief in one's capability for a specific task; it is not overall self-worth (self-esteem), not attributing outcomes to fate or chance (external locus of control, Rotter) and not the striving to overcome inferiority (Adler). The theorist who developed self-efficacy is Bandura, not Beck, Ellis or Skinner. Bandura's claim that most behaviour comes from self-regulation is the right answer when the alternatives are negative reinforcement, "reciprocal dualism" or "behavioural determinism". In a match-the-list question: Dollard and Miller = early social learning; Bandura and Walters = later social learning; Skinner = radical behaviourism; Maslow = self-actualisation. Behaviour that is emitted and operates on the environment is operant behaviour, not Jung's principle of opposites, Rotter's behaviour potential or Allport's personal dispositions.

6.9 Other Learning TheoristsLower yield · 1 Q

Between about 1930 and 1960, learning was the battleground on which American psychology fought out its biggest theoretical arguments. Pavlov, Thorndike, Skinner, Tolman and Bandura, whom you have already met, were not alone on that field. A handful of other theorists built complete systems that tried to explain all learning with a few principles. None of these "grand theories" survived intact, but their ideas and their vocabulary run all through later psychology, and their names run all through examination papers. The standard guide to them was Theories of Learning (1948) by Ernest Ropiequet Hilgard (1904 to 2001), a book that went through five editions (the later ones with Gordon Bower) and taught generations of students to compare the theories by putting the same few questions to each. (Hilgard is also remembered for his work on hypnosis, Chapter 5.) This section begins with those questions, then takes the theorists one at a time.

Two questions that divided the theorists

The first question was what is learned. For the stimulus-response theories (S-R theories), what is learned is a connection between a stimulus and a response. Thorndike's bonds, Watson's conditioned reflexes and the theories of Guthrie and Hull below all belong here. They explain learning without any reference to the mind: the organism does not come to know anything; it comes to respond in a new way. Textbooks often call this the stimulus-response behaviouristic approach, and it is the approach that "emphasises learning through stimulus-response associations, mainly via conditioning". For the cognitive theorists, Tolman and the Gestalt psychologists such as Köhler, what is learned is knowledge: an expectancy that one event leads to another, a map of a maze, an insight into how the parts of a problem fit together. The organism then uses that knowledge flexibly. Tolman's place-learning experiment (Section 6.7) was the classic test case. An S-R theorist says the rat learned a turning response; a cognitive theorist says it learned where the food is.

The second question was what makes learning happen. Contiguity theorists answered that a stimulus and a response need only occur together. Pavlov's account of conditioning rested on contiguity, and Watson, who regarded Thorndike's talk of "satisfaction" as unscientific, explained learning by two laws of his own: the law of frequency (the response made most often in a situation is the one learned) and the law of recency (the response made most recently is the one that will occur next time). Reinforcement theorists, from Thorndike through Hull to Skinner, answered that a response must be followed by a consequence. Tolman took a third position: learning happens through exploration, with or without reward (latent learning), but reward decides whether what was learned is performed.

Skinner, characteristically, refused to play the game. In a 1950 paper titled "Are theories of learning necessary?" he argued that explanations in terms of unobserved events at some other level, whether neural, mental or mathematical, were unnecessary and distracting; a science of behaviour should simply describe how behaviour changes with its environment. That is why Skinner's system is often called descriptive behaviourism. The theorists in the rest of this section disagreed with him. They believed that good theory, the more precise the better, was exactly what the study of learning needed.

Exam trap

"Which approach emphasises learning through stimulus-response associations, mainly via conditioning?" The answer is the stimulus-response (S-R) behaviouristic approach. The cognitive approach emphasises knowledge, expectancy and insight; the psychoanalytic approach emphasises unconscious conflict (Freud); the existential approach emphasises freedom, choice and meaning. Within learning theory, keep the two dimensions apart: Guthrie is an S-R contiguity theorist, Hull an S-R reinforcement theorist, and Tolman a cognitive theorist.

Guthrie: we learn what we do

Edwin Ray Guthrie (1886 to 1959) spent his whole career at the University of Washington in Seattle. Trained first in mathematics and philosophy, he wrote clearly, with homely examples from farms, schools and family life, and did few experiments himself. His book The Psychology of Learning (1935, revised 1952) offered the simplest theory of learning ever proposed. It had one law, the law of contiguity: "A combination of stimuli which has accompanied a movement will on its recurrence tend to be followed by that movement." Nothing about satisfaction, reward or drive. If a stimulus pattern and a movement occur together, they become associated, and that is all learning is.

Two corollaries follow. The first is one-trial learning: in Guthrie's words, a stimulus pattern gains its full associative strength on the occasion of its first pairing with a response. Learning does not grow bit by bit; each association is made all at once. The second is the recency principle: whatever an organism did last in a situation is what it will do when the situation recurs, because that was the last movement attached to those stimuli. "We learn only what we do," Guthrie said, and teachers have quoted him ever since. A student who reads a chapter passively learns to read passively; if you want her to be able to explain the ideas, she must practise explaining them.

If learning is complete in one trial, why does practice help? Guthrie's answer rested on a distinction between movements and acts. A movement is a specific muscle contraction; an act is a class of movements defined by what it achieves, such as playing a cover drive or parking a scooter. An act is made up of thousands of movements, and each movement must be attached to its own stimuli, including the movement-produced stimuli (the sensations from the muscles and joints) generated by the movement before it, which link the movements into a chain. An act must also be performed in many different situations: against fast bowling and slow, on a green pitch and a dry one. Practice attaches the act to more and more of these stimulus combinations. The learning curve for the act is gradual even though every single association is formed in one trial.

What, then, does a reward do? For Guthrie, nothing special. A reward simply changes the stimulus situation. When the cat in the puzzle box operates the latch, the door opens and the cat leaves; the last movement it made in the box is therefore preserved, because the cat has no opportunity to do anything else in the presence of those stimuli. If the cat stayed in the box after escaping, it would go on to make other movements, and those would be the ones attached to the box. Forgetting, likewise, is not the fading of an association with time; it occurs only when a new response becomes attached to the old stimuli. Guthrie was one of the first to argue that forgetting is caused by interference rather than decay (Chapter 7).

Key study: Guthrie and Horton's cats in a puzzle box (1946)

Question. If the last movement before escape is what is learned, will each cat repeat its own particular movement? Method. Guthrie and George P. Horton built a puzzle box with a glass front and a slender pole standing in the middle of the floor. Tilting the pole in any direction opened the door, and a camera triggered by the pole photographed each cat at the instant it escaped. Result. Each cat settled into its own stereotyped way of moving the pole, backing into it, pushing it with a paw, or rubbing against it with its head or flank, and repeated that movement with remarkable consistency, escape after escape, while different cats used different movements. Conclusion. Guthrie and Horton took the photographs as strong support for contiguity and recency: what each cat learned was simply the movement it had happened to make at the moment of escape. Criticism. In 1979 Bruce Moore and Susan Stuttard showed that cats in such a box rub the pole with their heads and flanks whenever a person is in view, even when rubbing never opens the door. Head-rubbing is a feline greeting. Much of what the photographs showed was species-typical behaviour released by the watching experimenters, the same lesson the Brelands drew from instinctive drift (Section 6.6).

Guthrie's view of punishment followed directly from his one law. Punishment works, he said, only if it makes the animal do something incompatible with the punished response in the presence of the stimuli that produced it. Slap a dog on the nose as it runs at a passing car, and it recoils, so recoiling becomes attached to the sight of cars; slap it on the rump, and you may simply drive it forward, strengthening the chase. What matters is not the pain but what the pain makes the animal do.

Guthrie's methods of breaking habits

Guthrie's most practical contribution, and the one most often examined, was his advice on breaking habits. If a habit is a response attached to cues, it cannot be removed by wishing; the only way to break it is to attach a different response to the same cues. He described three methods (Table 6.11).

  • In the threshold method, the stimulus is introduced at an intensity so weak that it does not produce the unwanted response, and is then strengthened so gradually that it always stays below the threshold for that response. Guthrie's example was breaking a horse to the saddle: first a light blanket is laid on its back, then a slightly heavier one, then a light saddle, and so on, each so mild that the horse never bucks. By the time the full saddle and rider arrive, standing quietly has been attached to the feel of weight on its back.
  • In the fatigue method, the stimulus is presented again and again, and the unwanted response is allowed or even made to occur until the organism is so tired that it stops and does something else in the presence of the stimulus. The "bronco buster" rides a bucking horse until it is too exhausted to buck. A child who keeps playing with matches can be made to strike match after match until he is heartily sick of it.
  • In the incompatible response method, the stimulus for the unwanted response is presented together with other stimuli that produce a response incompatible with it, so that the incompatible response becomes attached to the old cue. A small child frightened of a toy panda is given the panda while she sits relaxed on her mother's lap; the warmth and calm that her mother produces cannot coexist with fear, and calm becomes attached to the panda.

Guthrie added a fourth piece of advice that is sometimes called sidetracking. Find the cues that start the unwanted habit and practise a different response in their presence. A man who smokes with his morning tea should not try to resist the cigarette at noon, when the urge is weak; he should change what he does with his tea. Guthrie's best-known anecdote makes the same point. A mother complained that her ten-year-old daughter had for two years thrown her hat and coat on the floor every time she came home, despite constant scolding. On a psychologist's advice, the next time it happened the mother made the girl put the coat and hat back on, go outside, come in again and hang them up. A few repetitions cured the habit. Scolding had attached picking up the coat to the mother's voice; the new routine attached it to the cue that really mattered, walking in through the door.

Exam trap

Guthrie's theory is contiguity (stimulus and movement occurring together), with one-trial learning and the recency principle; reinforcement is not needed. Do not confuse Guthrie's one-trial learning (true, he said, of every association) with the one-trial learning of Garcia's taste aversion (a special, biologically prepared case, Section 6.4). His three methods of breaking habits are threshold (gradual increase below the threshold), fatigue (repeat the response to exhaustion) and incompatible response (pair the cue with a competing response). A question that describes introducing a saddle a little at a time wants the threshold method, not the fatigue method. Guthrie's book is The Psychology of Learning (1935); his cat study was with Horton (1946).

Hull: a mathematical theory of habit

If Guthrie built the smallest theory of learning, Clark Leonard Hull (1884 to 1952) built the largest. Before turning to learning, Hull had written books on aptitude testing (1928) and on hypnosis and suggestibility (1933). At Yale University's Institute of Human Relations from 1929, he set out to make psychology as rigorous as Newton's physics, using the hypothetico-deductive method described in Chapter 2: state a set of postulates, deduce precise predictions from them, test the predictions in experiments and revise the postulates when they fail. Principles of Behavior (1943) presented sixteen postulates; Essentials of Behavior (1951) and A Behavior System (1952), published in the year of his death, revised them. In the 1940s and 1950s Hull was one of the most cited psychologists in experimental research, and the motivational side of his theory, drive reduction, is described in Chapter 10. Here we need its learning side.

Hull began from survival. An organism has biological needs, for food, water, air and the avoidance of injury. A need produces a drive (D), an aroused state that energises behaviour. Whatever response reduces the drive is reinforced. This is drive-reduction theory, and for Hull it was the definition of reinforcement: a reinforcer is anything that reduces a drive (or, in later versions, the stimuli that accompany a drive). Primary reinforcement reduces a biological drive directly; secondary reinforcement comes from stimuli that have been associated with drive reduction, the click before the food or the coin before the meal.

Each time a response is followed by reinforcement in the presence of a stimulus, the connection between them is strengthened. Hull called the strength of this learned connection habit strength, written SHR. Habit strength grows with the number of reinforced trials, and it grows in a particular way: each reinforcement adds a fixed fraction of the habit strength still to be gained. The early trials therefore add a great deal and the later ones less and less, which is why Hull's theory predicts the negatively accelerated learning curve you saw in Figure 6.3. Once formed, habit strength is permanent; it can be opposed by inhibition, but never unlearned.

Habit alone does not produce behaviour. A rat that knows the maze perfectly but has just eaten sits in the start box. Hull therefore distinguished the learned connection from the tendency to act on it, which he called reaction potential (SER). In 1943 the relation was a simple product:

SER = SHR × D

The multiplication sign is the heart of the theory. If habit strength is zero (the rat has never been trained), there is no response however hungry the rat is; if drive is zero (the rat is satiated), there is no response however well it is trained. Push numbers through it. A rat with habit strength 0.8 (on a scale from 0 to 1) and moderate hunger (D = 0.5) has a reaction potential of 0.4; double its hunger to D = 1.0 and the reaction potential doubles to 0.8, though it has learned nothing new; feed it to satiation (D = 0) and the reaction potential falls to zero, though it has forgotten nothing.

The experiments of the 1940s forced additions. Crespi's rats (Section 6.6) changed their running speed abruptly when the size of the reward changed, far too quickly for habit strength to have changed, so Hull added incentive motivation (K), the motivating effect of the size and quality of the reward. He added stimulus-intensity dynamism (V), the fact that a stronger stimulus evokes a learned response more readily, and he subtracted two kinds of inhibition. In its final form:

Effective reaction potential = (SHR × D × K × V) − (IR + SIR)

Some versions also include J, a factor for delay of reinforcement (Chapter 10, Table 10.4, lists all the symbols). Two last refinements made the theory probabilistic. The effective reaction potential fluctuates randomly from moment to moment (oscillation, SOR), which is why a well-trained rat sometimes fails to respond; and it must exceed a reaction threshold (SLR) before any response occurs. Hull then measured reaction potential by four observable outcomes: how quickly the response begins (latency), how vigorous it is (amplitude), how likely it is, and how many unreinforced trials it takes to extinguish.

The two inhibition terms deserve a closer look, because they explain three phenomena at once. Every response, Hull proposed, produces reactive inhibition (IR), a fatigue-like negative drive that builds up with repeated responding and dissipates with rest. Resting reduces this fatigue-like drive, and drive reduction is reinforcing, so the response of not responding becomes conditioned to the situation; this learned habit of not responding is conditioned inhibition (SIR). During extinction, with no reinforcement to offset them, the two kinds of inhibition accumulate until they cancel the reaction potential and responding stops. After a rest, reactive inhibition dissipates but conditioned inhibition remains, so the response returns, but only partly: spontaneous recovery. And because reactive inhibition piles up when trials are crowded together and dissipates when they are spaced out, Hull's theory predicts that distributed practice should beat massed practice and that performance should improve after a rest, two findings you will meet in Section 6.10.

Hull's answers to purpose, and Spence's revisions

Tolman's rats seemed to act with purpose, to expect food and to know where it was. Hull set out to explain purpose without leaving S-R theory, and three of his concepts are still examined.

The goal gradient hypothesis (1932) states that the strength of a response increases the nearer it is to the goal. Rats run faster as they approach the goal box, and in a complex maze they eliminate the blind alleys nearest the goal first and those nearest the start last. The effect is not confined to rats. In a 2006 study by Ran Kivetz and colleagues, customers given a coffee-shop loyalty card bought coffee more and more often as they approached the free cup, an everyday goal gradient.

The fractional anticipatory goal response (rG) was Hull's mechanical version of an expectation. When a hungry rat eats in the goal box, its eating (licking, chewing, salivating) is the goal response. Small fragments of that response become classically conditioned to the stimuli of the maze leading up to the goal, so that the rat makes tiny anticipatory licking and chewing movements as it runs. These fractional responses produce their own internal stimuli (sG), which guide the rat towards the goal. What Tolman called an expectancy of food, Hull called an rG-sG mechanism.

The habit-family hierarchy explained flexibility. Any goal can usually be reached by several routes, and each route has its own habit strength, so they form a family ranked from strongest to weakest. If the preferred route is blocked, the organism takes the next one in the hierarchy. Where Tolman saw a rat consulting a cognitive map, Hull saw a family of habits of different strengths.

Hull's most important student, Kenneth Wartinbee Spence (1907 to 1967) of the University of Iowa, refined the system so thoroughly that it is often called Hull-Spence theory. It was Spence who argued for incentive motivation and explained it through the fractional anticipatory goal response: a bigger reward produces a bigger rG, and so more vigour. In his later work Spence concluded that habit strength grows with the number of trials whether or not they are reinforced, and that reinforcement works through incentive. His version was, in effect, a contiguity theory of learning combined with a reinforcement theory of performance. In 1937 Spence also gave a celebrated S-R explanation of transposition, the Gestalt psychologists' favourite evidence for relational learning (Section 6.10).

Clinical link

If drive multiplies habit, then anxious people, whose general level of drive is high, should condition faster on simple tasks. To test this, Janet Taylor Spence (1923 to 2015), who trained in Spence's laboratory at Iowa and later married him, selected items from the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory to build the Taylor Manifest Anxiety Scale (1953). High scorers did acquire conditioned eyeblinks faster than low scorers. On complex tasks with many competing responses, however, high drive strengthened the wrong responses too, and anxious participants did worse. The finding helps explain why a moderately anxious student may race through simple revision but freeze on a hard, unfamiliar problem (the Yerkes-Dodson law, Chapter 10, makes a related point).

How should Hull be judged? His system collapsed under its own weight. Each new finding required a new constant or a new term, and the predictions became so hedged that they could hardly be tested. Drive reduction itself failed as a universal account of reinforcement: rats learned to run for saccharin, which reduces no need, and monkeys worked for the chance to look out of a window (Chapter 10). Latent learning showed that learning can occur without reinforcement at all. Yet Hull's influence was enormous. He showed what a precise, testable theory of behaviour would look like; his intervening variables (habit, drive, incentive) became the common language of motivation research; and his students and colleagues, among them Spence, Neal Miller, John Dollard, Hobart Mowrer, Carl Hovland and Robert Sears, carried his ideas into personality, social psychology, attitude change and therapy. Wolpe's reciprocal inhibition was framed in Hull's terms, and Dollard and Miller's translation of Freud (Section 6.8) used his vocabulary of drive, cue, response and reward.

Exam trap

Drive-reduction theory is Hull's (Principles of Behavior, 1943); the word "drive" was introduced earlier by Woodworth (1918), and homeostasis is Cannon's term. Hull's method was hypothetico-deductive. In SER = SHR × D, E is reaction potential, H habit strength, D drive; K (incentive motivation) was added after Crespi's contrast effects, and V is stimulus-intensity dynamism. Reactive inhibition is Hull's explanation of why spaced practice beats massed practice. The goal gradient and the fractional anticipatory goal response are Hull's; the cognitive map is Tolman's. And the "reciprocal causation model" belongs to Bandura's social cognitive theory, not to drive-reduction theory.

Estes: stimulus sampling theory

You have already met William Kaye Estes twice: in 1941, with Skinner, he invented the conditioned-suppression measure of fear (Section 6.4), and in 1944 he showed that punishment suppresses a response without erasing it (Section 6.5). Estes had taken his doctorate under Skinner at the University of Minnesota, but in 1950, in a paper titled "Toward a statistical theory of learning", he did something his teacher would never have done. He built a mathematical model of learning, and he built it on Guthrie's ideas.

Estes's stimulus sampling theory starts by treating any stimulus situation, such as a Skinner box or a classroom, as a large population of small stimulus elements: a particular sound, a patch of light, a smell, a sensation from the body. On any trial the learner samples, or notices, only a fraction of these elements, a fraction Estes called θ (theta). Following Guthrie, each sampled element becomes connected, completely and at once, to whatever response occurs on that trial (an all-or-none, one-trial process). The probability that the learner will make the response on the next trial is simply the proportion of all the elements in the situation that are now connected to it.

From these assumptions Estes derived a learning curve. If P1 is the probability of the response on the first trial, then on trial n:

Pn = 1 − (1 − P1)(1 − θ)n − 1

Work through it with θ = 0.2 and a response that never occurs at first (P1 = 0). On trial 2 the probability is 1 − 0.8 = 0.20; on trial 3, 1 − 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.36; on trial 4, 1 − 0.512 = 0.49; on trial 5, about 0.59. Each trial conditions a fifth of the elements not yet conditioned, so the gains shrink as fewer unconditioned elements remain. The result is the familiar negatively accelerated curve, yet every individual element was conditioned in a single step. Notice the family resemblance: Hull's habit strength, Estes's θ and the αβ of the Rescorla-Wagner model (Section 6.4) all describe learning as the capture of a constant fraction of what is still to be learned.

The theory explained other phenomena with the same economy. Generalisation occurs because two similar situations share many elements, so a response conditioned to one is partly conditioned to the other. Spontaneous recovery, Estes argued in 1955, occurs because the elements available to the learner fluctuate from session to session: elements that were conditioned during acquisition but happened not to be sampled during extinction drift back into the available set after a rest, carrying the response with them. And the theory predicted a striking pattern called probability matching. Ask people to guess, trial after trial, whether a light will come on, when in fact it comes on at random on 70 per cent of trials, and most of them end up predicting "on" on about 70 per cent of trials, rather than on every trial, which would maximise their score. The first hints of this pattern had appeared in a 1939 experiment by Lloyd Humphreys, and Estes's model predicted it.

Stimulus sampling theory, together with the linear models of Robert R. Bush and Frederick Mosteller, launched mathematical learning theory. Estes himself later moved into cognitive psychology, building models of memory and categorisation, and he is one of the few learning theorists whose work bridges the behaviourist and cognitive eras. He made one further point that every reader of learning curves should remember. In 1956 he showed that a curve averaged over many learners can look smooth and gradual even when each individual learner improves in sudden jumps (Section 6.10).

Exam trap

Stimulus sampling theory is Estes's (1950), a statistical (mathematical) learning theory that formalised Guthrie's contiguity and one-trial ideas; it is not Skinner's. Its key quantity, θ, is the proportion of stimulus elements sampled on a trial. Estes is also examined for conditioned suppression (with Skinner, 1941) and for showing that punishment suppresses rather than eliminates responding (1944).

Mowrer: fear, hope and the neurotic paradox

Orval Hobart Mowrer, whose two-factor theory of avoidance you met in Section 6.5, was another product of Hull's Yale institute, and his career moved through almost every question in this chapter. His first lasting contribution was practical. In 1938, with his wife Willie Mae Mowrer, he described the bell-and-pad method for treating nocturnal enuresis (bed-wetting). The child sleeps on a pad that completes an electrical circuit at the first drop of urine and rings a bell. In classical-conditioning terms the bell is a UCS that produces waking and contraction of the bladder sphincter (the UCR); the sensations of a full bladder, which immediately precede the bell, are the CS. After a number of pairings the full bladder alone wakes the child or inhibits urination. (Some writers describe the later stages as avoidance learning, since the child learns to prevent the bell.) The enuresis alarm descended from it is still one of the most effective treatments available.

In 1947 Mowrer proposed that there are two fundamentally different kinds of learning. Sign learning is classical conditioning: signs (stimuli) become attached to emotional responses, especially fear, which are involuntary and governed by the autonomic nervous system. Solution learning is instrumental learning: skeletal, voluntary responses that solve problems are strengthened because they reduce a drive. Two-factor theory is the combination of the two, fear learned by sign learning and avoidance learned by solution learning.

A year later Mowrer used the theory to attack what he called the neurotic paradox: why do people go on behaving in ways that are obviously self-defeating, avoiding what they fear at the cost of jobs, friendships and happiness? A simple law of effect would predict that behaviour with bad consequences should die out. Mowrer's answer was timing. The avoidance response brings immediate relief from anxiety, while its costs are delayed and diffuse, and immediate consequences control behaviour far more strongly than delayed ones. Avoidance also prevents extinction, because the person never stays in the feared situation long enough to discover that nothing terrible happens. The man who leaves every party after ten minutes feels better each time he leaves, and never learns that he could have survived the evening.

Two-factor theory was soon challenged. Solomon's dogs (Section 6.5) went on jumping long after they showed any outward signs of fear, which raised the question of what was being reduced. In 1966 Richard Herrnstein and Philip Hineline showed that rats would learn to press a lever that merely lowered the overall frequency of shocks arriving at unpredictable moments, with no warning signal to be afraid of; the avoidance response seemed to be reinforced directly by the reduction in shock. And in 1970 Robert Bolles pointed out that rats learn some avoidance responses in a trial or two (running away, jumping out of a box) but need hundreds of trials to learn others (pressing a lever). Avoidance is learned quickly, Bolles argued, only when the required response is one of the animal's species-specific defence reactions: fleeing, freezing or fighting. Biology constrains avoidance learning just as it constrains taste aversion and operant training.

Mowrer, meanwhile, had changed his mind. In Learning Theory and Behavior (1960) he concluded that all learning is sign learning. What organisms learn, he now argued, is to attach emotions to stimuli, including the stimuli produced by their own movements. Four emotions do all the work, depending on whether a stimulus signals the beginning or the end of something painful or something pleasant (Table 6.12). When the feedback from a response has become attached to hope, the organism continues the response; when it has become attached to fear, the organism stops. Even reward and punishment, on this view, work through classically conditioned emotions.

Mowrer applied learning theory to language too. He suggested that a talking bird, or a babbling infant, first reproduces the sounds of its caregiver's voice because those sounds have been paired with food and comfort and so have become secondary reinforcers; producing them is then self-rewarding. He called this the "autism theory" of speech, using "autism" in its old sense of self (it has nothing to do with autism spectrum disorder). Later still he broke sharply with Freud, arguing that neurotic suffering comes from real guilt over real wrongdoing rather than from repressed impulses, and he founded the confession-based integrity groups of the 1960s (Clinical Psychology book).

Exam trap

"Two-factor theory" in a learning question is Mowrer's theory of avoidance (1947): classical conditioning of fear plus operant (negative) reinforcement of the avoidance response by fear reduction. It is not Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory of emotion (Chapter 11) and not Herzberg's two-factor theory of work motivation. Sign learning versus solution learning is Mowrer's distinction; but "sign learning theory" is also a name for Tolman's expectancy theory (Section 6.7), in which "signs" are cues to what leads to what. Read the options. The bell-and-pad for enuresis is Mowrer and Mowrer (1938), a classical-conditioning treatment. The neurotic paradox (1948) is Mowrer's question of why self-defeating avoidance persists.

Gagné: the conditions of learning

The theorists so far wanted one theory for all learning. Robert Mills Gagné (1916 to 2002), an American educational psychologist who had spent much of the 1940s and 1950s in military research on training pilots and technicians, drew the opposite conclusion. In The Conditions of Learning (1965) he argued that there is not one kind of learning but several, and that each requires its own conditions: internal conditions (what the learner must already know or be able to do) and external conditions (what the teacher or the situation must provide). His central claim was that the kinds of learning form a hierarchy. Each type builds on the ones below it, so that a higher type cannot be learned until its prerequisites have been mastered, a view he called cumulative learning. Do not mix the eight types with his five learning outcomes (verbal information, intellectual skills, cognitive strategies, motor skills, attitudes) or his nine events of instruction. The book is The Conditions of Learning (1965).

6.10 Learning in PracticeLower yield · 3 Qs

The theories in this chapter were built in laboratories, with rats, pigeons, dogs and cats. But the people who most need to know about learning are not in laboratories. A student with four weeks left before an entrance examination, a nursing trainee learning to give injections, a child learning to read, a batsman remodelling his technique: all of them face the same practical questions. How quickly will I improve, and why have I stopped improving? How much should I practise, and in one long session or many short ones? Should I learn the whole thing at once or in parts? How much does feedback matter? Will what I learn here help me there? Can a machine teach me? Psychologists have been studying these questions since the 1890s, and the answers are among the most useful in this book.

Learning curves

A learning curve is a graph of performance plotted against the amount of practice, measured in trials, sessions or time. Ebbinghaus's curves of 1885 (Chapter 7) and Thorndike's escape-time curves for his cats (Section 6.5) were among the first. Performance can be measured in many ways: correct responses, words typed per minute, runs scored, or, the other way up, errors made and seconds taken. When errors or time are plotted, a curve of improvement falls instead of rising, as in Tolman's maze data (Figure 6.5). What matters is the shape of the improvement, not which way the line slopes. Three shapes are distinguished (Figure 6.7, left).

A negatively accelerated curve (a decelerating curve) rises steeply at first and then more and more slowly, levelling off as performance approaches its limit. Each unit of practice adds less than the one before, a law of diminishing returns. This is the most common shape, especially for simple tasks and for learners who already have the relevant background. Someone learning to type sees huge gains in the first week and tiny ones in the tenth. You have already met three explanations of the shape: in Hull's theory, Estes's theory and the Rescorla-Wagner model, each trial captures a constant fraction of what remains to be learned, and as less remains, each gain shrinks. There is also a simpler reason: learners tend to master the easy parts of a task first, leaving the hard parts for later.

A positively accelerated curve (an accelerating curve) does the opposite: progress is slow at first and then speeds up. It appears when a task is difficult or unfamiliar and the learner must first acquire basic components before the real gains can come. An adult beginning a new language spends weeks on sounds and vocabulary with little to show for it; once enough words and grammar are in place, comprehension suddenly takes off. Positively accelerated curves are common in the early stages of complex learning and in learners with little prior knowledge.

An S-shaped curve (a sigmoid curve) combines the two: a slow start while the learner gets oriented, a rapid middle phase, and a slowing as performance nears its limit. It is the typical curve when a complex skill, such as driving a car or playing the tabla, is learned from scratch and followed to mastery. Straight-line (linear) curves, in which every trial adds the same amount, are rare.

One caution, which comes from William Estes (Section 6.9). In 1956 he showed that a curve averaged over many learners can look smooth and gradual even when each individual learner improves in sudden jumps. If ten students each have their "aha" at a different session, the average of their scores rises gently, though none of them improved gently. A smooth curve in a textbook is therefore not proof that each learner improved bit by bit; Köhler's chimpanzees, plotted one at a time, would have produced steps.

Plateaus and the limits of improvement

In the 1890s, at Indiana University, William Lowe Bryan (1860 to 1955) and Noble Harter studied people learning the telegraphic Morse code, measuring each week how many letters per minute they could send and receive. Their reports (1897 and 1899) contain the most famous learning curves in the history of psychology. Sending improved smoothly. Receiving improved rapidly for some weeks, then stopped improving for a long stretch, sometimes for several weeks, and then began to rise again, eventually to the level of an expert operator (Figure 6.7, right).

Such a flat stretch in the middle of a learning curve, preceded and followed by improvement, is called a plateau. Bryan and Harter explained it by a hierarchy of habits. A beginner receives code letter by letter. Only when letter habits have become automatic can the learner's attention move on to grasping whole words as units, and later whole phrases. The plateau is the period during which the lower-order habits are being perfected but are not yet automatic enough to free attention for the higher-order ones. When word habits take over, improvement resumes.

Later writers listed several other causes of plateaus, which suggest their remedies:

  • A shift from a lower-order to a higher-order method of working, as in the telegraphers, during which neither method is yet efficient.
  • Loss of motivation, boredom or fatigue after the excitement of early rapid progress has worn off.
  • Faulty methods or bad habits that limit performance until they are corrected, such as typing with two fingers.
  • A particularly difficult stage of the task, or a period in which recently learned material is being consolidated.

Plateaus can therefore often be shortened: by rest, by changing the method of practice, by setting new goals, by giving precise feedback, and by encouraging the learner, who may be on the verge of a breakthrough without knowing it. They should not be overstated, though. When later researchers looked again at telegraphy and other skills, many found no plateau at all, and in 1958 Fred Keller titled his review of the evidence "The phantom plateau". A plateau is common but not inevitable.

A plateau must also be distinguished from the physiological limit, the final ceiling of performance set by the limits of the body and nervous system, beyond which no amount of practice brings improvement. A plateau is temporary and in the middle of the curve; the physiological limit is permanent and at the end. In practice, most people stop practising long before they reach their physiological limit; what they reach is the level that satisfies them.

Exam trap

Negatively accelerated means rapid early gains and slowing later (the commonest curve); positively accelerated means slow early gains and faster later; S-shaped combines them. A plateau is a temporary flat period of no apparent progress in the middle of learning (Bryan and Harter, telegraphy, 1897 and 1899), explained by a hierarchy of habits; it is not the physiological limit (the final ceiling), not extinction (the weakening of a response when reinforcement stops) and not forgetting. Do not confuse a learning plateau with the plateau phase of Masters and Johnson's sexual response cycle (Physiological Psychology book).

From clumsy to automatic: the stages of skill learning

Bryan and Harter's telegraphers were acquiring a skill, and the course of skill learning in general was described by Paul Fitts (1912 to 1965) and Michael Posner (born 1936) in Human Performance (1967). They proposed three stages. In the cognitive stage the learner works out what to do, often talking himself through it ("clutch in, first gear, release slowly"); performance is slow, effortful, inconsistent and full of errors, and it needs all of his attention. In the associative stage the components are linked into smooth sequences, errors fall, and the learner begins to notice and correct his own mistakes. In the autonomous stage the skill runs almost automatically: it is fast, accurate and consistent, needs little attention, and leaves the mind free for other things, so that an experienced rider can weave a scooter through Mysuru traffic while carrying on a conversation. The cost of automaticity is that the expert often cannot say how she does what she does, and finds bad habits hard to change.

The stages map onto brain systems described in the Physiological Psychology book. Early, effortful learning depends heavily on the prefrontal cortex. As a skill becomes automatic, control shifts to systems for procedural learning: the cerebellum, which supports error correction and the fine coordination of movement, and the basal ganglia, which support the selection of actions and the formation of habits (procedural memory is in Chapter 7).

Overlearning

When have you learned something? A natural answer is: when you can do it once without error. Overlearning is practice that continues beyond that point. The classic study was published in 1929 by William C. F. Krueger. Participants learned lists of single-syllable nouns until they could recite a list once without error. Some stopped there; others continued for half as many trials again as they had needed (50 per cent overlearning), or for as many trials again (100 per cent overlearning). Retention was tested after intervals of up to 28 days. Fifty per cent overlearning produced a clear improvement in retention at every interval. Doubling the extra practice to 100 per cent produced a further, smaller gain, so that the benefit per extra trial fell, another example of diminishing returns.

Push a case through it. If you need 12 readings to recite a 20-line poem for the first time, 50 per cent overlearning means 6 more readings, and 100 per cent overlearning means 12 more. The first 6 extra readings are well worth the time; the next 6 are worth less. Overlearned material resists forgetting, resists interference from later learning, and, importantly for anyone who has to perform under pressure, resists disruption by stress and anxiety. This is why airline pilots, surgeons and emergency teams drill procedures they already know, and why a dancer rehearses an item long after she can perform it correctly. (Ebbinghaus's savings method, which first revealed the benefits of extra repetitions, is in Chapter 7.)

Massed versus distributed practice

Suppose you have twenty hours to give to a subject. You could spend them in one long weekend, or as ten two-hour sessions spread over a fortnight. In massed practice, practice sessions are crowded together with little or no rest between them; in distributed practice (spaced practice), the same amount of practice is spread out over time with rest intervals. For both verbal learning and motor skills, distributed practice usually produces better learning and, above all, better long-term retention. The advantage is called the spacing effect, and it is one of the most reliable findings in all of psychology; a 2006 review by Nicholas J. Cepeda and colleagues analysed several hundred experiments on it.

Ebbinghaus was the first to show it, in 1885. Twelve years later Adolf Jost stated what is still called Jost's law (1897): if two associations are equally strong but of different ages, a further repetition strengthens the older one more, and the older one is forgotten more slowly. Spaced repetitions, which give each association time to age between repetitions, therefore gain more from each repetition than massed ones. A striking applied study came from Alan Baddeley and D. J. A. Longman in 1978. British postal workers learning to type postcodes on a new keyboard were trained in one or two daily sessions lasting one or two hours each. Those given a single one-hour session a day learned the keyboard in fewer total hours of practice than those on the more massed schedules, though it took them more days; and, interestingly, the workers preferred the massed schedules, which felt more productive.

Why does spacing help? Hull's answer was reactive inhibition (Section 6.9): a fatigue-like inhibition builds up when responses are crowded together and dissipates during rest. Hull's theory also explains reminiscence, the improvement in performance after a rest with no further practice, which Philip Boswood Ballard reported in 1913 when schoolchildren recalled more of a poem two or three days after learning it than immediately afterwards. In motor skills, reminiscence is often seen when a learner returns to massed practice after a break and performs better than before the break. Other explanations stress memory: spaced sessions allow consolidation, they encode the material in more varied contexts, and they force effortful retrieval at each new session, a "desirable difficulty" in Robert A. Bjork's phrase, which strengthens memory more than easy repetition does.

Massed practice is not always worse. It is efficient for short, simple tasks; for tasks that require a long warm-up before useful work can begin; and for problems whose solution depends on keeping the whole problem in mind at once, which a break would interrupt. Cramming the night before can also raise performance the next morning. What massed practice does badly is long-term retention: the crammed material is largely gone a month later.

Exam trap

Overlearning is practice beyond the first errorless performance (Krueger, 1929: 50 per cent overlearning clearly helps; 100 per cent adds less), not learning so much that performance suffers. Distributed (spaced) practice generally beats massed practice, especially for long-term retention; Hull's explanation is reactive inhibition. Reminiscence (Ballard, 1913) is improvement after rest without practice; it is not spontaneous recovery, which is the return of an extinguished conditioned response. Jost's law (1897) says a repetition benefits an older association more than a newer one of equal strength.

Whole versus part learning

You have to learn a 40-line poem for a recitation, or a long Carnatic composition, or a new bowling action. Should you work through the whole thing each time, or break it into parts? In the whole method, the material is practised as a single unit from beginning to end on every trial. In the part method, it is divided into sections, each section is learned separately, and the sections are then joined. A compromise, the progressive part method, learns the first part, then the first and second together, then the first three, and so on, so that the joins are practised as the learning proceeds.

Studies from the early 1900s onwards found that neither method always wins; the answer depends on the material and the learner.

  • The whole method tends to be better when the material is meaningful, well organised and of moderate length, so that its unity helps (a poem with a clear story); when the learner is older, more able and well motivated; and when practice is distributed. It avoids the main weakness of the part method, which is that the parts must later be linked, and the learner tends to stumble at the junctions.
  • The part method tends to be better when the material is very long, difficult or made of loosely connected units (a list of dates, the bones of the hand); and for younger or less able learners. It also gives an early sense of success, which sustains motivation, whereas with the whole method progress may be invisible for a long time.

The same principle applies to motor skills. A highly integrated movement, in which each component depends on the others, such as a bowling action, a golf swing or a swimming stroke, is usually best practised whole, perhaps slowed down, because practising its pieces separately destroys their timing. A skill made of fairly independent components, such as a dance with distinct sections or the separate checks of a pre-flight routine, can be practised in parts. Many teachers use a whole-part-whole sequence: show and attempt the whole, practise the difficult parts, then put the whole together again.

Exam trap

There is no single "best" method. Whole learning favours short or moderate, meaningful, unified material and mature, able learners; part learning favours long, difficult, unconnected material and younger learners; the progressive part method adds each new part to those already learned. Do not confuse whole and part learning (how material is divided for practice) with the whole-report and partial-report methods of Sperling's sensory-memory experiments (Chapter 7).

Feedback and goals

You saw in Section 6.5 that Thorndike's blindfolded participants, drawing line after line and trying to make each one three inches long, did not improve at all when they were never told how close they had come. Practice alone, without information about its results, taught them nothing. Knowledge of results (KR) is information given to the learner about the outcome of his or her performance; in everyday language it is feedback. In 1932 Margery Trowbridge and Hulsey Cason repeated the line-drawing task with groups that received different kinds of feedback after each line: none, an irrelevant nonsense syllable, a simple "right" or "wrong", or the exact size and direction of the error. The group given no feedback did not improve; the "right or wrong" group improved; the group told exactly how far off they were improved most.

Research since then has added several practical rules. Feedback works best when it is precise rather than vague ("two centimetres too long" beats "wrong"), and when it arrives soon after the performance, while the learner can still relate it to what he did. Feedback after every single trial helps performance during practice, but learners who receive it too often can come to depend on it and perform worse when it is withdrawn, so skilled coaches give frequent feedback early and gradually reduce it. Teachers of motor skills distinguish knowledge of results, which concerns the outcome (the ball went for four), from knowledge of performance, which concerns the movement itself (your front elbow dropped). Feedback can also come from the task itself (intrinsic feedback, the feel of a clean strike) or from an outside source (extrinsic feedback, the coach's comment). Feedback works in two ways at once: it is information, telling the learner what to change, and it is a reinforcer and motivator, telling him that his efforts are paying off. Biofeedback (Section 6.6) and programmed instruction (below) are both built on it.

Feedback has a natural partner. In 1968 the American psychologist Edwin Locke (born 1938) proposed, and in later work with Gary Latham demonstrated in hundreds of studies, that people who set specific, difficult but attainable goals perform better than people who are told to "do your best" or who set no goal at all. A specific goal directs attention to what matters, mobilises effort, increases persistence and prompts the learner to search for better strategies. Goals and feedback need each other: a goal without feedback leaves the learner unable to tell whether he is on course, and feedback without a goal gives him nothing to compare it with. So practice is most effective when the learner has a specific goal and receives knowledge of results about progress towards it. (Goal-setting theory as a theory of motivation is in Chapter 10; self-efficacy, which decides whether a person will commit to a hard goal, is in Section 6.8.)

Exam trap

"Learning always leads to efficacy, provided ..." The keyed answer is that the learner has a specific goal (Locke and Latham's goal setting). Ability, aptitude and knowledge help, but on their own they do not make practice purposeful. Knowledge of results is feedback about the outcome; without it, as in Thorndike's line drawing, practice brings no improvement. In a question about what social cognitive theory stresses in motivation, the answer is self-efficacy; "goal difficulty" belongs to Locke's goal-setting theory, and "external rewards only" and "biological drives" to behaviourist and drive theories.

Clinical link

The principles of this section are the backbone of teaching children with learning difficulties. Effective programmes present material through several senses at once, a multisensory approach in which the child sees, hears, says and traces a word, as in the method Grace Fernald described in 1943 (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic and tactile, VAKT) and the Orton-Gillingham method; they break skills into small steps, give immediate feedback and reinforcement, and build in regular, spaced repetition and overlearning. Relying solely on written instructions, refusing accommodations, or drilling rote memorisation alone are the opposite of good practice. (Specific learning disorder itself is covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.)

Transfer of training

Nobody learns anything for use in only one situation. You study psychology at a desk in order to use it in a hospital ward; a pilot trains in a simulator in order to fly an aircraft. Transfer of training is the effect that learning one task has on the learning or performance of another. It can take three forms. In positive transfer, earlier learning makes later learning easier: someone who rides a bicycle learns to ride a motorcycle faster, and a student who reads Hindi in the Devanagari script finds written Marathi easy. In negative transfer, earlier learning makes later learning harder, usually because the same stimuli now require different responses: a driver from India, where traffic keeps left, keeps reaching for the wrong side of the road in a country where traffic keeps right, and a driver who switches from a Japanese car to a European one switches on the wipers every time he means to signal a turn. In zero transfer, the first task has no effect on the second: learning to swim neither helps nor hinders learning Sanskrit grammar.

Transfer is measured with a simple design. An experimental group learns Task A and then Task B; a control group learns only Task B (often after resting or doing an unrelated task for the same time). If the experimental group learns Task B faster than the control group, transfer was positive; if slower, negative; if the same, zero. Several special kinds of transfer have names. In bilateral transfer, a skill practised with one side of the body improves on the other: people who practise mirror drawing with the right hand also draw better with the left. Gagné distinguished lateral transfer, the application of learning to a new situation at the same level of complexity (calculating the area of the classroom floor after learning to calculate areas of rectangles in a textbook), from vertical transfer, in which lower-level learning makes higher-level learning possible (concepts making principles possible, as in his hierarchy). And Harlow's learning sets (Section 6.7) show non-specific transfer: learning how to learn a kind of problem carries over even when no specific content is shared.

Why does transfer occur, and when? For two thousand years educators had a confident answer. According to the doctrine of formal discipline, based on the old faculty psychology, the mind consists of general faculties, such as memory, reasoning, attention and will, that are strengthened by exercise as muscles are. On this view it hardly matters what pupils study, so long as it is hard: Latin, Greek and geometry were taught not for their content but because they were supposed to train the mind for everything else. Three pieces of evidence demolished the doctrine.

  • William James (1842 to 1910) tested it on himself and reported the result in The Principles of Psychology (1890). He learned 158 lines of Victor Hugo's poem Satyr, which took him 131 and five-sixths minutes. He then spent twenty-odd minutes a day for 38 days learning the first book of Milton's Paradise Lost, to train his "memory faculty". Finally he learned another 158 lines of Satyr. It took him 151 and a half minutes: if anything, he was slower. (James had a few friends repeat the experiment, with mixed results, but the point was made.)
  • In 1901 Thorndike and Robert Sessions Woodworth trained people to estimate the areas of rectangles of certain sizes. Accuracy improved for the trained shapes and sizes, but transferred very little to estimating the areas of other shapes, or to related judgements of length and weight.
  • In 1924 Thorndike compared the gains in reasoning test scores of more than 8,000 American high-school pupils over a school year according to the subjects they had studied. The subjects studied made very little difference: the gains that could be credited to Latin or geometry were tiny, and no larger than those from several "practical" subjects. The pupils who took the classical subjects did score higher, but mainly because they had been abler to begin with.

In place of formal discipline, Thorndike proposed his identical elements theory: transfer occurs only to the extent that the two tasks share identical elements, whether facts, procedures, stimulus-response connections, attitudes or methods of work. Learning to drive a car transfers to driving a van because nearly all the elements are the same; studying Latin helps with learning Latin-derived English vocabulary but not with reasoning in general. The theory had a large practical effect: if transfer depends on shared content, schools should teach directly what pupils will need, and American curricula became more practical as a result.

A second view emphasised understanding. In 1908 Charles Hubbard Judd (1873 to 1946) had two groups of boys throw darts at a target placed under about 12 inches of water. One group had first been taught the principle of refraction, how water bends light so that an object appears displaced; the other had not. Both groups did equally well at first, since practice was what counted. But when the target was moved to a depth of only 4 inches, the boys who understood refraction adjusted quickly, while the others were confused and made many errors. Judd's generalisation theory of transfer holds that what transfers is a general principle or rule that the learner has understood and can apply to new situations. Good teaching, on this view, emphasises principles rather than isolated facts.

The Gestalt psychologists offered a third view through the phenomenon of transposition: the transfer of a learned relationship to new stimuli. In 1918 Köhler trained chickens to peck grain from the darker of two grey papers and not from the lighter. He then tested them with the darker grey and a new, still darker one. Most of the chickens pecked at the new paper, the one they had never been rewarded on, because it was now "the darker one". They had learned a relation, not a response to a particular grey. Spence's 1937 S-R reply showed that overlapping generalisation gradients of excitation and inhibition around the two training stimuli could produce the same choice, and the argument between relational and S-R accounts of transposition ran for decades.

Finally, Charles Egerton Osgood (1916 to 1991) drew the findings together in 1949. Whether transfer is positive or negative, he showed, depends on the similarity of both the stimuli and the responses in the two tasks. When the stimuli are similar and the responses are the same, transfer is positive, and the more similar the stimuli the more positive it is. When the stimuli are similar but the responses must be different, especially opposite, transfer is negative, and the more similar the stimuli the worse the interference. The driver crossing from left-hand to right-hand traffic faces nearly identical stimuli (roads, junctions, cars) that demand opposite responses, the worst combination of all. Programmed instruction and teaching machines

If learning depends on active responding, immediate feedback and practice at the learner's own pace, then an ordinary classroom, with forty pupils listening to one teacher and homework returned a week later, breaks almost every rule. Two psychologists tried to fix this with machines.

The first was Sidney Leavitt Pressey (1888 to 1979) of Ohio State University. In the mid-1920s he built a small device, described in 1926, that presented a multiple-choice question in a window. The student pressed one of four keys. In its teaching mode the machine would not move on to the next question until the right key had been pressed, so that every error was corrected at once, and it kept count of errors; it could even be fitted with an attachment that dropped a sweet when the student reached a set score. Pressey predicted an "industrial revolution" in education. It did not come, and his teaching machine, a device that presents material, requires a response and gives immediate knowledge of results, was largely forgotten.

The second was Skinner. On 11 November 1953 he visited his younger daughter's fourth-grade arithmetic class on a parents' day and was appalled: every child was being taught at the same pace, whatever his understanding, and no child learned whether an answer was right until long afterwards. From an operant point of view, the reinforcement was too rare and too delayed to shape anything. Within days he had built a prototype machine, and in "The science of learning and the art of teaching" (1954) and "Teaching machines" (1958) he set out the method that came to be called programmed instruction. Its principles are those of shaping, applied to knowledge.

  • Small steps. The material is broken into a long sequence of small units called frames, each a little harder than the last.
  • Active responding. The learner must respond to every frame, usually by writing a word or completing a sentence.
  • Immediate knowledge of results. The correct answer is revealed at once, so that each right answer is reinforced and each wrong one corrected before it can be practised.
  • Self-pacing. Each learner moves through the programme at his own speed.
  • A low error rate. The steps are made so small, with prompts that are gradually faded, that the learner almost always succeeds; Skinner believed that errors are themselves practised and should be avoided.

Skinner's design is the linear programme: every learner works through the same fixed sequence of frames, writing his own (constructed) answers. Around 1960 Norman Crowder developed the alternative branching programme (also called intrinsic programming). Each frame presents a larger amount of material followed by a multiple-choice question, and the answer the learner chooses decides where he goes next: a correct answer leads on to new material, while each wrong answer leads to a remedial frame that explains that particular mistake before returning him to the main line. Crowder treated errors as useful diagnostic information rather than something to be avoided; his programmes were often printed as "scrambled textbooks" in which the learner's answer sent him to a different page.

Programmed instruction was a sensation in the early 1960s and then faded, partly because many programmes were poorly written and boring, with steps too small for able learners. But its principles survived and spread. Computer-assisted instruction, beginning with the PLATO system at the University of Illinois in 1960, put programmes on screens. Fred Simmons Keller (1899 to 1996), Skinner's friend since their graduate-student days, developed the Personalized System of Instruction in the 1960s, described in his 1968 paper "Good-bye, teacher...", in which university students work through a course in small written units at their own pace and must pass a test on each unit before moving on. The same logic underlies mastery learning and the adaptive learning applications on today's phones, which give small steps, instant feedback and spaced review. Programmed instruction works best for factual material and well-defined skills; it is less suited to teaching open-ended reasoning, discussion and creativity.

Exam trap

The first teaching machine was Pressey's (1920s); Skinner popularised teaching machines and programmed instruction (1954, 1958), based on operant conditioning (shaping, active responding and immediate reinforcement), not on classical conditioning, insight or observational learning. Linear programmes (fixed sequence, constructed answers, very few errors) are Skinner's; branching (intrinsic) programmes (multiple choice, remedial frames for wrong answers) are Crowder's. The Personalized System of Instruction is Keller's (1968).

The case of Little Albert, revisited

The chapter opened with three questions about Albert, and you can now answer all of them. How could a placid baby be taught to fear a white rat in a week? Because seven pairings of a neutral stimulus (the rat) with an intense unconditioned stimulus (the clang of the steel bar) were enough to make the rat a conditioned stimulus for fear, especially since the rat appeared just before the noise and was still in view when it came, the most effective delay arrangement. Seligman's idea of preparedness adds a further reason: a furry, moving animal is just the kind of stimulus human beings are prepared to fear, which may be why Elsie Bregman failed when she tried to condition infants to fear wooden blocks. Why did the fear spread to the rabbit, the dog, the sealskin coat, the cotton wool and the Santa Claus mask, but not to the blocks? Because of stimulus generalisation: the more a stimulus resembled the furry white rat, the more fear it produced, while the blocks shared nothing with it. And could the process be run backwards? Mary Cover Jones showed four years later, with Peter and the rabbit, that it could, by counterconditioning, and Wolpe turned her method into systematic desensitisation. A modern clinician would add two warnings. Had Albert been allowed to crawl away and feel relief, his avoidance would have been negatively reinforced, which, on Mowrer's two-factor theory, would have protected his fear from extinction. And even a fear that has been successfully extinguished can return through spontaneous recovery, reinstatement or renewal.

One question Watson and Rayner never answered is who Albert was. In 2009 Hall P. Beck and his colleagues, after years of detective work in birth records and census returns, identified him as Douglas Merritte, the son of a wet nurse at the Harriet Lane Home, who died of hydrocephalus in 1925 at the age of six. In 2012 Alan J. Fridlund and colleagues went further and argued, from the film and from Douglas's medical records, that he had been neurologically impaired from birth, which would mean that Watson's "healthy" and "stolid" baby was nothing of the kind. Then, in 2014, Russell A. Powell, Nancy Digdon and their colleagues pointed to another candidate, William Albert Barger, the son of another wet nurse at the same home, whose age, weight and name fitted Watson's records better. Barger, known in later life as Albert Martin, lived until 2007, apparently without ever knowing that he might have been the most famous baby in psychology; a relative recalled that he had always disliked dogs, though nobody can say whether that had anything to do with a white rat and a steel bar. The debate is not settled. What is settled is the lesson of the experiment itself: fears can be learned, they spread to similar things, and they can be unlearned, which is the foundation on which the behaviour therapies described in this chapter were built. No ethics committee today would allow the experiment, and its story is one of the reasons such committees exist.

Chapter summary

6.1 What learning is, and what it is not. Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour, or in the potential for behaviour, that results from experience (Kimble). It differs from performance, which is what an organism actually does, and from changes caused by maturation, fatigue, drugs, injury or motivation. Fixed action patterns, triggered by sign stimuli (Tinbergen), are innate. The simplest, non-associative learning involves a single stimulus: habituation is a decrease in response to a repeated harmless stimulus, shown to be learning by its stimulus specificity, spontaneous recovery and dishabituation; sensitisation is an increase in responding after a strong stimulus. Kandel traced both to synaptic changes in Aplysia, and Fantz used habituation to study infant perception. Imprinting (Spalding, Lorenz) is rapid, largely irreversible, unrewarded learning confined to a critical period, peaking at 13 to 16 hours in Hess's ducklings.

6.2 Pavlov and the language of classical conditioning. Pavlov, a Nobel Prize-winning physiologist of digestion, noticed "psychic secretions" and from about 1897 studied them as conditioned reflexes; Twitmyer had seen the same thing in the knee jerk. In classical conditioning a neutral stimulus, paired repeatedly with an unconditioned stimulus (UCS) that produces an unconditioned response (UCR), becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that produces a conditioned response (CR). The CR resembles the UCR but is not identical. Conditioning is strongest in delay (forward) conditioning, where the CS begins first and overlaps the UCS, weaker in trace conditioning, weak in simultaneous conditioning and weakest, often inhibitory, in backward conditioning.

6.3 The phenomena of classical conditioning. Acquisition produces a negatively accelerated rise in the CR; extinction, the omission of the UCS, weakens it. Spontaneous recovery, disinhibition, rapid reacquisition, reinstatement (Rescorla and Heth) and renewal (Bouton) show that extinction suppresses rather than erases learning, which is why fears relapse. Stimulus generalisation produces a generalisation gradient (Hovland); stimulus discrimination is learned by differential conditioning, and an impossible discrimination produced experimental neurosis in Shenger-Krestovnikova's dog. In higher-order conditioning an established CS acts as the UCS for a new stimulus; in sensory preconditioning (Brogden) two neutral stimuli are paired before one of them is conditioned. A conditioned inhibitor signals that the UCS will not occur.

6.4 Fear, prediction and preparedness. Watson and Rayner's Little Albert (1920) showed that fear can be classically conditioned and generalised; Mary Cover Jones's Peter (1924) showed that it can be removed by counterconditioning, the principle that Wolpe developed into reciprocal inhibition and systematic desensitisation (relaxation from Jacobson, a hierarchy rated in SUDS, then graded exposure). Flooding, implosion (Stampfl), aversion therapy, covert sensitisation (Cautela) and exposure and response prevention (Meyer) also rest on conditioning. Rescorla's contingency, Kamin's blocking, overshadowing and Lubow and Moore's latent inhibition showed that a CS must predict the UCS; the Rescorla-Wagner model (1972) formalised learning as driven by prediction error, which dopamine neurons signal (Schultz). The CS acts as a signal rather than a substitute (Siegel's compensatory responses). Garcia and Koelling's taste aversion (1966), learned in one trial and across long delays, refuted equipotentiality and led to Seligman's preparedness (1970), with applications in chemotherapy, wildlife management and advertising.

6.5 Thorndike, Skinner and the consequences of behaviour. Thorndike's cats in puzzle boxes learned by trial and error, and his law of effect (1911) states that satisfying consequences strengthen responses; his connectionism also included the laws of exercise and readiness, and he later abandoned the law of exercise. Skinner distinguished elicited respondent behaviour from emitted operant behaviour, studied it in the Skinner box with the cumulative recorder, and described the three-term contingency of discriminative stimulus, response and consequence. Reinforcement increases behaviour and punishment decreases it; "positive" adds a stimulus and "negative" removes one, so negative reinforcement is not punishment. Secondary and generalised reinforcers (Wolfe's chimpanzees) underlie the token economy (Ayllon and Azrin). Escape terminates and avoidance prevents an aversive stimulus, and Mowrer's two-factor theory explains avoidance and compulsions. Punishment (including time-out and response cost) suppresses rather than teaches and has side effects (Estes). Shaping reinforces successive approximations; chaining, prompting and fading build complex behaviour; extinction produces an extinction burst and a return to the operant level.

6.6 Schedules of reinforcement and other operant phenomena. Continuous reinforcement teaches fastest and extinguishes fastest; partial reinforcement produces the partial reinforcement extinction effect (Humphreys). Fixed-ratio schedules give high rates with post-reinforcement pauses, variable-ratio schedules the highest, steadiest rates and greatest resistance to extinction, fixed-interval schedules a scallop, and variable-interval schedules moderate, steady rates (Ferster and Skinner). Differential reinforcement (DRO, DRI, DRA, DRL, DRH) weakens problem behaviour without punishment. Premack showed that a more probable behaviour reinforces a less probable one. Accidental reinforcement produces superstitious behaviour (Skinner, 1948). Uncontrollable shock produces learned helplessness (Overmier, Seligman and Maier, 1967), a model of depression later reformulated in attributional terms. The Brelands' instinctive drift and Brown and Jenkins's autoshaping show biological limits on operant learning; Herrnstein's matching law describes choice; Crespi's contrast effects show that reward size affects motivation and performance; and biofeedback (Miller, Kegel) brings bodily processes under operant control. Classical and operant conditioning differ in what is associated and in the kind of behaviour involved, but constantly interact.

6.7 Cognitive learning. Tolman's purposive behaviourism held that animals learn expectancies and cognitive maps; in Tolman and Honzik's latent learning experiment (1930) rats rewarded only from day 11 immediately matched always-rewarded rats, showing that reinforcement affects performance rather than learning, and place learning and Tinklepaugh's monkey supported the idea of expectancy. Köhler's chimpanzees, Sultan above all, solved stick and box problems by insight, a sudden perceptual reorganisation accompanied by the "aha" experience, though Birch showed that insight depends on earlier experience. Harlow's monkeys formed learning sets, learning to learn, which reconcile trial-and-error and insight. Concepts are formed in the stages of observation, discrimination, generalisation and abstraction.

6.8 Observational learning. Miller and Dollard (1941) treated imitation as reinforced behaviour. Bandura's Bobo doll studies (1961, 1963, 1965) showed that children learn new aggressive acts simply by watching a live or filmed model, and that the model's consequences affect performance rather than learning. Observational learning requires attention, retention, motor reproduction and motivation; reinforcement, direct or vicarious, governs only the last. Bandura's social cognitive theory added reciprocal determinism (person, behaviour and environment influencing one another), self-regulation and self-efficacy (1977), with four sources led by performance attainments. Rotter's separate social learning theory explains behaviour potential through expectancy and reinforcement value.

6.9 Other learning theorists. The grand theories, surveyed in Hilgard's Theories of Learning (1948), divided over whether learning consists of S-R connections or knowledge and whether it requires contiguity or reinforcement. Guthrie's contiguity theory held that learning is complete in one trial and that practice attaches acts to more stimuli; he proposed the threshold, fatigue and incompatible-response methods of breaking habits, and his cat studies with Horton were later reinterpreted as greeting behaviour. Hull's hypothetico-deductive theory made reinforcement equal to drive reduction and reaction potential equal to habit strength multiplied by drive, later with incentive motivation, stimulus-intensity dynamism and reactive and conditioned inhibition; his goal gradient, fractional anticipatory goal response and habit-family hierarchy were S-R answers to Tolman, and Spence extended the system. Estes's stimulus sampling theory (1950) gave learning a statistical form and predicted probability matching. Mowrer developed the bell-and-pad method, two-factor theory with its sign and solution learning, the neurotic paradox, and later a theory of fear, relief, hope and disappointment; Herrnstein and Hineline and Bolles challenged two-factor theory. Gagné's The Conditions of Learning (1965) arranged eight types of learning in a hierarchy from signal learning to problem solving. Constructivism (Piaget, Vygotsky, Bruner) sees learners as active builders of knowledge.

6.10 Learning in practice. Learning curves may be negatively accelerated (the commonest), positively accelerated or S-shaped, and group averages can hide sudden individual jumps (Estes). Bryan and Harter's telegraphers showed a plateau, explained by a hierarchy of habits; a plateau is temporary, unlike the physiological limit. Skills pass through cognitive, associative and autonomous stages (Fitts and Posner). Overlearning improves retention (Krueger, 1929). Distributed practice usually beats massed practice (Ebbinghaus, Jost, Baddeley and Longman), which Hull explained by reactive inhibition; reminiscence (Ballard) is improvement after rest. Whole learning suits meaningful, unified material and part learning long, difficult material. Knowledge of results is essential (Trowbridge and Cason), and specific, difficult goals improve performance (Locke and Latham). Transfer of training may be positive, negative or zero; formal discipline was refuted by James and Thorndike, and transfer has been explained by identical elements (Thorndike), generalisation of principles (Judd), transposition (Köhler) and the similarity of stimuli and responses (Osgood). Programmed instruction, from Pressey's teaching machine to Skinner's linear and Crowder's branching programmes and Keller's Personalized System of Instruction, applies shaping and immediate feedback to teaching.

From the past papers

  1. The phenomenon that is nearly opposite of habituation is termed: (RML 2024)

    (a) Sensitisation (b) Stabilization (c) Inhabitation (d) Habitation

    Answer: (a) Sensitisation. Habituation is a decrease in responding to a repeated, harmless stimulus; sensitisation is an increase in responding, usually to many stimuli, after a strong or noxious one (Section 6.1). The other three are not learning terms; "inhabitation" and "habitation" are sound-alike distractors.

  2. In Pavlov's experiment with dogs, the sound of the bell/metronome was the: (RML 2020)

    (a) operant stimulus after food was associated (b) neutral stimulus before the food was associated and conditioned stimulus after food was associated (c) unconditioned stimulus before the food was associated (d) conditioned stimulus before the food was associated and neutral stimulus after food was associated

    Answer: (b). Before pairing, the metronome does not produce salivation, so it is a neutral stimulus; after pairing with food, it is a conditioned stimulus. Food, not the bell, is the unconditioned stimulus; "operant stimulus" is not a term of classical conditioning; and (d) reverses the order.

  3. Which of the following is most effective in conditioning? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Backward Conditioning (b) Delayed Conditioning (c) Simultaneous Conditioning (d) Trace Conditioning

    Answer: (b) Delayed (delay) conditioning. The CS begins first and is still present when the UCS arrives, so it serves as a warning. Trace conditioning leaves a gap and is weaker; simultaneous conditioning gives no advance warning; backward conditioning is the least effective and often makes the CS a signal of safety.

  4. A child has learned to avoid a furry, black cat. However, she still plays with her grandmother's short-haired tabby. Her response demonstrates: (RML 2024)

    (a) Discrimination (b) Extinction (c) Successive approximation (d) Negative transfer

    Answer: (a) Discrimination. She responds differently to similar stimuli. Her fear of the black cat has not gone, so it is not extinction; successive approximation is shaping; negative transfer is earlier learning hindering new learning (Section 6.10).

  5. In reinforcement learning, the "prediction error" signal in the brain is primarily associated with: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Serotonin release (b) Dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area (c) GABA inhibition (d) Acetylcholine release

    Answer: (b). Schultz found that dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra fire to an unexpected reward, shift their firing to a cue that predicts it, and dip when a predicted reward fails to arrive: the prediction error of the Rescorla-Wagner model (Section 6.4). Serotonin, GABA and acetylcholine do not carry this signal.

  6. Taste aversion is an example of: (RML 2022)

    (a) Classical Conditioning (b) Second-order conditioning (c) Operant conditioning (d) Spurious association

    Answer: (a) Classical conditioning. The taste is the CS and the illness the UCS, paired once, often hours apart. It is not second-order conditioning (no established CS is used to condition a new one), not operant (no response is reinforced) and not a spurious association, which is a statistical term.

  7. Systematic desensitization technique is based upon the idea of: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Reciprocal inhibition (b) Law of effect (c) Habituation (d) Adaptation to stress

    Answer: (a) Reciprocal inhibition. Wolpe paired each step of an anxiety hierarchy with relaxation, a response incompatible with anxiety (Section 6.4). The law of effect is Thorndike's operant principle; habituation underlies exposure and response prevention; adaptation to stress belongs to stress theory (Chapter 11).

  8. Exposure and response prevention procedure is based on behaviour therapy principal of: (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Counter conditioning (b) Habituation and extinction (c) Reciprocal inhibition (d) Graded exposure

    Answer: (b) Habituation and extinction. The client meets the obsessional cue and is prevented from performing the compulsion, so the anxiety rises, peaks and falls without the escape response. No incompatible response is trained, so it is neither counterconditioning nor reciprocal inhibition (those describe systematic desensitisation), and graded exposure is a way of presenting stimuli, not the learning principle behind the change.

  9. The "law of effect," formulated by Edward Thorndike, states that: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Behaviors followed by pleasurable consequences are more likely to be repeated. (b) Organisms learn by observing and imitating models. (c) Insight occurs when the elements of a problem are suddenly restructured. (d) All behavior is motivated by unconscious drives.

    Answer: (a). Responses followed by satisfaction are strengthened (Section 6.5). Option (b) is Bandura's observational learning, (c) is Köhler's insight and (d) is Freud.

  10. The behavioral technique that involves removing a desirable stimulus following an undesirable behavior in order to reduce the frequency of that behavior is called: (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Negative reinforcement (b) Response cost (c) Positive punishment (d) Extinction

    Answer: (b) Response cost, a form of negative punishment. Negative reinforcement removes something to increase behaviour; positive punishment adds something unpleasant; extinction simply stops delivering the reinforcer that maintained the behaviour.

  11. The first-grade teacher gives students stickers when they perform well. If they earn five stickers in one day they are exempt from homework. The stickers in this example could also be called: (RML 2024)

    (a) Primary reinforcer (b) Token (c) Generalized reinforcer (d) Prepotent responses

    Answer: (b) Token. Stickers exchanged for a backup reinforcer are tokens, which are secondary reinforcers, not primary ones. A generalised reinforcer buys many different backups (money), whereas these buy one; a prepotent response is a dominant response, not a reinforcer.

  12. A parent deliberately ignores a child's temper tantrums in an attempt to discourage them. The parent's strategy is in accordance with: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Negative reinforcement (b) Extinction (c) Operant escape (d) Operant avoidance

    Answer: (b) Extinction. The source key marks (c), but that is wrong: ignoring withholds the attention that reinforced the tantrums, which is extinction (Williams, 1959; Section 6.5). Escape terminates, and avoidance prevents, an aversive stimulus; negative reinforcement would increase behaviour.

  13. A client with OCD washes their hands excessively to neutralize the anxiety caused by intrusive thoughts of contamination. This hand-washing behavior is BEST conceptualized as a(n): (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Conditioned stimulus (b) Unconditioned response (c) Avoidance response / Safety behavior (d) Operant behavior maintained by positive reinforcement

    Answer: (c). Washing reduces anxiety and is therefore negatively reinforced, the second factor of Mowrer's theory (Section 6.5). It is a learned response, not a stimulus and not an unconditioned response, and it is maintained by the removal of anxiety, not by positive reinforcement.

  14. Sheena is studying for her final exam in her psychology class. For every 45 minutes that she studies, she gives herself a mini chocolate, then goes back to studying. What Schedule of reinforcement is Sheena using? (RML 2021)

    (a) Variable ratio schedule (b) Variable interval schedule (c) Fixed interval schedule (d) Fixed ratio schedule

    Answer: (c) Fixed interval. The requirement is a constant amount of time, not a number of responses (ratio) and not an unpredictable period (variable). Strictly, reward after a fixed time regardless of responding is a fixed-time arrangement, but among these options fixed interval is correct (Section 6.6).

  15. Among the following schedules of reinforcement, which typically produces the highest rate of response and is most resistant to extinction? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Fixed Ratio (b) Variable Ratio (c) Fixed Interval (d) Variable Interval

    Answer: (b) Variable ratio. Ratio schedules reward fast responding, and unpredictability removes any reason to pause and makes it hard to detect that reinforcement has stopped, as with slot machines. Fixed ratio produces pauses after reinforcement, fixed interval a scallop and variable interval a moderate rate.

  16. According to the Premack Principle, a high-probability behavior (for example, playing video games) can be used to: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Reinforce a low-probability behavior (for example, completing homework). (b) Punish a low-probability behavior. (c) Reinforce another high-probability behavior. (d) Extinguish a maladaptive behavior.

    Answer: (a). A more preferred activity, made available only after a less preferred one, reinforces the less preferred one: grandma's rule (Section 6.6). The principle concerns reinforcement, not punishment or extinction, and runs from high to low probability, not between two high-probability activities.

  17. Learned helplessness phenomenon is offered by: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Aaron T. Beck (b) Martin Seligman and Steven F. Maier (c) Harry Stack Sullivan (d) Daniel Kahneman

    Answer: (b). Seligman and Maier's yoked dogs, given inescapable shock, later failed to escape avoidable shock (Section 6.6). Beck is known for the cognitive triad of depression, Sullivan for interpersonal theory and Kahneman for heuristics and biases.

  18. The Crespi effect in learning experiments refers to: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Loss of previously learned behavior due to retroactive inhibition (b) Increase in motivation when the magnitude of reinforcement increases (c) Decrease in response strength after extinction training (d) Sudden improvement in performance due to insight

    Answer: (b). Crespi's rats shifted from a small to a larger reward ran faster than rats always given the larger reward (positive contrast), and the reverse shift produced negative contrast; the changes were too abrupt to be learning, so they reflect motivation (Section 6.6). Option (a) is memory interference, (c) is extinction and (d) is Köhler's insight.

  19. The technique that is based on the idea that the autonomic nervous system can come under voluntary control through operant conditioning is known as: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Biofeedback (b) Hypnosis (c) Sublimation (d) Systematic desensitization

    Answer: (a) Biofeedback. A bodily process is measured and displayed so that changes in the desired direction are reinforced by the feedback (Miller; Section 6.6). Hypnosis is an altered state (Chapter 5), sublimation a Freudian defence mechanism, and systematic desensitisation a classical counterconditioning technique.

  20. What is the main difference between classical and operant conditioning? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Classical conditioning relies on reinforcement; operant conditioning does not (b) Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli; operant conditioning relies on reinforcement and punishment (c) Operant conditioning involves cognitive processes; classical conditioning does not (d) Both are entirely different learning processes

    Answer: (b). Classical conditioning associates two stimuli; operant conditioning associates a response with its consequences (Table 6.9). Option (a) is backwards, (c) is false because classical conditioning depends on prediction (Rescorla), and (d) ignores how constantly the two interact (two-factor theory, autoshaping).

  21. Latent learning was demonstrated by: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Tolman (b) Bandura (c) Skinner (d) Kohler

    Answer: (a) Tolman, with Honzik (1930): rats that found food only from day 11 immediately performed as well as rats rewarded throughout (Section 6.7). Bandura is observational learning, Skinner operant conditioning and Köhler insight.

  22. Concept formation stages include the following: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Observation, Discrimination, Generalization, Abstraction (b) Abstraction, Generalisation, Discrimination, Assimilation (c) Observation, Generalisation, Discrimination, Abstraction (d) Observation, Assimilation, Generalisation, Discrimination

    Answer: (a). The learner observes instances, discriminates them from non-instances, generalises to all instances sharing the essential features, and abstracts the defining properties (Section 6.7). Option (c) puts generalisation before discrimination, and assimilation, in (b) and (d), is Piaget's term, not a stage of concept formation.

  23. According to Albert Bandura, which of the following is NOT a necessary component process for observational learning to occur? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Attention (b) Motivation (c) Reinforcement (d) Reproduction

    Answer: (c) Reinforcement. The four processes are attention, retention, motor reproduction and motivation; the 1965 Bobo doll study showed that children learn a model's behaviour whether or not anyone is reinforced, and reinforcement affects only whether the behaviour is performed (Section 6.8).

  24. Reciprocal causation model has been described under: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Social cognitive theory (b) Two-factor theory (c) Drive reduction theory (d) Constructivist theory

    Answer: (a) Social cognitive theory. Bandura's triadic reciprocal causation (reciprocal determinism) holds that person, behaviour and environment influence one another (Section 6.8). Two-factor theory is Mowrer's account of avoidance, drive-reduction theory is Hull's, and constructivism (Piaget, Vygotsky, Bruner) concerns how learners build knowledge (Section 6.9).

  25. Learning always leads to efficacy, provided presence of a significant factor which is: (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) The learner has ability. (b) The learner has aptitude. (c) The learner has knowledge. (d) The learner has a specific goal.

    Answer: (d). Locke and Latham showed that specific, difficult goals, together with knowledge of results, direct attention, mobilise effort and increase persistence, so that practice becomes purposeful (Section 6.10). Ability, aptitude and knowledge help, but on their own they do not turn learning into effective performance.

  1. Systematic desensitisation (Joseph Wolpe, Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition, 1958) rests on reciprocal inhibition: relaxation, a response incompatible with anxiety, is paired step by step with a graded anxiety hierarchy. It is counterconditioning, not operant extinction, habituation or the law of effect. asked 17 times
  2. The token economy is operant conditioning: tokens are secondary (conditioned) reinforcers exchanged for backup reinforcers. It was developed on a psychiatric ward by Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin (The Token Economy, 1968), and it is the listed therapy NOT derived from classical conditioning. Stickers that earn a reward are tokens. asked 10 times

Schedules (Ferster and Skinner, 1957): variable ratio gives the highest rate and greatest resistance to extinction; fixed ratio, high rates with a post-reinforcement pause; fixed interval, the scallop (a reward every 45 minutes of study is keyed fixed interval); variable interval, moderate steady rates; continuous reinforcement, every response reinforced, learns fastest and extinguishes fastest. asked 7 times
  4. Extinction: a conditioned response weakens when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS (classical), or a response fades when it is no longer reinforced (operant), falling back to its earlier operant level. A parent who ignores tantrums is using extinction; the Amity 2023 key "operant escape" is wrong. asked 7 times
  5. In Pavlov's experiment the bell or metronome is a neutral stimulus before pairing with food and a conditioned stimulus after; a neutral stimulus becomes a CS by repeated pairing with an unconditioned stimulus, and the fundamental element is association. Fear of the ground where you lost a match is classical conditioning. asked 6 times
  6. Operant behaviour is emitted and operates on the environment (Skinner), and a response made more frequent by reinforcement is operant conditioning. Reinforcement always increases behaviour and punishment decreases it; positive adds and negative removes, so switching off an ongoing shock is negative reinforcement (escape), not punishment. asked 6 times
  7. Shaping (Skinner) is reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour; chaining links a sequence of responses, prompting adds a cue and fading withdraws it. asked 5 times

  12. Bandura's four processes of observational learning are attention, retention, motor reproduction and motivation. Reinforcement is not necessary for learning by observation (it governs performance), and the model must demonstrate the target behaviour. asked 4 times
  13. Escape terminates an aversive stimulus already present; avoidance prevents its onset; both are negative reinforcement. On Mowrer's two-factor theory (1947), OCD compulsions such as hand-washing are escape (avoidance) behaviours, or safety behaviours. asked 3 times
  14. Behaviour analysis (applied behaviour analysis) and functional analysis are based on operant conditioning: they examine a behaviour's antecedents and consequences. asked 3 times
  15. Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli; operant conditioning relies on consequences (reinforcement and punishment). The hallmark of behaviour therapy is the use of classical and operant conditioning techniques to eliminate unwanted behaviour. asked 3 times
  16. Reciprocal determinism (triadic reciprocal causation) belongs to Bandura's social cognitive theory: person, behaviour and environment influence one another in both directions. It is not Wolpe's reciprocal inhibition. asked 3 times
  17. Time-out and response cost (removing a desirable stimulus after an unwanted behaviour) are both negative punishment. asked 3 times
