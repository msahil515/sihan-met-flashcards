In 1913 a young German psychologist named Wolfgang Köhler (1887 to 1967) sailed to Tenerife, in the Canary Islands, to run a small research station of the Prussian Academy of Sciences. The station kept a colony of chimpanzees, and Köhler, one of the three founders of Gestalt psychology, wanted to answer a question that had been nagging him: could an ape solve a problem by understanding it, or only by blundering about until it stumbled on the answer? The First World War broke out the following year and kept him on the island far longer than he had planned, until 1920. The war's loss was psychology's gain.

The brightest of Köhler's chimpanzees was a male called Sultan. In one test a banana lay on the ground outside the bars of Sultan's enclosure, too far to reach by hand. Inside lay two hollow bamboo sticks, neither long enough on its own to reach the fruit. Sultan tried one stick, then the other. He pushed one stick out with the other until its tip touched the banana, but he could not draw the fruit in. Eventually he seemed to give up and sat playing idly with the sticks. Then, according to the record Köhler published, he happened to hold the two sticks in a line, pushed the thinner one into the hollow end of the thicker, jumped up, ran to the bars and raked in the banana with his new, longer tool. When the test was repeated he made the double stick at once, without fumbling.

Other chimpanzees stacked boxes to reach fruit hung from the roof, or fetched a stick from another room to use as a rake. To Köhler, these solutions did not look like the slow, accidental trial and error that Edward Thorndike had described in cats escaping from puzzle boxes in 1898. They appeared suddenly, as a whole, and once found they were kept. Köhler called this Einsicht, insight, and described the work in a 1917 book that appeared in English in 1925 as The Mentality of Apes.

What was going on inside Sultan's head in the moment before he jumped up? Did he "see" a picture of the joined sticks? Did he have a concept of a tool? Was he reasoning? And here is the question that will follow us to the end of this chapter: Sultan had no words, so was he thinking at all? Remember Sultan; you will meet him again at the end of the chapter.

8.1 What Is Thinking?Lower yield · 3 Qs

Ask ten people what thinking is and most of the answers will go round in a circle ("thinking is when you think about things"). Psychologists define thinking (or thought) as the mental manipulation of internal representations of information to form new representations: to understand something, solve a problem, reason, decide or create. The key word is manipulation. Seeing the chai stall in front of you is not thinking, and neither is simply remembering what a cup cost yesterday. Working out whether you can afford a second cup and still catch the bus is. Thinking takes what perception and memory supply and transforms it.

A broader word you will meet constantly is cognition: all the mental processes by which we acquire, store, transform and use knowledge, from attention and perception through memory to language and thought. Thinking is the part of cognition that does the transforming. Attention, perception and memory have their own chapters (4 to 7); this chapter is about what the mind does with what they deliver.

How Thought Came Back Into Psychology

If you want a science of thought you need some way to observe it, and for the first half of the twentieth century psychology could not agree on one. The structuralists, led in America by Edward Bradford Titchener (1867 to 1927), held that every thought is built from sensations, images and feelings, and that trained introspection could reveal them. In the first decade of the 1900s a group of researchers at the University of Würzburg, led by Oswald Külpe (1862 to 1915), gave trained observers simple tasks (find a word that is a superordinate of "bird", for example) and asked them to describe what passed through their minds. To everyone's surprise the observers often reported thoughts with no image or sensation in them at all. This became known as imageless thought. The Würzburg group also noticed that the task itself (the Aufgabe) steered thinking without being consciously present; Narziss Ach called this guiding influence the determining tendency.

Titchener's observers at Cornell, doing the same kind of introspection, insisted that they always found images. Who was right? There was no way to tell, because introspective reports cannot be checked by anyone else. That deadlock was one reason John Broadus Watson (1878 to 1958) threw introspection out of psychology in 1913. For Watson thinking was nothing more than implicit, subvocal speech: tiny movements of the larynx and tongue (you will see in Section 8.9 how that idea was tested and failed).

Thought came back with the cognitive revolution of the 1950s and 1960s. Noam Chomsky (born 1928) attacked the behaviourist account of language; Allen Newell (1927 to 1992) and Herbert Alexander Simon (1916 to 2001) built computer programs that solved problems; Jerome Seymour Bruner (1915 to 2016) and his colleagues published A Study of Thinking (1956); and in 1967 Ulric Neisser (1928 to 2012) wrote the textbook Cognitive Psychology, which gave the new field its name. Notice the irony. Cognitive psychology returned to the very topics the structuralists had cared about (consciousness, mental imagery, thought) but measured them objectively, with reaction times and error rates, instead of trusting introspective reports. (The history of the schools is told in Chapter 2.)

Exam trap

Which modern approach is closest to structuralism? Cognitive psychology, because it studies the structuralists' topics (consciousness, imagery, thought processes), not behaviourism (which banned them), not functionalism (which asked what the mind is for) and not neuroscience (which studies the nervous system that underlies all behaviour rather than continuing the structuralists' programme). Who pioneered cognitive psychology? Neisser named the field (1967) and is often called its father; Chomsky, Newell and Simon, Bruner and George Miller drove the revolution. Jean Piaget, who studied how children's thinking develops, is keyed as "a pioneer contributor to cognitive psychology" in one recent paper. Köhler was a forerunner (Gestalt work on insight). Lawrence Kohlberg is the name to reject: he is examined for moral development.

The Elements of Thought

What is thought made of? Textbooks list five building blocks. To see them at work, imagine you are deciding how to get to an exam hall across Mysuru early on a Monday morning.

  • Images. You "see" the route in your mind's eye: the crowded junction, the bus stop beside the temple. A mental image is a quasi-perceptual representation of something not present to the senses, and images can be auditory or tactile as well as visual.
  • Concepts. You think about "buses", "auto-rickshaws" and "traffic jams". A concept is a mental category that groups things sharing common properties (Section 8.2 is devoted to them).
  • Symbols and signs. The ₹ sign on the fare chart and the number 201 on the bus are symbols: they stand for something purely by convention. A sign, in the older textbook usage, has a natural or causal link to what it signals: dark clouds are a sign of rain, smoke a sign of fire.
  • Propositions. "The eight o'clock bus is usually late." A proposition is the smallest unit of knowledge that can be judged true or false; it asserts a relation between concepts.
  • Language. Finally you tell yourself, in words, "Take the auto." Language is the most powerful symbol system we have, because a finite set of words and rules can express an unlimited number of propositions.

Older textbooks sometimes add a sixth element, implicit muscular activity, which reflects Watson's motor theory of thought. Most modern accounts drop it.

Mental Imagery: Are There Pictures in the Head?

When you imagine the route to the exam hall, is there really something like a picture in your head, or does it only feel that way? Psychologists define mental imagery as the mental representation of a stimulus that is not physically present, experienced as if one were perceiving it. For decades the question of what images "are" seemed unanswerable, for exactly the reasons that sank the imageless-thought debate. Then, in 1971, an experiment showed that images could be measured with a stopwatch.

Key study: Shepard and Metzler's mental rotation (1971)

The question. When you judge whether two objects in different orientations are the same, do you turn one of them in your mind? The method. Roger Newland Shepard (1929 to 2022) and Jacqueline Metzler showed eight adults pairs of line drawings of three-dimensional objects, each built from ten cubes joined face to face. The second object was rotated by 0 to 180 degrees relative to the first, either in the picture plane or in depth, and was either the same object or its mirror image. Participants pressed "same" or "different" as fast as they could.

The result. The time needed to say "same" increased in a straight line with the angle between the two objects, by roughly one second for every 60 degrees (Figure 8.1), and rotation in depth took no longer than rotation in the picture plane. The conclusion. People appear to rotate an analogue representation through intermediate positions, much as one would turn a real object; Shepard called this mental rotation. The criticism. A straight line shows that the process is gradual and continuous, but it does not prove that the representation is literally a picture.

Stephen Michael Kosslyn (born 1948) extended the stopwatch approach. In one experiment (Kosslyn, Ball and Reiser, 1978) participants memorised a map of a fictional island with seven landmarks (a hut, a tree, a rock, a well, a lake, a patch of sand and a patch of grass). Later, with eyes closed, they imagined a black speck travelling from one landmark to another. The time taken rose in a straight line with the real distance on the map, as if they were scanning across a picture. In another study, people asked to imagine a rabbit beside an elephant took longer to "see" whether the rabbit had whiskers than people who imagined the same rabbit beside a fly: next to the elephant, the imagined rabbit was small and its details hard to inspect. Kosslyn concluded that images are depictive representations that preserve spatial layout, size and distance.

Not everyone was convinced. In 1973 Zenon Walter Pylyshyn (1937 to 2022) launched what became known as the imagery debate. According to his propositional theory, the experience of an image is an epiphenomenon, a by-product that does no work; underneath, knowledge is stored as abstract, language-like propositions ("the hut is north of the lake"). Why, then, does scanning time follow distance? Pylyshyn's answer was tacit knowledge: participants know how long it takes to look across a real map and, consciously or not, they reproduce that timing when asked to "scan" an image.

The debate was eventually settled largely by the brain. Imagining an object activates early visual cortex, including the primary visual cortex, and does so in a spatially organised way. Brain damage that disturbs vision tends to disturb imagery in parallel. In a famous demonstration, Edoardo Bisiach and Claudio Luzzatti (1978) asked patients with left-sided neglect after right-hemisphere damage to imagine standing on the cathedral steps in Milan's Piazza del Duomo and describe what they "saw". They listed buildings on the right of the square only. Asked to imagine standing at the opposite end, they now listed the buildings they had left out, and omitted the ones they had just named. A purely propositional store has no left or right side to neglect. Yet images are not simply photographs either: Deborah Chambers and Daniel Reisberg (1985) found that people who formed an image of the ambiguous duck-rabbit figure could not "flip" it in imagery, although they flipped it at once when they drew their image on paper. The modern consensus is that images are depictive, but they come with their interpretation attached.

Another Canadian psychologist, Allan Paivio (1925 to 2016), asked a related question about memory. His dual-coding theory (1971) proposes two separate but interconnected systems for representing information: a verbal system for words and a nonverbal or imagery system for pictures and objects. Concrete words such as "elephant" can be coded in both systems, whereas abstract words such as "justice" are coded mainly in the verbal one. Two codes give two routes to retrieval, which explains why concrete words are remembered better than abstract ones (the concreteness effect) and pictures better than words (the picture superiority effect). Imagery mnemonics such as the method of loci exploit the same advantage (Chapter 7).

Exam trap

"According to dual-coding theory, which information is more easily remembered?" The answer is visual (pictorial, imageable) information, because it can be coded twice. Verbal, auditory and abstract "semantic" material usually gets only the verbal code. Dual coding belongs to Paivio (1971); do not confuse it with Craik and Lockhart's levels of processing (1972) or with Baddeley and Hitch's working memory model (1974). And keep the two imagery theorists straight: Kosslyn = depictive images (scanning the island); Pylyshyn = propositions and tacit knowledge.

Types of Thinking

Not all thinking is alike. Some of it is aimed at a goal and checked against reality; some drifts. Some narrows down to one answer; some fans out. The distinctions below recur in every entrance paper, usually as "match the type with its description" or "who coined the term" items. Table 8.1 gathers them.

The most basic contrast is between directed and undirected thinking. Directed (or realistic) thinking is controlled by a goal and constrained by logic and reality: planning a journey, solving a sum, writing an answer. Undirected or associative thinking lets one idea call up the next by association, with no goal steering it; daydreaming in a warm afternoon lecture is the everyday example, and Freud's free association is its clinical use.

At the far end of undirected thinking lies what the Swiss psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler (1857 to 1939) called autistic thinking in his 1911 monograph on dementia praecox, the book that made his new terms "schizophrenia" and "autism" famous. Autistic thinking is thinking governed by wishes and needs rather than by reality: fantasy in which you top the merit list, receive the award and silence your critics. Because the word "autistic" invited confusion, Bleuler later offered the alternative dereistic thinking (thinking "away from reality"). Jean Piaget borrowed Bleuler's term and placed the young child's egocentric thought halfway between autistic thought and mature directed thought, a point you will meet again in Section 8.9.

The American psychometrician Joy Paul Guilford (1897 to 1987) contributed the most famous pair. In convergent thinking, thought narrows down to the single best or correct answer, as when you solve a multiple-choice question or a crossword clue. In divergent thinking, it fans out to generate many possible answers, as when you list all the uses you can think of for a brick. Divergent thinking is the core of Guilford's account of creativity and of most creativity tests (Section 8.6).

By critical thinking psychologists mean the careful evaluation of claims, arguments and evidence before accepting them; the philosopher Robert Ennis's widely used definition is "reasonable reflective thinking focused on deciding what to believe or do". Checking who wrote a health message forwarded on a family WhatsApp group, and on what evidence, is critical thinking. Creative thinking, by contrast, produces ideas or products that are both new and useful. Critical thinking evaluates; creative thinking generates. Good problem solvers alternate between them.

The term reflective thinking belongs to the American philosopher and educator John Dewey (1859 to 1952). In How We Think (1910) he defined it as the active, persistent and careful consideration of a belief in the light of the grounds that support it, and described it as beginning with a felt difficulty and moving through defining the problem, suggesting solutions, reasoning out their implications and testing them. (Those five steps are also one of the earliest models of problem solving; see Section 8.3.) Finally, the Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer (1880 to 1943), in Productive Thinking (published in 1945, after his death), distinguished productive thinking, which restructures a problem and so produces genuine understanding, from reproductive thinking, which merely reapplies a learned method. A child who sees that a parallelogram can be turned into a rectangle by cutting off a triangle at one end and moving it to the other end has thought productively; a child who simply plugs numbers into a memorised formula has thought reproductively.

Autistic thinking is not autism spectrum disorder. It is Bleuler's 1911 term for reality-ignoring, wish-driven fantasy, and it appears in thinking chapters, not in the developmental disorders. Clinical link

In the mental status examination, disturbances of thinking are classically sorted under four headings. The stream of thought (its speed and flow: pressure of thought, poverty of thought, thought block); the form of thought, meaning how ideas are linked (loosening of associations or derailment, tangentiality, circumstantiality, flight of ideas, "knight's move" thinking, word salad, neologisms), the formal thought disorder characteristic of schizophrenia, of which word salad is the most severe form; the content of thought (delusions, overvalued ideas, preoccupations); and the possession of thought, the sense that one's thoughts are one's own (obsessions, and thought insertion, withdrawal and broadcasting). A patient whose answer wanders through needless detail but finally reaches the point shows circumstantiality, a disorder of form; one who drifts off and never returns shows tangentiality. The disorders themselves are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

8.2 ConceptsMedium yield · 4 Qs

Imagine a world without concepts. Every dog you met would be a brand-new creature: you would not know whether it might bite, whether it eats grass, or whether it can be stroked. A concept is a mental representation of a category, a class of objects, events, ideas or people that share common properties. Concepts give us two enormous savings. They provide cognitive economy, because we can store what we know about dogs once rather than separately for every dog; and they support inference, because once you classify a new animal as a dog you can predict a great deal about it that you have not observed. Most of the elements of thought described in Section 8.1 depend on concepts.

How Concepts Are Formed

The term concept formation refers to the process by which we learn to group things into categories and to attach the right properties to each. Many textbooks, especially in India, describe it as passing through a series of steps. Follow a two-year-old learning the concept "dog".

  1. Observation. The child meets many particular instances: the neighbour's dog, the street dog near the gate, a dog in a picture book.
  2. Discrimination. The child notices differences: dogs bark and cows do not; dogs are smaller than cows; cats have a different face.
  3. Generalisation. The child notices what the instances have in common (four legs, fur, a tail, barking) and extends the label to new animals that share these features.
  4. Abstraction. The child pulls out the common features and represents them apart from any particular dog, so that "dog" now means the shared pattern, not Tommy next door.

Many books add a final step of naming or labelling, because attaching a word to the abstraction makes the concept stable and shareable. The steps overlap in real life, and mistakes along the way are informative: the toddler who calls every four-legged animal "dog" has generalised too widely (an overextension; see Section 8.8).

The first laboratory study of concept learning was carried out by Clark Leonard Hull (1884 to 1952), better known for his learning theory (Chapter 6). In 1920 Hull taught participants nonsense names for Chinese characters. Characters that shared a particular radical (a component stroke pattern) all received the same name, although the radical was embedded in otherwise different characters. Participants gradually came to name new characters correctly, often without being able to say what the common element was. Hull concluded that a concept is learned by the gradual, reinforced abstraction of a common element, an associative, step-by-step view.

The Classical View and Artificial Concepts

The oldest account of concepts goes back to Aristotle. According to the classical view (also called the defining-attribute view), every concept is defined by a set of defining features that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient. "Triangle" is a closed figure with three straight sides: anything with all three features is a triangle, and anything lacking one of them is not. "Bachelor" is an unmarried adult male. On this view membership is all-or-none, the boundaries are sharp, and every member is as good a member as any other.

The classical view fits well-defined, rule-based categories, and these are exactly the categories that dominated early laboratory work. An artificial concept (or formal concept) is one defined by an explicit rule, typically invented by an experimenter, such as "all cards with two red borders". The rules can be combined in three logical ways:

  • A conjunctive concept requires the joint presence of two or more attributes ("red and square"). An eligible voter in India is a conjunctive concept: an Indian citizen and aged eighteen or over and on the electoral roll.
  • A disjunctive concept requires one attribute or another, or both ("red or square"). "Out" in cricket is beautifully disjunctive: bowled, or caught, or leg before wicket, or run out, or stumped, and so on. Two dismissals can share no feature at all and still both be "out".
  • A relational concept is defined by a relationship between attributes rather than by the attributes themselves: "taller than", "between", "uncle" (a brother of one's parent), or "a card with more figures than borders".

People find conjunctive concepts easiest and disjunctive ones hardest, partly because we expect members of a category to share something.

Key study: Bruner, Goodnow and Austin's A Study of Thinking (1956)

The question. When people learn an artificial concept, do they gradually strengthen associations (as Hull thought), or do they actively form and test hypotheses? The method. Jerome Bruner, Jacqueline Goodnow and George Austin used an array of 81 cards that varied on four attributes, each with three values: the shape of the figures (cross, circle or square), their colour (red, green or black), the number of figures (one, two or three) and the number of borders (one, two or three). The experimenter had a concept in mind, such as "red circles". In the selection paradigm the participant chose cards one at a time and was told whether each was a positive instance; in the reception paradigm the experimenter chose the cards.

The result. Participants did not behave like associative machines. They formed hypotheses about the rule and used recognisable strategies to test them (Table 8.2). The conclusion. Concept attainment is an active process of hypothesis testing. This is the "hypothesis-testing model of concept formation". The criticism. The concepts were artificial and rule-defined; the real concepts of everyday life, as Eleanor Rosch was to show, are not like that.

Exam trap

"The researcher whose theory explains how we form artificial concepts by developing hypotheses" is Jerome Bruner (with Goodnow and Austin, 1956). Eleanor Rosch is the name for natural concepts and prototypes. Clark Hull (1920) did study concept learning, but as gradual associative abstraction of a common element, not hypothesis testing. John Watson has nothing to do with concepts. Also remember: in cricket, "out" is disjunctive (any one of several conditions), a voter is conjunctive (all conditions), "uncle" is relational.

Natural Concepts and Prototype Theory

Try to give a classical definition of "game". The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889 to 1951) tried, in his posthumous Philosophical Investigations (1953), and failed. Cricket has teams and a winner; solitaire has no team; ring-a-ring-a-roses has no winner; chess involves skill, snakes and ladders only luck. There is no single feature that all games share. Instead, Wittgenstein said, games are related by a network of overlapping similarities, the way members of a family resemble one another: one has the father's nose and the mother's eyes, another the mother's eyes and the grandmother's chin, yet no single feature runs through them all. He called this family resemblance.

Eleanor Rosch (born 1938) turned Wittgenstein's insight into experimental psychology in the 1970s. A natural concept is a category that people form through ordinary experience with the world (bird, fruit, furniture, vehicle), as opposed to an artificial concept defined by an experimenter's rule. Rosch showed that natural concepts have three properties that the classical view cannot explain.

First, members differ in how typical they are. Asked to rate how good an example of "bird" each animal is, people agree that a robin or a sparrow is an excellent example, a hen a poorer one and a penguin a poor one. Second, typicality has measurable consequences. In a sentence verification task, people say "true" faster to "A robin is a bird" than to "A penguin is a bird", although both are equally true; this is the typicality effect. Typical members are also named first when people list category members, and children learn them first. Third, the boundaries of natural concepts are fuzzy. Michael McCloskey and Sam Glucksberg (1978) found that people disagreed about whether a stroke is a disease or a tomato a fruit, and many even changed their own answers when asked again a few weeks later.

Rosch's explanation is prototype theory. A prototype is the best, most typical representative of a category: an abstract average or central tendency of the members we have encountered, not any particular one of them. Rosch and Carolyn Mervis (1975) showed that an item's typicality depends on its family resemblance score, the number of features it shares with other members of the category. A new object is classified by comparing it with the prototype; the more similar it is, the faster and more confidently it is accepted.

Rosch made a second discovery about how categories are arranged vertically. Rosch and colleagues (1976) distinguished three levels: the superordinate level (furniture, animal), the basic level (chair, dog) and the subordinate level (kitchen chair, Labrador). The basic level is psychologically privileged. It is the most general level at which members share a common shape and are used with similar movements, it is the level children learn first, and it is the level people use to name things: shown pictures of objects, Rosch's participants used basic-level names 1,595 times, subordinate names 14 times and a superordinate name just once. The basic level gives the best trade-off between being informative and being distinctive. Figure 8.2 shows both of Rosch's discoveries side by side. (Experts shift downwards: a birdwatcher says "Indian robin" where you say "bird".)

Exam trap

"An idealised representative object of a category" is a prototype, and a prototype is abstract and averaged: it need not be any real member you have met. Do not confuse it with three terms from pattern recognition (Chapter 4): a template is an exact stored copy that input must match; features are the elementary parts (lines, angles) that feature-analysis theories detect; geons are the 36 or so simple three-dimensional shapes in Irving Biederman's recognition-by-components theory (1987). Family resemblance began with Wittgenstein (1953) and was tested by Rosch and Mervis (1975).

Exemplar Theory

Prototype theory has a weakness. If all you keep is an average, how do you know that birds vary a great deal in size, or that a small, plain brown bird may still be a sparrow? An alternative, exemplar theory, developed by Douglas Medin and Marguerite Schaffer (1978) and later Robert Nosofsky, proposes that we store many specific remembered instances (exemplars) of each category, and classify a new item by comparing it with all of them. A new animal is called a dog if it resembles the dogs you remember more than it resembles the cats you remember. Typicality effects still appear, because typical items resemble many stored exemplars; but the theory also explains why we know about a category's variability and its unusual members.

Most researchers now think we use both kinds of representation: prototypes when categories are large and well learned, exemplars when they are small or newly learned. A third approach, the theory or knowledge-based view, adds that concepts are embedded in our wider explanatory knowledge: you know that a whale is a mammal not because it looks like other mammals (it looks like a fish) but because of what you know about how it breathes and bears its young. How Concepts Are Organised: Networks

Concepts do not float about separately; they are linked. The first influential model of how, the hierarchical network model of Allan Collins and Ross Quillian (1969), pictured knowledge as a semantic network: concepts are nodes, and labelled links connect them ("canary IS A bird", "bird HAS wings"). To save space (cognitive economy), each property is stored only once, at the highest level to which it applies: "has skin" at the animal node, "can fly" at the bird node, "can sing" at the canary node. The model predicts that verifying a sentence takes longer the more links must be travelled, and so it does: "A canary can sing" is verified faster than "A canary can fly", which is faster than "A canary has skin". But the model could not explain the typicality effect, or why "A pig is an animal" is verified faster than "A pig is a mammal" (Rips, Shoben and Smith, 1973), which the hierarchy says should be the other way round.

Collins and Elizabeth Loftus (1975) replaced the rigid hierarchy with a spreading activation model. Links now vary in strength, short for closely related concepts and long for distant ones, and activating one node spreads activation outward to its neighbours. This explains semantic priming: you recognise "nurse" faster just after reading "doctor" than after reading "bread" (Meyer and Schvaneveldt, 1971).

A different kind of network arrived in 1986 with Parallel Distributed Processing by David Rumelhart, James McClelland and the PDP Research Group. In connectionism, knowledge is not stored in individual nodes that each stand for a concept. A concept is a pattern of activation distributed across many simple, neuron-like units, and all knowledge lies in the strengths (weights) of the connections between units, which change gradually with experience. Connectionist networks learn from examples, generalise to new ones, and degrade gracefully when damaged rather than losing whole facts, which makes them attractive models of the brain. Organised knowledge structures for familiar situations are also called schemas, and schemas for routine event sequences (going to a restaurant, attending a wedding) are called scripts (Schank and Abelson, 1977); their role in memory is discussed in Chapter 7.

Exam trap

"Which model proposes that memory is organised in a network of interconnected nodes?" A recent paper keyed connectionist model. That works, because connectionist (PDP) models are networks of interconnected units. But be careful: Collins and Quillian's semantic network model is also literally a network of nodes, so an option reading "semantic network model" would be equally defensible. When "network model" and "connectionist model" both appear, follow the key (connectionist), and remember the difference: semantic networks have one node per concept (localist); connectionist networks spread each concept over many units (distributed). "Associative model" is the older umbrella idea from Aristotle and the British associationists; "linkage model" is not a standard term.

8.3 Problem SolvingMedium yield · 9 Qs

Sultan had a problem, and so do you most days. A problem exists whenever you are in one state (the initial state), want to be in another (the goal state), and the route from one to the other is not obvious. Problem solving is thinking directed at getting from the first to the second. Psychologists have studied it in two very different traditions: the Gestalt tradition of Köhler and Duncker, which asked how a problem is suddenly seen in a new way, and the information-processing tradition of Newell and Simon, which asked how a problem is searched. You need both.

Kinds of Problems

A well-defined problem has a clearly specified initial state, a clear goal and a clear set of allowable moves: an algebra equation, a sudoku, the Tower of Hanoi. An ill-defined problem leaves one or more of these vague: "write a good answer on attachment theory", "choose a career", "help this client feel less hopeless". Most of the problems of real life, and nearly all clinical problems, are ill-defined, and much of the work of solving them lies in defining them. Researchers also distinguish knowledge-lean problems, which can be solved with the information given, from knowledge-rich problems, which need expertise; and insight problems, solved by a sudden change of view, from non-insight (analytic) problems, solved step by step.

The Problem Space

In 1956 Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, working with the programmer Cliff Shaw, unveiled the Logic Theorist, a computer program that proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica. They followed it in the late 1950s with the General Problem Solver (GPS), designed to solve any well-defined problem, and set out the full theory in their 1972 book Human Problem Solving. Their method was to have people think aloud while solving puzzles, record every utterance (a verbal protocol), and check whether the program went through the same steps.

Their central idea was the problem space: an abstract description of all the possible states a problem can be in. It contains the initial state, the goal state, the operators (the legal moves that change one state into another), all the intermediate states those moves can produce, and the constraints on which moves are allowed. Solving a problem means searching this space for a path from the initial state to the goal. Take the Tower of Hanoi: three discs of decreasing size sit on the left of three pegs; the goal is to move them all to the right peg, moving one disc at a time and never placing a larger disc on a smaller. The shortest solution takes seven moves (in general, 2n minus 1 moves for n discs). For three discs you could map the whole space on a sheet of paper; for a game such as chess, the number of possible paths is astronomically large. Because short-term memory is small and we can do only one thing at a time, Newell and Simon argued, people cannot search such spaces exhaustively. They must take shortcuts.

Algorithms Versus Heuristics

An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure that is guaranteed to produce the solution if it is followed correctly. Long division is an algorithm. So is trying every combination of a three-digit padlock: there are only 1,000 combinations, and if you try them all, one will open the lock. Algorithms are certain but often slow, and for many problems they are impossibly slow.

A heuristic is a rule of thumb: a mental shortcut that usually leads to a solution quickly but does not guarantee one. The word comes from the same Greek root as "eureka" (to find), and the mathematician George Pólya popularised it in How to Solve It (1945). Suppose you must unscramble MSTAE into an English word. An algorithm would test all 120 possible orderings. A heuristic says: put consonant clusters that often begin English words at the front, and try familiar endings. "ST" looks promising, and STEAM (or MATES, MEATS, TAMES) appears within seconds. The price of speed is that a heuristic can mislead; you will see in Section 8.5 that the same trade-off runs through judgement and decision making.

Exam trap

"A rule of thumb that allows an efficient search for the solution of a problem" is a heuristic, not an algorithm. The defining contrast: an algorithm guarantees a solution; a heuristic is efficient but not guaranteed, "a general rule that is usually right". Wrong options to reject: "a heuristic always produces a solution" (that is an algorithm), "a heuristic is more accurate than an algorithm" (it is less certain, not more accurate), "heuristics can only be used by computers", "heuristics always produce errors". Education and learning are not problem-solving methods at all.

Heuristic Strategies

Newell and Simon regarded means-end analysis as the most important heuristic of all, and it was the engine of GPS. It has three steps: note the most important difference between the current state and the goal state; set a subgoal of reducing that difference; and select an operator that achieves the subgoal. Then repeat. Suppose you are in Mysuru and need a certificate attested at an office in Bengaluru by Friday. The biggest difference is distance, so the subgoal is "get to Bengaluru"; the operator is a train. A new difference appears (you are at home, not at the station), creating a sub-subgoal ("get to the station") with its own operator (an auto). Breaking a problem into such subgoals is the heart of the method.

The strategy of working backward starts from the goal and works back towards the initial state. It is useful when the goal is clearly specified and there are many possible paths forward from the start. Try this: water lilies on a pond double the area they cover every day, and the pond is completely covered on day 30. On which day was it half covered? Working forward is hopeless; working backward gives the answer at once: day 29. Planning a revision timetable from the exam date backwards is the same strategy.

The heuristic of hill climbing is simpler than means-end analysis: at each step you choose the move that takes you closest to the goal state, judged by some simple measure of progress. It is like trying to reach the top of a hill in thick fog by always stepping uphill. It works when progress is steady, but it fails whenever reaching the goal requires a move that takes you temporarily further away, because hill climbing looks only one step ahead and never plans. The classic demonstration is the Hobbits and Orcs problem (a version of the missionaries and cannibals puzzle): three hobbits and three orcs must cross a river in a boat that holds two, and the orcs must never outnumber the hobbits on either bank. At one point the only legal move is to bring two creatures back across the river, apparently undoing progress, and that is precisely where solvers stall (Thomas, 1974). Climbers call the trap a false summit; problem solvers call it a local maximum.

Solving by analogy means using the solution of an earlier, similar problem to solve a new one. It is powerful when people notice the analogy, and the striking finding is how rarely they do.

Key study: Gick and Holyoak's radiation problem (1980)

The problem. Karl Duncker had posed it in 1945. A patient has an inoperable stomach tumour. A ray strong enough to destroy the tumour will also destroy the healthy tissue it passes through; a ray weak enough to spare the tissue will not destroy the tumour. What can the doctor do? The method. Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak first had participants memorise a story about a general who wished to capture a fortress. Every road to the fortress was mined so that a large force would set off the mines, but small groups could pass safely; the general divided his army and had the groups converge on the fortress at the same moment. Then participants attempted the radiation problem.

The result. Only about 10 per cent solved the radiation problem without the story. Of those who had memorised the story, about 40 per cent used it spontaneously, but about 80 per cent solved the problem once told that the story might help. (The answer: aim several weak rays at the tumour from different directions so that they converge on it.) The conclusion. People can transfer a solution across problems that share structural similarity (the underlying principle of convergence), but they often fail to notice an analogy when the surface features (armies versus rays) differ. In a later study, Mark Keane (1987) used a story that was superficially similar to the problem (a surgeon using rays on a cancer), and 88 per cent of participants recalled it spontaneously.

The most primitive strategy of all is trial and error: trying responses more or less at random and keeping whatever works. Edward Lee Thorndike's cats in their puzzle boxes (1898) escaped this way, their escape times falling gradually over many trials (Chapter 6). Trial and error works when the possible moves are few and mistakes are cheap; it is hopeless for problems with large problem spaces. Table 8.4 summarises the strategies.

Insight and Incubation

The Gestalt psychologists did not think that problems were solved by search. For Köhler, Wertheimer, Karl Duncker (1903 to 1940) and Norman Maier (1900 to 1977), many problems are solved by insight: a sudden restructuring of the way the problem is represented, so that its elements are seen in a new relationship and the solution becomes obvious, often with an "aha" experience. Köhler listed the marks of insightful solutions in his apes: the transition from helplessness to solution is sudden and complete; the solution, once found, is performed smoothly; it is retained; and it transfers to similar problems. Compare Thorndike's cats, whose times fell gradually and erratically over dozens of trials.

Try the nine-dot problem in Figure 8.3 before you read on: join all nine dots with four straight lines without lifting your pen or retracing a line. Norman Maier used it in research in 1930, and most people fail. They assume, without being told, that the lines must stay inside the square the dots form. The solution requires going outside it. Yet even when people are told explicitly that they may go outside the square, only a minority succeed, which tells us that restructuring the problem is necessary but not sufficient: after the insight, there is still a search to do.

Maier's other famous task, the two-string problem (1931), showed that insight can be nudged without awareness. Two strings hang from the ceiling, too far apart to hold both at once, and the task is to tie them together. The solution is to tie a heavy object, such as a pair of pliers, to one string and set it swinging like a pendulum, then catch it while holding the other. When Maier, walking past, casually brushed one string so that it swung, many participants solved the problem shortly afterwards, and most of them denied having noticed his hint.

Key study: Metcalfe and Wiebe's warmth ratings (1987)

The question. Is insight really sudden, or does it only feel sudden? The method. Janet Metcalfe and David Wiebe gave people insight problems and ordinary analytic problems (such as algebra), and at short, regular intervals asked them to rate how "warm" they felt, that is, how close to the solution. The result. On analytic problems warmth rose gradually and steadily, as you would expect from step-by-step search. On insight problems warmth stayed low and flat until just before the solution, then jumped. The conclusion. Insight problems are solved by a qualitatively different, sudden process, as the Gestaltists had claimed, and people cannot sense their approach to an insight solution. The criticism. Feelings of warmth are self-reports; some later researchers argue for a "business as usual" view in which insight uses ordinary memory and search processes that simply happen to end abruptly.

Köhler's own conclusions also met a challenge. Herbert Birch (1945) found that young chimpanzees with no previous experience of handling sticks rarely solved stick problems; after a few days of playing with sticks, they solved them readily. Insight, it seems, builds on past experience rather than replacing it.

What should you do when you are stuck? Often, the best advice is to stop. Researchers call the period during which a problem is set aside incubation; after it, the solution sometimes arrives with surprisingly little effort. The term comes from Graham Wallas's stages of creative thought (Section 8.6). Why might a break help? Perhaps the mind continues to work on the problem unconsciously; perhaps fatigue lifts; but the best-supported explanation is that a break lets you forget a misleading approach that had been blocking you (the forgetting fixation hypothesis of Steven Smith and Steven Blankenship). A meta-analysis by Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod (2009) concluded that incubation effects are real but modest, and largest for creative problems that have many possible solutions.

Obstacles to Problem Solving

If problem solving were only a matter of search and insight, more of us would be good at it. The Gestaltists were especially interested in how past experience, usually our best friend, can get in the way.

The first obstacle, functional fixedness, is the tendency to think of an object only in terms of its usual function, which blinds us to other uses that would solve a problem. Karl Duncker's candle problem, published in his monograph On Problem Solving (English translation 1945), is the classic demonstration. Participants received a candle, a book of matches and a box of drawing pins, and were asked to fix the candle to the wall so that it would burn without dripping wax onto the table. Most tried to pin the candle directly to the wall or to stick it on with melted wax. The solution is to empty the box, pin it to the wall as a shelf, and stand the candle in it. Far more people solved the problem when the box was presented empty, with the pins beside it, because an empty box does not look so much like a container. Functional fixedness is a case of top-down processing working too well: what we know about an object's typical use dominates what we perceive about its actual properties. Curiously, it seems to be learned. Tim German and Margaret Defeyter (2000) found that five-year-olds, who have not yet learned to think of objects in terms of their designed function, showed no functional fixedness on a version of the task, whereas six- and seven-year-olds did.

A mental set is the broader tendency to approach a problem in a way that has worked before, even when a better or simpler method is available. Its classic demonstration is the water-jar experiment of Abraham Luchins (1942), whose German term for the phenomenon, Einstellung ("setting" or "attitude"), gives it its other name, the Einstellung effect. The other obstacles are easier to describe. "Mental barrier" is not a technical term, and trial and error is a strategy, not an obstacle. Seeing a matchbox only as a container, not as a platform, is functional fixedness.

Stages of Problem Solving and the Expert Advantage

Several writers have broken problem solving into stages. Dewey (1910), as you saw, described a felt difficulty, its definition, suggested solutions, reasoning about them and testing. Pólya's How to Solve It (1945) offered four steps for mathematics students: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, and look back to check. Robert Jeffrey Sternberg (born 1949) describes a problem-solving cycle of seven steps: identify the problem, define and represent it, formulate a strategy, organise the information, allocate resources (time, effort, money), monitor progress, and evaluate the solution. It is a cycle because evaluation often reveals a new problem. Whatever the labels, every scheme moves from understanding the problem, through generating and choosing a strategy, to carrying it out and checking the result.

Why are experts better problem solvers? Not, it turns out, because they have better general reasoning. William Chase and Herbert Simon (1973), building on the Dutch psychologist Adriaan de Groot, showed chess positions for five seconds and asked players to reconstruct them. Masters reproduced real game positions far more accurately than novices, but when the pieces were placed at random, their advantage disappeared. Experts store thousands of meaningful patterns, or chunks, and see a board as a few familiar configurations rather than as twenty-odd separate pieces. Similarly, Michelene Chi and her colleagues (1981) found that physics experts sorted problems by their underlying principle (conservation of energy, say), whereas novices sorted them by surface features (problems with inclined planes, problems with springs). Experts spend longer representing a problem before starting, and then often work forward from what they know, whereas novices plunge in and work backward from the unknown.

Clinical link

Thomas D'Zurilla and Marvin Goldfried (1971) turned research on problem solving into a therapy. Problem-solving therapy teaches clients a structured sequence: a positive problem orientation, defining and formulating the problem, generating many alternative solutions without judging them, deciding among them, and implementing and verifying the chosen solution. It is used for depression, for people at risk of self-harm (who often show narrow, rigid problem solving under stress), and in primary care. Notice how much of it is the research of this section put to work: defining ill-defined problems, brainstorming to escape mental set, and evaluating outcomes.

8.4 ReasoningHigh yield · 9 Qs

Reasoning is the process of drawing conclusions from premises or evidence. It overlaps with problem solving (you often reason your way to a solution) but its focus is on the inference: what follows from what? Philosophers have studied the rules of correct reasoning since Aristotle. Psychologists ask a different question: how do people actually reason, and why do they so often go wrong?

Deduction, Induction and Abduction

The first kind, deductive reasoning, moves from general premises to a specific conclusion that must be true if the premises are true. It is sometimes called top-down reasoning. "All autos in Mysuru must charge by the meter. This is a Mysuru auto. Therefore this auto must charge by the meter." The second kind, inductive reasoning, moves from specific observations to a general conclusion that is probable but never certain; it is bottom-up. "Every auto I have taken in Mysuru has asked for more than the meter. Therefore Mysuru autos overcharge." The next auto may prove you wrong. Deductive conclusions go no further than the premises; inductive conclusions go beyond them, which is both their power and their risk.

Two terms keep deduction honest. An argument is valid when its conclusion follows necessarily from its premises because of its logical form, whatever the content. An argument is sound when it is valid and its premises are true. "All psychologists are vegetarians; Sihan is a psychologist; therefore Sihan is a vegetarian" is perfectly valid but unsound, because the first premise is false. So the truth of a deductive conclusion is only as good as the truth of the premises (or theory) it starts from.

The American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce (1839 to 1914) described a third kind of inference, abductive reasoning: inference to the best explanation. A surprising fact is observed; if some hypothesis were true, the fact would follow naturally; therefore there is reason to suspect the hypothesis is true. Diagnosis is abductive. A clinician sees early waking, weight loss, slowed speech and hopelessness, and infers the disorder that best explains them, while remaining ready to revise the conclusion.

Science combines all three. In the hypothetico-deductive method, associated with the philosopher Karl Raimund Popper (1902 to 1994), a researcher derives a hypothesis from a theory, deduces a prediction that could be observed, and tests it. If the prediction fails, the hypothesis is refuted; if it succeeds, the hypothesis survives, but it is not proved, because another hypothesis might have made the same prediction. (You will see in a moment that this asymmetry is simply modus tollens versus affirming the consequent.) In research methods, a deductive approach starts from theory and tests it against data, as most quantitative research does; an inductive approach starts from data, looks for patterns and builds theory, as most qualitative research does (grounded theory is the purest example).

Exam trap

Recent papers ask the research-methods face of reasoning. "Starts with a theory and tests it with observations" = deductive reasoning. "Gathers data, looks for patterns, then generates a theory" or "moves from specific observations to broader generalisations" = inductive reasoning, which is the reasoning of theory development and of qualitative research. Abductive = best explanation. The hypothetico-deductive method is the full cycle of hypothesis, deduced prediction and test; when both "deductive reasoning" and "hypothetico-deductive method" appear, the keys have chosen the plain label. One paper asked what the "validity of conclusions" in deduction depends on, keying the soundness of the initial theory. Strictly, logical validity depends on form alone, and soundness on true premises; the item uses "validity" loosely to mean "trustworthiness", so choose the soundness option.

Syllogisms, Belief Bias and the Atmosphere Effect

A syllogism is an argument with two premises and a conclusion, introduced by Aristotle. In a categorical syllogism each statement uses one of four quantifiers: all, no, some, or some ... not. "All B are C; all A are B; therefore all A are C" is valid. "All A are B; all C are B; therefore all A are C" is not: all cats are mammals and all dogs are mammals, but it does not follow that all cats are dogs. People make many errors on syllogisms, and the errors are systematic.

The most famous is belief bias: the tendency to accept invalid conclusions that are believable and to reject valid conclusions that are unbelievable. Consider: "All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly. Therefore some roses fade quickly." Most people accept it because the conclusion is plausible, yet it is invalid: the flowers that fade quickly might all be marigolds. Now consider: "All mammals can fly. Elephants are mammals. Therefore elephants can fly." The conclusion is absurd, but the argument is perfectly valid. Jonathan Evans, Julie Barston and Paul Pollard (1983) showed that belief and logic both influence judgements and interact: belief has its biggest effect on invalid arguments, where believable conclusions are accepted far more often than unbelievable ones.

An older account of syllogistic error is the atmosphere effect, proposed by Robert Woodworth and Saul Sells in 1935. The "mood" of the premises creates an atmosphere that biases the conclusion people accept: if either premise is particular ("some"), people favour a particular conclusion; if either premise is negative ("no"), they favour a negative one. So "Some A are B; some B are C; therefore some A are C" feels right, although it is invalid. Loren and Jean Chapman (1959) offered a rival explanation, illicit conversion: people treat "All A are B" as if it also meant "All B are A".

Exam trap

Belief bias (reasoning: judging an argument by the believability of its conclusion) is easy to confuse with three social biases that share the word: correspondence bias or the fundamental attribution error (explaining others' behaviour by their dispositions), self-serving bias (credit for success, blame situations for failure), and confirmation bias (seeking supporting evidence). Atmosphere effect = Woodworth and Sells (1935).

Key study: The Wason selection task (1966)

The task. The British psychologist Peter Cathcart Wason (1924 to 2003) showed people four cards, each with a letter on one side and a number on the other (Figure 8.4), and a rule: "If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other side." Which cards must you turn over to find out whether the rule is true? Decide before reading on.

The result. Most people choose E alone, or E and 4. Typically fewer than one person in ten gives the correct answer: E and 7. E must be turned because an odd number behind it would break the rule (this is modus ponens). The 7 must be turned because a vowel behind it would break the rule (modus tollens). The 4 is irrelevant: the rule says nothing about what must be behind an even number, so whatever is there cannot falsify the rule (choosing it is affirming the consequent). The conclusion. People look for cards that could confirm the rule, or simply cards that match the items named in it (Jonathan Evans's matching bias), rather than cards that could falsify it.

The story took a twist when the same logical task was given meaningful content. Richard Griggs and James Cox (1982) told participants to imagine they were police officers checking the rule "If a person is drinking beer, then the person must be over 19 years of age". The four cards read "drinking beer", "drinking cola", "16 years of age" and "22 years of age". About three-quarters now chose correctly ("drinking beer" and "16 years"), although the logic was identical to the vowels and numbers. Why? Patricia Cheng and Keith Holyoak (1985) proposed that people reason with pragmatic reasoning schemas, rules of thumb for familiar situations such as permissions and obligations ("if you want to do X, you must satisfy Y"). The evolutionary psychologist Leda Cosmides (1989) went further: she argued that natural selection equipped us with a specialised cheater-detection mechanism for social contracts ("if you take the benefit, you must pay the cost"), and that the task becomes easy whenever it is framed as catching someone who has cheated. Rules about what people must or may do (deontic rules) are reliably easier than rules about what is the case (indicative rules), though researchers still argue about why.

Mental Models

If people do not reason with formal logic, how do they reason? The most influential answer comes from the British psychologist Philip Nicholas Johnson-Laird (born 1936), whose book Mental Models appeared in 1983. A mental model is an internal representation of a possible state of affairs described by the premises, built to resemble the situation rather than the sentences. Given "The cup is to the right of the plate; the spoon is to the left of the plate", you build one model (spoon, plate, cup) and read the conclusion off it: the cup is to the right of the spoon. Reasoning, on this view, has three steps: construct a model of the premises, draw a tentative conclusion from it, and search for an alternative model in which the premises are true but the conclusion false. If you find none, the conclusion is valid. The theory predicts, correctly, that problems are harder the more models they require, and that people often stop after the first model, which produces errors when other models exist. The rival mental logic approach holds that people do have inference rules in their heads, such as modus ponens, but lack others, such as modus tollens, which must be derived in several steps.

Inductive Reasoning and Hypothesis Testing

Most of the reasoning that keeps us alive is inductive: generalising from experience, reasoning from one case to a similar one (analogy), and inferring causes from patterns of events. The Scottish philosopher David Hume pointed out in the eighteenth century that induction can never be logically guaranteed. Europeans observed thousands of swans, every one of them white, and concluded that all swans are white; then, in 1697, Dutch explorers found black swans in Western Australia. Concept formation (Section 8.2) is inductive, and so is everyday judgement under uncertainty (Section 8.5).

Key study: Wason's 2-4-6 task (1960)

The method. Wason told participants that the sequence 2-4-6 fits a rule he had in mind, and asked them to discover the rule by proposing sequences of their own; after each, he said whether it fitted. They were to announce the rule only when sure. The result. Most people formed a hypothesis such as "numbers increasing by two" and tested it with sequences like 8-10-12 and 20-22-24, all of which fitted. Confident, they announced their rule, and were told it was wrong. Only about one in five announced the correct rule the first time. The rule was simply "any three increasing numbers". The conclusion. People test hypotheses by seeking instances that would confirm them, never trying a sequence such as 1-2-3 or 6-4-2 that could have shown their hypothesis to be wrong. Later writers called this confirmation bias; Wason himself, a few years later, spoke of a "verification bias". The criticism. Joshua Klayman and Young-Won Ha (1987) pointed out that testing instances that fit your hypothesis (a positive test strategy) is not irrational in itself; it fails on the 2-4-6 task because the true rule is much broader than the hypothesis people start with.

The term confirmation bias, now firmly attached to Wason's experiments, refers to the tendency to search for, interpret, favour and remember information in ways that confirm what we already believe, and to neglect evidence that could disconfirm it. It is one of the most robust findings in psychology, and it matters wherever people test beliefs: in science, in courtrooms, in diagnosis and in prejudice. A self-fulfilling prophecy (Robert Merton, 1948) is a different thing. Confirmation bias changes how we see the evidence; a self-fulfilling prophecy changes the world, because our expectation alters our behaviour, which in turn makes the expected event more likely (a teacher's low expectations lead to less attention and so to poorer results). The social face of these ideas is covered in the Social Psychology book.

Exam trap

"Which does NOT reflect inductive reasoning: probabilistic conclusions, heuristics, decision making, concept formation?" The key is heuristics. Inductive conclusions are probabilistic, concept formation is induction from instances, and decision making under uncertainty rests on inductive judgements of likelihood; a heuristic is a shortcut strategy rather than a form of inference. The item is debatable (heuristics generate probabilistic judgements), but follow the key. "Confirmation bias is closely related to which heuristic?" One paper keyed representativeness, reasoning that both involve judging by fit to a preconceived model. No standard textbook ties confirmation bias to one particular heuristic; it is a bias in hypothesis testing (Wason's tasks). Use the key if the item recurs. The recognition heuristic (Gerd Gigerenzer's group) means choosing the option you recognise, and is the least related. Finally, "acting in line with one's own expectation so that the expected event becomes more likely" is a self-fulfilling prophecy, not confirmation bias (which distorts only how evidence is read), not the base-rate fallacy (Section 8.5) and not "motivational reaction", which is not a technical term.

Reasoning Across the Lifespan: a Pointer

Reasoning develops. In Jean Piaget's theory (taught fully in the Developmental Psychology book), the concrete operational child of about 7 to 11 years can reason logically about concrete, present objects: she understands conservation, reversibility (mentally undoing an action) and transitive inference (if Asha is taller than Bina and Bina taller than Chitra, Asha is taller than Chitra). From about 11 or 12, in the formal operational stage, the adolescent can reason about the abstract and the possible. Formal operations include hypothetico-deductive reasoning (forming hypotheses and testing them systematically, as in Piaget and Inhelder's pendulum problem), propositional reasoning about statements rather than objects, counterfactual reasoning about states of affairs contrary to fact, and a new capacity for thinking about thinking, which Piaget described as "operations on operations" and which we would now call metacognition.

Some theorists argue that adult thought goes beyond formal operations. What they call dialectical thinking, described by Klaus Riegel (1973) and Michael Basseches (1984), is the ability to hold contradictory perspectives together, to see truth as relative to context and changing over time, and to integrate opposing ideas into a new synthesis. It is the hallmark of postformal thought, not of formal operations.

Exam trap

"Formal operational reasoning involves all EXCEPT: hypothetical, counterfactual, dialectical, metacognitive." The answer is dialectical (postformal; Riegel, Basseches). Hypothetico-deductive reasoning is a capacity that emerges in the formal operational stage; it is not itself one of Piaget's four stages, and a concrete operational child "struggles with" it. Reversibility, transitive inference, conservation and reduced egocentrism are concrete operational achievements. Earlier milestones make tempting distractors: object permanence (knowing that hidden objects still exist) and deferred imitation (copying a model after a delay) belong to the sensorimotor stage (birth to about 2), and symbolic or pretend play to the preoperational stage (about 2 to 7), whose child still lacks conservation, the understanding that quantity stays the same when only appearance changes (water poured into a taller, thinner glass is not "more"). The four stages themselves are sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational; "reversibility of formal operations" is a scrambled option, since reversibility is concrete operational.

8.5 Judgement and Decision MakingMedium yield · 4 Qs

Should you revise attachment theory or the heuristics tonight? Take the auto or wait for the bus? Accept the first seat you are offered in counselling or hold out for a better college? Two related processes are at work in such choices. Judgement is estimating how likely something is, usually from incomplete information ("How likely is it that this topic will be asked?"). Decision making is choosing among options, and it usually depends on judgements. For most of the twentieth century economists assumed that people make both rationally. Psychologists discovered otherwise, and in 2002 one of them won the Nobel Prize in Economics for showing it.

How We Should Decide: Normative Models

It helps to separate three kinds of model. A normative model says how an ideal, fully rational agent should decide, using the rules of logic and probability. A descriptive model says how people actually decide. A prescriptive model offers practical advice for bringing real decisions closer to the normative ideal. Most of this section is about the gap between the first two.

The central normative idea is expected value: multiply each possible outcome by its probability and add the results. A lottery ticket costs ₹100 and gives a 1 in 1,000 chance of winning ₹50,000. Its expected value is ₹50,000 divided by 1,000, which is ₹50, so on average you lose ₹50 on every ticket. In 1738 the Swiss mathematician Daniel Bernoulli pointed out that people value outcomes not by their money value but by their utility (subjective worth), and that utility shows diminishing returns: an extra ₹1,000 means far more to a student than to a millionaire. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern (1944) formalised expected utility theory: a rational decision maker, whose preferences are consistent (if she prefers A to B and B to C, she must prefer A to C), always chooses the option with the highest expected utility.

When options differ on several attributes, a fully rational chooser uses a compensatory strategy: weigh every attribute of every option, so that a weakness on one attribute can be compensated by a strength on another. Choosing a paying-guest room in Mysuru, you would score each room on rent, distance from college, food and safety, multiply by how much each attribute matters to you, and total the scores. Real people, faced with many options, more often use non-compensatory shortcuts. In elimination by aspects, described by Amos Tversky in 1972, you pick the most important attribute, eliminate every option that falls below your cut-off on it ("nothing over ₹8,000 a month"), move to the next attribute, and continue until one option is left. A room that is perfect in every other way is gone the moment it fails the first test.

Bounded Rationality and Satisficing

The first serious challenge to the rational model came from Herbert Simon, the same Simon who, with Newell, modelled problem solving. In a series of papers from 1955 and in his 1957 book Models of Man, Simon argued that real decision makers have bounded rationality: their rationality is limited by the information available to them, by the limited capacity of their minds to process it, and by the time available to decide. A person who tried to compute the expected utility of every possible option before buying a mobile phone would still be comparing specifications when the phone became obsolete.

Instead, said Simon, people practise satisficing, a word he coined in 1956 by blending "satisfy" and "suffice". Satisficing means setting an aspiration level (good enough on the features that matter), examining options one at a time, and choosing the first that meets it. You decide that any phone under ₹20,000 with a good camera and a day's battery will do, and you buy the first one that qualifies. Satisficing does not find the best option, but it finds a good one at a sensible cost. Simon received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978, largely for this work.

The German psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer (born 1947) has developed Simon's idea in a more optimistic direction. Gigerenzer argues that the mind holds an "adaptive toolbox" of fast and frugal heuristics that use little information and yet perform well in the environments they evolved for. In the recognition heuristic, if you recognise one of two options and not the other, you infer that the recognised one scores higher. Daniel Goldstein and Gigerenzer (2002) asked American students which of two German cities was larger. When the students recognised only one city of a pair, they chose it about 90 per cent of the time. Strikingly, the same students did slightly better on German cities, many of which they had barely heard of, than on American cities, where they usually recognised both names and had to rely on what they knew: a "less-is-more" effect. For Gigerenzer, heuristics are not mere sources of error but tools that are ecologically rational.

Exam trap

Bounded rationality and satisficing belong to Herbert Simon (1950s; Nobel 1978), not to Kahneman or Tversky, whose names go with heuristics and biases and prospect theory. Howard Gardner is the multiple-intelligences theorist (Chapter 9). Elimination by aspects is Tversky (1972); the recognition heuristic and fast and frugal heuristics are Gigerenzer.

Heuristics and Biases

In 1969 two Israeli psychologists at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Amos Tversky (1937 to 1996) and Daniel Kahneman (1934 to 2024), began a collaboration that would change the study of judgement. Their method was disarmingly simple: they posed short problems to ordinary people and to experts, and catalogued the systematic ways in which the answers departed from the rules of probability. In a famous 1974 paper in Science, "Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases", they argued that people judge probability using a small number of heuristics. These usually serve us well, but they lead to predictable errors, called biases. Tversky died in 1996; when the Nobel Prize recognised the work in 2002, it went to Kahneman alone, because the prize is not awarded after death.

The availability heuristic (Tversky and Kahneman, 1973) is judging the frequency or probability of an event by how easily examples come to mind. Answer quickly: are there more English words that begin with the letter K, or more words that have K as their third letter? Most people say more begin with K, because words are easy to retrieve by their first letter (kitchen, king, kind) and hard to retrieve by their third (ask, make, like). In fact there are about twice as many with K in third position. Availability usually works because frequent events really are easier to recall, but anything that makes an event memorable distorts it: dramatic, vivid or recent events. After a widely reported train accident, people overestimate the danger of train travel; deaths from plane crashes and terrorism are overestimated, and deaths from diabetes and asthma underestimated. Norbert Schwarz and his colleagues (1991) showed that it is the ease of recall, not the number of examples, that matters: people asked to list twelve occasions on which they had been assertive (a hard task) later rated themselves as less assertive than people asked to list only six.

The representativeness heuristic (Kahneman and Tversky, 1972) is judging the probability that something belongs to a category by how much it resembles the typical member of that category. The trouble is that resemblance ignores other information that matters for probability, above all base rates.

The most important casualty is base-rate neglect: the failure to take account of how common something is in the population. Kahneman and Tversky (1973) described "Jack", a 45-year-old man who is conservative, careful and ambitious, shows no interest in politics and enjoys carpentry and mathematical puzzles. Participants were told that Jack's description had been drawn at random from 100 descriptions, either 70 engineers and 30 lawyers or 30 engineers and 70 lawyers. Both groups judged it about 90 per cent likely that Jack was an engineer: the description sounded like an engineer, and the base rate made almost no difference. Now try the taxi-cab problem yourself. A cab was involved in a hit-and-run at night. In the city, 85 per cent of cabs are Green and 15 per cent are Blue. A witness says the cab was Blue, and tests show that she identifies colours correctly 80 per cent of the time under those conditions. What is the probability that the cab was Blue? Most people answer 80 per cent. Work it through with 100 accidents instead of percentages. Fifteen involve Blue cabs, and the witness correctly calls 12 of them Blue. Eighty-five involve Green cabs, and she wrongly calls 17 of them Blue. So she says "Blue" 29 times, and is right 12 times: the probability is 12 out of 29, about 41 per cent. The low base rate of Blue cabs pulls the answer far below the witness's accuracy.

Key study: Linda the bank teller (Tversky and Kahneman, 1983)

The problem. "Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations." Which is more probable: (a) Linda is a bank teller, or (b) Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement? The result. In one version about 85 per cent of participants chose (b). Why it is wrong. The probability of two things both being true can never exceed the probability of either one alone: every feminist bank teller is a bank teller, so (a) must be at least as likely as (b). Choosing (b) is the conjunction fallacy. The explanation. The description is representative of a feminist, so the conjunction "feels" more probable. The criticism. Some participants may read (a) as "a bank teller who is not a feminist", and the error shrinks when the problem is posed in frequencies ("Of 100 women like Linda, how many..."), as Gigerenzer has emphasised. But the fallacy survives careful rewording.

Representativeness also explains the gambler's fallacy: the belief that after a run of one outcome, the opposite outcome is "due". On 18 August 1913, at the casino in Monte Carlo, the roulette ball fell on black 26 times in a row, and gamblers lost fortunes betting on red in the belief that red had become ever more likely. But a roulette wheel has no memory; each spin is independent. People expect even a short sequence to look like a "representative" random sequence, with reds and blacks well mixed. The same expectation produces insensitivity to sample size. A large hospital delivers about 45 babies a day and a small one about 15. Over a year, which hospital records more days on which over 60 per cent of babies born are boys? Most people say the two are about the same; in fact the small hospital will, because small samples stray further from the population average.

The third heuristic is anchoring and adjustment: estimating a quantity by starting from an initial value (the anchor) and adjusting from it, usually too little. In Tversky and Kahneman's demonstration (1974), a wheel of fortune, apparently random but rigged, stopped at either 10 or 65 in front of participants, who were then asked what percentage of African countries were members of the United Nations. Median estimates were 25 per cent after seeing 10 and 45 per cent after seeing 65: an obviously irrelevant number dragged the estimates. In another study, people given five seconds to estimate 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 gave a median answer of 2,250; those shown 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 × 6 × 7 × 8 gave 512. Both anchored on the first few products and adjusted too little; the true answer is 40,320. The struck-out "MRP" above a sale price in a shop window is an anchor. Framing, Prospect Theory and Loss Aversion

According to expected utility theory, the way a choice is described should not matter if the outcomes are the same. Tversky and Kahneman showed that it matters a great deal. The framing effect is the finding that logically equivalent descriptions of a choice lead to different decisions.

Key study: The Asian disease problem (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981)

The method. Participants were told that the United States was preparing for an unusual disease expected to kill 600 people, and that two programmes had been proposed. One group chose between: Programme A, "200 people will be saved", and Programme B, "a one-third probability that 600 people will be saved and a two-thirds probability that no one will be saved". A second group chose between: Programme C, "400 people will die", and Programme D, "a one-third probability that nobody will die and a two-thirds probability that 600 people will die".

The result. In the first (gain) frame, 72 per cent chose the certain option A. In the second (loss) frame, 78 per cent chose the risky option D. Yet A and C are identical (200 live, 400 die), and so are B and D. The conclusion. When outcomes are framed as gains, people are risk averse; when the same outcomes are framed as losses, people become risk seeking. Rational choice cannot explain this; prospect theory can.

Kahneman and Tversky set out prospect theory in 1979 as a descriptive theory of decisions under risk. It has three key ideas. First, people evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, usually the current state or an expectation, not in terms of final wealth. A salary of ₹50,000 feels like a gain if you expected ₹40,000 and like a loss if you expected ₹60,000. Second, the value function shows diminishing sensitivity in both directions (Figure 8.5): the difference between gaining ₹100 and ₹200 feels larger than the difference between gaining ₹1,100 and ₹1,200. The curve is therefore concave for gains, which makes people risk averse for gains (a sure ₹500 beats a 50 per cent chance of ₹1,000), and convex for losses, which makes them risk seeking for losses (a 50 per cent chance of losing ₹1,000 beats a sure loss of ₹500). Third, the curve is steeper for losses than for gains. This is loss aversion: losing ₹1,000 hurts roughly twice as much as winning ₹1,000 pleases, so most people refuse a fifty-fifty bet unless they stand to win about twice what they might lose. Prospect theory adds that people overweight small probabilities, which helps explain why the same person buys both a lottery ticket and an insurance policy.

Loss aversion explains a family of everyday biases. The endowment effect, named by the economist Richard Thaler in 1980, is the tendency to value something more simply because we own it. In a classic experiment (Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler, 1990), students who were given a university coffee mug demanded roughly twice as much to sell it as other students were willing to pay to buy one. Giving up the mug is coded as a loss, and losses loom large. Status quo bias (William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser, 1988) is the preference for keeping things as they are, because any change involves losing something. Defaults exploit it: in countries where citizens are organ donors unless they opt out, far more people are donors than in countries where they must opt in.

The sunk-cost fallacy is continuing to invest in a course of action because of what has already been spent on it, although past costs cannot be recovered and should not affect a decision about the future. Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer (1985) sold season tickets for a university theatre at full price or at a discount, assigned at random. Those who had paid full price attended more plays in the first half of the season, although the money was equally gone in both cases. Staying in a film you dislike because the ticket cost ₹300, or continuing a failing project "because we have already put two years into it", are the same error, captured by the phrase "throwing good money after bad".

Overconfidence and Hindsight

We are also poor judges of our own knowledge. Overconfidence is the tendency for confidence to exceed accuracy. In calibration studies, people answer general-knowledge questions and rate how sure they are; when they say they are 100 per cent certain, they are wrong far more often than that would allow. A close relative is the planning fallacy (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979): we underestimate how long our own tasks will take, even when we know that similar tasks took longer in the past. (Check your revision timetable.)

Then there is hindsight bias, the "I knew it all along" effect: the tendency, once an outcome is known, to believe that it was more predictable than it really was. Baruch Fischhoff (born 1946) called it "creeping determinism". Before President Richard Nixon's visits to China and the Soviet Union in 1972, Fischhoff and Ruth Beyth (1975) asked people to estimate the probabilities of various possible outcomes. After the visits, the same people were asked to recall their estimates. They remembered having given higher probabilities to the events that had actually happened, and lower ones to those that had not. Hindsight bias makes the past look inevitable, which is why it is so hard to learn from it.

Exam trap

"The tendency to assume we would have been better at predicting actual events than is really true" is the hindsight effect (Fischhoff, 1975). The just-world hypothesis (Melvin Lerner) is the belief that people get what they deserve, a social psychology concept. "Decisiveness" is not a bias. Confirmation bias concerns testing beliefs, not misremembering predictions. And keep the decision terms straight: sunk cost = past investment; endowment = ownership; status quo = preference for no change; all three are driven by loss aversion.

Two Systems of Thought

Try this. A cricket bat and a ball cost ₹1,100 in total. The bat costs ₹1,000 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? The answer that leaps to mind is ₹100, and it is wrong: if the ball cost ₹100, the bat would cost ₹1,100, and together they would cost ₹1,200. The ball costs ₹50. This is an Indian version of the "bat-and-ball" item from Shane Frederick's Cognitive Reflection Test (2005), and it neatly separates two kinds of thinking.

According to dual-process theory, judgement and reasoning involve two different kinds of processing. Keith Stanovich and Richard West (2000) gave them the neutral labels that Kahneman later made famous in Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011). System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless, associative and intuitive; it generates impressions and is the home of the heuristics. System 2 is slow, deliberate, effortful and rule-based; it is limited by working memory and can check, and sometimes override, the answers System 1 proposes. "₹100" is System 1's answer; ₹50 is what System 2 finds if you let it work. Belief bias in syllogisms (Section 8.4) is the same conflict: System 1 judges the believability of the conclusion, System 2 its logic. The "systems" are best thought of as modes of processing, not separate places in the brain.

Feeling plays a larger part in decisions than the rational model admits. Paul Slovic's affect heuristic describes judging risks and benefits by how we feel about something: a technology we like seems low in risk and high in benefit. The neurologist Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis (1994) proposes that bodily signals of emotion, learned from past outcomes, steer choices; patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, who can reason perfectly well about a decision, nonetheless make disastrous real-life choices. (Emotion is the subject of Chapter 11.) Decisions made by groups bring further distortions, such as groupthink (Irving Janis, 1972), in which a cohesive group's desire for agreement overrides realistic appraisal and leads to poor decisions; these are covered in the Social Psychology book.

Clinical link

In the client. Aaron Beck's cognitive distortions are clinical relatives of the biases in this section. Catastrophising is expecting that the worst will happen; all-or-nothing (dichotomous) thinking sees experiences as wholly good or wholly bad; selective abstraction (the mental filter) focuses on negative details and ignores positive ones; personalisation takes events too personally; overgeneralisation draws sweeping conclusions from one event. In the clinician. Clinicians anchor on a first impression, judge by availability after a memorable case, and neglect base rates. If a screening test detects 90 per cent of cases of a disorder that affects 1 per cent of people, and wrongly flags 10 per cent of healthy people, then of 1,000 people screened, 9 true cases and 99 healthy people test positive: only about 8 per cent of positives have the disorder. Loren and Jean Chapman (1967) found that clinicians "saw" correlations between Draw-a-Person signs (such as large eyes) and symptoms (such as suspiciousness) that were not in the data, an illusory correlation. Paul Meehl's Clinical Versus Statistical Prediction (1954) showed that simple actuarial formulas usually predict at least as well as clinical judgement.

8.6 CreativityMedium yield · 5 Qs

What do a new raga improvisation, a vaccine designed in record time and a child's drawing of a purple elephant playing the tabla have in common? Each is creative. Most researchers now define creativity as the ability to produce work that is both novel (original, unexpected) and appropriate (useful, or fitted to the constraints of the task), a definition associated with Robert Sternberg and Todd Lubart. Both halves matter. Novelty without appropriateness is merely odd: a random string of words is original but not creative. Appropriateness without novelty is merely competent.

Creativity can be studied from four directions, which Mel Rhodes (1961) called the four Ps: the creative person (traits and abilities), the creative process (the stages of thought), the creative press (the environment that supports or stifles it) and the creative product (what is made, and how it is judged). Creativity also varies in scale. "Big-C" creativity is eminent, field-changing work (Srinivasa Ramanujan's mathematics, Rabindranath Tagore's poetry); "little-c" creativity is everyday originality (a new recipe, a clever way to rearrange a small room). James Kaufman and Ronald Beghetto (2009) added mini-c, the personally meaningful insights of learning, and Pro-c, professional-level creativity, to make a Four C model.

Guilford and Divergent Thinking

The scientific study of creativity is usually dated to September 1950, when J. P. Guilford gave his presidential address to the American Psychological Association. Guilford complained that creativity had been shamefully neglected: of some 121,000 titles indexed in Psychological Abstracts over the previous 23 years, only 186 bore definitely on creativity, fewer than two in every thousand. In his Structure of Intellect model (Chapter 9), Guilford distinguished convergent production, the generation of the one correct answer, from divergent production, the generation of many varied answers, and identified creativity largely with divergent production. He broke divergent thinking into four abilities, which are still the standard scoring categories of creativity tests:

  • The first is fluency: the sheer number of relevant ideas produced.
  • The second is flexibility: the number of different categories the ideas fall into, that is, the ease of shifting from one kind of idea to another.
  • The third is originality: the statistical unusualness of the ideas, how rarely other people give them.
  • The fourth is elaboration: the amount of detail used to develop and embellish an idea.

Guilford also listed sensitivity to problems (noticing that something needs doing) and redefinition (seeing familiar things in new ways) among the creative abilities. Here is how scoring works. Asked to list uses for a brick in two minutes, Ravi writes: build a wall, build a house, build a temple, a doorstop, a paperweight, a pestle for crushing masala, a marker for the stumps in street cricket. His fluency is 7. The ideas fall into four categories (construction, weight, kitchen tool, sport), so his flexibility is 4. If, in a large group, only Ravi thought of the masala and the stumps, those two earn originality credit. Had he written "a doorstop wrapped in an old sari so it will not scratch the floor", he would earn elaboration credit as well.

The Creative Process: Wallas's Four Stages

The most famous account of the creative process came from Graham Wallas (1858 to 1932), an English political scientist and one of the founders of the London School of Economics. In The Art of Thought (1926), drawing on the self-reports of the physicist Hermann von Helmholtz and the mathematician Henri Poincaré, he proposed four stages (Table 8.8).

The order is preparation, incubation, illumination, verification (a memory hook: "Please Incubate Ideas Very carefully"). Second stage = incubation; third = illumination ("achieving insight"); "leaving the problem while considering other things" = incubation; "testing or carrying out the solution" = verification; "formulating the problem and making initial attempts" = preparation. Some books add intimation between incubation and illumination, which is why you may read of "five stages". Do not confuse the stages with Guilford's abilities (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration, sensitivity) or with Sternberg's analytical, creative and practical intelligences. A sequence such as "problem-finding, brainstorming, selection, implementation" resembles the later applied creative problem-solving schemes that grew out of Osborn's brainstorming work, not Wallas.

Measuring Creativity

The most widely used creativity test is the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT), published by Ellis Paul Torrance (1915 to 2003) in 1966. The TTCT has a verbal form, with activities such as Ask-and-Guess (asking questions about a picture and guessing its causes and consequences), Product Improvement (making a toy more fun to play with), Unusual Uses and Just Suppose (imagining the consequences of an improbable event), and a figural form, with Picture Construction, Picture Completion and Repeated Figures (turning many identical circles or parallel lines into different pictures). Responses are scored on Guilford's dimensions of fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration; later editions of the figural test also score abstractness of titles and resistance to premature closure. Torrance followed some of his child participants for decades, and their test scores predicted later creative achievement modestly but genuinely.

Sarnoff Mednick (1928 to 2015) approached creativity through association. In his associative theory (1962), creativity is the forming of new combinations of remote associative elements that are useful. People differ in their associative hierarchies. An uncreative person has a steep hierarchy: one or two strong, conventional associations to a word ("table" calls up "chair", then little else). A creative person has a flat hierarchy: many weaker associations, including remote ones, are almost equally available. Mednick's Remote Associates Test (RAT) presents three words and asks for a fourth word linked to all three. What links cottage, Swiss and cake? (Cheese.) Cream, skate and water? (Ice.) Note the irony: although the RAT measures remote association, each item has only one correct answer, so it also demands convergent thinking.

Michael Wallach and Nathan Kogan (1965) showed that the conditions of testing matter. When divergent-thinking tasks were presented to children as relaxed, untimed games rather than as tests, the creativity scores hung together and were largely independent of intelligence scores. Instead of a test, some researchers judge products directly. In Teresa Amabile's consensual assessment technique, experts in a domain rate the creativity of products (poems, collages, designs) independently, and their agreement is usually high: creativity, like beauty, is recognised reliably by those who know the field.

Enhancing Creativity: Lateral Thinking and Brainstorming

The Maltese physician and writer Edward de Bono (1933 to 2021) introduced the term lateral thinking in The Use of Lateral Thinking (1967). Ordinary vertical thinking, he said, is logical and sequential: it digs the same hole deeper. Lateral thinking deliberately moves sideways to dig a hole somewhere else, by challenging assumptions, reversing the problem, using a random word as a starting point, or making a deliberately provocative statement (which he marked with the made-up word "po") to jolt thinking out of its usual tracks. In Six Thinking Hats (1985) he proposed that a group take one perspective at a time: white for facts, red for feelings, black for caution, yellow for benefits, green for new ideas and blue for managing the thinking process.

Brainstorming was developed by the advertising executive Alex Faickney Osborn (1888 to 1966) at his agency from 1939 and popularised in his book Applied Imagination (1953). Its four rules are: defer judgement (no criticism during idea generation); go for quantity, because quantity breeds quality; welcome wild ideas; and combine and improve on others' ideas. The rules are sound, but the group format is not. Many studies have compared interacting brainstorming groups with nominal groups, the same number of individuals working alone whose ideas are pooled afterwards. The nominal groups usually produce more ideas, and better ones. Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe (1987) identified the main culprit as production blocking: in a group only one person can speak at a time, so others forget or suppress ideas while they wait. Evaluation apprehension and social loafing add to the loss. Writing ideas down silently first ("brainwriting") or brainstorming electronically recovers much of it.

Creativity and Intelligence

Are creative people simply intelligent people? The usual answer is the threshold hypothesis, associated with Guilford, Torrance and others. Below an IQ of about 120, creativity and intelligence are positively correlated: you need a certain level of intelligence to be creative. Above that threshold the correlation largely disappears, and other factors (personality, motivation, knowledge, environment) decide who is creative. Intelligence, in short, is necessary but not sufficient for creativity.

Jacob Getzels and Philip Jackson (1962) compared adolescents at a Chicago school who scored very high on creativity tests but not on IQ tests with others who scored very high on IQ but not on creativity. Both groups did equally well in school achievement, but teachers preferred the high-IQ students; the high-creative students showed more humour, more unconventional goals and more playful, imaginative stories. A 2005 meta-analysis by Kyung Hee Kim put the overall correlation between creativity and intelligence scores at only about 0.17. Creativity and intelligence overlap, but they are not the same thing (theories of intelligence are the subject of Chapter 9).

Theories of Creativity

Several traditions have offered explanations of creativity. For Freud, creative work is a sublimation of instinctual energy into socially valued activity, and the psychoanalyst Ernst Kris spoke of creativity as "regression in the service of the ego", a controlled dipping into primary-process thought. The humanists saw creativity as an expression of growth: Abraham Maslow distinguished the rare "special-talent" creativity of the genius from the "self-actualising" creativity open to anyone, and Carl Rogers argued that psychological safety and freedom foster it. Mednick's associative theory you have already met. Three modern theories are most examined.

The investment theory of creativity was proposed by Robert Sternberg and Todd Lubart (1991) and developed in their book Defying the Crowd (1995). Creative people, they argued, are like good investors: they "buy low", pursuing ideas that are unknown or out of favour but have growth potential, and they "sell high", moving on once the idea has become accepted and valuable. Doing this requires a confluence of six resources: intellectual abilities (the synthetic ability to see problems in new ways, the analytic ability to judge which ideas are worth pursuing, and the practical ability to persuade others of their value), knowledge (enough to know the field, but not so much that it entrenches you), a legislative thinking style (a preference for creating one's own rules and ways of doing things), personality (tolerance of ambiguity, willingness to take sensible risks, perseverance), intrinsic motivation, and a supportive environment.

Teresa Amabile (born 1950) proposed a componential model (1983) with three components: domain-relevant skills (knowledge and technique in the field), creativity-relevant processes (a cognitive style that breaks set, tolerates ambiguity and persists), and intrinsic task motivation. Her "intrinsic motivation principle" holds that people are most creative when they work for the interest and challenge of the task itself. In one study (1985), creative-writing students who had first ranked extrinsic reasons for writing (money, recognition) wrote less creative poems than those who had first ranked intrinsic reasons (enjoyment, self-expression). Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934 to 2021) proposed a systems model (1988) in which creativity is not inside a person at all but emerges from the interaction of three parts: the individual, who produces a variation; the domain, the body of symbolic knowledge (mathematics, Carnatic music) in which the variation is made; and the field, the gatekeepers (editors, critics, examiners) who decide whether it enters the domain. The Creative Personality

What are highly creative people like? In the 1950s and 1960s, researchers at the Institute of Personality Assessment and Research at Berkeley, including Frank Barron and Donald MacKinnon, invited eminent writers, scientists and architects for intensive weekends of assessment. MacKinnon's (1962) study of architects, judged creative or less creative by their peers, became the classic. The most creative architects were independent in judgement, self-confident and open to their own feelings and to new experience; they preferred complexity (on Barron's art-preference tests they liked complex, asymmetrical figures that less creative people disliked), had wide interests and high aesthetic sensitivity, and showed a notable openness to traits stereotyped as feminine. Later work confirms the picture. In Gregory Feist's (1998) meta-analysis, openness to experience was the personality trait most consistently linked to creativity in both artists and scientists, along with autonomy, drive and confidence; tolerance of ambiguity and willingness to take risks complete the profile.

Clinical link

Is there a link between creativity and mental illness? Nancy Andreasen (1987) interviewed writers at the University of Iowa Writers' Workshop and found far higher lifetime rates of mood disorder, particularly bipolar disorder, than in matched controls, and Kay Redfield Jamison's Touched with Fire (1993) documented the same pattern in the lives of eminent poets and artists. The samples were small and selective, and the link, where it exists, seems strongest for milder mood elevation and for first-degree relatives rather than for severe illness. For a clinician the practical point is the reverse one: many clients fear that treatment will dull their creativity, and this fear needs to be discussed openly.

8.7 The Structure of LanguageLower yield · 3 Qs

You are reading these words without effort, yet you have almost certainly never met this exact sentence before. How is that possible? Language is so familiar that we rarely notice how strange it is. A language is a system of arbitrary symbols (spoken words or manual signs) that are combined according to rules, allowing the members of a community to communicate an unlimited number of messages. Many animals communicate; as you will see in Section 8.9, whether any other animal has language is a much harder question. To answer it we first need to know what makes language language.

The Defining Properties of Language

In 1960 the American linguist Charles Francis Hockett (1916 to 2000) listed thirteen design features that together distinguish human language from animal communication systems. The thirteen are: the vocal-auditory channel, broadcast transmission and directional reception, rapid fading, interchangeability (anyone who can hear a message can also send it), total feedback (speakers hear themselves), specialisation, semanticity, arbitrariness, discreteness, displacement, productivity, cultural (traditional) transmission and duality of patterning. With Stuart Altmann he later added prevarication (the ability to lie), reflexiveness (using language to talk about language) and learnability. Five of the features carry most of the weight, and they are the ones you are examined on.

  • Semanticity. Linguistic symbols carry meaning: they refer to objects, events, actions and ideas.
  • Arbitrariness. There is no natural connection between the form of a word and what it means. The same animal is "dog" in English, kutta in Hindi and nayi in Kannada, and nothing about any of these sounds is doglike. The Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure (1857 to 1913) made this "arbitrariness of the sign" the first principle of modern linguistics (1916). Onomatopoeic words such as "buzz" are the rare exceptions.
  • Displacement. Language allows displacement, communication about things that are not here and now: yesterday's lecture, next month's results, a city you have never visited, a unicorn that never existed.
  • Generativity (Hockett's productivity): a finite set of words and rules can generate an infinite number of new sentences, which listeners understand at once although they have never heard them.
  • Duality of patterning. Language is organised on two levels. Meaningless sounds (phonemes) combine into meaningful units (morphemes and words), and these combine into sentences. The same three sounds /k/, /æ/ and /t/ make "cat", "act" and "tack".

A related property, recursion, allows a phrase to be embedded inside another phrase of the same kind, without limit: "the girl who met the boy who lost the pen that his father bought". Marc Hauser, Noam Chomsky and Tecumseh Fitch (2002) proposed that recursion is the one feature of language that is uniquely human. The same embedding applied to minds gives recursive thought ("I think that you think that I am bluffing"), a form of perspective taking that develops in late childhood and is covered in the Developmental Psychology book. Do not confuse it with Piaget's reversibility (mentally undoing an action, Section 8.4), with Katherine Nelson's referential style of early word learning (Section 8.8), or with reconstruction, the rebuilding of a memory at recall (Chapter 7).

Indian context

The scientific study of language structure has a long Indian history. Around the fourth century BCE (the dates are uncertain), the grammarian Pāṇini composed the Aṣṭādhyāyī, a grammar of Sanskrit in nearly 4,000 compact rules (sūtras) that generate the correct forms of words from roots and affixes. Modern linguists regard it as the earliest generative grammar, a set of finite rules that produces all and only the correct forms. Some nine centuries later, the philosopher Bhartṛhari, in the Vākyapadīya, argued that meaning is grasped as an indivisible whole (sphoṭa) rather than sound by sound, and that "there is no cognition in the world that does not follow language": an Indian position on the language and thought question of Section 8.9.

The Units of Language

Language is a hierarchy of units, each built from the one below (Table 8.10). At the bottom is the phoneme, the smallest unit of sound that can distinguish one word from another in a particular language. The /p/ in "pat" and the /b/ in "bat" are different phonemes because swapping them changes the word. A phoneme has no meaning of its own: /p/ means nothing. English has about 40 to 45 phonemes, depending on the dialect, and the world's languages range from about a dozen to more than a hundred. Languages also differ in which sound differences count. In Hindi the unaspirated /k/ of kal (yesterday or tomorrow) and the aspirated /kh/ of khal (a villain) are different phonemes; in English the same two sounds occur (in "skin" and "kin") but never distinguish words, so English speakers barely hear the difference. The study of a language's sound system is phonology.

Phonemes combine into the morpheme, the smallest unit of language that carries meaning. "Cat" is one morpheme; "cats" is two (cat plus the plural -s); "unhappiness" is three (un + happy + ness). A free morpheme can stand alone as a word (happy, cat, run). A bound morpheme cannot (un-, -ness, -s, -ed). Bound morphemes are either inflectional, adding grammatical information without changing the word's class (the plural -s, the past tense -ed, -ing), or derivational, creating a new word, often of a new class (happy becomes happiness; teach becomes teacher). The study of word structure is morphology. Notice that a syllable (ba-na-na) is a unit of pronunciation organised around a vowel: it is neither the smallest unit of sound nor the smallest unit of meaning.

Words are combined into phrases and sentences according to syntax, the rules of sentence structure. "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" contain the same words; only syntax tells you who needs the injection. English is a subject-verb-object language; Hindi and Kannada put the verb last (subject-object-verb: Ram ne seb khaaya, "Ram the apple ate"). Grammar is the umbrella term for a language's phonological, morphological and syntactic rules.

The study of meaning is semantics: the meaning of words, and how word meanings combine into the meaning of sentences. The study of how language is used in social context is pragmatics: how speakers convey, and listeners infer, more than the literal words say. "Can you pass the salt?" is literally a question about ability, but everyone understands it as a request. The philosopher H. Paul Grice (1975) argued that conversation rests on a cooperative principle expressed in four maxims: give as much information as needed and no more (quantity), say what you believe true (quality), be relevant (relation), and be clear (manner). Finally, prosody is the melody of speech: its rhythm, stress and intonation. "You're coming?" and "You're coming." differ only in prosody. Prosody also carries emotion (the warmth, sarcasm or anger in a voice), and this emotional prosody depends chiefly on regions of the right hemisphere that mirror the left hemisphere's language areas; damage there produces aprosodia, flat or uninterpretable emotional tone (Elliott Ross, 1981).

Chomsky's Transformational Grammar

In 1957 a 28-year-old linguist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Noam Chomsky, published a short book, Syntactic Structures, that changed both linguistics and psychology. He began with a sentence that has since become famous: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously." It is meaningless, yet every English speaker recognises it as grammatical, while "Furiously sleep ideas green colorless" is not. So our knowledge of grammar cannot be knowledge of meaning, and it cannot be knowledge of which words usually follow which, because none of these words normally follow one another. It must be knowledge of abstract rules.

Chomsky proposed a generative grammar: a finite set of rules that can generate all, and only, the grammatical sentences of a language. Phrase-structure rules describe how a sentence breaks into its constituents: a sentence (S) consists of a noun phrase (NP) and a verb phrase (VP); a noun phrase can consist of a determiner and a noun ("the dog"); a verb phrase can consist of a verb and a noun phrase ("chased the cat"). Transformational rules then convert one structure into another, turning an active sentence into a passive one or a statement into a question.

In Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965), Chomsky distinguished two levels of structure. The surface structure is the actual form of the sentence as it is spoken or written: the words in their order. The deep structure is the underlying, abstract structure that represents the basic grammatical relations, who did what to whom. Transformations map deep structure onto surface structure. Two kinds of example show why both levels are needed:

  • One deep structure can appear as different surface structures. "The dog chased the cat" and "The cat was chased by the dog" look different but say the same thing.
  • One surface structure can come from different deep structures. "Visiting relatives can be boring" means either that it is boring to visit relatives or that relatives who visit are boring. "The shooting of the hunters was terrible" is ambiguous in the same way: were the hunters shooting badly, or being shot?

Chomsky also distinguished competence, a speaker's tacit, unconscious knowledge of the rules of the language, from performance, the actual use of language in real situations, which is marred by slips of the tongue, false starts, hesitations and the limits of memory. (The distinction echoes Saussure's earlier contrast between langue, the language system, and parole, actual speech.) Linguistics, for Chomsky, is the study of competence. In later work, Chomsky replaced deep and surface structure with a theory of universal principles and parameters (1981) and then the Minimalist Program (1995), but the 1957 and 1965 ideas are the ones psychology absorbed, and the ones you will be asked about.

Competence is knowledge; performance is use.

8.8 Acquiring LanguageMedium yield · 8 Qs

By the age of five, without a single grammar lesson, a typical child has a vocabulary of thousands of words and commands most of the grammar of her language, including rules that her parents could not state if asked. She does this whether her language is Kannada, Tamil, English or Mandarin, and whether her parents are professors or labourers. How? Three families of theory compete to explain it.

Learning Theory: Skinner's Verbal Behavior

For Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990), language was behaviour like any other, acquired through reinforcement, shaping and imitation. In Verbal Behavior (1957) he classified "verbal operants" by their controlling conditions. A mand is a demand or request reinforced by what it asks for (the child says "milk" and gets milk). A tact names something in the environment and is reinforced socially ("car!", "Yes, that's a car!"). An echoic repeats what has just been heard. An intraverbal responds to someone else's words without repeating them ("How are you?" "Fine."). Textual behaviour is reading aloud, and transcription is writing down what is heard (taking dictation). An autoclitic modifies the speaker's own verbal behaviour ("I think...", "I'm not sure, but..."). Learning theory explains some things well: children learn the particular language, words and accent they hear, and they learn frequent words earlier.

In 1959 Chomsky published a review of Verbal Behavior in the journal Language that is often said to have ended behaviourism's claim on language. His arguments were these. Children constantly produce sentences they have never heard and therefore cannot have imitated or been reinforced for. Their characteristic errors ("I goed", "two mouses") are not imitations of anything adults say; they show that the child has extracted a rule and is over-applying it. Parents rarely correct grammar anyway: Roger Brown and Camille Hanlon (1970) found that parents responded to the truth of what children said, not its form, approving "Mama isn't boy, he a girl" because it was true. And children everywhere acquire language too fast and too uniformly for trial-and-error learning to explain.

The Nativist Theory: Chomsky's Language Acquisition Device

Chomsky's alternative was that children are born with an innate, biologically based capacity for language, which he called the language acquisition device (LAD). The LAD contains a universal grammar: a set of principles and categories common to all human languages (every language has nouns and verbs, consonants and vowels, ways of negating and of asking questions). Exposure to a particular language does not teach the child grammar from scratch; it sets the values of a small number of parameters, like switches. English puts the verb before its object; Hindi and Kannada put it after; a child hearing either language needs only to set the switch.

The central argument for nativism is the poverty of the stimulus: the speech children hear is fragmentary, full of errors and false starts, and contains almost no information about which sentences are not allowed, yet every normal child arrives at the same intricate grammar. The input, Chomsky argued, is too poor to explain the output, so much of the knowledge must be innate. Further evidence came from the regular, universal sequence of milestones (Table 8.12), from deaf children who, brought together without an adult model, create a full sign language of their own (as happened in Nicaragua from the late 1970s), and from the critical period discussed below. Steven Pinker (born 1954) popularised the nativist case in The Language Instinct (1994).

Critics reply that the LAD is a label rather than an explanation, that nativism ignores the rich social interaction in which language is learned, and that the input is richer than Chomsky supposed. Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin and Elissa Newport (1996) played eight-month-old infants a continuous stream of nonsense syllables for just two minutes. The infants learned which syllables tended to follow one another, and so where the "word" boundaries lay: powerful statistical learning that needs no innate grammar.

Interactionist Theories

Interactionist theories accept that children bring special capacities to the task but insist that social interaction does essential work. Jerome Bruner, in Child's Talk (1983), answered Chomsky's LAD with a language acquisition support system (LASS): the structured help that adults provide. Bruner emphasised formats, routine, predictable games such as peekaboo and picture-book reading ("What's that?" "A cow!" "Yes, it's a cow!"), within which the child can work out what words refer to. The adult's support is a form of scaffolding, a term introduced by David Wood, Bruner and Gail Ross in 1976 for help that is adjusted to the child's level and withdrawn as competence grows.

Part of the support system is the special way adults talk to infants, known as motherese, baby talk or, more neutrally, child-directed speech (infant-directed speech). Compared with speech between adults, it has a higher overall pitch and a wider, exaggerated range of pitch (a sing-song melody), a slower rate, shorter and simpler utterances (a lower mean length of utterance), more repetition, more questions, clearer pronunciation, more frequent and longer pauses, and a focus on the here and now. Infants prefer to listen to it, and its exaggerated contours help them pick out words and phrase boundaries. But it is not strictly necessary: in some cultures adults talk relatively little to infants, and their children still learn to speak.

The developmental psycholinguist Dan Isaac Slobin (born 1939), comparing acquisition across dozens of languages, proposed that children bring a language-making capacity (LMC) to the task (1985). Unlike the LAD, the LMC is not innate knowledge of grammar. It is a set of cognitive and perceptual abilities, which Slobin called operating principles, that are highly specialised for learning language: for example, "pay attention to the ends of words" (where many languages put their grammatical endings) and "avoid exceptions" (which produces overregularisation). Is There a Critical Period for Language?

In Biological Foundations of Language (1967), Eric Heinz Lenneberg (1921 to 1975) argued that language must be acquired during a critical period, a biologically fixed window of development (roughly from the age of two to puberty, in his account) during which the brain is especially receptive to linguistic input and after which full acquisition becomes impossible. He linked the end of the window to the completion of brain maturation and lateralisation. Today most researchers prefer the softer term sensitive period: a window during which learning is easiest and most complete, although some learning is still possible afterwards.

The most famous, and saddest, evidence came from a girl known as Genie. In November 1970, social workers in Los Angeles found a 13-year-old girl who had been kept by her father in near-total isolation, strapped to a potty chair or confined in a crib, from about the age of 20 months. She had heard almost no language and could not speak. Over the following years, studied in detail by the linguist Susan Curtiss (1977), Genie learned many words and could communicate, but her grammar never developed. Her utterances remained telegraphic ("Applesauce buy store"), with little use of function words or word-order rules. The case supports a sensitive period for grammar in particular, but it is not clean: Genie was also severely abused and malnourished, and nobody knows whether she was developmentally normal to begin with.

Key study: Johnson and Newport's immigrants (1989)

The question. Does the age at which you start learning a second language limit how well you finally learn it? The method. Jacqueline Johnson and Elissa Newport tested Korean and Chinese immigrants to the United States who had all lived there for years, but who had arrived at ages ranging from 3 to 39. Participants judged whether each of 276 spoken English sentences was grammatical. The result. Those who had arrived between 3 and 7 performed like native speakers. Performance fell steadily with each later age of arrival up to puberty; those who arrived as adults scored lower and varied widely, unrelated to their age at arrival. The conclusion. There is a maturational sensitive period for acquiring grammar that closes gradually through childhood. The criticism. Age of arrival is confounded with amount and kind of exposure, schooling and motivation.

Exam trap

"Infants who have no exposure to language show severely limited language development that is not reversible" supports sensitive (critical) periods of development. Semantics and syntax are levels of language, not developmental principles; nativism is the broader theory that language is innate, and the item asks for the term the evidence supports most directly. Critical period for language = Lenneberg (1967); Genie studied by Susan Curtiss.

Milestones of Language Development

Language begins before the first word. Newborns already prefer their mother's voice to a stranger's (Anthony DeCasper and William Fifer, 1980), having heard it in the womb. Young infants can discriminate the speech sounds of all the world's languages, but their perception narrows to their own language during the first year. Janet Werker and Richard Tees (1984) found that English-learning infants of 6 to 8 months could distinguish two Hindi consonants (a retroflex "ta" and a dental "ta") that English does not use, but by 10 to 12 months most could not; Hindi-learning infants kept the distinction. Infants become specialists in the sounds that matter around them.

Production follows its own timetable (Table 8.12). At about two months infants begin cooing, producing vowel-like sounds ("ooo", "aah"), often in response to a smiling face. At about six months babbling begins: consonant-vowel combinations, soon repeated in strings ("ba-ba-ba", "da-da-da"). Babbling is at first universal, and early babbling sounds similar whatever language the child hears; but it gradually drifts towards the sounds of the home language. Deaf infants' vocal babbling is delayed and reduced, and deaf infants exposed to sign language "babble" with their hands, producing rhythmic, syllable-like hand movements (Laura-Ann Petitto and Paula Marentette, 1991). Babbling, it seems, is the practice of language's sound units in whatever medium the child meets.

The first recognisable words appear around the first birthday, often for people, animals, food and toys that move or can be acted on. In the one-word stage a single word can do the work of a whole sentence: "Milk!" may mean "I want milk", "The milk spilled" or "That is milk". Such a word is called a holophrase. Katherine Nelson (1973) noticed two styles among children at this stage: referential children's first fifty words were mostly names for objects, while expressive children used more social formulas ("stop it", "thank you", "I want it"). Vocabulary grows slowly at first and then, around 18 months and about 50 words, accelerates sharply: the vocabulary spurt or naming explosion.

Early word meanings rarely match adult ones. In overextension a child applies a word too broadly: "dog" for every four-legged animal, "papa" for every man. In underextension the child applies it too narrowly: "dog" only for the family dog, "car" only for cars seen moving in the street. And children learn words astonishingly fast. Susan Carey and Elsa Bartlett (1978) casually asked preschoolers to "bring me the chromium tray, not the red one", where the tray was olive green. A week later, some of the children still had a partial idea of what "chromium" meant, after a single exposure. This ability to form a quick first hypothesis about a word's meaning from one encounter is called fast mapping.

Between 18 and 24 months children begin to combine two words. Roger Brown (1925 to 1997) called this telegraphic speech, because, like a telegram, it keeps the content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) and leaves out function words and grammatical endings: "Mummy go", "more milk", "allgone ball", "Daddy shoe". These short utterances already express a small set of meaning relations: agent and action, action and object, possession, location, recurrence and disappearance. Brown, in A First Language (1973), followed three children (Adam, Eve and Sarah) and measured progress by the mean length of utterance (MLU) in morphemes, which predicts grammatical development better than age. Grammatical morphemes then appear in a remarkably regular order, beginning with -ing and the plural -s.

From about two to three years, children's grammar shows its most revealing error. Having learned rules such as "add -ed for the past" and "add -s for the plural", children apply them to irregular words they had previously used correctly: "I goed", "two foots", "the mouses". This overregularisation means that performance gets worse before it gets better (a U-shaped pattern), and it is strong evidence that children are learning rules, not imitating. Jean Berko (1958) showed the same thing with her "wug test". She showed children a picture of a small imaginary creature: "This is a wug. Now there is another one. There are two of them. There are two ...?" Preschoolers happily answered "wugs", applying a rule to a word they had never heard. Between three and five, sentences lengthen and become complex, with questions, negatives and embedded clauses; pragmatic skills grow too (four-year-olds simplify their speech when talking to two-year-olds), and in the school years children develop metalinguistic awareness, the ability to think about language itself, which shows up in their delight in puns and riddles.

Clinical link

Language delay is often the first thing that brings a child to a clinic. Widely used red flags include no babbling or pointing by 12 months, no single words by 16 months, no spontaneous two-word phrases by 24 months, and any loss of language or social skills at any age. Hearing should always be checked first. Of the neurodevelopmental conditions, language and communication are most consistently compromised in autism spectrum disorder, where delayed speech is often accompanied by echolalia, pronoun reversal ("you want juice" meaning "I want juice") and, even in fluent speakers, impaired pragmatics. Developmental language disorder, by contrast, affects language with relatively intact social communication. In attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder language difficulties are common but are not a defining feature; specific learning disability is defined by difficulty with academic skills (reading, writing, arithmetic) rather than by delayed speech; and childhood dissociative conditions do not characteristically arrest language development. Does Language Shape Thought? The Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis

Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897 to 1941) was a fire-prevention inspector for an insurance company in Connecticut and, in his spare time, a student of linguistics under the anthropologist-linguist Edward Sapir (1884 to 1939). In his work he noticed that workers who were careful around drums labelled "gasoline" smoked carelessly near drums labelled "empty", although the "empty" drums were full of explosive vapour. The word, it seemed, had shaped the behaviour. Whorf went on to argue, from his study of the Hopi language of Arizona, that the Hopi conceive of time quite differently from speakers of European languages because their grammar treats it differently. His essays, collected after his death as Language, Thought, and Reality (1956), gave his name and Sapir's to the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis.

The hypothesis comes in two strengths. The strong version, linguistic determinism, holds that language determines thought: we can think only in the categories our language provides, and speakers of very different languages live in different mental worlds. The weak version, linguistic relativity, holds that language influences thought: the habits of a language make some distinctions easier to notice, remember or express, without making others unthinkable.

The strong version has not survived. Later fieldwork found that Hopi has plenty of ways of talking about time. The often-repeated claim that "Eskimo" languages have dozens or hundreds of words for snow turned out to be an exaggeration that grew with each retelling. And the most systematic test, on colour, pointed the other way. Brent Berlin and Paul Kay (1969), in Basic Color Terms, examined 98 languages and found that they have between two and eleven basic colour terms, added in a nearly universal order: a language with only two terms has words for dark and light (black and white); a language with three adds red; then come green and yellow; then blue; then brown; and finally purple, pink, orange and grey. Colour vocabularies, in other words, are constrained by universal features of perception. Eleanor Rosch, publishing as Eleanor Rosch Heider (1972), then studied the Dani of New Guinea, whose language has only two basic colour terms (mili for dark, cool colours and mola for light, warm ones). The Dani remembered the "focal" colours (the best red, the best green) better than non-focal ones, and learned names for them more easily, just as English speakers do. Lacking a word for a colour did not stop them perceiving and remembering it.

The weak version, however, has strong support. Russian has two basic words for what English calls blue: goluboy (light blue) and siniy (dark blue). Jonathan Winawer and his colleagues (2007) found that Russian speakers discriminated two blues faster when the shades fell on opposite sides of the goluboy-siniy boundary than when they fell on the same side; English speakers showed no such effect. Speakers of languages that describe space in absolute terms (north, south) rather than relative ones (left, right) keep track of compass directions with remarkable accuracy. You can see the same thing in kinship terms. A Hindi speaker must choose between chacha (father's younger brother), tau (father's elder brother), mama (mother's brother), mausa and phupha, where English offers only "uncle", so Hindi speakers habitually attend to which side of the family a relative belongs to. English speakers can understand the distinction perfectly well; they simply do not have to make it every time they speak. That is linguistic relativity in its defensible, weak form. Steven Pinker (1994) argues against even this much emphasis on language: we think, he says, in a language-independent "mentalese", which is why translation is possible and why babies and animals can think without words.

Exam trap

The linguistic relativity hypothesis is associated with Benjamin Lee Whorf (and his teacher Edward Sapir). Chomsky (universal grammar), Piaget (thought before language) and Pinker (mentalese, The Language Instinct) are all wrong answers, and Pinker is a critic. Strong version = determinism; weak version = relativity (the one with support). Berlin and Kay's colour universals and Heider's Dani study count against the strong version.

Piaget Versus Vygotsky

Recall Watson's claim from Section 8.1 that thinking is merely subvocal speech. It was put to a dramatic test in 1947, when the anaesthetist Scott Smith had himself injected with curare, the drug that paralyses all the skeletal muscles, including those of the larynx and tongue (his breathing was maintained artificially). Afterwards he reported that he had remained fully conscious throughout, had understood what was said around him and had been able to think clearly. Thinking does not require movements of the speech muscles. But that does not settle how language and thought are related, and on this the two great developmental theorists disagreed.

For Jean Piaget (1896 to 1980), thought comes first. Children build concepts through action on the world, and language is a way of expressing understanding that is already there; a child says "allgone" only after understanding that objects persist and can disappear. In The Language and Thought of the Child (1923) Piaget described egocentric speech: speech that is not adapted to a listener, such as the "collective monologue" of a group of preschoolers, each talking about his own activity without responding to the others. For Piaget, egocentric speech reflected the egocentrism of young children's thought, a stage between Bleuler's autistic thought and socialised, directed thought, and it simply faded away, around the age of seven, as thought became socialised.

Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (1896 to 1934), the Russian founder of sociocultural theory, read Piaget closely and disagreed. In Thinking and Speech (1934; translated as Thought and Language in 1962), he argued that thought and speech have different genetic roots. There is pre-linguistic thought, the practical intelligence of infants and of Köhler's chimpanzees; and there is pre-intellectual speech, the crying, babbling and first words of the infant, used socially and emotionally but not yet as a tool of thought. At around two years of age the two lines meet: the child, in the words Vygotsky quoted, "discovers that each thing has its name", asks constantly for names, and from then on thought becomes verbal and speech becomes rational.

Speech, for Vygotsky, begins as social communication with others. Children then turn it on themselves as private speech (Piaget's egocentric speech) to plan and guide their own actions, and it gradually "goes underground" to become inner speech, silent verbal thought that is abbreviated, condensed and dense with personal meaning. So private speech does not simply disappear: it is internalised. Vygotsky supported this with an ingenious experiment. When he introduced obstacles into children's activities (for example, taking away the coloured pencil a child needed for a drawing), the amount of egocentric speech nearly doubled, because the children used it to work out what to do. Later research, notably by Laura Berk, confirms that children use more private speech on harder tasks and that those who do so tend to perform better. Vygotsky summarised development in four stages of speech and thought: a primitive or natural stage of pre-intellectual speech and pre-verbal thought; a naive psychology stage in which the child uses grammatical forms such as "because" before grasping the logical relations they express; an external sign stage of egocentric speech and external aids such as counting on fingers; and an ingrowth stage in which these operations are internalised as inner speech and mental calculation.

Vygotsky also studied how true concepts develop. With his colleague Leonid Sakharov he gave children blocks of different colours, shapes, heights and sizes, each marked underneath with one of four nonsense words (lag, bik, mur, cev) that stood for combinations of height and size. Young children grouped the blocks into unorganised heaps; older children formed complexes, groupings based on shifting, concrete links, including pseudoconcepts that look like real concepts but rest on visible similarity; only in adolescence do true concepts appear, based on abstract, systematic features. Vygotsky linked this achievement to the new tasks that society sets adolescents, to their participation in social institutions such as school and work, and to the growth of self-reflection, the ability to step back and reflect on one's acting self. He distinguished everyday (spontaneous) concepts, picked up in daily life, from scientific concepts, learned systematically through instruction; the two meet and enrich each other in the zone of proximal development, the gap between what a learner can do alone and what she can do with guidance (Developmental Psychology book). The standard contrast: Vygotsky argued that thought depends on language; Piaget argued that language depends on thought. Private speech, for Vygotsky, guides and regulates behaviour and is internalised as inner speech; it is not a regression to autistic thinking, mere imitation, a sign of disorder, or egocentrism that fades only with schooling. In Vygotsky's account of adolescent conceptual thinking, societal tasks, social institutions and self-reflection all matter; gender differences play no part. One recent paper asked whether a person's language and thought involve simultaneous, independent, shared or alternate processes, with "simultaneous" as the expected answer: in mature verbal thought, language and thought run together and interdependently. "Independent" describes only their separate early roots, and "shared" is the most defensible alternative if the key is disputed.

Bilingualism

Most people in the world, and most people in India, speak more than one language. Bilingualism is the ability to use two languages; a person fluent in many languages is a polyglot or multilingual (a linguist, by contrast, studies language scientifically, whether or not she speaks many, and an orator is simply a skilled public speaker). In simultaneous bilingualism, a child learns two languages from birth, as two first languages; in sequential bilingualism, a second language is added after the first is established. Uriel Weinreich (1953) distinguished compound bilinguals, who learned both languages in the same context and attach two labels to a single set of meanings, from coordinate bilinguals, who learned them in separate contexts and have partly separate meaning systems (and subordinate bilinguals, who reach the second language through the first). Wallace Lambert distinguished additive bilingualism, in which a second language is added while the first remains valued and maintained, from subtractive bilingualism, in which the second language gradually replaces the first, as often happens to minority languages.

Early studies claimed that bilingualism harmed children's intelligence, but they compared poor immigrant children with middle-class monolinguals and tested them in their weaker language. When Elizabeth Peal and Wallace Lambert (1962) compared balanced French-English bilingual children in Montreal with monolinguals matched for social class, the bilinguals did better on both verbal and non-verbal tests. Since then Ellen Bialystok and others have reported that bilinguals show greater metalinguistic awareness and, in some studies, better executive control, presumably from constantly managing two languages; some studies even report a later onset of dementia symptoms. These advantages are now debated, and several large studies find small or no executive benefits. The costs are better established and modest: bilingual children reach the main milestones of language development on the usual timetable, but bilinguals tend to have a smaller vocabulary in each language and to retrieve words slightly more slowly. Code-switching, moving between languages within a conversation ("Kal exam hai, so I'm going to sleep early"), follows grammatical rules and is a sign of competence, not confusion.

Indian context

India is one of the most multilingual societies on earth, with 22 languages listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution and hundreds of mother tongues. Multilingualism is the ordinary condition: a Mysuru student may speak Kannada at home, English in class, Hindi with friends from the north and some Tamil or Telugu with neighbours. The three-language formula, adopted in the National Policy on Education of 1968, asks schools to teach the regional language, Hindi and English (with another modern Indian language in Hindi-speaking states). For a clinician, this means assessing and treating clients in their dominant language whenever possible, since emotional memories are often encoded in the language in which they were lived.

Can Animals Learn Language?

Animals certainly communicate. The Austrian zoologist Karl von Frisch, who shared a Nobel Prize in 1973, showed that a honeybee's "waggle dance" tells its hivemates the direction and distance of a food source: a genuine case of displacement, but the system is fixed, cannot express anything new, and is not learned. Vervet monkeys give different alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, and other vervets respond appropriately (running up trees, looking up, or standing up and scanning the ground) even to recordings of the calls (Robert Seyfarth, Dorothy Cheney and Peter Marler, 1980): semanticity, but no combination of calls into new messages.

Could an ape do better if taught? Early attempts to teach chimpanzees to speak failed; Vicki, raised as a child by Keith and Catherine Hayes in the late 1940s, learned to say only about four words (mama, papa, cup and up), because the ape vocal tract cannot produce human speech. So researchers turned to other media. From 1966 Beatrix Gardner and Allen Gardner taught the chimpanzee Washoe American Sign Language; she learned about 130 signs and combined them, famously signing "water bird" on seeing a swan. David Premack taught a chimpanzee called Sarah to use plastic tokens on a magnetic board, and Duane Rumbaugh taught Lana to press symbols (lexigrams) on a computer keyboard.

Then came the sceptics. Herbert Terrace raised a chimpanzee with the pointed name Nim Chimpsky and taught him sign language. When Terrace and his colleagues analysed hours of videotape (1979), they found that most of Nim's signs were prompted by his teachers or were partial imitations of signs the teacher had just made; his utterances did not grow longer in a rule-governed way, and his longest, sixteen signs long, was "Give orange me give eat orange me eat orange give me eat orange give me you". Terrace's answer to the question in the title of his paper, "Can an ape create a sentence?", was no. The most impressive evidence came later from Sue Savage-Rumbaugh's bonobo Kanzi, who learned lexigrams spontaneously as an infant by watching researchers try to train his adoptive mother, and who could carry out novel spoken requests ("put the soap on the apple") at a level roughly comparable to a child of two to two and a half. The current verdict is that apes can learn symbols with meaning (semanticity, arbitrariness, some displacement), but show little productive grammar and no clear recursion. On Hockett's list, generativity remains the human signature.

Speech Perception

Listen to someone speaking a language you do not know and you will notice something important: you cannot tell where one word ends and the next begins. The speech signal is continuous; the gaps we "hear" between words in our own language are mostly supplied by our knowledge (the segmentation problem). Worse, each phoneme is pronounced differently depending on the sounds around it (coarticulation), so there is no fixed acoustic pattern for, say, /d/. How do we perceive speech at all?

Part of the answer is categorical perception: the tendency to perceive a continuously varying stimulus as falling into discrete categories, with sharp boundaries between them and poor discrimination within them. Alvin Liberman (1917 to 2000) and his colleagues at the Haskins Laboratories (1957) created synthetic syllables that changed in equal acoustic steps from one consonant to another. Consider the difference between /ba/ and /pa/. Both are made by opening the lips; they differ in voice onset time (VOT), the delay between the release of the lips and the start of vocal-cord vibration, which is short for /ba/ and longer for /pa/. When VOT is varied smoothly, listeners do not hear a gradual change. They hear /ba/, /ba/, /ba/, and then, at a boundary of about 25 milliseconds in English, an abrupt switch to /pa/ (Figure 8.6). Two sounds 20 milliseconds apart are easy to tell apart if they straddle the boundary and almost impossible if they fall on the same side. Peter Eimas and his colleagues (1971) found the same boundary in infants only one and four months old, which at first suggested a special inborn mechanism for speech; but chinchillas show it too, so it may reflect general properties of mammalian hearing that languages have exploited.

Speech perception also uses the eyes. In the McGurk effect, described by Harry McGurk and John MacDonald (1976), the sound of a person saying "ba" is dubbed onto a video of the same person's lips saying "ga". Viewers hear "da", a compromise between what they hear and what they see; when they close their eyes, they hear "ba" again. The illusion persists even when you know exactly how it works, which shows how automatically sight and sound are combined. Knowledge and context fill in gaps too. In the phonemic restoration effect, Richard Warren (1970) replaced the first /s/ in "legislatures" in a recorded sentence with a cough. Listeners "heard" the missing /s/ and could not say where the cough had occurred. Remarkably, a word heard later in the sentence can determine which sound is restored: "the *eel was on the axle" is heard as "wheel", "the *eel was on the orange" as "peel". Speech perception is as much top-down as bottom-up, a point you met for vision in Chapter 4.

Reading and Aphasia: Pointers

Reading is a recent cultural invention, a few thousand years old, built on top of spoken language. Before children can learn to read in an alphabetic script, they must already have spoken vocabulary and grammar, and above all phonological awareness: the knowledge that spoken words are made of smaller sounds (syllables, rhymes and phonemes) that can be manipulated. Phonological awareness in the preschool years is one of the best predictors of later reading. So the cognitive skill that learning to read builds on most directly is spoken language, not theory of mind (the understanding that other people have beliefs and desires of their own), imagination, or the general capacity for mental representation. In the alphasyllabic scripts of India, such as Kannada and Devanagari, the basic written unit is the akshara (roughly a consonant with its vowel), and awareness of syllables and aksharas plays the same role. Skilled reading, according to Max Coltheart's dual-route model, uses two routes: a lexical route that recognises familiar words as wholes (essential for irregular words such as "yacht" and "colonel") and a non-lexical route that converts letters to sounds by rule (essential for new words and nonwords such as "blint"). Damage to the lexical route produces surface dyslexia (irregular words are "regularised", so "pint" is read to rhyme with "mint"); damage to the non-lexical route produces phonological dyslexia (familiar words can be read, but nonwords cannot). Developmental dyslexia and specific learning disorders are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

Finally, language can be lost. An acquired disturbance of language caused by brain damage, typically to the left hemisphere after a stroke, is called aphasia. It is a disorder of language specifically, not of intelligence, memory, abstract thinking or the muscles of speech (weakness of the speech muscles is dysarthria). In 1861 the French physician Paul Broca (1824 to 1880) described a patient, Louis Victor Leborgne, nicknamed "Tan" because it was almost the only syllable he could say; at autopsy Broca found damage in the left inferior frontal gyrus, now called Broca's area. In 1874 the German neurologist Carl Wernicke (1848 to 1905) described a different syndrome after damage to the posterior part of the left superior temporal gyrus, now Wernicke's area. The two classic aphasias, and the brain's language network, are treated fully in the Physiological Psychology book; the essentials are in the box below.

Clinical link

Broca's aphasia (expressive, non-fluent): effortful, halting, agrammatic speech much like telegraphic speech, with relatively preserved comprehension and impaired repetition; patients are usually aware of their difficulty and frustrated by it. Broca's area is involved in speech production. Wernicke's aphasia (receptive, fluent): fluent, well-articulated speech with normal prosody but empty of meaning, full of word substitutions (paraphasias) and invented words (neologisms), with poor comprehension and repetition; patients are often unaware of their errors. Conduction aphasia: fluent speech and good comprehension but strikingly poor repetition, after damage to the arcuate fasciculus linking the two areas. Global aphasia affects everything. Damage to the corresponding right-hemisphere regions spares grammar but disturbs emotional prosody, humour, metaphor and the flow of conversation. Three neighbours are not aphasias of speech: damage to the left angular gyrus disturbs reading and writing (alexia with agraphia); pure alexia (alexia without agraphia), in which the patient can write but cannot read, follows left occipital damage; and inability to recognise faces is prosopagnosia, a disorder of visual recognition.

The case of Sultan and the two sticks, revisited

Go back to Tenerife, to the moment before Sultan jumped up. You can now describe what happened in several vocabularies. In Newell and Simon's terms, the banana problem was well defined (a clear initial state, a clear goal), but Sultan's problem space lacked the one operator that would solve it: joining two sticks into one. Hill climbing could never have found it. Pushing one stick out with the other brought a tip closer to the banana without bringing the banana any closer to Sultan, a local maximum from which no small step led further. In Gestalt terms he solved the problem by insight: the two short sticks were suddenly seen as parts of one long one, and the solution appeared all at once, was performed smoothly and was kept, exactly the marks of insight that Köhler listed. His idle play with the sticks even resembles Wallas's incubation followed by illumination, and Metcalfe and Wiebe's warmth ratings suggest that, had he been able to tell us, he would have felt no nearer the answer a moment before he found it. Herbert Birch's work adds the caution: Sultan had handled sticks before, and insight grows out of experience rather than replacing it.

Was he thinking without words? Everything in this chapter says yes. Of the five elements of thought he plainly had images and concepts (a concept of "stick" flexible enough to include a stick made of two sticks), though not conventional symbols, propositions or language. The curare experiment showed that human thought does not need the muscles of speech, and Vygotsky placed Köhler's chimpanzees at the very start of his account of thought and language, as the clearest case of pre-linguistic thought: practical intelligence that has not yet met speech. What Sultan lacked is what the last three sections of the chapter describe, a generative symbol system that can refer to things not present. Washoe, Nim and Kanzi showed that apes can learn symbols that mean something, but not a grammar that produces endless new sentences. In children, Vygotsky argued, the two lines meet at about the age of two, and from then on thought becomes verbal: we can reason about what is absent, test hypotheses we have never acted out, and tell someone else how we solved a problem. Sultan could do the first thing this chapter describes. He could not do the last: he had no way to tell another chimpanzee how he had done it.

Chapter summary

8.1 What Is Thinking? Thinking is the mental manipulation of internal representations to form new ones, the part of cognition that transforms what perception and memory supply. The Würzburg school of Oswald Külpe reported imageless thought, against Titchener's view that every thought contains images; Watson (1913) reduced thinking to subvocal speech; and the cognitive revolution (Chomsky, Newell and Simon, Bruner, and Neisser's Cognitive Psychology of 1967) brought thought back with objective measures. The elements of thought are images, concepts, symbols and signs, propositions and language. Shepard and Metzler (1971) found that the time to compare rotated objects rises linearly with the angle (mental rotation), and Kosslyn's island-scanning studies suggested depictive images; Pylyshyn's propositional theory (1973) explained such results by tacit knowledge, but imagery's use of visual cortex and the imaginal neglect of Bisiach and Luzzatti's patients favour depictive images. Paivio's dual-coding theory (1971) explains why pictures and concrete words are remembered best. Thinking may be directed or undirected, autistic or dereistic (Bleuler, 1911), convergent or divergent (Guilford), critical, creative, reflective (Dewey, 1910), and productive or reproductive (Wertheimer, 1945).

8.2 Concepts. A concept is a mental representation of a category; concepts give cognitive economy and support inference. Concept formation is described as observation, discrimination, generalisation and abstraction, often followed by naming; Hull (1920) treated it as gradual associative abstraction. The classical view defines a concept by necessary and sufficient features and fits artificial concepts, which may be conjunctive (this and that), disjunctive (this or that) or relational. Bruner, Goodnow and Austin (1956) showed that people attain artificial concepts by testing hypotheses, with conservative focusing the most efficient strategy, alongside focus gambling and simultaneous and successive scanning. Natural concepts, as Eleanor Rosch showed, have typicality gradients, fuzzy boundaries and Wittgenstein's family resemblance; prototype theory represents them by an abstract, averaged best example, and the basic level (chair, dog) is learned and named first. Exemplar theory (Medin and Schaffer, 1978) stores many instances instead. Concepts are linked in semantic networks (Collins and Quillian, 1969; spreading activation, Collins and Loftus, 1975) or, in connectionism (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986), distributed across many interconnected units.

8.3 Problem Solving. A problem is a gap between an initial state and a goal state; problems may be well defined or ill defined. Newell and Simon (General Problem Solver; Human Problem Solving, 1972) described solving as search through a problem space of states and operators. An algorithm guarantees a solution; a heuristic is an efficient rule of thumb that does not. The main heuristics are means-end analysis (reducing differences by subgoals), working backward, hill climbing (which fails at a local maximum, as in the hobbits and orcs problem) and analogy, which people use readily when told to but rarely notice spontaneously (Gick and Holyoak, 1980); trial and error is the most primitive strategy (Thorndike, 1898). Gestalt psychologists stressed insight, a sudden restructuring of the problem (Köhler's apes, Maier's nine-dot and two-string problems), and Metcalfe and Wiebe's warmth ratings (1987) confirmed that insight solutions arrive suddenly; incubation helps, probably by letting a misleading approach be forgotten. The obstacles are functional fixedness (Duncker's candle problem, 1945), mental set or Einstellung (Luchins's water jars, 1942), confirmation bias and irrelevant information. Stage models run from Dewey and Pólya to Sternberg's problem-solving cycle, and experts excel because they store meaningful chunks and represent problems by deep principles (Chase and Simon, 1973; Chi, 1981).

8.4 Reasoning. Deductive reasoning moves from general premises to a conclusion that must be true if the premises are; an argument is valid by its form and sound if its premises are also true. Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to probable generalisations and is the logic of theory development and qualitative research, while abductive reasoning (Peirce) is inference to the best explanation; Popper's hypothetico-deductive method combines them. In syllogisms people show belief bias (Evans, Barston and Pollard, 1983) and the atmosphere effect (Woodworth and Sells, 1935). Of the four conditional arguments, modus ponens and modus tollens are valid, while affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are fallacies. In Wason's selection task (1966) few people choose the correct cards (E and 7), but performance improves with familiar permission rules (Griggs and Cox, 1982), explained by pragmatic reasoning schemas (Cheng and Holyoak, 1985) or cheater detection (Cosmides, 1989). Johnson-Laird's mental models theory (1983) holds that we reason by building and checking models of possible situations. Wason's 2-4-6 task (1960) revealed confirmation bias. In Piaget's theory, hypothetico-deductive, counterfactual and metacognitive reasoning emerge with formal operations; dialectical thinking is postformal.

8.5 Judgement and Decision Making. Normative models (expected value; expected utility, von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944) say how a rational agent should decide; descriptive models say how people actually decide. Simon's bounded rationality holds that information, mental capacity and time limit choice, so people satisfice rather than optimise; Tversky's elimination by aspects (1972) is a non-compensatory shortcut, and Gigerenzer's fast and frugal heuristics, such as the recognition heuristic, can be ecologically rational. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) described the availability heuristic (the K-words), the representativeness heuristic, which produces base-rate neglect, the conjunction fallacy (Linda, 1983), the gambler's fallacy and insensitivity to sample size, and anchoring and adjustment. The framing effect (the Asian disease problem, 1981) shows risk aversion for gains and risk seeking for losses, explained by prospect theory (1979): outcomes are judged from a reference point, sensitivity diminishes, and losses loom about twice as large as gains (loss aversion), which also underlies the endowment effect, status quo bias and the sunk-cost fallacy. Overconfidence, the planning fallacy and Fischhoff's hindsight bias (1975) distort judgements of our own knowledge. Dual-process theory contrasts fast, intuitive System 1 with slow, deliberate System 2, and Beck's cognitive distortions are the clinical cousins of these biases.

8.6 Creativity. Creativity is the production of work that is both novel and appropriate, studied through Rhodes's four Ps (person, process, press, product) and at levels from mini-c to Big-C. Guilford's 1950 address launched the field; he identified creativity with divergent production and scored it by fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration. Wallas (1926) described four stages: preparation, incubation, illumination and verification, with intimation sometimes added. Creativity is measured by the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (1966), Mednick's Remote Associates Test (1962), which rests on his associative theory, and Amabile's consensual assessment technique. De Bono's lateral thinking (1967) and Osborn's brainstorming (rules: defer judgement, go for quantity, welcome wild ideas, combine) aim to enhance it, although nominal groups outperform interacting ones because of production blocking. The threshold hypothesis holds that intelligence is necessary but not sufficient for creativity: the two are correlated below an IQ of about 120 but hardly at all above it, and overall the correlation is weak (about 0.17 in Kim's 2005 meta-analysis); Getzels and Jackson (1962) found high-creative and high-IQ adolescents equally successful at school. Sternberg and Lubart's investment theory (buy low, sell high; six converging resources), Amabile's componential model with its intrinsic motivation principle, and Csikszentmihalyi's systems model (individual, domain and field) are the main modern theories. Creative people are above all open to experience, independent, tolerant of ambiguity and willing to take risks.

8.7 The Structure of Language. A language is a system of arbitrary symbols combined by rules to produce unlimited messages. Hockett (1960) listed design features, of which semanticity, arbitrariness, displacement, generativity (productivity) and duality of patterning are the most examined; recursion may be uniquely human. The units of language form a hierarchy: phonemes (smallest units of sound that distinguish words), morphemes (smallest units of meaning, free or bound, inflectional or derivational), words, syntax (rules of sentence structure), semantics (meaning), pragmatics (use in social context, including Grice's maxims) and prosody (rhythm, stress and intonation, with emotional prosody served by right-hemisphere regions). Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957) proposed a transformational generative grammar of phrase-structure and transformational rules, and Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965) distinguished surface structure from deep structure (ambiguous sentences have one surface structure and two deep structures) and competence (tacit knowledge) from performance (actual use).

8.8 Acquiring Language. Skinner's Verbal Behavior (1957) explained language by reinforcement of verbal operants (mand, tact, echoic, intraverbal, textual, transcription, autoclitic). Chomsky's 1959 review replied that children produce novel sentences, make rule-based errors and are rarely corrected for grammar, and proposed an innate language acquisition device containing universal grammar, defended by the poverty of the stimulus; Pinker popularised nativism, while statistical learning (Saffran, Aslin and Newport, 1996) shows the input is richer than Chomsky supposed. Interactionists stress social support: Bruner's language acquisition support system (formats and scaffolding) and child-directed speech, with its higher, wider pitch, slower rate, more pauses and shorter utterances; Slobin's language-making capacity is a set of language-tuned cognitive and perceptual operating principles. Lenneberg (1967) proposed a critical period from about two to puberty, supported, imperfectly, by Genie and by Johnson and Newport's immigrants (1989). The milestones are cooing (about 2 months), babbling (about 6 months), holophrases (about 12 months), the vocabulary spurt with fast mapping (about 18 months), telegraphic speech (18 to 24 months), overregularisation (2 to 3 years), and complex sentences and metalinguistic awareness after that, with overextension and underextension along the way. Language is most consistently compromised in autism spectrum disorder.

8.9 Language, Thought and Communication. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis has a strong version, linguistic determinism, which has failed (Hopi time, Berlin and Kay's colour universals of 1969, Heider's Dani study of 1972), and a weak version, linguistic relativity, which is supported (Russian blues, absolute spatial languages, kinship terms); Pinker argues that we think in a language-independent mentalese. The curare experiment (1947) refuted Watson's motor theory. Piaget held that thought comes first and egocentric speech simply fades; Vygotsky held that thought and speech have separate roots that merge at about two, after which private speech guides behaviour and is internalised as inner speech; he also traced concepts from heaps through complexes to true concepts in adolescence. Bilingualism may be simultaneous or sequential, compound, coordinate or subordinate (Weinreich), additive or subtractive (Lambert); balanced bilinguals can show advantages (Peal and Lambert, 1962), though executive benefits are debated. Bees and vervet monkeys communicate, and apes (Washoe, Sarah, Lana, Nim, Kanzi) learn meaningful symbols but little productive grammar. Speech perception is categorical (Liberman, 1957; voice onset time), audiovisual (the McGurk effect, 1976) and partly top-down (phonemic restoration). Reading builds on spoken language and phonological awareness (dual-route model), and aphasia is an acquired language disorder: Broca's (production) and Wernicke's (comprehension), treated fully in the Physiological Psychology book.

From the past papers

  1. Who is a pioneer contributor to Cognitive Psychology? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Jean Piaget (b) Kohler (c) Chomsky (d) Kohlberg

    Answer: (a) Jean Piaget (the keyed answer). Piaget's studies of how children's thinking develops, begun in the 1920s, were among the first systematic accounts of mental structures and processes, decades before the cognitive revolution. Chomsky is the strongest alternative, since his attack on behaviourism helped launch that revolution, so the item is debatable; Köhler was a Gestalt forerunner whose insight research predates cognitive psychology; Kohlberg is examined for moral development and is never the answer here.

  2. According to the dual-coding theory of memory, which type of information is more easily remembered? (RML 2023)

    (a) Verbal information (b) Visual information (c) Auditory information (d) Semantic information

    Answer: (b) Visual information. In Paivio's theory (1971), pictures and concrete, imageable material are coded both in the imagery system and in the verbal system, giving two routes to retrieval (the picture superiority and concreteness effects). Verbal and auditory material, and abstract meaning, usually receive only the verbal code. "Semantic" tempts students who confuse dual coding with Craik and Lockhart's levels of processing.

  3. The researcher who formulated the theory which explains how we form artificial concepts rather than natural concepts by developing a hypothesis is: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Clark Hull (b) Eleanor Rosch (c) Jerome Bruner (d) John Watson

    Answer: (c) Jerome Bruner. With Goodnow and Austin (1956), Bruner showed that people learning artificial, rule-defined concepts form and test hypotheses, using strategies such as conservative focusing. Rosch is the name for natural concepts and prototypes; Hull (1920) treated concept learning as gradual associative abstraction of a common element, not hypothesis testing; Watson did no work on concepts.

  4. Which model proposes that memory is organized in a network of interconnected nodes? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Associative model (b) Connectionist model (c) Network model (d) Linkage model

    Answer: (b) Connectionist model (the keyed answer). Connectionist, or parallel distributed processing, models (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) represent knowledge as patterns of activation across a network of interconnected units whose connection weights store what has been learned. "Network model" is the strongest distractor, because Collins and Quillian's semantic network is also a network of nodes, but follow the key. "Associative model" is the older, general idea of linked ideas, and "linkage model" is not a standard term.

  5. A rule-of-thumb that allows for an efficient search for the selection of a problem is termed as (RML 2024)

    (a) Algorithm (b) Education (c) Learning (d) Heuristic

    Answer: (d) Heuristic. ("Selection" in the stem is a slip for "solution".) A heuristic is an efficient shortcut that usually works but does not guarantee success. An algorithm is the opposite case, a step-by-step procedure that guarantees a solution but may be slow; education and learning are not problem-solving strategies at all.

  6. The tendency to think of the use of objects only in terms of their habitual use is known as: (RML 2024)

    (a) Mental barrier (b) Functional fixedness (c) Einstellung (d) Trial and error

    Answer: (b) Functional fixedness, demonstrated by Duncker's candle problem (1945), in which the box is seen only as a container. Einstellung (mental set) is the related fixation on a previously successful strategy (Luchins's water jars), not on an object's use; "mental barrier" is not a technical term; trial and error is a problem-solving strategy, not an obstacle.

  7. Which is the most basic and common obstacle to problem solving? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Confirmation bias (b) Mental set (c) Functional fixedness (d) Fixation

    Answer: (b) Mental set, the tendency to keep using an approach that worked before. Functional fixedness is usually treated as a special case of mental set, applied to objects, so it is narrower. Fixation is the Gestaltists' umbrella word for being stuck rather than a specific obstacle, and confirmation bias concerns how hypotheses are tested rather than how a problem is approached.

  8. In which method does the researcher start with a theory and then test it with observations? (RML 2023)

    (a) Inductive reasoning (b) Deductive reasoning (c) Abductive reasoning (d) Hypothetico-deductive method

    Answer: (b) Deductive reasoning, which runs from the general (a theory) to the specific (a prediction tested against observations). Inductive reasoning runs the other way, from data to theory; abductive reasoning infers the best explanation of a surprising observation. The hypothetico-deductive method also begins with theory, but it is the whole cycle of hypothesis, deduced prediction and test, and the key chooses the plain label.

  9. In deductive reasoning, the validity of conclusions depends on the: (RML 2023)

    (a) Breadth of observations (b) Depth of data collected (c) Soundness of the initial theory (d) Use of statistical analysis

    Answer: (c) Soundness of the initial theory. A deductive conclusion is only as trustworthy as the premises it starts from: a valid argument from a false premise can reach a false conclusion. (Strictly, logicians say validity depends on form and soundness on true premises, so the item uses "validity" loosely.) Breadth of observations and depth of data are concerns of inductive, data-first research, and statistics is a tool, not the basis of deduction.

  10. Which of the following does NOT reflect inductive reasoning? (RML 2024)

    (a) Probabilistic conclusions (b) Heuristics (c) Decision making (d) Process involved in concept formation

    Answer: (b) Heuristics (the keyed answer). Inductive conclusions are probabilistic by nature; concept formation abstracts general categories from specific instances; decision making under uncertainty rests on inductive estimates of likelihood. A heuristic is a shortcut strategy rather than a form of inference, although it can produce inductive judgements, which is why the item is debatable.

  11. Formal operational reasoning involves all the following EXCEPT: (RML 2022)

    (a) hypothetical reasoning (b) counterfactual reasoning (c) dialectical reasoning (d) metacognitive reasoning

    Answer: (c) Dialectical reasoning. Holding contradictory perspectives together and integrating them is postformal thought (Riegel, 1973; Basseches, 1984), beyond Piaget's final stage. Hypothetical (hypothetico-deductive), counterfactual and metacognitive reasoning ("operations on operations") are exactly what emerges in the formal operational stage from about 11 or 12.

  12. Who is known for his work on the concept of bounded rationality, which suggests that decision-making is often constrained by factors such as limited information and cognitive resources? (RML 2023)

    (a) Herbert Simon (b) Daniel Kahneman (c) Amos Tversky (d) Howard Gardner

    Answer: (a) Herbert Simon, who also coined "satisficing" (1956) and won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978. Kahneman and Tversky tempt because they too showed that people depart from rational choice, but their contributions are heuristics and biases and prospect theory; Gardner is the theorist of multiple intelligences.

  13. The representativeness heuristic can lead to errors when: (RML 2023)

    (a) Individuals ignore base rates and focus solely on similarity (b) Individuals rely on readily available information (c) Individuals fail to consider alternative explanations (d) Individuals adjust their judgements based on initial estimates

    Answer: (a). Representativeness judges probability by resemblance to the typical case, and its classic failure is base-rate neglect, as with Jack the "engineer" and the taxi-cab problem. Option (b) describes the availability heuristic, (c) confirmation bias and (d) anchoring and adjustment.

  14. Confirmation bias is closely related to which heuristics? (RML 2023)

    (a) Availability heuristic (b) Representativeness heuristic (c) Anchoring and adjustment heuristic (d) Recognition heuristic

    Answer: (b) Representativeness heuristic (the keyed answer). The key reasons that both involve judging by fit to a preconceived model: evidence that "looks like" what we expect is accepted. No standard textbook ties confirmation bias to one heuristic (it is a bias in hypothesis testing, Wason, 1960), so treat this as a recall of the key. Availability concerns ease of retrieval and anchoring an initial value; the recognition heuristic (Gigerenzer) simply favours the option one recognises and is the least related.

  15. The tendency to assume that we would have been better at predicting actual events than is really true is termed as: (RML 2024)

    (a) Hindsight effect (b) Just world hypothesis (c) Decisiveness (d) Confirmation

    Answer: (a) Hindsight effect, the "I knew it all along" bias shown by Fischhoff and Beyth (1975) with predictions about Nixon's 1972 visits. The just-world hypothesis (Melvin Lerner) is the belief that people get what they deserve; decisiveness is not a bias; confirmation bias concerns how we seek and read evidence for a belief, not how we misremember our own predictions.

  16. The correct sequence of the four stages of creative thinking, as proposed by Graham Wallas, is: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Preparation, Illumination, Incubation, Verification (b) Incubation, Preparation, Verification, Illumination (c) Preparation, Incubation, Illumination, Verification (d) Illumination, Preparation, Verification, Incubation

    Answer: (c), from The Art of Thought (1926). The other options scramble the logic of the sequence: incubation (setting the problem aside) has to follow the effortful work of preparation and comes before the sudden illumination it prepares, and verification must come last because there has to be an idea to test.

  17. Which is the smallest unit of meaning in speech perception? (RML 2024)

    (a) Syllables (b) Consonant (c) Phonemes (d) Morphemes

    Answer: (d) Morphemes, such as the two in "cat-s". Phonemes are the smallest units of sound that distinguish words, but they carry no meaning of their own; a consonant is just one kind of phoneme; a syllable is a unit of pronunciation built around a vowel.

  18. The brain region most critically involved in processing the prosody or emotional tone of speech (speech intonation) is typically: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area) (b) Right hemisphere regions homotopic to language areas (c) Primary motor cortex (d) Occipital lobe

    Answer: (b). Emotional prosody depends chiefly on right-hemisphere regions that mirror the left hemisphere's language areas, and damage there produces aprosodia (Ross, 1981). Broca's area serves speech production and grammar rather than emotional tone; the primary motor cortex moves the speech muscles without interpreting anything; the occipital lobe is visual.

  19. Adults typically talk to babies in a special way. This has been defined as 'Motherese', 'baby talk register' or 'adult-child speech'. Identify one characteristic of this type of adult-child speech. (RML 2021)

    (a) The mean length of utterance is shorter in adult-child speech than it is in adult-adult speech. (b) Adults speak much more quickly to babies than they do to other adults. (c) Adults pause more frequently when they talk to other adults than they do when they talk to babies. (d) Adult-child speech has a lower pitch and a narrower range of pitch.

    Answer: (a). Child-directed speech uses shorter, simpler utterances. Each other option reverses a real feature: adults speak more slowly to babies, pause more often with babies than with adults, and use a higher pitch with a wider range.

  20. When infants and very young children live in environments where they have no exposure to or experience with language, their language development is severely limited. These limitations are not reversible. This supports which of the following terms? (RML 2021)

    (a) Sensitive periods of development (b) Semantics (c) Syntax (d) Nativism

    Answer: (a) Sensitive periods of development. Irreversible deficits after early deprivation (Genie; Lenneberg, 1967) are the defining evidence for a sensitive or critical period. Semantics and syntax are levels of language, not developmental principles; nativism is the broader claim that the capacity for language is innate, which such evidence fits but does not name directly.

  21. Who emphasised the linguistic relativity hypothesis? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Noam Chomsky (b) Jean Piaget (c) Steven Pinker (d) B.L. Whorf

    Answer: (d) B. L. Whorf, Sapir's student, who argued from the Hopi language that language shapes habitual thought. Chomsky stresses the universal grammar common to all languages, the opposite emphasis; Piaget held that thought precedes and shapes language; Pinker is an outspoken critic of Whorf who argues that we think in "mentalese".

  22. What is the primary difference between Piaget and Vygotsky's theories of language development? (RML 2021)

    (a) Piaget argued thought depends on language while Vygotsky argued language depends on thought (b) Vygotsky argued thought depends on language while Piaget argued language depends on thought (c) Vygotsky was concerned with early language development while Piaget was concerned with later language development. (d) None of the above

    Answer: (b). For Piaget, language expresses concepts already formed through action; for Vygotsky, thought and speech merge at about two, after which thought becomes verbal and depends on language. Option (a) swaps the names, and (c) is false, because both studied the same preschool years (Piaget's egocentric speech and Vygotsky's private speech are the same phenomenon, interpreted differently).

  23. In Vygotsky's writings, conceptual thinking is linked to all the following EXCEPT: (RML 2022)

    (a) the distinction between the acting 'I' and the reflecting 'I' (b) the problems and developmental tasks that society creates for adolescents (c) participation in particular societal institutions (d) gender differences

    Answer: (d) Gender differences. Vygotsky linked the emergence of true concepts in adolescence to the tasks society sets adolescents, to their participation in social institutions such as school and work, and to self-reflection, the ability to step back from the acting self. Gender plays no part in his account.

  24. A person's language and thought involve (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Simultaneous processes. (b) Independent processes. (c) Shared processes. (d) Alternate process.

    Answer: (a) Simultaneous processes (the expected answer; the official key is not available). In mature verbal thought, language and thought run together and depend on each other, as Vygotsky described once their two lines merge at about two. "Independent" describes only their separate early roots (pre-linguistic thought and pre-intellectual speech); "shared" is the most defensible alternative if the key is disputed; "alternate", meaning that they take turns, describes no theory.

  25. Aphasia is the disturbance in: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Language function due to organic disease (b) Intellectual functioning (c) Abstract thinking (d) Memory

    Answer: (a). Aphasia is an acquired disorder of language caused by brain damage, classically to Broca's area (non-fluent aphasia) or Wernicke's area (fluent aphasia) in the left hemisphere. Intelligence, abstract thinking and memory can all be intact; a general loss of intellectual functioning suggests intellectual disability or dementia, and a loss of memory suggests amnesia.
