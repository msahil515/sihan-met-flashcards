Part One · Foundations: How Psychology Became a Science

Psychology did not begin in a laboratory. For more than two thousand years the questions psychology now asks (What is the mind? How do we perceive? Why do we feel and remember and decide?) belonged to philosophers, and the answers were arrived at by argument rather than by experiment. To understand why a single year, 1879, is treated as the birth of psychology as a science, you first have to see what came before it, and why that earlier way of working could not settle anything.

The Pre-Scientific Roots: Philosophy and Physiology

The philosophical thread runs from the ancient Greeks onward. Plato argued that knowledge is innate, present in the soul before birth (the nativist view), while Aristotle held the opposite, that the mind begins blank and is written on by experience. This argument resurfaced in the seventeenth century as the great debate between the rationalists and the empiricists. René Descartes (1596–1650) proposed mind–body dualism, the idea that mind and body are two distinct substances that nonetheless interact, and he is also remembered for the notion of innate ideas. Against him, the British empiricist John Locke (1632–1704) revived Aristotle's view with his famous image of the mind at birth as a tabula rasa, a blank slate filled in entirely by sensory experience. From this empiricist tradition grew associationism, the claim that complex thought is built up from simple sensations linked together by laws of association (contiguity, similarity, frequency).

The second thread was the rise of experimental physiology in the nineteenth century, which mattered because it showed that mental processes could be measured. Hermann von Helmholtz measured the speed of the nerve impulse and studied vision and hearing. Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner founded psychophysics, the study of the mathematical relationship between a physical stimulus and the sensation it produces (the famous Weber–Fechner law). For the first time, something as inward as a sensation was being attached to a number. When these two streams met (the philosophical questions about the mind, and the physiological methods for measuring response) the ground was ready for a science of mental life.

High-yield

Psychology's two parents are philosophy (the questions: mind, knowledge, perception) and physiology (the method: measurement and experiment). The year 1879 matters because it is when these combined into a dedicated experimental laboratory devoted to studying the mind for its own sake.

Introspection as a Method

Because the first scientific psychologists wanted to study consciousness (the contents of immediate experience) they needed a method for getting at something that, by definition, only the experiencing person can observe. Their answer was introspection. It is essential to understand that introspection in this technical sense was not casual reflection, daydreaming, or "thinking about your feelings." It was systematic, trained, controlled self-observation. An observer (often called the reagent) underwent extensive training, sometimes hundreds of practice trials, learning to report only the raw contents of conscious experience and to avoid what was called the stimulus error (describing the meaning of an object rather than the bare sensation it produced).

In a typical experiment a controlled stimulus was presented (a tone of a fixed pitch, a coloured patch, a light flashed for a measured duration) and the trained observer reported, in fine detail, the elementary sensations, images, and feelings that arose in consciousness at that moment. Reaction times were timed; conditions were held constant; the stimulus was varied deliberately. This is why it is sometimes called experimental introspection. The aspiration was rigour: the same controlled inwardness that physics brought to the outer world, turned onto the inner world.

Definition

Introspection (in early scientific psychology): the method of systematically observing and reporting the contents of one's own conscious experience, by trained observers, under controlled stimulus conditions, in order to discover the elements of consciousness. It is not ordinary introspective musing.

Structuralism: Wundt and Titchener

In 1879, at the University of Leipzig in Germany, Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920) established the first formal psychology laboratory. This act, more than any single discovery, is taken as the founding of psychology as an independent experimental science. Wundt's project was to use experimental introspection to break consciousness down into its basic elements, much as a chemist breaks a compound into elements, and then to understand how those elements combine. He distinguished sensations (the raw elements arising from stimulation) from feelings, and he proposed that feelings vary along three dimensions: pleasant–unpleasant, tension–relaxation, and excitement–calm (the tridimensional theory of feeling).

A point that examiners love: Wundt did not believe that all of mental life could be reached by laboratory introspection. He held that the higher mental processes (language, thought, myth, custom, the products of culture) had to be studied through observation of human communities, a project he called Völkerpsychologie ("folk" or cultural psychology), which occupied a vast ten-volume work late in his life. So Wundt was both the founder of experimental psychology and, at the same time, an early advocate of a cultural, social approach.

Wundt's most influential English-speaking student was Edward Bradford Titchener (1867–1927), an Englishman who took the approach to the United States, to Cornell University. It was Titchener, not Wundt, who coined the name "structuralism." Titchener's goal was to map the structure of the mind by cataloguing its elements. He concluded that conscious experience reduced to three kinds of elements: sensations (the elements of perceptions), images (the elements of ideas), and affections (the elements of emotions). He was famously exhaustive, claiming to have identified on the order of forty-four thousand distinct elementary sensations, the great majority of them visual. Each element, he said, could be described by attributes such as quality, intensity, duration, and clearness. Structuralism, then, asked one central question: What is the mind made of?

Exam trap

Three classic confusions are tested constantly. (1) 1879 + Leipzig + Wundt is the founding of the first lab; memorise all three together. (2) Structuralism was named by Titchener, not by Wundt, even though Wundt founded the lab and the introspective method. Wundt's own approach is sometimes called voluntarism. (3) Wundt is German (Leipzig); Titchener is English but worked in the United States (Cornell). Do not swap the men or the countries.

Why Introspection Failed

Structuralism collapsed within a generation, and the reasons it failed are themselves an examined topic, because they explain why psychology turned toward behaviour and objective measurement. The deepest problem was that introspection is private and unverifiable. If you and I introspect the same tone and report different elementary sensations, there is no third party who can step inside either mind to settle which report is correct. Science requires that observations be replicable by independent observers, and introspective reports often could not be replicated, even by similarly trained reagents in different laboratories.

A second, fatal limitation was reach: introspection requires a trained adult who can articulate inner experience in language. That rules out the study of young children, animals, and people with mental illness or intellectual disability, populations that any complete psychology must address. A psychology that cannot study a rat, a baby, or a patient with psychosis is badly hobbled.

The decisive crisis was the imageless-thought controversy. Researchers at the Würzburg school in Germany (associated with Oswald Külpe) reported that some thoughts seemed to occur with no accompanying image or sensation at all, a "thought" that simply arrived. Titchener's school insisted all thought must reduce to sensations and images. The two camps, using the same method, reached opposite conclusions and could not adjudicate between them. This demonstrated, embarrassingly and publicly, that introspection could not arbitrate even its own central disputes. The method had no way to be wrong-proofed, and a method that cannot resolve disagreement is not a scientific method.

High-yield

Introspection failed for four linked reasons: it was unreliable and unverifiable (private, not replicable); it could not study children, animals, or abnormal minds; it was circular (the observer is also the observed); and the imageless-thought debate (Würzburg school, Külpe) showed it could not settle its own disputes.

Functionalism: James, Dewey, Angell

The first major reaction came not against introspection itself but against structuralism's question. Where structuralism asked what the mind is made of, functionalism asked what the mind is for. This shift was driven directly by Charles Darwin: if physical traits evolve because they help an organism survive and reproduce, then mental processes (consciousness, memory, emotion, habit) must also have evolved because they do something useful. The proper study of mind, the functionalists said, is its purpose and adaptive function, not a static inventory of its parts.

The towering figure is the American William James (1842–1910), whose two-volume Principles of Psychology (1890) is one of the foundational texts of the discipline. James argued that consciousness is not a collection of separable elements at all but a continuous, flowing, ever-changing process, which he named the stream of consciousness. Trying to dissect that stream into static elements, he said, was like trying to understand a river by freezing it into blocks. James was also interested in habit, emotion, the self, and the practical usefulness of mental life, in keeping with the American philosophy of pragmatism.

Functionalism became a school proper at the University of Chicago under John Dewey (whose 1896 paper on the reflex arc is often cited as its manifesto) and James Rowland Angell. Although functionalism did not survive as a distinct named school, its legacy was enormous: by insisting that psychology study purpose and adaptation, and by opening the door to studying animals, children, and individual differences, it set the stage for applied psychology and for behaviourism.

Exam trap

William James = functionalism + "stream of consciousness" + Principles of Psychology (1890). Do not attribute the stream of consciousness to a structuralist. And note the pairing of opposites: structuralism asks what the mind is made of; functionalism asks what the mind is for. Functionalism was influenced by Darwin.

Behaviourism: The Revolt Against Introspection

If functionalism softened the focus on inner elements, behaviourism abolished it. In his 1913 manifesto (often called "the behaviourist manifesto"), the American John B. Watson (1878–1958) argued that psychology, to be a real science, must abandon consciousness and introspection entirely and study only what can be publicly observed and measured: behaviour, the observable response to an observable stimulus. The mind, for the strict behaviourist, was a "black box" whose internal contents were either unknowable or irrelevant; what mattered was the relationship between stimulus and response (S–R).

Watson's most famous (and ethically notorious) demonstration was the Little Albert experiment (1920, with Rosalie Rayner), in which an infant was conditioned to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud, startling noise; the fear then generalised to other furry objects. The point was to show that even emotions are learned through conditioning, not innate structures of consciousness. Watson's boast that, given a dozen healthy infants, he could train any one to become any kind of specialist captures behaviourism's radical environmentalism.

Behaviourism descended from the work on conditioning by the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936), who discovered classical (respondent) conditioning while studying salivation in dogs, and it reached its most systematic form in the operant conditioning of B. F. Skinner (1904–1990), who studied how consequences (reinforcement and punishment) shape voluntary behaviour. This line (Pavlov → Watson → Skinner) dominated American psychology for roughly the first half of the twentieth century and is treated in full detail in the learning chapter; here, the key point is that behaviourism was psychology's decisive break from the introspective study of consciousness.

High-yield

Watson founded behaviourism and ran Little Albert. The conditioning lineage is Pavlov (classical) → Watson → Skinner (operant). Behaviourism's core move: reject unobservable inner states; study only the observable stimulus–response relationship.

Gestalt Psychology: The Whole and Its Parts

At the same time, a very different objection to structuralism arose in Germany. Gestalt psychology attacked the structuralist assumption that experience can be reduced to elements at all. Gestalt is German for "form," "shape," or "configuration," and the school's slogan is that "the whole is greater than (or other than) the sum of its parts." A melody is recognisably the same melody when transposed to a different key, even though every individual note has changed; the perceived form is a property of the whole, not of the parts.

The founder is Max Wertheimer (1880–1943), whose 1912 study of the phi phenomenon launched the movement. The phi phenomenon is the perception of apparent motion: when two stationary lights flash in quick succession, we see a single light moving between them (the principle behind motion pictures and neon signs). Crucially, the motion is in the perception, not in the stimulus, which proved that the mind actively organises sensory input into wholes rather than passively registering elements. Wertheimer's two close collaborators were Wolfgang Köhler (1887–1967) and Kurt Koffka (1886–1941). Köhler's famous studies of problem solving in chimpanzees (notably the ape Sultan, who stacked boxes or joined sticks to reach a banana) introduced the concept of insight, sudden perceptual reorganisation of a problem, in deliberate contrast to the trial-and-error learning emphasised by the behaviourists.

The Gestalt psychologists catalogued the laws of perceptual organisation, principles by which the mind groups elements into wholes: proximity (things near each other are grouped together), similarity (things that look alike are grouped), closure (we mentally complete incomplete figures), continuity or good continuation (we perceive smooth, continuous lines rather than abrupt changes), and figure–ground (we organise a scene into an object of focus standing out against a background). These laws are summarised under the overarching law of Prägnanz, the tendency to perceive the simplest, most stable, most "good" form possible.

Exam trap

Match the man to the contribution: phi phenomenon = Wertheimer (founder); insight (apes) = Köhler; Koffka is the third co-founder. A common distractor swaps Köhler's insight for Wertheimer or vice versa. Also remember that insight learning is the Gestalt answer to behaviourist trial-and-error.

Psychoanalysis: The Unconscious

While the academic schools fought over consciousness and behaviour, an entirely separate tradition grew out of the clinic. Psychoanalysis, founded by the Viennese neurologist Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), proposed that behaviour is governed largely by the unconscious, a reservoir of wishes, memories, and conflicts of which we are unaware but which nonetheless drive us. Freud divided the personality into three structures: the id (the primitive, unconscious source of drives, operating on the pleasure principle), the ego (the realistic, executive self operating on the reality principle), and the superego (the internalised moral conscience). When the ego is threatened by conflict, it deploys defence mechanisms such as repression, denial, projection, displacement, and sublimation to manage anxiety.

Freud also proposed the theory of psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital), arguing that personality is shaped by how conflicts at each stage are resolved, and that fixation at a stage leaves a lasting mark on character. His methods (free association, dream analysis as "the royal road to the unconscious," and analysis of the transference) launched the whole enterprise of talk therapy.

Two of Freud's followers broke away to found their own schools. Carl Jung (1875–1961) founded analytical psychology, rejecting Freud's emphasis on sexuality and adding the collective unconscious, a layer of the mind shared across all humanity and containing universal archetypes (recurring symbolic figures such as the shadow, the anima/animus, and the self). Alfred Adler (1870–1937) founded individual psychology, holding that the central human motive is the striving to overcome feelings of inadequacy; his key concept is the inferiority complex and its compensation through a striving for superiority and mastery.

Exam trap

Keep the three straight: Freud = psychoanalysis, id/ego/superego, psychosexual stages; Jung = analytical psychology, collective unconscious + archetypes; Adler = individual psychology, inferiority complex. A favourite trick is to attribute the collective unconscious to Freud (it is Jung) or the inferiority complex to Jung (it is Adler).

Humanistic Psychology: The "Third Force"

By the mid-twentieth century many psychologists were dissatisfied with the two dominant forces, behaviourism (which they felt reduced humans to conditioned machines) and psychoanalysis (which they felt reduced humans to the playthings of dark unconscious drives). In reaction, humanistic psychology arose and called itself the "third force." It emphasised human freedom, dignity, growth, and the potential for self-direction, and it took the conscious, present-focused, subjective experience of the whole person as its subject.

Abraham Maslow (1908–1970) is best known for the hierarchy of needs, usually drawn as a pyramid: physiological needs at the base, then safety, then love and belonging, then esteem, and at the summit self-actualisation, the drive to realise one's full unique potential. Lower needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher ones become motivating. Carl Rogers (1902–1987) developed client-centred (person-centred) therapy, in which the therapist provides unconditional positive regard (full, non-judgmental acceptance of the client), along with empathy and genuineness, so that the client can grow. Rogers held that people have an innate organismic valuing process that guides them toward growth when they are not distorted by others' conditions of worth, and he distinguished the real self from the ideal self, with psychological health lying in their congruence.

High-yield

Humanistic = the third force (after behaviourism and psychoanalysis). Maslow = hierarchy of needs / self-actualisation; Rogers = client-centred therapy, unconditional positive regard, organismic valuing.

The Cognitive Revolution

From roughly the 1950s onward, psychology swung back toward the mind, but on new, rigorous terms. The cognitive revolution rejected strict behaviourism's refusal to talk about internal states, arguing that you simply cannot explain language, memory, or problem solving without referring to internal mental processes. The key move was a powerful new metaphor: the mind as an information processor, taking in input, encoding and storing and transforming it, and producing output, much like a computer. This made internal processes respectable again, because they could now be modelled, predicted, and tested without direct introspection.

Several blows landed on behaviourism at once. Noam Chomsky's devastating 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior argued that conditioning could not possibly account for how children acquire the infinite generativity of language; the mind must contain rich internal structure. Ulric Neisser's textbook Cognitive Psychology (1967) gave the new field its name and its identity. Together with advances in computer science, information theory, and linguistics, these developments established cognitive psychology as the dominant approach in much of the discipline, studying attention, memory, perception, language, and thinking as forms of information processing.

Memory aid

Read the schools as a swinging pendulum. Structuralism (inner elements) → functionalism (inner purpose) → behaviourism (outer behaviour only, mind banished) → cognitive revolution (mind returns, now as an information processor). Gestalt, psychoanalysis, and humanism are the off-axis reactions that grew alongside this main swing.

Part Two · Intelligence: The Full Theory Ladder

Intelligence is the topic this entrance examines more than any other, and it rewards students who can climb the whole historical ladder rather than memorise a single definition. The reason it is so examinable is that intelligence theory is a genuine intellectual argument that ran for over a century: each theorist was answering, correcting, or rebelling against the one before. If you learn it as a lineage, the names stop being a random list and become a story you can reconstruct under pressure. This part teaches that story from the ground up.

What "intelligence" means, and the psychometric approach

There is no single agreed definition of intelligence, and the exam likes to exploit that. The most quoted working definitions are Wechsler's: intelligence is "the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment." Notice three ideas packed in there: it is global (not one narrow skill), it is goal-directed, and it is adaptive (dealing with the environment). A 1921 symposium of experts and a later 1994 statement ("Mainstream Science on Intelligence") both converged on intelligence as a very general mental capability involving reasoning, planning, problem-solving, abstract thinking, and learning from experience, not merely book learning.

The psychometric approach is the dominant tradition behind almost every theory in this part. "Psychometric" means measurement of the mind: you build tests, administer them to many people, and study the pattern of scores statistically. The psychometric school does not start by guessing what intelligence is; it starts with test data and asks what underlying structure best explains the data. This is why factor analysis (covered under Spearman) is so central, the entire enterprise is about discovering hidden factors from observed correlations.

Definition

The positive manifold is the robust empirical finding that scores on virtually all cognitive tests are positively correlated: a person who does well on a vocabulary test also tends to do well on arithmetic, spatial, and memory tests. This is the central fact every intelligence theory must explain. Spearman explained it with one common factor (g); Thurstone tried to explain it away with several abilities.

Galton: the starting point

Sir Francis Galton (a cousin of Darwin) is treated as the founding figure of the scientific study of individual differences. In his 1869 book Hereditary Genius he argued that intellectual ability runs in families and is largely inherited, an early and controversial nature-side position. He set up an "anthropometric laboratory" and tried to measure intelligence through sensory and reaction-time tests, such as how finely a person could discriminate weights, sounds, or visual stimuli, and how quickly they reacted. His reasoning was that all information enters through the senses, so keener senses should mean a keener mind. This sensory approach largely failed (sensory acuity correlates poorly with real-world intelligence), but Galton's lasting contributions were the idea of measuring mental ability quantitatively, the use of correlation and statistics, and coining the phrase "nature versus nurture."

Spearman: the two-factor theory and the birth of factor analysis

Charles Spearman (1904, England) gave the field its first true statistical theory. Looking at the positive manifold, he reasoned that if every cognitive test correlates with every other, there must be one common cause running through all of them. He called this g, the general factor of intelligence: a general mental energy or efficiency that contributes to performance on every cognitive task. But tests are not perfectly correlated, so each task also draws on something unique to itself. He called this s, the specific factor, the abilities specific to one particular type of task (a musical task has its own s, an arithmetic task another). Hence the name two-factor theory: every performance = g + s.

To demonstrate g, Spearman invented (or pioneered) factor analysis, a statistical method that takes a large table of correlations and extracts the smaller number of underlying factors that produce them. This is one of the most reliably tested facts on the paper: factor analysis is associated with Spearman. The very existence of the positive manifold was his evidence: if a single g did not exist, tests would not all correlate positively.

High-yield

Spearman = two-factor theory = g (general) + s (specific), and he invented factor analysis. The positive manifold is his evidence for g. Every subsequent theorist is essentially arguing about whether g is real and singular, or whether intelligence is actually many separate abilities.

Thurstone: Primary Mental Abilities, the anti-Spearman

Louis Leon Thurstone (1938, USA) was the great challenger to a single g. Using a more sophisticated form of factor analysis (multiple-factor analysis with rotation), he argued that intelligence is not one general thing but a set of several independent abilities. He proposed seven Primary Mental Abilities (PMA): Verbal comprehension, Word fluency, Number (numerical ability), Spatial visualisation, Associative Memory, Perceptual speed, and Reasoning. In his view there was no overarching g, only these roughly equal, distinct factors.

The historical irony, which the exam sometimes probes, is that Thurstone's own seven abilities turned out to be positively correlated with each other, which actually re-implies a higher-order g. The eventual resolution was that both were partly right: there are distinct abilities, but they share common variance. That reconciliation is exactly what the hierarchical and CHC models (below) deliver.

Memory aid

Thurstone's seven PMAs: think "V W N S M P R", Verbal, Word fluency, Number, Spatial, Memory, Perceptual speed, Reasoning. Mnemonic sentence: "Very Wise Numbers Speak Memorably, Perceiving Reason." Seven abilities, no single g, that is Thurstone.

Cattell and Horn: fluid vs crystallised, and the CHC model

Raymond Cattell (later extended with his student John Horn) split general intelligence into two broad kinds. Fluid intelligence (Gf) is the capacity to reason, solve novel problems, and see patterns in unfamiliar situations independent of acquired knowledge; it is biologically based, tied to raw processing, and it peaks in young adulthood and then declines with age. Crystallised intelligence (Gc) is accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, facts, and skills built up through education and experience; it holds steady or even rises across the lifespan. The classic example: solving a never-seen-before logic puzzle is Gf; knowing the capital of France or a wide vocabulary is Gc.

Memory aid

"Fluid drains" with age, fluid intelligence declines as you get older. Crystallised "crystallises and stays", knowledge keeps building. If a question asks which type declines with age, the answer is fluid, not crystallised.

This Gf–Gc distinction merged with the work of John Carroll, whose massive re-analysis of decades of data produced a three-level (three-stratum) structure. The combined result is the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory, today's most widely accepted psychometric model and the basis of most modern IQ tests. CHC is a three-stratum hierarchy: at the top (Stratum III) sits general intelligence g; below it (Stratum II) sit around eight to ten broad abilities (including Gf, Gc, processing speed, short-term memory, visual processing, auditory processing, long-term retrieval, and so on); and at the bottom (Stratum I) sit many narrow abilities. CHC therefore reconciles Spearman and Thurstone: there is a g at the top, but multiple distinct abilities beneath it.

Vernon: the hierarchical compromise

Philip E. Vernon proposed a tidy hierarchical model that explicitly splits the difference between Spearman's single g and Thurstone's many independent factors. At the very top sits g (general intelligence). Below g, intelligence divides into two major group factors: v:ed (verbal-educational), covering verbal, numerical, and academic abilities, and k:m (practical-mechanical, also called spatial-motor), covering spatial, mechanical, and manual abilities. Each major factor then divides into minor group factors, and finally into specific factors at the bottom. The shape is a pyramid: one g at the apex broadening down to many specifics at the base. Remember Vernon as "g at the top, then v:ed and k:m," the hierarchical compromise.

Guilford: Structure of Intellect, and convergent vs divergent thinking

J. P. Guilford went in the opposite direction from any single g, proposing an explosively large number of distinct abilities in his Structure of Intellect (SOI) model. He arranged intelligence as a three-dimensional cube with three crossing dimensions: Operations (what you do, such as cognition, memory, divergent production, convergent production, evaluation), Contents (the kind of material, such as figural, symbolic, semantic, behavioural), and Products (the form of the result, such as units, classes, relations, systems, transformations, implications). Multiplying the dimensions gives the cells: the model grew from 120 to 150 to a final 180 separate abilities, one per cube cell.

Guilford's most examined legacy is the distinction between convergent thinking (zeroing in on the single correct answer, as in a standard IQ or maths problem) and divergent thinking (generating many different possible answers or ideas, the cognitive basis of creativity). When a question links creativity to a type of thinking, the answer is divergent thinking, and the source is Guilford.

High-yield

Guilford = Structure of Intellect = a 3-D cube of Operations × Contents × Products = up to 180 factors, and the origin of convergent (one answer) vs divergent (many answers, creativity) thinking.

Thorndike: the multifactor theory and the coining of social intelligence

Edward L. Thorndike (the learning-theory and connectionism figure) also proposed a multifactor theory of intelligence, arguing there is no single general intelligence but rather many independent abilities. He famously distinguished three kinds of intelligence: Abstract intelligence (handling ideas, words, symbols, the academic kind), Mechanical or Concrete intelligence (handling objects, tools, and the physical world), and Social intelligence (handling people, understanding and managing others in social situations). Crucially for the exam, Thorndike coined the term "social intelligence" in 1920, decades before emotional intelligence existed as a concept. If a question asks who first described social intelligence, the answer is Thorndike.

Gardner: the theory of Multiple Intelligences

Howard Gardner (1983, in Frames of Mind) rejected the idea that intelligence is a single quantity captured by one IQ number. Instead he argued for several relatively autonomous multiple intelligences, each with its own brain basis and developmental path. He originally proposed seven: Linguistic (words, language), Logical-mathematical (logic, numbers, reasoning), Spatial (visualising, navigating), Musical (rhythm, pitch, melody), Bodily-kinaesthetic (body control, athletes, dancers, surgeons), Interpersonal (understanding other people), and Intrapersonal (understanding oneself). He later added an eighth, Naturalistic (recognising and classifying living things and the natural world), and has discussed a possible ninth, Existential (the "big questions" of meaning), which remains debated and not formally confirmed.

Exam trap

Do not confuse the two "personal" intelligences. Interpersonal = understanding other people ("inter" = between people). Intrapersonal = understanding yourself ("intra" = within). Also: Gardner started with 7, added Naturalistic to make 8, with Existential debated as a possible 9th, so "7, 8, or 9" can all appear as correct depending on how the question is phrased. Sternberg, by contrast, has exactly 3.

Sternberg: the Triarchic theory

Robert Sternberg proposed the Triarchic theory ("triarchic" = three parts), built on cognitive processing rather than factor analysis. The three sub-theories are: Componential / Analytical intelligence, the internal mental components used to analyse, evaluate, and solve academic-type problems (this is what conventional IQ tests measure); Experiential / Creative intelligence, the ability to deal with novelty and to automate familiar tasks, the basis of insight and creativity; and Contextual / Practical intelligence, the ability to adapt to, shape, and select real-world environments, popularly called "street smarts." Sternberg later folded these into the idea of successful intelligence (using your strengths to achieve your goals in your culture) and the WICS model (Wisdom, Intelligence, Creativity, Synthesised).

Memory aid

Sternberg's three = "A-C-P": Analytical, Creative, Practical (componential, experiential, contextual). Practical = street smarts. Three, always three, that is what separates Sternberg from Gardner's seven-to-nine.

Emotional intelligence (EI/EQ)

Emotional intelligence is the ability to perceive, understand, use, and manage emotions, in oneself and in others. The exam consistently tests who did what, so be precise. Peter Salovey and John Mayer (1990) formally coined the term "emotional intelligence" and built the original ability model, treating EI as a genuine intelligence made of four branches: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotions, and managing emotions. Daniel Goleman (1995) wrote the best-selling book Emotional Intelligence and popularised the concept for the general public; his is a broader mixed / competency model blending abilities with personality traits and competencies (self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills). Reuven Bar-On developed another mixed model and the EQ-i inventory, and is credited with popularising the term "EQ" (emotional quotient).

Exam trap

The IQ lineage and measurement

Running alongside the theories is the practical history of how intelligence was measured. Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon (1905, France) built the first practical intelligence test, commissioned to identify French schoolchildren who needed special help. Their key concept was mental age (MA): a child who can answer the items that an average eight-year-old can answer has a mental age of eight, regardless of actual age. William Stern then took mental age and chronological age and coined the Intelligence Quotient (IQ), defined as IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100. This is the ratio IQ: an eight-year-old with a mental age of ten has an IQ of (10 ÷ 8) × 100 = 125. Lewis Terman at Stanford imported and revised the test for the USA as the Stanford-Binet, and it was Terman's version that put the ratio IQ formula into wide practical use.

The ratio formula breaks down in adulthood (mental age stops climbing while chronological age keeps rising, so ratio IQ would falsely fall with age). David Wechsler solved this with the deviation IQ: instead of a ratio, a person's score is compared to the average score of their own age group, expressed as how far above or below the group mean they fall. By definition the mean = 100 and the standard deviation (SD) = 15, so a score of 115 is one SD above average and 130 is two SDs above. Wechsler also split his tests into Verbal and Performance scales and produced the family of tests still dominant today: WAIS (adults), WISC (children), and WPPSI (preschool/primary).

High-yield

Binet & Simon (1905) = first practical test + mental age. Stern = coined IQ, ratio formula IQ = MA/CA × 100. Terman = Stanford-Binet. Wechsler = deviation IQ, verbal/performance scales, WAIS/WISC/WPPSI, with mean 100 and SD 15. Ratio IQ is for children; deviation IQ replaced it for adults.

One final measurement fact is frequently tested: the Flynn effect (named after James Flynn) is the observed rise in average IQ scores across successive generations, roughly three points per decade through much of the twentieth century, attributed to better nutrition, education, environmental complexity, and test familiarity. Because the population mean keeps drifting up, IQ tests must be periodically re-standardised to keep the average pinned at 100.

Both areas reward precise terminology over vague familiarity. The paper does not ask "what is memory?"; it asks you to tell proactive interference from retroactive, to name the four quadrants of the Johari Window, or to identify who proposed the working-memory model. Section A: Linguistic Relativity & the Language Debate

Does the language you speak shape the way you think and perceive the world? This single question generates one of psychology's most quoted, most misquoted, and most reliably tested topics. The position that language shapes thought is associated above all with two American linguists: Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf. Their combined view is known as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis or, more neutrally, linguistic relativity. Note the teacher-and-student relationship: Sapir was the senior figure, Whorf the pupil who pushed the ideas furthest, drawing on his study of Native American languages such as Hopi. Neither man ever co-authored a single statement of "the hypothesis"; the name was applied later, which is partly why the idea splits into a strong and a weak form.

Definition

Linguistic relativity (Sapir-Whorf hypothesis): the proposal that the structure and vocabulary of a person's language influences or determines the way that person thinks about and perceives reality. Sapir was the teacher, Whorf the student.

The strong versus weak split: the crux of every question

The single most important thing to master here is that the hypothesis comes in two strengths, and almost every exam item turns on telling them apart.

The strong version is called linguistic determinism. It claims that language determines thought outright: cognition is a prisoner of vocabulary and grammar, so that if your language has no word or grammatical category for a concept, you literally cannot think it. This strong, absolute form is largely rejected by modern psychology and linguistics. People plainly entertain ideas before they have words for them, learn new concepts, and translate (imperfectly but really) between languages, all of which a hard determinism struggles to explain.

The weak version is linguistic relativity proper. It makes the gentler claim that language influences thought and perception, nudging habitual attention and making some distinctions easier or quicker to draw, without imprisoning the mind. This weak form enjoys some empirical support, for example in studies showing that speakers of languages with finer colour vocabularies discriminate those colours slightly faster.

The classic examples and their real status

Three examples recur, and the paper likes to test whether you know they are overstated.

The most famous is the "Eskimo words for snow" claim, the idea that Inuit languages have dozens or hundreds of words for snow and therefore perceive snow in a richer way. This is the textbook case of an overstated, largely debunked anecdote: the word counts were inflated through retelling, and English itself has many snow terms (slush, sleet, blizzard, powder). Treat it as a cautionary tale, not as evidence for the hypothesis.

More serious is the work on colour terms. Languages carve the colour spectrum at different points; some have only two or three basic colour words. Early relativists argued this proved that colour perception itself is shaped by language. The decisive counter came from Brent Berlin and Paul Kay (1969), who showed that across very different languages people pick out the same best examples, or focal colours, and that basic colour terms emerge in a near-universal order. Berlin & Kay's universal focal colours are the standard counter-evidence to strong Whorfianism: perception of colour is largely universal even where vocabulary differs.

A third, more modern line concerns grammatical gender. Languages that assign masculine or feminine gender to nouns (a bridge is feminine in German, masculine in Spanish) seem to nudge speakers to describe the same object with stereotypically masculine or feminine adjectives. This is offered as mild support for the weak version, since it is an influence on description, not a determinant of what can be thought.

High-yield

Berlin & Kay (1969) and their universal focal colours are the go-to evidence against strong linguistic determinism. If a question asks what undermines the strong Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, the colour-universals work is the canonical answer.

The opposing camp: Chomsky, nativism, and Pinker

Standing against the relativist tradition is the nativist camp, which holds that the deep structure of language is innate and universal rather than shaped by the particular tongue you happen to learn. Its towering figure is Noam Chomsky. Chomsky proposed a universal grammar: a set of grammatical principles wired into every human mind, supported by an innate language acquisition device (LAD) that lets children extract grammar from messy input far faster and more uniformly than learning alone could explain. On this view, the surface differences between languages sit atop a shared, biologically given core, which is the opposite emphasis to Sapir-Whorf.

Steven Pinker, in his popular book The Language Instinct (1994), carried this nativist critique to a wide audience, arguing that language is a biological instinct and explicitly attacking strong linguistic relativity. Pinker is the name to attach to the popularisation of the anti-Whorfian, instinct-based position.

Exam trap

Keep the two great language debates separate, because the paper deliberately blurs them.
(1) Whorf vs Chomsky = does language shape thought? (relativity vs universal grammar/nativism).
(2) Chomsky vs Skinner = how is language acquired? (innate faculty vs learned behaviour).
Chomsky is in both, but on different questions. Don't let an option fuse them.

The other debate: Chomsky versus Skinner

The second famous controversy is about how children come to speak at all. B. F. Skinner, in Verbal Behavior (1957), treated language as ordinary learned operant behaviour: children produce sounds, those that resemble adult speech are reinforced, and language is shaped word by word through reinforcement and imitation. Chomsky's 1959 review of that book is one of the most consequential papers in the field. He argued that the sheer speed of acquisition, the creativity of novel sentences children have never heard, and the "poverty of the stimulus" (children hear imperfect, limited input yet master grammar) cannot be explained by reinforcement. The review is widely credited with helping launch the cognitive revolution, shifting psychology away from behaviourism toward the study of internal mental structures.

Stages of language acquisition and the critical period

Basic developmental items appear, so know the ordered milestones of early language. Infants begin with cooing (vowel-like sounds, around 2-4 months), progress to babbling (repeated consonant-vowel strings such as "ba-ba-ba", around 6 months), then reach the holophrastic or one-word stage (single words standing for whole ideas, around 12 months), and then telegraphic speech (two-word and short utterances stripped of function words, like "want milk", around 18-24 months). The order coo → babble → one-word → telegraphic is the testable sequence.

Overlaying this is the critical-period hypothesis: the idea, associated with Eric Lenneberg, that there is a biologically constrained window (roughly early childhood up to puberty) during which language is acquired easily and natively, after which full mastery, especially of grammar and accent, becomes much harder. Cases of extreme deprivation are cited as tragic support, and the difficulty of late second-language learning is the everyday illustration.

Section B: Memory & Cognition

Memory is treated in the paper as a structured system with named stores, named researchers, and named processes. Build the picture from the architecture outward.

The modal model (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968)

The foundational framework is the multi-store or modal model proposed by Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin in 1968. It pictures memory as three stores through which information flows: the sensory register (very brief, holds raw sensory input) feeds the short-term store (limited, fragile) which, with rehearsal, feeds the long-term store (vast, durable). Movement between stores is governed by control processes, the most important of which is rehearsal: actively repeating material keeps it in the short-term store and transfers it to long-term memory. The model's lasting contribution is the idea of distinct stores with different capacities and durations; its weakness, addressed later, is that it treats the short-term store as a single passive holding tank.

Definition

Modal (multi-store) model, Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968): sensory register → short-term store → long-term store, with control processes (especially rehearsal) regulating the flow.

Sensory memory: iconic and echoic

Sensory memory is the briefest store, holding a near-complete sensory snapshot for a fraction of a second before it fades. Its visual form is iconic memory, demonstrated by George Sperling (1960). Sperling flashed a grid of letters for a moment; with a "whole-report" people recalled only about four, but with a tone cueing one row (the "partial-report" technique) they could report almost any row, proving that nearly all the letters were briefly available before decaying within about a quarter to half a second. The auditory form is echoic memory, which holds sound for a few seconds (longer than iconic), explaining how you can "replay" the end of a sentence you weren't quite attending to.

Memory aid

Icon = a picture, so iconic = visual. Echo = a sound, so echoic = auditory. Sperling pairs with iconic.

Short-term memory: 7±2 and chunking

The short-term store (STM) holds a small amount of information for a short time. Its capacity is the most famous number in psychology: about seven, plus or minus two, items. This comes from George Miller's 1956 paper "The Magic Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." Crucially, the limit is on the number of chunks, not raw bits, and chunking (grouping items into meaningful units, like turning the digits 1-9-4-7 into the year 1947) lets us pack far more into those few slots. Without rehearsal, STM lasts only about 15 to 30 seconds before the trace decays.

High-yield

7±2 = George Miller (1956), "The Magic Number Seven." Capacity is measured in chunks. STM duration without rehearsal ≈ 15-30 seconds. These three facts are frequently tested as a set.

Working memory (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974)

The passive "single tank" view of STM was replaced by the working memory model of Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch (1974), which recasts short-term memory as an active, multi-component workspace that holds and manipulates information. Its components are:

The central executive, an attentional controller that directs resources and coordinates the subsystems (it does not store much itself). The phonological loop, which holds and rehearses verbal and acoustic material (the "inner voice", responsible for keeping a phone number alive by repeating it). The visuospatial sketchpad, which holds visual and spatial information (the "inner eye", used to picture a route or rotate a shape). Later, in 2000, Baddeley added the episodic buffer, a limited store that integrates information from the loop, the sketchpad, and long-term memory into a single coherent episode.

The advance over the modal model is conceptual: working memory explains why we can do two different tasks at once if they use different subsystems (talking while driving) but interfere when they compete for the same one, something a single passive STM store cannot capture.

Long-term memory: explicit versus implicit

Long-term memory (LTM) has effectively unlimited capacity and can last a lifetime. It divides first into explicit (declarative) memory, which is conscious and can be "declared" in words, and implicit (non-declarative / procedural) memory, which is unconscious and expressed through performance. Explicit memory subdivides, following Endel Tulving, into episodic memory (personally experienced events, tied to a time and place, "what I had for breakfast") and semantic memory (general facts and knowledge, "Paris is the capital of France"). Implicit memory's main form is procedural memory: skills and habits such as riding a bicycle or typing, which you perform without being able to state the steps.

Encoding: levels of processing and rehearsal

How well something is later remembered depends on how it was encoded. Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart (1972) proposed the levels-of-processing framework: the depth at which you process material at encoding determines retention. Shallow / structural processing (noticing how a word looks, its case or font) and shallow phonemic processing (how it sounds) yield weak memory; deep / semantic processing (thinking about what the word means and how it connects to other knowledge) yields strong, durable memory. Deep semantic processing beats shallow structural processing.

This maps onto two kinds of rehearsal. Maintenance rehearsal is simple rote repetition that merely keeps material in mind (shallow, poor for long-term storage). Elaborative rehearsal links new material to existing knowledge and meaning (deep, excellent for long-term storage). Elaborative rehearsal is the route to durable learning.

High-yield

Craik & Lockhart (1972), levels of processing. Deep/semantic (meaning) > shallow/structural (appearance). Elaborative rehearsal (linking to meaning) beats maintenance rehearsal (rote repetition) for long-term retention.

Forgetting: Ebbinghaus, decay, and interference

The scientific study of forgetting begins with Hermann Ebbinghaus, who in the 1880s tested his own memory using nonsense syllables (meaningless consonant-vowel-consonant strings like "ZUF") precisely so that prior meaning could not aid recall. He plotted the forgetting curve, showing that retention drops sharply soon after learning and then levels off. He measured retention by the savings method (how much faster relearning is than original learning). He also noted the serial position effect: in a list, the first items (the primacy effect, rehearsed into LTM) and the last items (the recency effect, still in STM) are recalled best, while the middle is recalled worst.

Why do we forget? Two classic explanations compete. Decay theory says memory traces simply fade with the passage of time if unused. Interference theory says other memories get in the way, and it comes in two directions that the paper loves to test.

Proactive interference is when old learning blocks new learning. The old material reaches forward in time to disrupt the new. Example: after years with your old phone number, you keep reciting it and struggle to recall the new one you just got.

Retroactive interference is when new learning blocks old learning. The new material reaches backward to disrupt the old. Example: after learning a new password, you can no longer recall the old one you used for years.

Schemas and reconstruction: Bartlett and Loftus

Memory is not a video recording; it is reconstructive. Frederic Bartlett (1932) demonstrated this with his famous study using the Native American folk tale "War of the Ghosts." When British participants recalled the unfamiliar story over time, they unconsciously altered it to fit their own cultural expectations: dropping strange details, rationalising odd elements, and shortening it. Bartlett concluded that we store the gist and reconstruct the rest using schemas, mental frameworks of prior knowledge, which is why recall is systematically distorted toward what we expect.

Elizabeth Loftus extended reconstruction into the high-stakes domain of eyewitness testimony. Her work on the misinformation effect showed that information introduced after an event, especially through leading questions, can alter a witness's memory of it. In the classic study, asking how fast cars were going when they "smashed" (versus "hit") each other produced higher speed estimates and even false memories of broken glass that was never there. The lesson: memory is malleable and eyewitness accounts are far less reliable than people assume.

Section A · Classical conditioning

Classical conditioning is learning by association. It was discovered, almost by accident, by the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936) around 1900 while he was studying digestion in dogs. Pavlov noticed that his dogs began to salivate not only when food touched their tongues but when they merely heard the footsteps of the assistant who fed them. He realised the dogs had learned to treat a previously meaningless signal as a predictor of food, and he set out to map this process precisely.

The mechanism turns on four terms. Food naturally and automatically produces salivation; food is therefore the unconditioned stimulus (UCS) and the salivation it triggers is the unconditioned response (UCR). The word "unconditioned" means "unlearned": this reflex is wired in. A bell, before any training, means nothing to the dog. It is a neutral stimulus (NS). Pavlov then repeatedly rang the bell just before presenting food. After enough pairings, the bell alone made the dog salivate. The bell has now become a conditioned stimulus (CS), and the salivation it now produces is the conditioned response (CR). Note that the salivation looks the same whether triggered by food or by the bell; what changes is the stimulus that triggers it.

Definition

Classical conditioning is a form of associative learning in which a neutral stimulus, by being repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus that already produces a reflexive response, comes to elicit a similar response on its own. The dog learns that the bell predicts food.

Several processes govern the conditioned response. Acquisition is the initial phase in which the NS-UCS pairings build the association; learning is usually fastest when the NS slightly precedes the UCS. Extinction is the weakening and eventual disappearance of the CR when the CS is presented repeatedly without the UCS (ring the bell often enough with no food, and salivation fades). Spontaneous recovery is the reappearance of an extinguished CR after a rest period, showing that extinction suppresses rather than erases the learning. Generalisation is responding to stimuli similar to the CS (a dog conditioned to a bell may also salivate to a buzzer), while discrimination is the learned ability to respond only to the specific CS and not to similar ones. Finally, higher-order conditioning occurs when an established CS is used to condition a second neutral stimulus: pair a light with the already-conditioned bell, and the light too can come to elicit salivation, without the light ever meeting food.

The most famous demonstration that classical conditioning shapes human emotion is the Little Albert study (1920) by John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner. They presented an 11-month-old infant with a white rat (initially neutral) while striking a steel bar to make a frightening loud noise (UCS producing the UCR of fear). After several pairings, Albert cried and recoiled at the rat alone: a conditioned fear. His fear also generalised to other furry white objects. The study founded the behaviourist account of phobias and is ethically infamous because the fear was never extinguished.

Exam trap

Pavlov was a physiologist, not a psychologist, and his Nobel Prize was for digestion, not conditioning. Also: negative reinforcement is not part of classical conditioning; it belongs to operant conditioning below. And do not confuse generalisation (responding to similar stimuli) with higher-order conditioning (building a new CS on an old one).

Section A · Operant conditioning

Where classical conditioning is about reflexes triggered by stimuli, operant conditioning is about voluntary behaviour shaped by its consequences. The groundwork was laid by Edward Thorndike (1898), who placed hungry cats in a "puzzle box" they could escape by pressing a lever to reach food outside. Over repeated trials the cats escaped faster. Thorndike summarised this in the Law of Effect: responses followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened and more likely to recur, while those followed by discomfort are weakened. Thorndike came first; he set the stage.

B. F. Skinner (1904–1990) systematised this into operant conditioning, coining the term "operant" because the behaviour operates on the environment to produce consequences. His apparatus, the Skinner box (operant chamber), let a rat or pigeon press a lever or peck a key to deliver food, allowing precise measurement of response rates.

Definition

Reinforcement is any consequence that increases the future frequency of a behaviour; punishment is any consequence that decreases it. "Positive" means adding a stimulus; "negative" means removing one. These two dimensions cross to make a 2×2.

Exam trap

The single most-tested confusion in operant conditioning: negative reinforcement is NOT punishment. Negative reinforcement removes an aversive stimulus in order to increase a behaviour (taking a painkiller to remove a headache makes you more likely to take it again). Punishment, of either kind, decreases behaviour. "Negative" never means "bad"; it means "something is taken away".

Skinner showed how complex behaviour is built through shaping: reinforcing successive approximations to a target response until the full behaviour emerges (rewarding a rat first for facing the lever, then for touching it, then for pressing it). Extinction in the operant sense is the decline of a behaviour when reinforcement stops. Reinforcers themselves divide into primary reinforcers, which satisfy biological needs directly (food, water, warmth), and secondary reinforcers, which acquire value through association with primary ones (money, praise, tokens).

The schedules of reinforcement describe when reinforcement is delivered, and they powerfully affect how persistent a behaviour is. They cross two dimensions: ratio (based on number of responses) versus interval (based on time), and fixed (constant) versus variable (unpredictable).

Section A · Observational learning and the cognitive bridge

Not all learning requires personally experiencing reinforcement. Albert Bandura's social learning theory holds that we learn by watching others, a process called observational learning or modelling. His Bobo doll experiments (1961) showed children who watched an adult beat an inflatable Bobo doll later imitated that aggression, especially when the model had been rewarded; children who saw the model punished imitated less, demonstrating vicarious reinforcement.

Bandura identified four conditions for observational learning, easily remembered as A-R-R-M: Attention (you must notice the model), Retention (you must remember what you saw), Reproduction (you must be able to perform it), and Motivation (you must have a reason, often expected reinforcement, to do it). He later added two influential concepts: reciprocal determinism, the idea that behaviour, personal/cognitive factors, and the environment all influence one another in a three-way loop; and self-efficacy, a person's belief in their own ability to succeed at a task, which shapes what they attempt and how long they persist.

The bridge from behaviourism toward cognition was built earlier by Edward Tolman. His rats showed latent learning: rats allowed to wander a maze with no reward learned its layout anyway, and demonstrated that learning the moment a reward was introduced, running it as fast as previously-rewarded rats. Tolman argued they had formed a cognitive map, an internal mental representation of the maze. This proved learning can occur without reinforcement and without any visible behaviour change, undermining strict behaviourism.

Section B · Piaget's cognitive development

Jean Piaget (1896–1980), a Swiss psychologist, proposed that children are active little scientists who construct their understanding of the world through stages of increasing sophistication. Knowledge is organised into schemas, mental frameworks for categorising information. The child adapts schemas through two complementary processes. Assimilation fits new information into an existing schema (a child who knows "dog" calls a cat a "doggy"). Accommodation changes the schema to fit new information (the child learns cats are a separate category). The drive to resolve the discomfort of mismatched schemas, restoring balance, is equilibration, and it powers movement through the stages.

A few terms deserve their own definition. Object permanence, the sensorimotor milestone, is the understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen; before it develops, a hidden toy is treated as gone. Egocentrism in the preoperational stage is the inability to see a situation from another's viewpoint (famously shown in the "three mountains" task). Conservation is the understanding that quantity stays the same despite changes in appearance: a preoperational child thinks a tall thin glass holds more juice than a short wide one even after watching the same juice poured across. The concrete-operational child grasps that pouring is reversible and so conserves quantity.

Piaget proposed exactly four stages. Any option listing it as a stage is wrong. Likewise, remember object permanence belongs to the sensorimotor stage and conservation to the concrete operational stage, not the other way round.

The four Piaget stages in order: Sensorimotor, Preoperational, Concrete, Formal, "Some People Can Fly". Object permanence = stage 1; conservation = stage 3.

Section B · Vygotsky's sociocultural theory

Where Piaget saw the child as a solitary constructor, the Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934) argued that cognitive development is fundamentally social and cultural: children learn through interaction with more capable members of their culture, and language is the central tool of thought. His best-known concept is the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can achieve with guidance. Learning happens most effectively inside this zone.

Definition

The Zone of Proximal Development is the distance between a child's actual developmental level (independent problem-solving) and their potential level under the guidance of a more knowledgeable other (MKO). Scaffolding is the temporary, adjustable support an adult or peer provides within this zone, gradually withdrawn as competence grows.

Vygotsky also emphasised private speech, the out-loud self-talk of young children, which he saw not as Piaget's "egocentric" babble but as a vital tool for self-guidance that later becomes silent inner speech. The contrast is examinable: Piaget = individual constructivism, development largely precedes learning, biologically staged; Vygotsky = social constructivism, learning leads development, culture and language are the engine.

Section C · Erikson's psychosocial stages

Erik Erikson (1902–1994) reworked Freud's psychosexual theory into a lifespan theory of psychosocial development. Each of his eight stages centres on a crisis, a tension between two outcomes that must be resolved; healthy resolution yields a virtue, while failure carries forward as difficulty. Unlike Freud, Erikson covered the whole life, emphasised social relationships over sexual drives, and did not end development at puberty.

Section C · Kohlberg's moral development

Lawrence Kohlberg built directly on Piaget, extending cognitive-developmental logic to moral reasoning. He probed children and adults with moral dilemmas, most famously the Heinz dilemma (should a man steal an overpriced drug to save his dying wife?), and scored not the yes/no answer but the reasoning behind it. He proposed three levels, each with two stages, six in all.

Pre-conventional (typically children): morality is external. Stage 1 is obedience and punishment avoidance; stage 2 is instrumental self-interest ("what's in it for me?"). Conventional (most adolescents and adults): morality is conformity to social norms. Stage 3 seeks approval as a "good boy / good girl"; stage 4 upholds law and social order. Post-conventional (a minority): morality rests on abstract principles. Stage 5 recognises social contracts and individual rights; stage 6 follows universal ethical principles even against the law.

High-yield

Carol Gilligan's critique is a frequent question: she argued Kohlberg's theory was gender-biased because it was based on male samples and ranked an abstract "ethic of justice" above the relational "ethic of care" she found more typical of women, thereby unfairly scoring women as less morally developed.

Section D · Attachment

Attachment is the deep, enduring emotional bond between a child and caregiver. John Bowlby (1907–1990) provided the theory. Drawing on ethology, he argued attachment is an evolved survival system: infants are biologically primed to stay close to a caregiver because proximity protected ancestral infants from danger. Key Bowlby concepts are monotropy (one primary attachment figure is special and pre-eminent), the internal working model (an early mental template of relationships, formed from the first bond, that shapes later ones), and a critical/sensitive period (attachment must form within roughly the first two to three years or it is much harder thereafter).

Mary Ainsworth (1913–1999) provided the method that turned Bowlby's theory into measurable science: the Strange Situation procedure (1970s). An infant and caregiver enter an unfamiliar room; across eight short episodes a stranger enters, the caregiver leaves and returns twice, and researchers code the infant's exploration, response to separation, and crucially its behaviour at reunion. From this she derived attachment styles.

Exam trap

Do not credit the Strange Situation to Bowlby. Bowlby = the theory; Ainsworth = the Strange Situation procedure that operationalised it. The paper deliberately swaps these names.

The classic supporting evidence comes from Harry Harlow's rhesus monkey studies (1958). Part Five · Motivation, Social Behaviour & Group Dynamics

This part connects two big questions in psychology. First, what drives behaviour from inside a person (motivation), and second, how the presence of other people changes that behaviour (social psychology and group dynamics). The Amity entrance leans heavily on named theorists, their dates, and the precise distinction between near-identical terms. Learn the names attached to each idea, because the paper almost always asks "who proposed X" rather than "what is X".

Section A · Motivation

Motivation is the internal process that initiates, directs, and sustains goal-directed behaviour. The classical theories disagree about where the push comes from: from internal deficits (drive theory), from an optimal level of stimulation (arousal theory), or from the meaning of the activity itself (intrinsic motivation).

Definition

Homeostasis is the body's tendency to maintain a stable internal state (temperature, blood sugar, fluid balance). When a state drifts from its set point, a need arises, which produces a drive that pushes the organism to act and restore balance.

Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull)

Clark Hull (1943) proposed that a physiological need (e.g. lack of food) creates an internal state of tension called a drive (hunger). The organism behaves so as to reduce that drive, and drive reduction is reinforcing. Behaviour therefore returns the body toward homeostasis. Hull's famous formula was that performance depends on drive multiplied by habit strength, meaning even a well-learned response will not show unless there is a drive energising it. The weakness of drive-reduction theory is that it cannot explain behaviours that increase tension, like riding a rollercoaster, exploring, or eating dessert when already full. That gap is exactly what arousal theory fills.

Arousal theory and the Yerkes-Dodson law

Arousal theory says organisms seek an optimal level of arousal: too little is boring and we seek stimulation, too much is overwhelming and we withdraw. The key empirical statement is the Yerkes-Dodson law (Robert Yerkes & John Dodson, 1908), which describes an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance. Performance rises as arousal increases, peaks at a moderate level, then falls as arousal becomes too high. A refinement: the optimal arousal point is lower for difficult or complex tasks and higher for simple or well-practised tasks.

Memory aid

Picture an upside-down U: the peak in the middle is the "sweet spot" of arousal. Hard exam questions need calm (low arousal); a simple sprint can use high adrenaline.

Intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation

Intrinsic motivation comes from within: doing an activity because it is inherently interesting, enjoyable, or satisfying (reading for pleasure). Extrinsic motivation comes from outside: doing something for a separable reward or to avoid punishment (studying only for marks). A classic finding is the overjustification effect: giving external rewards for an already-intrinsically-enjoyed activity can reduce the intrinsic motivation, because the person reattributes their behaviour to the reward.

Murray's needs (the origin of the need list)

Henry Murray (1938) catalogued a long list of psychogenic needs (such as achievement, affiliation, dominance, autonomy, nurturance). This list is historically important because it is the source from which McClelland later drew his three motives. Murray also co-developed the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), a projective test in which a person tells stories about ambiguous pictures, revealing underlying needs. Remember the chain: Murray's need list → measured by the TAT → McClelland's nAch builds on both.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow (1943) arranged human needs in a pyramid. Lower needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher ones become motivating. The first four are deficiency needs (D-needs): they arise from lack and quiet down once met. The top is a growth need (B-need, being-need): it is never fully "filled" and keeps motivating.

McClelland's acquired (learned) needs

David McClelland (1961) argued that key motives are learned through culture and experience, not innate. He focused on three, building directly on Murray's list:

  • Need for achievement (nAch): the drive to excel, master tasks, and meet standards.
  • Need for affiliation (nAff): the desire for warm, friendly relationships and belonging.
  • Need for power (nPow): the desire to influence, control, or have impact on others.

People high in nAch prefer moderate-risk goals (not so easy that success is meaningless, not so hard that failure is guaranteed), they want frequent, concrete feedback on their performance, and they prefer personal responsibility for outcomes. These motives are measured using the TAT (the same projective test from Murray), by coding the achievement, affiliation, or power themes in the stories people tell.

High-yield

Section B · Social psychology

Attribution theory

Attribution is the process of explaining the causes of behaviour. Fritz Heider (1958) is the founder of attribution theory and drew the basic split: an internal (dispositional) attribution locates the cause inside the person (ability, personality, effort), while an external (situational) attribution locates it in the circumstances (luck, task difficulty, social pressure).

Harold Kelley's covariation model (1967) says that to decide between internal and external causes, people use three kinds of information: consensus (do others act the same way?), distinctiveness (does this person act this way only toward this stimulus?), and consistency (does the person act this way across time?). High consensus + high distinctiveness + high consistency points to an external cause; low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency points to an internal cause.

FAE is about judging other people (you assume the late colleague is lazy, not stuck in traffic). Self-serving bias is about yourself (I passed because I'm smart; I failed because the paper was unfair). Heider is the founder; Kelley gave the covariation model. Don't swap them.

Attitudes and cognitive dissonance

An attitude has three components, the ABC model: Affective (feelings), Behavioural (action tendencies), and Cognitive (beliefs). Leon Festinger (1957) proposed cognitive dissonance: the uncomfortable tension felt when holding two inconsistent cognitions, or when behaviour conflicts with belief. To reduce the discomfort, people change an attitude, add a justification, or downplay the conflict.

The landmark $1/$20 study (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959) had people do a boring task then lie to another participant that it was fun. Those paid $1 later rated the task as more enjoyable than those paid $20. The reason: the $20 group had ample external justification for lying ("I did it for the money"), so no dissonance; the $1 group had insufficient justification, so they reduced dissonance by changing their attitude ("maybe it really was interesting"). Counter-intuitively, less reward produced more attitude change.

Conformity and obedience

These four studies are frequently tested as a set; keep the names and paradigms straight.

  • Muzafer Sherif (1935), autokinetic effect: using the illusion of a stationary light appearing to move in the dark, Sherif showed how individuals converge on a group norm. This is the study of norm formation and informational influence.
  • Solomon Asch (1951), line-judgement: participants conformed to an obviously wrong majority on a simple line-length task, demonstrating conformity through normative social influence (wanting to fit in even when you know the group is wrong).
  • Stanley Milgram (1963), obedience: participants delivered what they believed were increasing electric shocks to a learner when instructed by an authority figure, showing obedience to authority.
  • Philip Zimbardo (1971), Stanford Prison Experiment: ordinary students assigned guard/prisoner roles rapidly adopted cruel or submissive behaviour, illustrating the power of situations, social roles, and deindividuation.
Exam trap

Sherif = ambiguous (autokinetic) → norm formation. Asch = unambiguous (lines) → conformity to a wrong majority. Milgram = obedience to authority (shocks). Zimbardo = roles/deindividuation (prison). The classic mix-up is Asch (conformity) vs Milgram (obedience): conformity is yielding to peers, obedience is following an authority's order.

Prosocial behaviour and aggression

The bystander effect is the finding that the more people present at an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help. The mechanism is diffusion of responsibility: each onlooker assumes someone else will act, so responsibility is spread thin. It was studied by Bibb Latané and John Darley (1968), prompted by the Kitty Genovese case (1964), in which a woman was attacked while reportedly many neighbours failed to intervene.

Prejudice and intergroup relations

Three terms are often confused. A stereotype is a cognitive belief about a group ("all X are Y"). Prejudice is an affective negative attitude toward a group. Discrimination is the behaviour, the unfair treatment of group members. (Notice they map onto the ABC components: cognitive, affective, behavioural.)

Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment (1954) put boys at a summer camp into two groups; competition bred hostility, supporting realistic conflict theory (groups in competition for scarce resources develop prejudice). Hostility dropped only when the groups had to cooperate on superordinate goals (shared goals neither could achieve alone). Henri Tajfel's social identity theory (1979) showed that merely categorising people into an in-group and out-group, even on a trivial basis (the "minimal group" paradigm), is enough to produce in-group favouritism, because group membership is part of self-esteem.

Exam trap

Robbers Cave (Sherif) = realistic conflict over real resources, cured by superordinate goals. Tajfel's social identity theory = bias arises from mere categorisation, no real competition needed. Both are Sherif-vs-Tajfel staples.

Section C · Group dynamics

These items appeared on the real Amity paper and reward precise name/date recall.

Kurt Lewin: two "father of" titles

Kurt Lewin is one of the most heavily tagged names in the paper because he holds two "father of" titles. He is the father of modern social psychology and the father of action research. Action research is his cyclical method of solving practical problems through repeated cycles of Plan → Act → Observe → Reflect.

Lewin also gave us several other named ideas. His field theory expresses behaviour as a function of the person and their environment: B = f(P, E). His force-field analysis models any situation as a balance between driving forces (pushing for change) and restraining forces (resisting it). His change model has three steps: Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze. And his leadership styles study (with Lippitt & White) identified three styles: autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire.

High-yield

Lewin's two titles appeared on the real paper. He is BOTH "father of modern social psychology" AND "father of action research" (Plan→Act→Observe→Reflect). Also remember B = f(P, E), force-field analysis, unfreeze-change-refreeze, and autocratic/democratic/laissez-faire.

For "father of social psychology" the distractors are usually Gordon Allport or Floyd Allport. The intended answer here is Lewin (modern social psychology), and Lewin specifically owns action research. If both Allport options and Lewin appear, the action-research clue locks it to Lewin.

Norman Triplett (1898): the first social-psychology experiment

Norman Triplett (1898) ran what is regarded as the first experiment in social psychology. Noticing that cyclists rode faster alongside others, he had children wind a fishing reel alone and in pairs, and found they wound faster in the presence of another. This is the founding demonstration of social facilitation.

High-yield

Triplett 1898 appeared on the real paper. Memorise the date (1898) and the apparatus (fishing reel) and the claim (the first social-psychology experiment, founding social facilitation).

Exam trap

Do NOT confuse Triplett (1898, fishing reel, social facilitation) with Ringelmann (rope-pulling, social loafing). Triplett = others make you faster; Ringelmann = others make you slack off. Opposite effects, different men.

Social facilitation vs social inhibition

The presence of others sometimes improves performance and sometimes worsens it. Robert Zajonc's drive theory (1965) resolved this: the mere presence of others raises arousal, and arousal strengthens the dominant response (the most likely/well-learned response in a situation). So on an easy or well-learned task the dominant response is correct, and you get social facilitation (better performance); on a hard or novel task the dominant response is often wrong, and you get social inhibition (worse performance). Nickolas Cottrell's evaluation-apprehension theory is the main rival explanation: it is not mere presence but the worry about being judged that raises arousal.

Exam trap

"The presence of others improves performance" by itself is wrong. It only boosts the dominant response, so it improves easy tasks and impairs hard ones. Zajonc = mere presence raises arousal; Cottrell = evaluation apprehension is the cause.

Social loafing vs social striving

Social loafing is the tendency to exert less individual effort when working in a group than when working alone, because individual contributions feel less identifiable. This is the Ringelmann effect, a finding rooted in individualist (Western) samples. Social striving is the reverse: in some collectivist cultures, people work harder in a group than alone, because the group's success matters more than personal credit.

High-yield

The testable point is the cross-cultural reversal: loafing (effort drops) is an individualist finding; striving (effort rises) can appear in collectivist cultures. Same group setting, opposite result depending on culture.

This part welds together the two halves of the entrance paper that examiners love because they reward precise vocabulary rather than essays: abnormal/clinical psychology and research methods/statistics. Section A · Abnormal & Clinical Foundations

1. What makes behaviour "abnormal"?

There is no single line that separates normal from abnormal. Psychology instead uses a cluster of criteria, the most examined of which is the four Ds. A behaviour leans toward abnormal as it accumulates more of them.

Definition

The Four Ds of abnormality: Deviance (the behaviour departs from social or statistical norms), Distress (it causes suffering to the person), Dysfunction (it impairs daily functioning, work, relationships, self-care), and Danger (risk of harm to self or others). Some texts add a fifth D, Duration.

Underneath the four Ds sit three competing conceptual approaches. The statistical approach calls a behaviour abnormal when it is rare, far from the average (e.g. an IQ far below the population mean); its flaw is that rare can also mean gifted, and common can be unhealthy. The social-norm (sociocultural) approach calls behaviour abnormal when it violates the rules and expectations of a culture; its flaw is cultural relativity, what is deviant in one society is unremarkable in another. The modern integrative view, Jerome Wakefield's harmful dysfunction model, says a disorder requires both an internal mechanism failing to perform its natural (evolved) function and harm to the person as judged by social values. Contemporary classification leans on a three-component view: clinically significant distress, dysfunction/disability, and dyscontrol (loss of behavioural or emotional control), with statistical rarity treated as supporting, not defining.

Exam trap

"Abnormal = simply rare/statistically uncommon" is the distractor. Statistical rarity alone is insufficient: a genius IQ is statistically rare but not a disorder. The defensible answer is the multi-criteria four Ds or harmful dysfunction, not pure deviance.

2. Classification systems: DSM-5-TR vs ICD-11

Two manuals carve mental disorders into named categories. The DSM-5-TR (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th edition, Text Revision) is published by the American Psychiatric Association (APA), originates in the United States, and is categorical (you either meet the criteria for a category or you don't). The ICD-11 (International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision) is published by the World Health Organization (WHO), is used internationally including for general medicine, and came into effect in January 2022. India officially uses the ICD system for health reporting.

3. Schizophrenia and the psychoses

Schizophrenia is a psychotic disorder marked by a break with reality. Its symptoms split into two families. Positive symptoms are added experiences not present in healthy people: hallucinations (perceptions without a stimulus, classically auditory voices), delusions (fixed false beliefs, e.g. persecution or grandeur), and disorganised speech/thought (derailment, word salad). Negative symptoms are reductions in normal functioning: flat/blunted affect (reduced emotional expression), avolition (loss of motivation/goal-directed activity), alogia (poverty of speech), and anhedonia (inability to feel pleasure).

Memory aid

Positive = symptoms added on (a plus sign); negative = normal function taken away (a minus). It has nothing to do with "good" or "bad". The four negatives cluster as the 4 A's: Affect (flat), Avolition, Alogia, Anhedonia.

Kurt Schneider's first-rank symptoms were historically treated as highly suggestive of schizophrenia: audible thoughts (thought echo), voices arguing or commenting on the person, thought withdrawal/insertion/broadcasting, "made" feelings or impulses imposed by an outside force, somatic passivity, and delusional perception. DSM-5 dropped the classic subtypes (paranoid, catatonic, disorganised/hebephrenic, undifferentiated, residual) because they were unstable and unreliable; schizophrenia is now rated on symptom dimensions instead. Biologically, the dopamine hypothesis proposes that positive symptoms arise from excess dopamine activity (especially mesolimbic), which is why dopamine-blocking antipsychotics reduce them.

Exam trap

Hallucinations and delusions are positive symptoms; flat affect, avolition, alogia and anhedonia are negative. A frequent trap pairs "positive" with "good outcome", it does not. Also note DSM-5 abolished the paranoid/catatonic subtypes.

4. Mood disorders

Mood disorders split into depressive and bipolar groups. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) requires a discrete episode of at least two weeks of depressed mood and/or loss of interest (anhedonia) plus associated symptoms (sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, worthlessness, suicidality). Persistent Depressive Disorder (dysthymia) is a chronic, lower-grade depressed mood lasting at least two years, less severe but longer. Depression-only presentations are termed unipolar.

In the bipolar disorders the mood swings to the high pole as well. Bipolar I requires at least one full manic episode (the depressive episode is common but not required). A manic episode is a distinct period (≥1 week, or any duration if hospitalisation is needed) of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood plus increased energy, with symptoms such as grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, and risky pleasure-seeking, severe enough to impair functioning or include psychosis. Bipolar II requires at least one hypomanic episode (a milder high, ≥4 days, no marked impairment, no psychosis) plus at least one major depressive episode.

High-yield

Bipolar I = full MANIA (can be without depression). Bipolar II = HYPOMANIA + major depression (never a full manic episode, if mania appears, it becomes Bipolar I). Unipolar = depression only.

5. The anxiety cluster and the DSM-5 reshuffle

The DSM-5 Anxiety Disorders chapter now contains: Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) (≥6 months of excessive, uncontrollable worry across many domains), Panic Disorder (recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus worry about future attacks), Specific Phobia (marked fear of a particular object/situation), Social Anxiety Disorder (social phobia) (fear of scrutiny/judgement), and Agoraphobia (fear of situations where escape is hard or help unavailable).

Exam trap

The headline DSM-5 change: OCD and PTSD are NO LONGER anxiety disorders. Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder moved into a new chapter, Obsessive-Compulsive & Related Disorders (with hoarding, body dysmorphic, trichotillomania, excoriation). PTSD moved into Trauma- & Stressor-Related Disorders (with acute stress disorder, adjustment disorders). In DSM-IV both sat under anxiety; the paper loves to ask "which of these is still an anxiety disorder?", the answer excludes OCD and PTSD.

6. Personality and neurodevelopmental disorders (brief)

Personality disorders are enduring, inflexible patterns of inner experience and behaviour that deviate from cultural expectation. They group into three clusters: Cluster A (odd/eccentric), paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal; Cluster B (dramatic/erratic), antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic; Cluster C (anxious/fearful), avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive personality disorder.

Memory aid

Clusters A-B-C: "Weird, Wild, Worried", A is weird/odd, B is wild/dramatic, C is worried/anxious. Note OCPD (Cluster C personality) is distinct from OCD (an OC-related disorder).

Neurodevelopmental disorders begin in the developmental period. Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) features persistent deficits in social communication plus restricted, repetitive behaviours/interests, DSM-5 merged the older Asperger's and autistic disorder into one spectrum. ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) features a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity with onset before age 12.

7. Psychoanalytic defence mechanisms

For Sigmund Freud, the ego protects itself from anxiety produced by conflict between id impulses, superego demands, and reality by deploying unconscious defence mechanisms. His daughter Anna Freud systematised and extended the list in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936). Repression is the master defence (pushing material out of awareness); sublimation is regarded as the most mature/healthy one (channelling unacceptable impulses into socially valued activity).

Section B · Research Methods & Statistics

8. Variables

An experiment manipulates one thing to see its effect on another. The independent variable (IV) is the one the researcher manipulates, the presumed cause. The dependent variable (DV) is the one that is measured, the presumed effect, which "depends on" the IV. An extraneous variable is any other variable that could influence the DV; when an extraneous variable systematically varies with the IV and offers a rival explanation, it becomes a confounding variable. An operational definition states precisely how a variable is measured or manipulated (e.g. "anxiety = score on the GAD-7"), making the study replicable.

Memory aid

The DV Depends on the IV. The experimenter Introduces/manipulates the IV. "I cause, D depends."

Exam trap

Reversing IV and DV is the classic slip. In "effect of sleep on memory", sleep (manipulated) is the IV, memory score (measured) is the DV. A confounder is not any extraneous variable, only one that co-varies with the IV and supplies an alternative explanation.

9. Reliability vs validity

Reliability is consistency: does the test give the same result on repetition? Its forms are test-retest (same test, two occasions), inter-rater (different scorers agree), internal consistency (items measure the same construct, indexed by Cronbach's alpha), and split-half (two halves of the test correlate). Validity is accuracy: does the test measure what it claims? Its forms are content validity (items cover the whole domain), construct validity (it taps the underlying theoretical trait), and criterion validity, which splits into concurrent (correlates with a current criterion) and predictive (forecasts a future outcome).

A test can be reliable without being valid, but it cannot be truly valid without being reliable. A broken scale stuck at 5 kg is perfectly reliable (consistent) yet invalid (wrong). Reliability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for validity.

10. Descriptive statistics and skew

Measures of central tendency summarise the "typical" value: the mean (arithmetic average, sensitive to outliers), the median (middle value when ordered, robust to outliers), and the mode (most frequent value). Measures of dispersion describe spread: the range (max − min), the variance (mean of squared deviations), and the standard deviation (SD) (the square root of variance, in the original units). Central tendency without dispersion is half the story; two datasets can share a mean yet differ wildly in spread.

Skew describes asymmetry, and its direction is named after the tail. In a positive (right) skew the long tail points to the high end, dragging the mean upward, so the order is mean > median > mode. In a negative (left) skew the tail points low, so mean < median < mode. The mean is always pulled toward the tail because it is the only one of the three sensitive to extreme scores.

11. The normal curve

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the bell-shaped curve that is perfectly symmetrical, so its mean = median = mode all coincide at the centre. It is governed by the empirical (68-95-99.7) rule: about 68% of cases fall within ±1 SD of the mean, about 95% within ±2 SD, and about 99.7% within ±3 SD. A z-score expresses any raw score as the number of standard deviations it lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean: z = (X − mean) / SD. A z of 0 is exactly average; a z of +2 is two SDs above the mean.

High-yield

Normal curve: symmetrical, mean = median = mode, and 68-95-99.7 within 1/2/3 SD. The total area under the curve = 1 (100%). Z-score = standardised distance from the mean in SD units.

12. Inferential statistics: hypotheses, errors, significance

Inferential statistics test whether a finding generalises beyond the sample. The null hypothesis (H₀) states there is no effect or no difference; the alternative hypothesis (H₁) states there is one. We never "prove" H₁, we either reject or fail to reject H₀ based on probability. The p-value is the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as observed if H₀ were true; when p falls below the chosen significance level alpha (α, usually .05), the result is "statistically significant" and we reject H₀.

Two decision errors are possible. A Type I error is a false positive: rejecting a true null, claiming an effect that isn't real. Its probability equals α. A Type II error is a false negative: retaining a false null, missing a real effect. Its probability is beta (β). Statistical power (1 − β) is the probability of correctly detecting a true effect.

Think of a courtroom where H₀ = "innocent". Type I = convicting an innocent person (false positive). Type II = letting a guilty person go free (false negative). Or: Type I has one wrong (you saw something that wasn't there); Type II you were too cautious (you missed it).

Exam trap

Type I = false positive = reject a TRUE null (probability α). Type II = false negative = retain a FALSE null (probability β). Examiners swap these constantly. Lowering α (being stricter) reduces Type I but raises Type II.

13. Correlation

A correlation coefficient (r) quantifies the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables. It ranges from −1 to +1: +1 is a perfect positive relationship (both rise together), −1 is a perfect negative/inverse relationship (one rises as the other falls), and 0 means no linear relationship. The sign gives direction; the absolute value gives strength. Crucially, correlation does not imply causation: a relationship may be driven by a third variable or by reverse causation.

Exam trap

r ranges −1 to +1, never beyond. r = 0 means no LINEAR relationship, not "no relationship at all", a strong curved (U-shaped) relationship can still give r ≈ 0. And correlation ≠ causation.

Statistical tests split into parametric (assume normally distributed interval/ratio data) and non-parametric (distribution-free, for ordinal/nominal data). Common parametric tests: the t-test (compares two group means) and ANOVA (compares three or more means). A common non-parametric test: chi-square (χ²), used for categorical/frequency data.

14. Sampling

A sample is the subset of a population actually studied. In probability sampling every member has a known, non-zero chance of selection, which supports generalisation: simple random (every member equally likely), stratified (population split into strata, sampled in proportion), systematic (every kth member from a list), and cluster (whole naturally occurring groups selected). In non-probability sampling selection is not random, so it risks bias: convenience (whoever is available), purposive (chosen for a specific characteristic), snowball (participants recruit others), and quota (preset numbers per category, filled non-randomly).

High-yield

Probability = random selection (random, stratified, systematic, cluster) → generalisable. Non-probability = non-random (convenience, purposive, snowball, quota) → cheaper but biased. Quota looks like stratified but is non-random in the final pick.

15. Research designs

Designs differ in how much control and inference they allow. An experimental design manipulates the IV and uses random assignment, permitting causal conclusions. A correlational design measures variables as they naturally occur and reports associations, but cannot establish cause. An observational design records behaviour without manipulation (naturalistic or structured). On the time axis, a cross-sectional study compares different groups at one point in time (fast, but confounds age with cohort), whereas a longitudinal study follows the same participants over time (captures genuine change, but is slow and suffers attrition/dropout).

Exam trap

Only the experimental design (manipulation + random assignment + control) supports causal claims. Correlational and observational designs show relationships only. Cross-sectional = many groups, one time; longitudinal = one group, many times.

High-yield

7.1 Orientation: the six darshanas and where psychology lives

Classical Indian thought is organised into six orthodox philosophical systems, the shad darshanas ("six visions"). They are āstika ("orthodox") schools because they accept the authority of the Vedas, as opposed to the nāstika ("heterodox") systems such as Buddhism, Jainism, and the materialist Charvaka, which do not. The six are traditionally paired: Nyaya and Vaisheshika (logic and atomism), Samkhya and Yoga, and Mimamsa and Vedanta (ritual interpretation and the philosophy of the Upanishads).

You do not need to master all six. What you need is to know which schools carry the psychological content, the material about the self, mind, consciousness, and personality. That content sits in three. Samkhya gives the metaphysical scaffolding: the two ultimate categories Purusha and Prakriti, and the three gunas that make up everything in nature, including the mind. Yoga (the system of Patanjali) turns Samkhya's map into an applied psychology, a practical method for controlling and stilling the mind. Vedanta (the philosophy distilled from the Upanishads) gives the model of the self in layers (the koshas), the states of consciousness, the inner instrument (antahkarana), and the identity of Atman and Brahman.

So when the paper says "Indian psychology", it does not mean a separate modern discipline. It means the psychological doctrines embedded in these traditions: how they conceive the self, the structure of the mind, the nature of consciousness, and the typology of personality. Everything below is that content, taught from the system it comes from.

7.2 The Trigunas (from Sāṁkhya): the three strands of nature

Samkhya holds that all of manifest nature, Prakriti, is woven from three fundamental qualities or strands called the gunas. The word guna literally means "strand" or "rope", and the image is exactly that: three strands twisted together into the single rope of experienced reality. The three are Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas.

Sattva is the quality of purity, clarity, harmony, balance, and knowledge. When sattva dominates, the mind is calm, luminous, and discerning; it is associated with wisdom, contentment, and lightness. Rajas is the quality of activity, passion, movement, and restlessness. When rajas dominates, there is drive, ambition, desire, agitation, and emotional turbulence; it is the principle of motion and craving. Tamas is the quality of inertia, dullness, heaviness, and darkness. When tamas dominates, there is laziness, sleep, confusion, ignorance, and resistance to change; it is the principle that obscures and weighs down.

The crucial doctrinal point, which the paper loves to test, is that everyone has all three gunas in varying proportion. The gunas are not three types of people in a rigid sense; they are three ingredients present in every mind and every object, and a person's temperament reflects which one currently predominates. They are also in constant flux: a single person is more sattvic in calm clear moments, more rajasic when driven or angry, more tamasic when dull or sleepy. Because the gunas belong to Prakriti, they characterise the body and mind, never the pure witnessing consciousness (this is the link to 7.3 below).

Indian-approaches research has operationalised this into formal psychometrics. The Guna scales (for example, instruments developed in the Indian psychology literature to measure sattva, rajas, and tamas as personality dimensions) attempt to assess a person's guna profile much as Western inventories assess traits. You should recognise that the trigunas are not only a philosophical idea but a measured personality model in the formal study of Indian approaches to personality.

7.3 Purusha vs Prakriti: Sāṁkhya dualism

Samkhya is fundamentally a dualist system. It says reality reduces to two ultimate, irreducible categories. Purusha is pure consciousness, the witness. It is inactive, attributeless, changeless, and merely observes. Importantly, in classical Samkhya Purusha is plural: there are many purushas, one for each conscious being, unlike Vedanta's single universal consciousness. Purusha does nothing; it simply illuminates, the way a lamp lights a room without acting on it.

Prakriti is primordial matter or nature, the active, creative principle. Prakriti is unconscious but dynamic, and it is Prakriti that carries the three gunas described above. In its unmanifest state the three gunas are in perfect equilibrium; when that balance is disturbed (in the presence of Purusha), Prakriti begins to evolve and unfolds into the whole manifest world, intellect, ego, mind, the senses, the elements, the body. Everything you can perceive, including your own mind and personality, is a product of Prakriti.

The manifest world arises from the interaction of these two. Purusha's mere presence, like a magnet near iron filings, disturbs Prakriti's equilibrium and sets evolution in motion; consciousness gets "reflected" in the evolving mind, producing the illusion that the witness is the doer. Bondage is precisely this confusion: pure consciousness mistaking itself for the activities of Prakriti. Liberation (kaivalya, "aloneness/isolation") is achieved through discriminative knowledge (viveka), the clear discernment that separates Purusha from Prakriti, so that consciousness recognises it was never the doer and rests in its own nature.

7.4 The Pancha Kosha: the five sheaths of the self

The doctrine of the five sheaths comes from the Taittiriya Upanishad and is a central Vedantic model of the self. The idea is that the true Self (Atman) is wrapped in five concentric coverings or koshas ("sheaths"), arranged from the grossest and most external to the subtlest and most internal, like the layers of an onion. Spiritual realisation involves seeing through each sheath in turn until the Atman, which is none of them, is realised beyond and through them.

The five, in order from gross to subtle, are: Annamaya kosha, the food sheath, the physical body built from and sustained by food (anna = food). Pranamaya kosha, the vital-energy sheath, the layer of breath and life-force (prana) that animates the body. Manomaya kosha, the mental sheath, the layer of mind and emotions, of sensory processing, feeling, and ordinary thought (manas = mind). Vijnanamaya kosha, the wisdom/intellect sheath, the layer of vijnana, intellect, discrimination, judgement, the higher knowing faculty. Anandamaya kosha, the bliss sheath, the subtlest layer, associated with deep peace and bliss (ananda), the covering closest to the Self.

Note the trajectory carefully: it runs body → bliss, gross → subtle, outer → inner. The physical body is outermost because it is the densest; bliss is innermost because it is the subtlest and nearest to Atman. The Atman itself is not a sixth sheath; it is the consciousness that the sheaths cover, realised when one no longer identifies with any of them.

7.5 The Four Avasthas: states of consciousness

The Mandukya Upanishad, the shortest of the principal Upanishads, sets out four states (avasthas) of consciousness, mapping them onto the sacred syllable AUM (OM). The first three are familiar experiential states; the fourth is the transcendent ground.

Jagrat is the waking state, in which consciousness is turned outward to the gross physical world through the senses. Swapna is the dream state, in which consciousness is turned inward and experiences a subtle world of mental impressions, with no external input. Sushupti is deep dreamless sleep, a state of undifferentiated rest in which there are no dreams and no duality of subject and object, experienced as peace but without awareness. And Turiya, literally "the fourth", is pure consciousness itself, the substratum that underlies and transcends the other three. Turiya is not a fourth experience alongside waking, dreaming, and sleeping; it is the silent awareness that is present in and beyond all of them. In the AUM mapping, the three sounds A-U-M correspond to waking, dream, and deep sleep, and Turiya is the silence after the sound of OM, the soundless awareness in which the syllable arises and subsides.

7.6 Antahkarana: the inner instrument of mind

Antahkarana means the "inner instrument" or inner organ, the internal apparatus of mind in Vedantic (and Samkhya-derived) psychology. It is the mental machinery through which consciousness functions, classically analysed into four functions. The key conceptual point is that the antahkarana is an instrument, a tool of Prakriti, not the conscious Self that uses it.

The four functions are: Manas, the sensory and processing mind, which receives sense impressions, coordinates the senses, and handles doubt, desire, and the back-and-forth of ordinary thinking. Buddhi, the intellect, the faculty of discrimination, judgement, decision, and determination, that which concludes and decides after manas has wavered. Ahamkara, the ego or "I-maker" (aham = I), the function that appropriates experience and generates the sense of "I" and "mine", identifying the Self with the body-mind. Chitta, the memory store or "mind-stuff", the reservoir of impressions (samskaras) where past experiences are stored and from which they resurface.

7.7 Atman and Brahman: the Vedantic identity

Vedanta's central teaching concerns two terms. Atman is the individual true Self, the innermost consciousness, what you really are beneath the koshas and behind the antahkarana. Brahman is the ultimate, infinite reality, the absolute ground of everything. The decisive claim of the Upanishads is that these two are, at the deepest level, identical. This is captured in the great saying (mahavakya) "Tat Tvam Asi", "That thou art", you are That, the individual Self is the universal reality.

The school that takes this most strictly is Advaita Vedanta ("non-dualism"), systematised by Shankara (Adi Shankaracharya). Advaita holds that there is only one reality without a second: Atman is Brahman, and the apparent multiplicity of the world is maya, a superimposition produced by ignorance. Liberation (moksha) is the direct realisation of this non-difference. Note the contrast with Samkhya: Samkhya is dualist (many purushas, separate from prakriti), whereas Advaita Vedanta is strictly non-dualist (one consciousness, Atman = Brahman). Do not let the paper blur these.

Advaita = "not-two", non-dualism. Atman (individual Self) and Brahman (ultimate reality) are one and the same; the sense of separateness is ignorance. The mahavakya expressing it is Tat Tvam Asi ("That thou art").

7.8 Yoga and Patanjali: the applied psychology of mind-control

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali are the foundational text of the Yoga darshana. Yoga shares Samkhya's metaphysics (Purusha, Prakriti, the gunas) but adds a practical method, which is why it functions as an applied Indian psychology of the mind. Patanjali's most famous definition, in the second sutra, is "yogaḥ citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ": yoga is the stilling (nirodha) of the modifications (vrittis) of the mind-stuff (chitta). The mind is normally a stream of fluctuations, perceptions, thoughts, memories, imaginings; yoga is the systematic quieting of those fluctuations so that the witnessing consciousness rests in its own nature.

The method is the eight limbs (ashtanga): Yama (ethical restraints toward others), Niyama (personal observances/disciplines), Asana (steady posture), Pranayama (regulation of breath), Pratyahara (withdrawal of the senses from objects), Dharana (concentration, fixing attention on a single point), Dhyana (sustained meditation, an unbroken flow of attention), and Samadhi (absorption, the union in which the meditator and object merge and the mind is fully stilled). The eight progress from outer ethical/physical discipline toward inner mental absorption.

Memory aid

Eight limbs in order: "Young Ninjas Always Practise Precise Deep Dedicated Stunts", Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi. The last three (Dharana → Dhyana → Samadhi) are the inner, concentrative trio. And remember the one-line definition: yoga = chitta vritti nirodha, stilling the modifications of the mind.

7.9 The Purusharthas: the indigenous model of motivation and well-being

Indian thought frames human motivation and a well-lived life through the four purusharthas, the legitimate aims of human life. Dharma is duty, righteousness, moral and social order, living in accordance with what is right. Artha is wealth, prosperity, and the material means of life. Kama is pleasure, desire, and the enjoyment of the senses and emotions. Moksha is liberation, release from the cycle of birth and death, the ultimate spiritual goal. Together they form an indigenous model of motivation and well-being: a balanced life pursues prosperity (artha) and pleasure (kama) within the bounds of righteousness (dharma), while orienting ultimately toward liberation (moksha). This contrasts usefully with Western need hierarchies, here the highest aim is explicitly spiritual liberation, not self-actualisation in the secular sense.

Memory aid

Four aims: Dharma (duty), Artha (wealth), Kama (pleasure), Moksha (liberation). Dharma frames the lot; Moksha crowns it.

7.10 RCI history: the tested date pair

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) is the statutory body that regulates and monitors training of rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists) and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register. For Indian clinical psychology this is directly relevant: RCI is the body that licenses and registers clinical psychologists in India, and practising as one requires RCI registration. So its history is fair game, and the paper tests a specific date pair.

The Act gave RCI the legal authority to regulate training programmes, prescribe standards, register professionals, and take action against unqualified practitioners. Section A · Grammar

Subject–verb agreement

Definition

A verb must agree in number with its subject, not with whatever noun happens to sit closest to it. Singular subject takes a singular verb; plural subject takes a plural verb.

The single most common trap is the noun between the subject and the verb. In The box of chocolates is on the table, the subject is box (singular), not chocolates. The phrase of chocolates merely describes the box. Mentally strike out the intervening phrase and the right verb becomes obvious: The box … is.

Indefinite pronouns built on -one, -body, and -thing are singular: everyone, somebody, nothing, each, either, neither. So is the construction one of. The expressions each of, every one of, either of, neither of, one of all take a singular verb: Each of the students is responsible; Neither of the answers is correct.

Collective nouns (team, committee, family, jury, government) take a singular verb when the group acts as a single unit, which is the usual case in Indian and exam English: The committee has decided. Use a plural only when members act separately, and even then most exams expect the singular.

High-yield

A number of = many → plural verb (A number of students are absent). The number of = a single figure → singular verb (The number of students is rising). Also: subjects joined by and are plural; subjects joined by or / nor / either…or / neither…nor agree with the nearer subject.

Memory aid

"A number = Are. The number = The verb is singular." And for the intervening-phrase trap: cross out any phrase starting with of, with, along with, as well as, including before you choose the verb. These add-ons never change the number of the subject: The manager, along with his staff, is attending.

Q1. Choose the verb: Each of the candidates (has / have) submitted the form.
Answer: has. Each of is singular regardless of the plural candidates that follows it.

Q2. Choose the verb: The list of items (was / were) lost.
Answer: was. The subject is list (singular); of items is a modifying phrase. Strike it out: The list … was lost.

Q3. Choose the verb: Neither the teacher nor the students (is / are) ready.
Answer: are. With neither…nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, which is students (plural).

Q4. Choose the verb: A number of patients (is / are) waiting.
Answer: are. A number of means "many" and is plural.

Q5. Choose the verb: The jury (has / have) reached its verdict.
Answer: has. The jury acts as one unit, signalled by the singular its verdict.

Tenses and sequence of tenses

Definition

Sequence of tenses: when the main clause is in a past tense, the verb in the subordinate clause normally shifts to a past form too (He said that he was tired, not is tired). The exception is a universal or still-true fact, which may stay present (The teacher said that water boils at 100°C).

Keep tenses consistent within a passage unless the meaning genuinely changes time. A frequent error is drifting between past and present in the same narration: She entered the room and sees the report is wrong; make it entered … saw. Watch the present perfect, which links the past to now and never takes a finished-time marker: write I have lived here since 2019 or I lived here in 2019, never I have lived here in 2019. After since use a point in time; after for use a duration (since Monday, for three days).

Exam trap

Reported speech back-shift: will→would, can→could, may→might, has→had, is→was, do→did. A question like She told me she ___ come wants would, not will.

Q1. Correct it: When I reached the station, the train already left.
Answer: … the train had already left. The leaving happened before the reaching, so the earlier action takes the past perfect (had left).

Q2. Choose: He has been working here (since / for) five years.
Answer: for. Five years is a duration. Since would need a start point such as since 2021.

Q3. Correct it: The scientist explained that the earth revolved around the sun.
Answer: Better as revolves. A permanent truth stays in the present even after a past reporting verb.

Q4. Choose: By the time we arrive, the meeting (will end / will have ended).
Answer: will have ended. The ending is completed before the future point of arrival, so use the future perfect.

Pronouns: who vs whom, agreement, reference

Definition

Who is the subject form (it does the action); whom is the object form (it receives the action or follows a preposition).

The reliable test: answer the embedded question with he/him or she/her. If he/she fits, use who; if him/her fits, use whom. (Who/Whom) did you call? → "I called him" → whom. (Who/Whom) is calling? → "He is calling" → who.

A pronoun must agree with its antecedent (the noun it stands for) in number and person: Every student must bring his or her ID (because every student is singular). And every pronoun must point clearly to one noun. When Ravi met Sameer, he was nervous is ambiguous: who was nervous? Rewrite to name the person.

Memory aid

"m goes with m": whoM pairs with hiM. If the answer ends in m (him, them, whom), they all belong together.

Q1. Choose: (Who / Whom) should I contact for the form?
Answer: Whom. Test: "I should contact him" → him fits, so whom.

Q2. Choose: The doctor (who / whom) treated me has retired.
Answer: who. Test: "He treated me" → he fits, so who (it is the subject of treated).

Q3. Fix the agreement: Each of the girls forgot their umbrella.
Answer: her umbrella. Each is singular, so the pronoun must be singular (her).

Q4. Fix the ambiguity: The manager told the clerk that he had made a mistake.
Answer: Rewrite to remove the unclear he, e.g. The manager admitted to the clerk that he, the manager, had made a mistake, or recast entirely. As written, he could mean either man.

Modifiers: misplaced and dangling

Definition

A misplaced modifier sits next to the wrong word and so describes the wrong thing. A dangling modifier describes a subject that is not actually present in the sentence.

Place a modifier right beside the word it describes. She almost drove the children to school every day (misplaced almost) wrongly suggests she nearly drove but did not; meaning She drove the children to school almost every day. The classic dangling case is an opening -ing phrase whose doer is missing: Walking to the station, the rain started implies the rain was walking. Fix by naming the real subject right after the comma: Walking to the station, I felt the rain start.

Exam trap

An introductory phrase ending in a comma must be followed immediately by the person or thing it describes. Having studied all night, the exam felt easy is wrong (the exam did not study). Correct: Having studied all night, she found the exam easy.

Q1. Fix it: Running down the road, the bag fell from his hand.
Answer: Running down the road, he dropped the bag (or As he ran down the road, the bag fell). The bag was not running; name the runner after the comma.

Q2. Fix it: I only ate two biscuits. (meaning: just two, no more)
Answer: I ate only two biscuits. Put only next to what it limits, the number two.

Q3. Fix it: To improve your score, the questions must be practised daily.
Answer: To improve your score, you must practise the questions daily. The opening phrase needs a person (you) who can do the improving.

Prepositions and articles

Definition

Use a/an for one non-specific countable thing, the for something specific or already known, and no article for general plural or uncountable nouns. Choose a or an by the sound of the next word, not its spelling.

An goes before a vowel sound: an hour, an honest man, an MBA (the M sounds like "em"). A goes before a consonant sound even when the letter is a vowel: a university, a one-rupee coin, a European (these start with a "y" or "w" sound). Use the for things already mentioned or unique: the sun, the Prime Minister, the book I lent you.

Prepositions are mostly fixed collocations you memorise. High-frequency pairs: good at, afraid of, interested in, depend on, consist of, accuse someone of, capable of, similar to, different from, married to, congratulate on, comply with, succeed in, suffer from.

Memory aid

"An before a vowel sound" — say the word aloud. An honest (silent h) but a hotel (sounded h); a university (yoo-) but an umbrella (uh-).

Q1. Choose: She is (a / an) honest officer.
Answer: an. The h in honest is silent, so the word begins with a vowel sound.

Q2. Choose: He has been working here for (a / an) year.
Answer: a. Year begins with a "y" consonant sound.

Q3. Choose the preposition: The patient was suffering ___ depression.
Answer: from. The fixed collocation is suffer from.

Q4. Choose: Her results are different ___ mine.
Answer: from. Standard exam English prefers different from.

Comparisons and parallelism

Definition

Fewer counts separate items (countable nouns); less measures an amount (uncountable nouns). Parallelism: items in a list or comparison must share the same grammatical form.

Write fewer mistakes, fewer people, fewer calories (you can count them) but less time, less water, less effort (you measure them). So fewer mistakes is right, less mistakes is wrong. The same logic separates many (countable) from much (uncountable), and number (countable) from amount (uncountable).

Parallel structure keeps a series in one form. She likes reading, writing, and to paint breaks parallelism; make it reading, writing, and painting. Correlative pairs must bracket matching forms: not only…but also, either…or, both…and. He is not only clever but also works hard is unbalanced; fix to not only clever but also hard-working. In comparisons, compare like with like: Her salary is higher than mine, not than me or than her colleague (which would compare salary to a person).

Memory aid

"Fewer = number, less = amount." If you can put a number in front (three mistakes), use fewer. If you cannot (three waters?), use less.

Q1. Choose: This route has (fewer / less) traffic lights.
Answer: fewer. Traffic lights are countable.

Q2. Choose: Drink (fewer / less) water during the test.
Answer: less. Water is uncountable (an amount).

Q3. Fix the parallelism: The therapist's goals were to listen, supporting, and to advise.
Answer: to listen, to support, and to advise (or listening, supporting, and advising). All three must take the same form.

Q4. Fix it: He is taller than me. (formal exam English)
Answer: taller than I (am). The comparison is between two subjects, so use the subject pronoun I.

Conditionals and common confusions

Definition

The three standard conditionals: First (real future) — If + present, will + base: If it rains, we will stay in. Second (unreal/hypothetical present) — If + past, would + base: If I were rich, I would travel. Third (unreal past) — If + past perfect, would have + past participle: If I had known, I would have come.

Never put will in the if-clause (If it will rain is wrong). In the second conditional use were for all persons (If I were you). A common confusion is mixing the second and third forms; keep each half on the same level of reality.

Q1. Complete: If she ___ harder, she would pass.
Answer: studied. This is a second conditional (hypothetical present): If + past, would + base.

Q2. Complete: If they had left earlier, they ___ the flight.
Answer: would have caught. Third conditional (unreal past): would have + past participle.

Q3. Fix it: If it will be sunny, we will go out.
Answer: If it is sunny, we will go out. The if-clause of a first conditional takes the present, not will.

Section C · Reading comprehension

High-yield

Read the questions first, then scan the passage for the relevant lines. Answer strictly from the text, never from your own opinion or outside knowledge. Identify the question type: main idea (the whole passage's point), detail (a stated fact), inference (what must be true but is not stated outright), tone (the author's attitude), or vocabulary-in-context (what a word means here).

Main-idea answers are broad but not too broad: reject options that cover only one paragraph and options that go beyond the passage. Inference answers must be supported by the text yet not directly quoted; if you cannot point to lines that force the conclusion, it is wrong. Tone answers turn on signal words (unfortunately, remarkably, admittedly). For vocabulary-in-context, ignore the dictionary's first meaning and substitute each option into the sentence.

Worked passage. Resilience is often misread as toughness, as if it meant feeling nothing in the face of hardship. In truth, resilient people feel loss as keenly as anyone; what distinguishes them is the capacity to recover function while the pain is still present. Far from suppressing emotion, they acknowledge it and act anyway. This is why support networks matter so much: recovery is rarely a solitary achievement.

Q1 (main idea). The passage is mainly about: (a) why toughness is a myth, (b) what resilience really involves, (c) how to suppress emotion, (d) the dangers of solitude.
Answer: (b). The passage defines and corrects the meaning of resilience throughout; (a) and (d) are details, and (c) contradicts the text.

Q2 (inference). The passage implies that resilient people: (a) do not feel pain, (b) recover faster by avoiding others, (c) function despite ongoing pain, (d) never need help.
Answer: (c). The text says they "recover function while the pain is still present." (a) and (d) are directly contradicted; (b) reverses the point about support networks.

Q3 (vocabulary-in-context). In this passage, keenly most nearly means: (a) sharply / intensely, (b) eagerly, (c) cleverly, (d) reluctantly.
Answer: (a). "Feel loss keenly" means feel it intensely. Keen can mean eager elsewhere, but context here is about depth of feeling.

Section D · Verbal reasoning

Para-jumbles and sentence ordering

High-yield

Find the opening sentence first: it introduces a noun in full (not a pronoun), contains no back-referring word, and does not start with an orphan connector like this, therefore, however, so, also. Then chain the rest by pronoun antecedents and linking words. The closing sentence usually carries thus, finally, in conclusion or resolves the point.

Worked jumble. Order these four sentences:
(P) However, it was abandoned within a year due to lack of funding.
(Q) In 1998, the city launched an ambitious recycling programme.
(R) This failure taught planners a lasting lesson about budgeting.
(S) It aimed to cut landfill waste by half.

Answer: Q, S, P, R. Q is the opener: it names the programme in full and gives the date, with no back-reference. S follows because It = the programme and explains its aim. P begins with However and reverses the aim (abandoned), so it must come after the aim is stated. R opens with This failure, referring to the abandonment in P, and closes with the lesson. The chain of pronouns (It → It → This failure) confirms the order.

Cloze and fill-in-the-blanks

Use three checks together: grammar (does the form fit?), collocation (does the word pair naturally?), and sense (does the sentence's logic point one way?). Watch contrast words (but, although, however) that demand an opposite, and cause words (because, since) that demand a consistent reason.

Q1. Although the medicine was effective, its side effects were so ___ that doctors hesitated to prescribe it. (a) mild (b) severe (c) pleasant (d) minor
Answer: (b) severe. Although signals contrast with "effective," and only severe effects would make doctors hesitate.

Q2. She is highly ___ to criticism and takes even gentle feedback to heart. (a) immune (b) sensitive (c) indifferent (d) hostile
Answer: (b) sensitive. "Takes feedback to heart" defines the blank; sensitive to is also the right collocation.

Q3. The committee will ___ the proposal before making a final decision. (a) reject (b) review (c) ignore (d) celebrate
Answer: (b) review. "Before making a final decision" implies examination, not a verdict yet, so review fits the logic and collocates with proposal.

A · Social & Group Dynamics (off your paper)

Full teaching in Part Five. The paper tested these as single-fact and trap-pair items.

Kurt Lewin carries two "father of" titles. Father of modern social psychology and father of action research (the Plan → Act → Observe → Reflect cycle). He also gave field theory, B = f(P, E) (behaviour is a function of the person and the environment), force-field analysis, the unfreeze → change → refreeze model of planned change, and the three leadership styles (autocratic / democratic / laissez-faire).
Trap: they offer Gordon Allport or Floyd Allport for "father of social psychology." On this paper Lewin is the one tagged, and he owns action research outright.

Norman Triplett (1898), the fishing-reel experiment. The first social-psychology experiment. Cyclists and children wound a fishing reel faster in the presence of others, the founding demonstration of social facilitation.
Trap: the date (1898) and the apparatus (fishing reel) are both testable. Do not confuse it with Ringelmann's rope-pulling study, which is social loafing.

Social facilitation vs social inhibition, Zajonc's drive theory. The presence of others raises arousal; arousal strengthens the dominant response. On an easy or well-learned task the dominant response is correct, so performance improves (facilitation); on a hard or novel task the dominant response is often wrong, so performance worsens (inhibition).
Trap: "the presence of others improves performance" on its own is wrong, it only boosts the dominant response. Cottrell's evaluation-apprehension is the rival explanation they may name.

Social loafing vs social striving. Loafing = individuals exert less effort in a group (the Ringelmann effect, an individualist-culture finding). Striving = in some collectivist cultures effort rises in a group. That cross-cultural reversal is the advanced half they test.
Trap: assuming loafing is universal.

Tuckman's stages of group development. Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning. Your list flagged Storming specifically: the conflict and jostling-for-position stage, which is stage 2.
