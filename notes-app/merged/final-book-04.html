In 1920 a 22-year-old American named Gordon Allport was travelling home after a year teaching at Robert College in Istanbul. He stopped in Vienna to see one of his brothers and, on an impulse, sent a note to Sigmund Freud asking whether he might call. Freud, then in his sixties and already world famous, invited him over. When Allport entered the study, Freud said nothing at all. He simply sat and waited for the young visitor to explain why he had come.

The silence stretched until Allport, desperate for something to say, described an incident he had just seen on the tram. A small boy had seemed terrified of dirt: everything looked dirty to him, he changed his seat, and he told his mother not to let a dirty man sit beside him. Freud studied the neat, carefully groomed young man in front of him and asked, "Was that little boy you?"

Allport was shaken. He had told the story only to fill a silence; Freud had heard it as a disguised confession of his visitor's own unconscious fears. Allport denied for the rest of his life that he was the little boy, yet 47 years later he still wrote that his "single encounter with Freud was traumatic". The meeting convinced him that psychoanalysis dug too deep, and that psychology should first take people's conscious motives at face value. In 1922 he finished a Harvard dissertation on personality traits, the first research on traits in the United States, and went on to found the trait approach. Two great personality theorists had heard the same small story and seen entirely different things in it. Which of them was right is a question this whole chapter keeps asking.

4.1 What Personality Is: Definitions, Approaches and Early Type TheoriesLower yield · 4 Qs

You call a cousin moody and a friend a born leader, and you use those labels to predict what each will do next. Personality psychology does the same job with evidence. This section defines personality and its neighbouring terms, maps the approaches that fill this chapter, and ends with the oldest answers, which sorted people into types by body fluids, faces and physiques.

Persona and Allport's definition

The word personality comes from the Latin persona, the mask a Roman actor wore to show which character he was playing. The origin points to the public face we show others, and it raises the question every theory in this chapter must answer: is personality the mask, or what lies behind it?

The most quoted definition is that of Gordon Willard Allport (1897 to 1967), who weighed some 50 earlier definitions and offered his own in 1937: personality is "the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment". In 1961 he replaced the last phrase with "characteristic behavior and thought". Dynamic organisation means personality grows, but in an organised way; psychophysical means mind and body work together; determine means it actively directs behaviour.

A working definition: personality is the relatively enduring, distinctive pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviour that characterises a person, with internal aspects (motives, emotions) and external aspects (what others see), consistent across situations and over time. "Relatively" matters: a calm friend may still panic in an exam hall.

Temperament, character, trait and type

Temperament is the biologically based, early-appearing and relatively stable style of emotional reacting: how easily a person is aroused, how strongly they respond and what their usual mood is. Allport counted it, with physique and intelligence, as the inherited raw material of personality; infant temperament (Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess's easy, difficult and slow-to-warm-up babies) is in Chapter 6. Character is personality judged against moral standards; in Allport's phrase, "character is personality evaluated, and personality is character devaluated". A trait is a stable dimension along which people differ consistently, such as sociability; a type is a discrete category into which a person is placed, such as "melancholic". Exam trap

"Biologically based", "inborn" or "visible from infancy" in a stem means temperament, not disposition (too loose a word), character (learned and moral) or trait (a descriptive dimension, not defined by biological origin). "Personality evaluated" is character.

What theories do: a map of the approaches

A personality theory is a set of related ideas that accounts for personality. A good one describes how people differ, explains why, predicts what a person will do and generates research that can test it. No theory does all four equally well, so the field is a family of approaches (Table 4.2, the map of this chapter).

Six questions about human nature

Behind every theory lies an image of human nature. Duane Schultz and Sydney Ellen Schultz compare theorists on six questions: free will versus determinism (do we direct our lives?), nature versus nurture (inheritance or upbringing?), past versus present (is the adult fixed by childhood, the view called historical determinism?), uniqueness versus universality, equilibrium versus growth (do we only reduce tension, or strive to grow?) and optimism versus pessimism (are people basically good?).

Freud gave the darkest answers (Figure 4.1): behaviour determined by instincts and the first five years, life a struggle to reduce tension, people seen pessimistically; on nature-nurture and uniqueness he took a middle position. The humanists stand near the opposite pole: Carl Rogers saw people as free, shaped more by the present than by childhood, growing and basically good.

Determinants of personality

Heredity supplies temperament and more: twin studies (identical versus fraternal twins) and adoption studies (children compared with biological and adoptive parents) suggest genes account for up to about half the variation in traits (Section 4.7). Environment shapes the rest: the family (parenting style, parental models, and birth order, which Alfred Adler stressed; Section 4.4), culture (individualist and collectivist societies reward different traits) and social class (opportunities, stresses and values). Surprisingly, the environment siblings share matters less than each child's own experiences. The situation matters too: the student talkative in the canteen is silent in the viva. Walter Mischel's 1968 critique began a twenty-year person-situation debate (Section 4.9) that ended in interactionism: behaviour depends on the person, the situation and their interaction.

How personality is studied and assessed

The clinical method builds theory from the case study, a detailed history of one person from many sources; Freud, Jung and Adler built their theories from patients this way. It gives depth, but its data are subjective and open to the therapist's bias. The experimental method manipulates an independent variable and measures its effect on a dependent variable with all else held constant, so it alone shows cause; Skinner and Bandura worked this way, at the price of studying narrow slices of behaviour. The correlational method measures how strongly two variables go together, such as anxiety and exam marks, so it cannot prove cause. Its extension, factor analysis, finds the few factors underlying many intercorrelated measures; Raymond Cattell and Hans Eysenck built their trait theories with it.

The German philosopher Wilhelm Windelband (1848 to 1915) named a second divide in 1894. Nomothetic research (Greek nomos, law) seeks general laws from groups; idiographic research (Greek idios, one's own) studies the single individual in depth, the approach Allport championed (Section 4.6).

Assessment uses self-report inventories (MMPI-2, 16PF, NEO-PI-R, EPQ), projective techniques that ask for interpretations of ambiguous material (Rorschach inkblots, the Thematic Apperception Test or TAT), interviews, behavioural observation and life records such as diaries and letters. Each is useful only if reliable (consistent) and valid (measures what it claims); the tests are in Chapter 2.

Humours and the four temperaments

The Greek physician Hippocrates (about 460 to 377 BC) taught that health depends on the balance of four bodily fluids, or humours: blood, yellow bile, black bile and phlegm. Galen (AD 129 to about 216), a Greek physician in Rome, is credited with turning this into a theory of temperament: the dominant humour sets a person's emotional style (Table 4.3). The chemistry is wrong, but the idea of a biological temperament was ahead of its time, and Hans Eysenck later fitted the four temperaments into the quadrants of his extraversion and neuroticism dimensions (Section 4.7).

Physiognomy and its look-alikes

Physiognomy judges character from facial features and outward appearance. The Greek Physiognomonica, attributed to Aristotle (384 to 322 BC) but probably by his followers, claimed that people who resemble certain animals share their characters. The Swiss pastor Johann Kaspar Lavater (1741 to 1801) made it a European fashion with his illustrated essays of the 1770s, which read each feature of the face as a sign of character. The Italian physician Cesare Lombroso (1835 to 1909) applied it to crime: in Criminal Man (1876) he claimed that "born criminals" carry criminal stigmata, such as a sloping forehead and large jaws, marks of a throwback to primitive humans. Later comparisons of prisoners with other citizens found no such type.

Phrenology, founded by Franz Joseph Gall (1758 to 1828) around 1800 and popularised under that name by Johann Gaspar Spurzheim (1776 to 1832), read the bumps of the skull, assuming each mental faculty has its own brain "organ" whose size pushes out the skull above it (Section 1.1): wrong about bumps, right that functions are localised. Graphology infers personality from handwriting style and shows no useful validity in controlled studies. Interpreting dreams for unconscious motives is psychoanalysis (Section 4.2).

Exam trap

Face as a sign of character = physiognomy. Skull bumps and brain localisation of functions = phrenology (Gall). Handwriting = graphology. Dreams and unconscious motives = psychoanalysis. Body build = Kretschmer and Sheldon, below.

Body types: Kretschmer, Sheldon and later typologies

The German psychiatrist Ernst Kretschmer (1888 to 1964) thought his patients' illnesses went with their build. In Physique and Character (1921) he described the pyknic type, short and round, with a cyclothymic temperament swinging between cheer and gloom and a liability to manic-depressive illness; the asthenic or leptosomic type, tall and thin, with a reserved, withdrawn schizothymic temperament and a liability to schizophrenia; the athletic type, muscular; and the dysplastic type, a disproportioned mix. One flaw: his manic-depressive patients were older, and people grow rounder with age.

The American physician William Herbert Sheldon (1898 to 1977) replaced boxes with dimensions. In The Varieties of Human Physique (1940) he rated thousands of standardised photographs of male college students on three components, named after the embryonic layers that form the dominant tissues: endomorphy (soft and round, digestive organs prominent), mesomorphy (muscle and bone) and ectomorphy (thin and fragile, a large skin surface for the body's mass). Each is rated from 1 to 7, so a somatotype is three numbers: 4-4-4 is the average build, 7-1-1 the extreme endomorph, 1-7-1 the extreme mesomorph and 1-1-7 the extreme ectomorph. In The Varieties of Temperament (1942) he matched each component to a temperament (Table 4.4).

Key study: Sheldon's somatotypes (1940, 1942)

Does body build predict temperament? Sheldon rated young men on dozens of temperament traits and compared the ratings with their somatotypes, reporting correlations of about .8, far above almost anything in personality research. The flaw: he rated both physique and temperament himself, knowing each man's build, so his expectations and the stereotypes of the jolly fat man and the nervous thin one could create the correlation. Independent studies found only weak links. The theory is regarded as discredited.

Exam trap

Sheldon's theory has been discredited: not supported by research, and not "accurate for thin people" or "for women only" (his subjects were men). Noisy, callous, fond of physical activity = mesomorph; relaxed and food-loving = endomorph; restrained and private = ectomorph. Pyknic and asthenic with psychosis = Kretschmer.

Type thinking outlived the body theories. The German philosopher Eduard Spranger (1882 to 1963) sorted people by dominant value into six types: theoretical (truth), economic (usefulness), aesthetic (beauty), social (love of people), political (power) and religious (unity); the Allport-Vernon-Lindzey Study of Values measures them (Section 4.6). Carl Jung's types are in Section 4.4; Type A and B (Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman), Type C and Johan Denollet's Type D in Section 1.11; the gunas and doshas in Section 4.10.

4.2 Freud's Psychoanalysis: Instincts, Structure and Psychosexual StagesMedium yield · 42 Qs

You mean to thank a teacher and hear yourself use the name of one you disliked; you "forget" a dental appointment you were dreading. Most of us call these accidents. Sigmund Freud did not. To him every slip, dream and symptom had a hidden cause, and personality was an uneasy truce between inborn urges, the outside world and the morality we absorb as children. His system, psychoanalysis, was the first formal theory of personality and is still the best known: at once a theory of personality and a method of treating mental disorder. We follow it from hysteria to the verdict of research.

Freud's life and the birth of psychoanalysis

Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) was born in Freiberg, Moravia (now Příbor in the Czech Republic), and from the age of four lived in Vienna for almost 80 years. He qualified in medicine at Vienna in 1881 and began his career as a neurologist, not a psychiatrist or philosopher; "psychoanalyst" was a profession he had yet to invent. Neurologists were sent the patients nobody could explain, those with hysteria: genuine physical symptoms such as paralysis, blindness or loss of feeling (anaesthesia) in a limb, with no damage to the nerves. Freud came to see these as bodily reactions to emotional trauma, produced by painful memories kept out of awareness.

Three teachers pointed the way. In Paris (1885 to 1886) the neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot (1825 to 1893) showed him hysterical symptoms produced and removed by hypnosis, and hinted that their roots were sexual. At Nancy, which Freud visited in 1889, Hippolyte Bernheim (1840 to 1919) showed that hypnosis worked through suggestion. In Vienna the physician Josef Breuer (1842 to 1925) had found that a patient lost her symptoms when she talked through their origins under hypnosis. Breuer and Freud reported this in "On the Psychical Mechanism of Hysterical Phenomena: Preliminary Communication" (1893), expanded into Studies on Hysteria (1895): hysterics, they wrote, suffer mainly from reminiscences.

Key study: Anna O. and the talking cure (Breuer and Freud, 1895)

Anna O., really Bertha Pappenheim (1859 to 1936), was a bright young Viennese woman who fell ill while nursing her sick father: blurred vision, weakness of her right arm and both legs, and trouble speaking. Breuer took one symptom at a time; under hypnosis she traced it back to the moment it began, relived the feeling, and the symptom faded. She called this the "talking cure". Breuer and Freud named the release of pent-up emotion catharsis (Greek for purification) and concluded that symptoms carry hidden meaning. Criticism: she was Breuer's patient, not Freud's, and she was not cured when treatment ended in 1882; she spent months in a Swiss sanatorium and took years to recover before becoming a pioneering social worker.

Freud went further than Breuer. By the mid-1890s he held that the buried trauma was always sexual: his seduction theory traced neurosis to sexual abuse in childhood, usually by an older male relative. In 1897 he abandoned it, deciding that many such memories were fantasies and that the wishes behind them mattered more than events. The same year he began a lifelong self-analysis through his own dreams, recovering childhood hostility to his father and attachment to his mother, the seed of the Oedipus complex. The major works followed (Table 4.5). He fled the Nazis to London in 1938 and died there in September 1939, after sixteen years of cancer of the mouth.

Freud's theory is psychoanalytic, not behaviouristic (Watson and Skinner: behaviour as learning), humanistic (Maslow and Rogers: growth and choice) or psychosocial (Erikson's social stages; Chapter 6). The broader family that grew from it is the psychodynamic perspective: many mental disorders can be traced to unconscious urges and to the struggle over their expression in the hidden depths of personality. Gestalt psychology studies perception as organised wholes (Chapter 1), and social learning theory explains behaviour by observation and reinforcement (Section 4.9).

Clinical link

Freud's hysteria survives as conversion disorder, called functional neurological symptom disorder in DSM-5-TR: real loss of movement or sensation that fits no neurological disease (Chapter 8).

Exam trap

The 1893 paper and Studies on Hysteria are Freud and Breuer, never Freud with Charcot, Adler or Jung (Adler and Jung were later followers who broke away; Section 4.4). Freud began as a neurologist. To Freud, hysteria meant physical symptoms produced by emotional trauma; a phobia of horses is the case of Little Hans, and an unresolved Oedipus complex or oral behaviour are unrelated ideas. The father of psychoanalysis is Freud, not Bernheim, Adler or Jung.

Freud's methods: free association, dreams and slips

Many patients could not be hypnotised, so Freud had them lie on a couch, with himself out of sight, and gave them one fundamental rule: say whatever comes to mind, however trivial, embarrassing or painful, without censoring or rearranging it. This is free association, a kind of daydreaming aloud, and it became the central technique of psychoanalysis. Because of psychic determinism (below), nothing in the chain was random; each idea led closer to the buried conflict.

The chain often stalls: the patient falls silent or changes the subject. This is resistance, the unconscious refusal to let painful material surface, which Freud took as a sign that the analysis was near the source. Patients also come to feel toward the analyst as they once felt toward their parents: transference. Both are worked through in therapy (Chapter 7).

Dream analysis was the second road in; Freud called the interpretation of dreams "the royal road to a knowledge of the unconscious". What you remember is the manifest content; its hidden meaning, a disguised wish, is the latent content. The dream-work that turns one into the other is taught in Section 1.5.

The third window was everyday mistakes. A parapraxis (plural parapraxes), popularly a Freudian slip, is a slip of the tongue or pen, a forgotten name or appointment, or a misplaced object that betrays an unconscious thought or wish. A student who writes to his professor "I look forward to missing your lecture" has, in Freud's eyes, said what he meant. Parapraxes are mistakes that reveal the unconscious; they need not be sexual. Their meaning is found as a symptom's is, through free association.

Freud reported his work as case studies, detailed histories of single patients: Anna O. (hysteria, Breuer's patient), Dora (hysteria, 1905), Little Hans (1909, a small boy's phobia of horses, read as Oedipal fear of his father), the Rat Man (obsessional neurosis, 1909), Schreber (paranoia, 1911, analysed only from his published memoir) and the Wolf Man (1918, a childhood dream of wolves). The method is rich but weak as evidence, as the evaluation shows.

Exam trap

Lying on the couch saying whatever comes to mind is free association, not dreaming, narration or storytelling, and it is the central technique of psychoanalysis, not of behaviour, person-centred or cognitive therapy (Chapter 7). Freudian slips are analysed through free association: case studies are how Freud reported patients, behaviour analysis records observable acts and their consequences, and a sleep study records sleep stages, not meanings. A slip is not a defence mechanism (an unconscious strategy against anxiety; Section 4.3), a cognitive distortion (Aaron Beck's errors of thinking, such as overgeneralisation) or a speech disorder (such as stuttering).

Instincts and psychic energy

What powers the mind? Freud's answer was the instincts (German Triebe, sometimes translated "drives"): the powerful innate forces that motivate all behaviour and set its direction. An instinct is the mental representation of a bodily need, the wish that hunger becomes in the mind. In "Instincts and Their Vicissitudes" (1915) Freud gave every instinct a source (the bodily need), an aim (to remove the excitation, that is, satisfaction), an object (whatever achieves the aim, the most changeable part) and an impetus or pressure (its strength). The aim is always tension reduction, so the model is homeostatic.

Freud grouped the instincts into two classes. The life instincts, or Eros, serve survival of the individual and the species: hunger, thirst and above all sex, which Freud defined broadly to include almost any bodily pleasure and affection. Their energy is the libido. In Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920), shaken by the slaughter of the First World War, he added the death instinct, an unconscious drive toward decay and a return to the inanimate state, which his followers named Thanatos, a word Freud himself did not use in his writings. Turned inward it is self-destruction; turned outward it becomes the aggressive drive to destroy, conquer and kill, so wars and violence are, for Freud, evidence of the death instinct. Sex and aggression are what the id wants; the pleasure and reality principles (below) are the rules by which the id and the ego pursue them.

Libido can be attached to anything. Cathexis is the investment of psychic energy in the mental representation of an object, person or idea: if you adore a cricketer, your libido is cathected to him. An anticathexis is the opposing investment by which the ego or superego blocks an id cathexis; keeping a wish repressed costs energy. The cost matters because Freud saw the mind as a closed energy system, a fixed quantity of psychic energy that can be shifted but never added to, so whatever the ego spends holding back the id is lost to work and love. Beside it stands psychic determinism, the principle that every mental event has a cause, so no slip, dream or symptom is an accident.

Exam trap

Freud's innate motivating forces are instincts; needs are Maslow's and Henry Murray's term, drives Clark Hull's, and the id is the reservoir that holds the instincts. Investing energy in an object is cathexis. Its neighbours: libido is the energy itself, fixation is libido left stuck at a childhood stage, sublimation channels it into socially valued work, displacement shifts an impulse to a safer target and repression keeps threatening material out of awareness (the last three are defences; Section 4.3).

The levels of the mind: the topographic model

Freud's first map, the topographic model (1900), sorts mental contents by how accessible they are. The conscious holds whatever you are aware of at this moment. The preconscious holds accessible memories, thoughts and perceptions that are not in awareness now but can be recalled at will: your mobile number, what you ate last night. The unconscious, by far the largest, holds instincts, repressed wishes and painful memories that cannot be recalled at will and show themselves only indirectly, in dreams, slips and symptoms. Freud likened the mind to an iceberg: the conscious is the small tip above the water, the preconscious lies just below the waterline, and the unconscious is the vast hidden mass, the real driving power of behaviour (Figure 4.2).

Exam trap

Accessible memories recalled at will are preconscious, not conscious (in awareness now) or unconscious (not recallable at will). Psychic determinism is a principle, not a level of the mind.

Id, ego and superego: the structural model

In The Ego and the Id (1923) Freud replaced the three levels with three interacting structures, the structural model. The levels became properties of the structures (Figure 4.2).

The id is the reservoir of unorganised instinctual drives, present at birth, wholly unconscious and the source of all psychic energy. It operates on the pleasure principle: seek pleasure and avoid pain, now. Impulsive, irrational, selfish and amoral, it demands the immediate gratification of primitive needs and tolerates no delay ("Just do it"). Having no contact with reality, it can satisfy a need only by reflex or by primary process thought, forming a wish-fulfilling image of the object it lacks, as a hungry infant pictures the breast. This wish fulfilment also drives dreams.

Images feed no one, so from the id grows the ego, the rational part of personality, which works on the reality principle. Using secondary process thought (perception, memory, judgement and planning) and reality testing, it decides when, where and how the id's wishes can safely be met. It does not block the id; it postpones and redirects ("Wait till later"). Freud compared it to a rider guiding a stronger horse. It is called the executive of personality because it directs the energies supplied by the id and mediates between the id's desires, the superego and reality or social rules. The ego is partly conscious, partly preconscious and partly unconscious.

The superego is the moral arm, working on the moral principle. It forms at about five or six, when the Oedipus complex is resolved and the child internalises parental and social standards, so that parental control becomes self-control. It has two parts. The conscience is built from behaviours for which the child was punished and enforces them with guilt; the ego-ideal is built from good, correct behaviours for which the child was praised and rewards with pride. Like the id it admits no compromise: it seeks not to delay forbidden impulses but to stop them ("Don't do it"), and it strives for perfection. The ego thus serves three harsh masters: the id, reality and the superego (Table 4.6).

Anxiety: the ego's danger signal

Squeezed by three masters, the ego feels anxiety, fear without a clear object. In Inhibitions, Symptoms and Anxiety (1926) Freud made anxiety a signal that warns the ego of danger, with the overwhelming stimulation of birth as its prototype. He named three kinds by their source. Reality anxiety (objective anxiety) is fear of a real danger in the outside world, such as a speeding lorry; it is useful and fades when the danger passes. Neurotic anxiety, a conflict between id and ego, is the unconscious fear that id impulses will break through and bring punishment. Moral anxiety, a conflict between id and superego, is fear of one's own conscience, felt as guilt or shame. When realistic action cannot remove the threat, the ego turns to unconscious defence mechanisms, the subject of Section 4.3.

The psychosexual stages

Freud believed adult personality was largely set by about the age of five, and his psychosexual stages (Three Essays, 1905) explain how. At each stage the libido centres on a different erogenous zone, a part of the body that gives pleasure, and each stage brings a conflict between the child's pleasure and the parents' demands. If a stage goes badly, part of the libido stays invested there: fixation. It results from frustration (needs unmet) or overindulgence (needs so richly met that the child will not move on), and it stamps a recognisable adult character. Under stress a person may show regression, a return to the behaviour of an earlier stage, usually the one where libido is fixated. Table 4.7 gives the sequence; the ages are approximate and differ between textbooks (some give oral birth to 1 year, anal 1 to 3, phallic 4 to 5 and latency 5 to puberty).

In the oral stage (birth to about 18 months) pleasure comes from the mouth, through sucking, biting and swallowing, and the central conflict is feeding and weaning. Its first mode, oral-incorporative (taking in), produces adults preoccupied with eating, drinking, smoking, kissing and nail-biting, who are dependent and gullible ("swallowing" whatever they are told). The later oral-aggressive or oral-sadistic mode, which comes with painful teething, leaves adults who are sarcastic ("biting" remarks), critical, hostile and argumentative. Freud linked alcohol dependence to oral fixation.

In the anal stage (about 18 months to 3 years) pleasure comes from holding back and expelling faeces, and toilet training is the first time society regulates an instinct. A child who defies it by soiling may become an anal-expulsive (anal-aggressive) adult: messy, wasteful, disorganised, even cruel. A child who resists by holding back may become anal-retentive: orderly, stingy, obstinate and compulsively neat.

The phallic stage (about 3 to 5 or 6 years) centres on the genitals and on incestuous fantasies. The boy faces the Oedipus complex, named after the king in Sophocles' play who unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother: he desires his mother and sees his father as a rival. Fearing that the father will cut off his penis (castration anxiety), he represses the desire and achieves identification with the same-sex parent, adopting his father's manner and standards; this gives him his sex role and creates the superego. The girl's first love is also her mother, but on finding she lacks a penis she feels penis envy, blames her mother and turns to her father; she resolves the conflict, less completely in Freud's view, by identifying with her mother. Freud called this the female Oedipus complex. Carl Gustav Jung (1875 to 1961) named it the Electra complex in 1913, after the Greek heroine who persuades her brother to kill their mother. Freud rejected the term, yet exams pair Electra with girls and Oedipus with boys.

In the latency period (about 6 to puberty) the sexual instinct is dormant, sublimated into school, sport, hobbies and friendships with children of the same sex; Freud did not count it as a true psychosexual stage, since no new zone appears. The genital stage (puberty onwards) reawakens sexuality, now directed to mature love. Its achievements are sex-role identity, adult social relationships and the capacity for love and work.

Evaluating Freud

Which parts of the theory survive testing? The strongest support is for an unconscious mind. In subliminal perception studies, stimuli flashed too briefly to be seen still shape thought: Howard Shevrin (1977) showed people a picture of a bee they could not report, and their free associations then included "sting" and "honey". Implicit memory (experience shaping performance without recollection) and priming point the same way. Some defences, such as repression, projection and displacement, have experimental support (Section 4.3), and Michael Motley (1987) found that men made anxious about an electric shock said "damn shock" for "sham dock", a laboratory Freudian slip. Reviewing some 2,500 studies, Seymour Fisher and Roger Greenberg (1977, 1996) judged the id, ego, superego, libido and death instinct untestable, found weak support for oral and anal character types and some Oedipal ideas, and found no support for the psychosexual stages or for dreams as disguised wishes.

The criticisms are heavy. Many concepts are unfalsifiable: if a man loves his mother it is Oedipal, and if he hates her he is defending against Oedipal love, so no result can count against the theory. The data came from Freud himself and a small, biased sample, a dozen or so published cases, mostly young, educated, well-off Viennese women. His notes were written from memory hours later, and patients' recollections were not corroborated. His account of women (penis envy, a weaker superego) is sexist, a charge led by Karen Horney (Section 4.5). His view of human nature is pessimistic and over-sexualised, and personality plainly goes on developing after five.

Still, Freud gave psychology its first comprehensive theory of personality, the ideas of unconscious motivation, defence and the weight of childhood, and the talking cure from which every psychotherapy descends. On the six questions about human nature (Section 4.1) Freud is a determinist (though analysis widens choice), takes a middle ground on nature and nurture (inborn id and stages, learned ego and superego), stresses the past (the first five years), sees both universality (shared instincts and stages) and uniqueness (the content of ego and superego), makes equilibrium (tension reduction) life's goal, and is a pessimist. In India, Girindrasekhar Bose, founder of the Indian Psychoanalytical Society (1922), corresponded with Freud and proposed his own theory of opposite wishes (Section 1.2).

Exam trap

Research has begun to support the unconscious mind, not the existence of an id, ego and superego (untestable), the order of the psychosexual stages or a universal Oedipus complex.

4.3 Defence Mechanisms: How the Ego Protects ItselfHigh yield · 38 Qs

Arjun has just failed his statistics paper. Within an hour he has told his friends that statistics is useless for a clinician anyway, and he has snapped at his younger brother over nothing. He is not lying, at least not knowingly. In the terms of Section 4.2, his ego is under threat: anxiety is signalling danger, and when the ego cannot remove a danger by realistic means it falls back on unconscious tricks that disguise it. This section defines those tricks, studies the most basic of them, repression, walks through the full catalogue, ranks them from psychotic to mature, and shows which ones mark particular disorders.

What defences are and what they share

Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) wrote of defence from his first papers on hysteria in the 1890s, although for years he spoke almost only of repression. In Inhibitions, Symptoms and Anxiety (1926) he returned to the wider term: anxiety is a signal of danger to the ego, and defence is the name for every technique the ego uses to keep that danger out of awareness. Formally, defence mechanisms are unconscious strategies the ego uses to protect itself against anxiety by denying or distorting reality. It was his daughter Anna Freud (1895 to 1982) who turned the idea into a catalogue. In The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936) she listed ten mechanisms (regression, repression, reaction formation, isolation, undoing, projection, introjection, turning against the self, reversal and sublimation), described others such as identification with the aggressor, and moved the ego to the centre of psychoanalysis. The standard list you learn today owes its full articulation to her.

The mechanisms differ in detail, but they share six features:

  • They operate unconsciously. The person neither chooses a defence nor knows that one is working; if we knew we were lying to ourselves, the lie would no longer protect us.
  • They deny or distort reality, either outer reality (a diagnosis, a loss) or inner reality (a wish, a feeling).
  • They protect the ego and reduce anxiety. That is their whole function, and we usually use several at once.
  • They have adaptive value in moderation. Freud held that everyone defends all the time and that we could not survive long without defences.
  • They become pathological when they are rigid, overused or used where realistic action is needed; then they produce neurotic or even psychotic symptoms.
  • They are not rational. A defence does not solve the problem; it changes how the problem is seen.
Exam trap

Asked which statement does NOT describe defence mechanisms, the answer is "rational unconscious protective measures". "Having adaptive value", "pushing painful experiences out of consciousness" and "resulting in a distorted view of reality" are all true. The single word rational makes the option false.

Defence should not be confused with coping, the conscious, deliberate, flexible and reality-oriented effort to manage stress (problem-focused and emotion-focused coping are taught in Section 1.11). If Arjun draws up a revision timetable he is coping; if he decides the subject is useless he is defending. The line blurs only at the top of the maturity ladder (see below), where suppression and anticipation look much like good coping.

Repression, the primary defence

Freud called the theory of repression "the corner-stone on which the whole structure of psycho-analysis rests" (1914). In full, repression is the involuntary, unconscious blocking of threatening or anxiety-provoking memories, wishes and feelings from awareness, or the barring of them from ever reaching it. Freud separated two forms: primal repression, which keeps instinctual ideas from ever becoming conscious, and repression proper (after-pressure), which pushes out material that was once conscious and is now linked with the forbidden. Repression is the primary defence against anxiety: it is the most basic and most frequently used, the other mechanisms are often described as back-ups when it fails, and Freud saw it in all neurotic behaviour. It can act on memories, on perception (we may fail to see a disturbing event in front of us) and even on bodily function, as when a man represses his sexual drive so strongly that he becomes impotent. What is repressed is not destroyed; it stays active in the unconscious and returns in disguise as dreams, slips and symptoms.

In memory terms, then, repression is an unconscious process that removes unpleasant memories from awareness. It is not a conscious effort to forget, not a strengthening of memory traces and not the deliberate recall of traumatic events. The recovered memory controversy. From the late 1980s, adults in therapy began to "recover" memories of childhood sexual abuse, and some accused relatives were tried and imprisoned on that evidence. Elizabeth Loftus (born 1944) and others showed how easily suggestion, hypnosis and dream interpretation can implant vivid memories of events that never happened. Abuse is real and can go unrecalled for years, but Loftus, reviewing the evidence in 2006, concluded that there is little support for the idea that trauma is commonly banished from awareness and later reliably recovered. Repression remains a clinical concept rather than a proven memory mechanism (misinformation and false memory are taught in Chapter 1).

Repression, hysteria and dissociation. The concept was born in Studies on Hysteria (1895), written by Freud with Josef Breuer (1842 to 1925). Hysterical symptoms, they argued, came from painful memories pushed out of awareness that found expression through the body. For the same reason most psychodynamic theorists hold that dissociative symptoms (amnesia, fugue, multiple identities) are produced by repression: a whole segment of experience is walled off from consciousness. Conflict is what sets defence going, not the mechanism itself; "ego states" describe the split-off parts rather than the process that splits them; and gender identity has no bearing on dissociation.

Five defences that redirect or disguise

Most questions on defences give a short story and ask you to name the mechanism. Five defences carry most of these stories. Three of them move an impulse somewhere, so the key question is where it goes; Figure 4.3 follows one impulse through all five.

In displacement a feeling, usually anger, is redirected from a threatening target to a safer one that cannot hit back. The impulse stays the same; only its object changes. The woman angry with her husband shouts at her child; the teenager furious with a teacher goes home and shouts at his younger sibling; the man upset with his girlfriend yells at his dog; the woman harassed by her boss picks a fight with her husband. Because the substitute never discharges the tension as fully as the real target would, undischarged tension builds up. Displacement of fear, rather than anger, is the classic mechanism of phobias (see below).

In projection a person attributes his or her own unacceptable thoughts and impulses to someone else. The person says, in effect, "I don't hate him; he hates me." A husband who struggles with his own wish to stray accuses his faithful wife of flirting. The impulse is still expressed, but as somebody else's, which feels far less threatening.

In sublimation, sexual or aggressive energy is rechannelled into a socially valued activity. Roshan, who is full of anger and frustration, enrols in a kickboxing class; aggressive drives go into competitive sport, and frustrated sexual energy goes into painting or poetry. Displacement changes only the target, whereas sublimation transforms the impulse's aim into something society admires. Freud saw art, science and civilisation itself as built on sublimation and treated it as the healthiest defence; Vaillant later placed it among the mature ones.

The term rationalisation, introduced by Freud's follower Ernest Jones (1879 to 1958) in 1908, means a logical, socially acceptable reason invented after the act for behaviour that was really driven by unconscious or unacceptable motives. Clare complains about her duties, rarely finishes her tasks and is regularly late; when she is fired she says a co-worker badmouthed her. Arjun's "statistics is useless anyway" is the "sour grapes" form, after the fox in the fable who decides that the grapes he cannot reach are sour. Unlike a lie, the excuse is believed by the person who gives it.

In reaction formation the opposite of a threatening impulse is expressed: someone troubled by sexual longings becomes a crusader against pornography, and a child who resents a new baby smothers it with exaggerated affection. The giveaway is the excessive, rigid quality of the displayed feeling.

Exam trap

Read the stem for its landing place: a safer target is displacement, a socially valued activity is sublimation, another person's mind is projection. Clare blames a co-worker, which tempts you towards projection or displacement, and she plays down her failings, which tempts you towards denial. But she does not deny the dismissal, and she moves no anger onto a safer target; she supplies an after-the-fact excuse that protects her self-esteem: rationalisation. Do not confuse rationalisation (an excuse) with intellectualisation (abstract thinking that keeps feeling away).

Several entries are worth separating. Three belong together because all appear in obsessional neurosis: isolation of affect keeps the idea in awareness but removes its emotion, intellectualisation goes further and buries the emotion under abstract analysis, and undoing adds an act that magically "cancels" the thought. Plain identification copies another's behaviour, and identification with the aggressor, Anna Freud's special case, copies the very person one fears; introjection goes deeper and swallows another's values whole, so that they feel like one's own. Note too that regression, a retreat to the behaviour of an earlier stage, is a general response to stress rather than the mark of any one disorder, and dissociation, though dramatic, is ranked neurotic rather than immature by Vaillant. In the same way, conversion (Freud's term from his work on hysteria) produces a single symbolic symptom such as paralysis or blindness, whereas somatisation is a general tendency to express distress through the body. Finally, splitting, described by Melanie Klein (1882 to 1960) in the infant's paranoid-schizoid position (Section 4.5), keeps good and bad images apart so that the bad cannot spoil the good; projective identification, also Klein's, adds the push that makes the other person actually carry the projected feeling. Projection, introjection, empathy and denial apart

Projection has a broad sense as well as the narrow defensive one. Freud also used the word for any externalising of inner states, and in this broad sense projection is any distorting or selective effect of a person's inner world on his perception or conception of the outer world. This broad sense underlies the projective hypothesis: given an ambiguous stimulus, people read their own needs and conflicts into it. Lawrence K. Frank named such tests projective methods in 1939 (they are taught in Chapter 2).

The neighbouring terms run in other directions. Introjection moves something from outside to inside, the reverse of projection. By contrast, empathy is perceiving and sharing another person's feelings from that person's point of view; it is an accurate act of understanding, not a defence, and it distorts nothing. And denial blocks a particular, unwelcome fact of outer reality ("my son cannot be dead"); it does not colour perception in general.

Exam trap

RML 2024 asked for "any distorting or selective effect of a person's inner world on his perception or conception of the outer world" and keyed denial. That wording is the broad definition of projection, the basis of the projective hypothesis, so the key is doubtful. Denial refuses a specific reality; introjection takes the world in; empathy reads others accurately. If the same stem appears, projection is the defensible answer.

Key study: Vaillant and the Grant Study men (1977)

Question: do the defences a person habitually uses predict how well his life turns out? Method: the Grant Study of Adult Development has followed 268 Harvard men since 1938 through interviews, questionnaires and medical examinations. For Adaptation to Life (1977), judges rated the defences the men showed in crises over more than three decades. Result: the maturity of defences predicted adult adjustment: men who relied on mature defences had better marriages, careers, and physical and mental health, and defences tended to mature with age. Criticism: the sample was all male, mostly white and privileged, and the ratings rest on judges' inferences from life histories.

Some psychiatry textbooks extend the lists, calling Level I narcissistic and adding, for example, rationalisation and inhibition to the neurotic group and regression and somatisation to the immature group. The DSM-IV Defensive Functioning Scale (1994) later spread the defences over seven levels, from the high adaptive level down to defensive dysregulation, placing splitting and projective identification at the major image-distorting level.

Exam trap

Repression, projection and reaction formation are not mature; only altruism, anticipation, humour, sublimation and suppression are. Splitting (primitive), sublimation (mature) and projection (immature) are not neurotic, so intellectualisation is the neurotic choice. Dissociation is neurotic, not immature. Denial, distortion and delusional projection together mark the psychotic level. No defence is "conscious" as a class; suppression is the one deliberate exception, and it sits in the mature group.

Key study: Little Hans (1909)

In Analysis of a Phobia in a Five-Year-Old Boy (1909), Freud described Herbert Graf, a Viennese boy who was terrified that a horse would bite him and would hardly go out into the street. The case was conducted mainly through the boy's father, a follower of Freud, who reported to him by letter. Freud concluded that Hans's Oedipal fear of his father had been displaced onto horses, whose blinkers and dark muzzles recalled the father's spectacles and moustache; a horse, unlike a father, can simply be avoided. Criticism: the evidence came from a sympathetic father asking leading questions, and Joseph Wolpe and Stanley Rachman (1960) argued that the phobia was conditioned after Hans saw a horse fall in the street.

The logic behind the table is worth knowing. In obsessional neurosis the disturbing idea stays fully conscious (that is what an obsession is), but its feeling is isolated, compulsions undo it, and excessive cleanliness or kindness stands in reaction to it; hysteria, by contrast, represses the idea itself. In phobia, a fear is displaced onto an object that can be avoided, as in Little Hans. In paranoia, the forbidden feeling is projected, so that hatred within becomes persecution from without. Otto Kernberg (born 1928) made splitting and projective identification the core of borderline personality organisation, which is why the person with borderline personality disorder swings between idealising and devaluing the same person; regression, undoing and sublimation are not characteristic. In depression, following Freud's Mourning and Melancholia (1917), the lost person is introjected and the anger meant for them is turned against the self.

Exam trap

Asked where displacement is typically seen, answer phobias (IHBAS 2020), not hysteria, OCD or depression.

Development, research and clinical use

Defences have a developmental order. Phebe Cramer, coding children's Thematic Apperception Test stories (1987), found that denial dominates in early childhood, projection rises in later childhood, and identification takes over in adolescence, as Figure 4.4 shows. Each step demands more cognitive skill: denial needs only a refusal, projection needs a sense of other minds, identification needs a stable self that can borrow from others. A later study of pupils from Grade 2 to college found the same pattern: denial and projection fell with age while identification rose.

Laboratory evidence for repression is debated. Sam Glucksberg and L. J. King (1967) paired some words with electric shock and found that people later forgot those words and related ones, which looks like repression. Others have found that habitual "repressors", people low in reported anxiety but high in defensiveness, recall fewer negative childhood memories. Yet David S. Holmes (1990), reviewing sixty years of studies, found no convincing controlled evidence that repression works as Freud described, and the recovered memory debate has kept the question open.

In everyday life defences are neither rare nor shameful: they soften losses, protect self-esteem and buy time. Clinically, they matter in two ways. The defences a person habitually uses help a clinician gauge the level of personality organisation, and in psychodynamic therapy defences show up as resistance, which the therapist gently interprets so that the patient can face what is being avoided and replace rigid, immature defences with mature ones (therapy is taught in Chapter 7).

4.4 Jung's Analytical Psychology and Adler's Individual PsychologyMedium yield · 25 Qs

In March 1907 a 31-year-old Swiss psychiatrist, Carl Jung, met Sigmund Freud in Vienna, and the two talked for almost thirteen hours. Six years later they were no longer speaking, and Alfred Adler had already walked out of Freud's circle. Each rebel kept part of Freud's framework but rebuilt the theory of personality at the point where he thought Freud had gone wrong.

The neo-Freudians and the break with Freud

The neo-Freudians began inside psychoanalysis, kept its belief in unconscious processes and in the weight of childhood, and then rebuilt the theory. The usual list is Carl Jung, Alfred Adler, Karen Horney, Erich Fromm, Harry Stack Sullivan and Erik Erikson (Horney, Fromm and Sullivan are in Section 4.5, Erikson in Chapter 6). Their changes ran one way: less sex and instinct; more social, cultural and conscious forces; and a person pulled toward goals, not only pushed by the past.

Adler left first. A member of Freud's circle from 1902 and president of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society, he argued that inferiority and social striving, not sexuality, lay behind neurosis. In 1911 he resigned with 9 of the 23 members, and in 1912 he founded the Society for Individual Psychology. Jung was Freud's chosen successor. Freud called him his "crown prince", took him on the 1909 lecture trip to Clark University in Massachusetts, and saw him become first president of the International Psychoanalytic Association in 1910. But Jung would not accept that libido was purely sexual, and they parted in 1913 (Table 4.12).

Jung's psyche: energy, ego and complexes

Carl Gustav Jung (1875 to 1961), a Swiss pastor's son, worked from 1900 at the Burghölzli hospital in Zurich under Eugen Bleuler. His analytical psychology begins with energy. Jung widened libido from sexual energy to general psychic energy, the energy of all perceiving, thinking, feeling and wishing; sex is only one of its uses. It obeys three principles borrowed from physics. By the principle of opposites, conflict between polarities, such as love and hate, generates energy. By the principle of equivalence, energy withdrawn from one interest reappears in another of equal value, as when a retired cricketer's passion flows into coaching. By the principle of entropy, energy flows from stronger to weaker parts of the personality, tending toward a balance that is never reached.

The psyche has three levels (Figure 4.5). The ego is the conscious mind, the centre of awareness. Below it lies the personal unconscious, material once conscious but forgotten or suppressed, much like Freud's preconscious. There related memories and feelings gather into complexes. A complex is an emotionally charged cluster of ideas organised around a theme, such as a mother complex or a power complex; its owner rarely notices it, though others do. To detect complexes Jung developed, from 1904 with Franz Riklin, the word association test.

Key study: Jung's word association test (1904 onwards)

Question: can a hidden complex be detected objectively? Method: Jung read out 100 stimulus words (head, green, water, death), the patient answered each with the first word that came to mind, and Jung timed every reply and recorded physiological signs such as breathing and skin resistance. Result: certain words produced complex indicators: a long delay, no answer, repeating the stimulus word, the same reply to different words, a slip, or a bodily reaction. These marked the theme of a complex. Legacy and criticism: the test fed projective testing and lie detection, but its interpretation stays subjective. Francis Galton and Wilhelm Wundt had studied word association earlier; as a clinical tool for complexes it is Jung's.

Exam trap

Word association test: Jung. Not Henry Murray (the Thematic Apperception Test, Chapter 2), not Freud (free association, talking freely with no stimulus words) and not Donald Winnicott (object relations: the transitional object and the good enough mother, Section 4.5).

The collective unconscious and the archetypes

Freud's unconscious was essentially personal. Jung held that the unconscious exists on two levels, the personal (individual) and the collective. The collective unconscious is the deepest layer of the psyche: inherited, universal, the same in every human being, the deposit of the experience of our human and pre-human ancestors. Jung accounted for it through evolution: the brain has the same structure in all human races, so it carries the same inherited predispositions. His evidence was that the same symbols (the dragon-slaying hero, the great flood, the sacred circle) recur in the myths of cultures that never met and in the dreams of patients who had never read those myths. We inherit readiness, not memories: not a fear of snakes, but the potential to fear them.

The contents of the collective unconscious are archetypes, which Jung also called primordial images: universal patterns that shape how we respond to life's recurring experiences, such as mother, birth and death (Table 4.13). The persona is the social mask; trouble starts when the ego mistakes it for the whole person. The shadow, the most powerful archetype, is the disowned animal side, the source of evil but also of vitality and creativity. The anima is the feminine side of a man and the animus the unconscious masculine side of a woman. The self, the archetype of wholeness and unity, is the centre of the whole psyche (the ego is only the centre of consciousness), symbolised by the mandala, a circle usually divided into four.

Attitudes, functions and the eight types

In Psychological Types (1921) Jung asked why people meet the same world so differently. His first answer was two attitudes, his own terms for the direction of psychic energy. In extraversion it flows outward, to people and things: the extravert is sociable and assertive. In introversion it flows inward, to one's own thoughts and feelings: the introvert is reserved and reflective. Everyone has both; one dominates consciousness, while the other sinks into the personal unconscious and still leaks out, as when a shy student is drawn to a loud friend.

His second answer was four functions in two opposed pairs (Figure 4.6). Thinking judges true or false; feeling judges pleasant or unpleasant. Because both evaluate, they are the rational functions. Sensing registers what is there, like a camera; intuiting grasps possibilities and hunches beyond the evidence. Because both perceive without evaluating, they are the irrational functions (also called nonrational). One function dominates; the other member of its pair, the inferior function, stays undeveloped and largely unconscious, so a strong thinker may be clumsy with feelings. Two attitudes times four functions give Jung's eight psychological types, from the extraverted thinking type (logical, rule-bound) to the introverted intuiting type (the visionary daydreamer).

Katharine Cook Briggs (1875 to 1968), who read Jung's book in 1923, and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers (1897 to 1980), neither trained in psychology, turned the types into the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), whose first form appeared in 1943. It scores four dichotomies, extraversion-introversion (E-I), sensing-intuition (S-N), thinking-feeling (T-F) and judging-perceiving (J-P, their own addition), giving 16 types such as INTP. Hugely popular in workplaces, it is psychometrically weak: it forces continuous traits into either-or boxes, and its test-retest reliability is poor, with many people (in some studies about half) typed differently when retested a few weeks later. Researchers prefer trait inventories (Section 4.7; Chapter 2).

Exam trap

Jung's two attitudes are extraversion and introversion; thinking, feeling, sensing and intuiting are functions; rational and irrational label the pairs of functions. The inventory based directly on Jung's types is the MBTI, not the MMPI (an empirically keyed clinical inventory), the 16PF (Raymond Cattell's factors, Section 4.6) or the NEO-PI-R (Paul Costa and Robert McCrae's Big Five, Section 4.7).

Individuation, method and evaluation of Jung

Freud thought personality was largely settled by five; Jung's crucial period was middle age, the time of his own breakdown after 1913. In the first half of life energy goes outward, to education, career and family, under consciousness and extraversion. Between about 35 and 40 many successful people feel empty. Jung saw this midlife crisis as natural, a signal to turn inward, from the material to the spiritual and from extraversion toward introversion. The goal of the second half of life is individuation, becoming a whole, undivided individual by integrating conscious and unconscious: dethrone the persona, accept the shadow, express the anima or animus, and transcend, so that the self, not the ego, becomes the centre. Such self-realisation is rarely complete. Synchronicity, a meaningful coincidence with no causal link, belongs to the same mystical side of Jung.

Jung assessed patients by word association, symptom analysis (free associations to the symptoms) and dream analysis. He studied series of dreams, saw them as prospective (preparing for the future) and compensatory (balancing a one-sided conscious attitude), and used amplification: circling back to one dream image and enlarging it with repeated associations and parallels from myth, instead of following free associations away from it. Critics call his evidence (myth, alchemy, the occult, his own visions) mystical, his writing obscure and the collective unconscious unfalsifiable. Yet introversion-extraversion (Section 4.7), complexes, word association, the midlife transition and individuation, a forerunner of Abraham Maslow's self-actualisation, all lasted.

Adler: the individual as a goal-seeking whole

Alfred Adler (1870 to 1937), born near Vienna, was a sickly second son with rickets who nearly died of pneumonia at four and envied a healthy older brother. A physician, he joined Freud's circle as a colleague, never a pupil, and after the break founded individual psychology; he died on a lecture tour in Scotland. Individual here means indivisible: each person is a unique, unified whole, not an id, ego and superego at war, and the whole person moves toward a goal. The basic assumption of individual psychology is that human behaviour is purposeful and goal-directed: Freud explained behaviour by past causes, Adler by future goals that pull us forward. The goals are social. In place of Freud's sexual instinct, Adler held that people are motivated primarily by social urges, the need to belong and to count among others. He gave the conscious more weight than the unconscious and saw people hopefully, as free to shape themselves.

Exam trap

The basic assumption is purposeful, goal-directed behaviour. Optimism is a feature of Adler's view, not its basic assumption; "social beings understood through their relationships" describes his social emphasis but fits Sullivan's interpersonal theory better (Section 4.5); lifelong development with ego identity at the centre is Erik Erikson (Chapter 6). The motive is social urges: not conscious drives (drive is Freud's language), not neurotic needs (Karen Horney, Section 4.5) and not empathic understanding (a therapist condition of Carl Rogers, Section 4.8). Individual psychology is Adler's, not Horney's, Jung's or Erikson's.

Inferiority, compensation and striving for superiority

Adler's Study of Organ Inferiority and Its Psychical Compensation (1907) argued that a weak organ or body part drives the person to make up for it, sometimes overshooting into strength, as the Greek orator Demosthenes overcame a stutter. This idea of organ inferiority grew into a theory of all motivation. Every child starts small and dependent among powerful adults, so inferiority feelings are universal and normal, and they are the source of all human striving. The effort to overcome them is compensation; going beyond the deficit, as the frail boy who becomes an athlete does, is overcompensation (Figure 4.7).

When compensation fails, the feelings harden into an inferiority complex, Adler's term: a lasting lack of self-worth, with helplessness, hopelessness and avoidance of challenge. It has three childhood sources: organ inferiority, pampering (the spoilt child crumbles when school does not revolve around him) and neglect (the unwanted child learns that others are hostile). Children with organic defects or born into minority groups are therefore at risk. Overcompensation can instead produce a superiority complex, an exaggerated self-importance shown in boasting, vanity and belittling others.

Neither complex is striving for superiority, the healthy, innate drive toward perfection (completion) that Adler called the fundamental fact of life: an effort to perfect oneself and one's community, not to dominate others.

Exam trap

The inferiority complex and the superiority drive are Adler's, not Jung's, Viktor Frankl's (logotherapy, Chapter 7) or Erikson's. Adler coined the term, not Franz Alexander (psychosomatic medicine), Gordon Allport (traits, Section 4.6) or Jung; obituaries wrongly credited Freud and Jung with it. Organ inferiority is Adler's too, not Sullivan's, Melanie Klein's (both Section 4.5) or Anna Freud's (Section 4.3). Overcompensation, lack of self-worth and hopelessness go with the complex; certainty in decision making is its opposite.

Fictional finalism, style of life and social interest

What goals pull us? Adler borrowed from the philosopher Hans Vaihinger (1852 to 1933), whose The Philosophy of "As If" (1911) argued that people live by useful fictions, unprovable ideas acted on as if they were true, such as "all people are created equal". Adler's fictional finalism is the idea that each person is guided by an imagined final goal, a subjective ideal of success or completeness; he preferred the terms subjective final goal or guiding self-ideal.

Each person pursues the goal through a unique style of life, the characteristic pattern of traits, habits and attitudes by which one strives for superiority. It grows from the child's compensations, birth order and parenting, and is largely fixed by four or five. Yet it is not fate: through the creative power of the self each child interprets heredity and environment and builds its own style.

The test of a healthy style is social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl, community feeling), an innate potential to cooperate with others for personal and social goals, which the mother must nurture. It is needed for the three life tasks of work, friendship and love, and its absence underlies neurosis and crime. 4.5 Horney, Fromm, Sullivan and the Object-Relations TheoristsLower yield · 10 Qs

Freud's followers kept his couch but argued about what drives the person lying on it. Karen Horney, Erich Fromm and Harry Stack Sullivan moved the source of personality from instinct to culture and relationships. Others kept more of Freud and asked how the ego adapts, how loved people become figures inside the mind, how a cohesive self is built and how language shapes the unconscious. Jung and Adler are in Section 4.4.

Horney: basic anxiety and the neurotic trends

Karen Horney (1885 to 1952), a Berlin-trained analyst who taught in the United States from 1932, argued in The Neurotic Personality of Our Time (1937) that personality is shaped primarily by social and cultural factors, above all the child's relationships with its parents, not by biological instincts, unconscious sexual conflicts or classical conditioning.

The helpless infant's overriding need is the childhood need for safety and security: to feel wanted and protected. What damages a child is parental indifference, a lack of genuine warmth shown through favouritism, unfair punishment, broken promises or ridicule. The child feels basic hostility towards such parents but must repress it, out of helplessness, fear, the wish to keep even counterfeit love, or guilt. The buried hostility returns as basic anxiety, in Horney's words an "insidiously increasing, all-pervading feeling of being lonely and helpless in a hostile world". It is the foundation on which every later neurosis is built.

Exam trap

Pervasive loneliness and helplessness, the foundation of neurosis, is basic anxiety. Neurotic anxiety is Freud's fear that id impulses will break through (Section 4.2); existential anxiety is dread of death, freedom and meaninglessness (Section 4.8); "innate anxiety" is not a technical term. Instincts, sexual conflicts or conditioning are never Horney's answer.

To quiet basic anxiety the child seizes a strategy (winning affection, submitting, gaining power or withdrawing), and used rigidly a strategy hardens into a need. Horney listed ten neurotic needs, irrational defences against anxiety that become permanent parts of the personality: affection and approval, a dominant partner, power, exploitation, prestige, admiration, achievement, self-sufficiency and independence, perfection, and narrow limits to life. Everyone feels them at times; they are neurotic when pursued compulsively as the only route to safety. In Our Inner Conflicts (1945) she grouped them into three neurotic trends (Figure 4.8). The compliant person moves toward people, seeking love and leaning on others; the aggressive person moves against people, treating life as a struggle; the detached person moves away from people, keeping an emotional distance. A healthy person uses all three flexibly; the neurotic is locked into one, and the clash among them is the core of neurosis.

Indian context

For basic anxiety "in the Indian context" the key is Sudhir Kakar (born 1938), the psychoanalyst of Indian childhood and society (The Inner World, 1978; Section 1.2). He wins as the only psychoanalyst offered: Anand C. Paranjpe co-edited the Handbook of Indian Psychology (2008), Durganand Sinha (1922 to 1998) urged the indigenisation of psychology, and "Kamala Chaudhuri" is a garbled name.

Horney on the idealised self, women and "momism"

In Neurosis and Human Growth (1950) Horney turned to the self. A healthy self-image rests on a realistic appraisal of strengths and weaknesses. The neurotic, torn between incompatible trends, builds an idealised self-image of flawless virtue and tries to become it. The result is the tyranny of the shoulds ("I should be the perfect student, never tired, never angry"), demands no one can meet, which end in self-hatred.

Horney also challenged Freud's view of women. From 1922 she rejected Freud's penis envy as an idea drawn from neurotic women and seen through male eyes. Men, she countered, may feel womb envy, envy of women's power to bear children, which they sublimate into achievement. In her feminine psychology, women's inferiority feelings have cultural causes, such as economic dependence and narrow roles, not anatomical ones. Her Self-Analysis (1942), drawn from her own lifelong practice, argued that people can ease many of their own neurotic conflicts without an analyst.

A fashion of the 1940s blamed mothers instead. Momism is the blaming of over-protective, idealised mothers for their sons' weakness. The writer Philip Wylie coined it in Generation of Vipers (1942), and the psychiatrist Edward Strecker took it up in Their Mothers' Sons (1946), blaming "moms" for the young men found unfit for military service in the Second World War.

Exam trap

"Mom-ism" was coined by Wylie; when Wylie is not an option, the key is Edward Strecker, not Freud, Jung or Horney.

Fromm: escape from freedom and the existential needs

Erich Fromm (1900 to 1980), a German analyst trained also in sociology, called his approach humanistic psychoanalysis and studied whole societies. In Escape from Freedom (1941), written as fascism spread, he argued that modern people have won freedom from the old ties of church, clan and guild but pay for it with isolation. Many flee it through three mechanisms of escape: authoritarianism (submitting to a leader or dominating others), destructiveness (attacking the threatening world) and automaton conformity (becoming exactly what society expects). The healthy answer is positive freedom through spontaneous love and productive work.

Sullivan's interpersonal theory

The American psychiatrist Harry Stack Sullivan (1892 to 1949) went furthest. His interpersonal theory (The Interpersonal Theory of Psychiatry, published in 1953, after his death) holds that personality exists only in relations with other people, real or imagined: it is "the relatively enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations which characterize a human life".

Anxiety, again, is the engine. It passes by empathy from the "mothering one" to the infant: a tense mother makes a tense baby. To avoid it the child builds a self-system, the organisation of experience that guards security, using security operations such as selective inattention, which ease anxiety but block learning. It forms personifications, images of self and others: the good-me (experiences met with tenderness), the bad-me (experiences met with the mother's anxiety) and the not-me (experiences so dreadful that they are dissociated, surfacing in nightmares and schizophrenia).

Experience matures through three modes. The prototaxic mode is the infant's undifferentiated flow of sensations. The parataxic mode links events that occur together as cause and effect, the prelogical thinking behind superstition. The syntaxic mode is logical and consensually validated through shared language. Sullivan's developmental epochs run from infancy and childhood through the juvenile era (the need for peers), preadolescence, and early and late adolescence to adulthood. Preadolescence brings the chum, a same-sex friend whose welfare matters as much as one's own: the first intimacy.

Exam trap

A practice-mock key for "Sullivan's interpersonal theory" is "personality as an open system interacting with the environment". Accept it by elimination, but "open system" is Allport's phrase (The Open System in Personality Theory, 1960; Section 4.6). "Unconscious processes" is Freud, "observable behaviour" is behaviourism, and "social structures and institutions" is sociology, nearer Fromm. The eight stages from oral-sensory to maturity are Erikson's (Chapter 6), never Sullivan's.

Ego psychology: Anna Freud, Hartmann and Erikson

Ego psychology kept Freud's structural model but gave the ego more power. Anna Freud (1895 to 1982), Freud's youngest daughter, catalogued the defences in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936; Section 4.3) and pioneered child analysis. Heinz Hartmann (1894 to 1970), the father of ego psychology, argued in Ego Psychology and the Problem of Adaptation (1939) that perception, memory, motor skill, language and thinking develop in a conflict-free ego sphere, not out of conflict with the id. These functions give the ego autonomy, and its task is adaptation to an "average expectable environment".

Erik Erikson (1902 to 1994), analysed by Anna Freud, carried the ego across the life span. His epigenetic principle holds that development unfolds by a ground plan, each part having its own time of special ascendancy, as organs form in an embryo. His stages are psychosocial, not psychosexual: each is a crisis between the growing person and society's demands, not a shift of libido to a new body zone, and they continue into old age. The adolescent identity crisis is his term; the eight stages are in Chapter 6.

Object relations and Melanie Klein

In psychoanalysis an object is the target of a drive or feeling: almost always a person, or the mental image of one, beginning with the mother. Object relations are the internalised mental representations of self and others (objects) and the relationships between them. A child does not just live with a mother; it carries a mother inside, coloured by love and hate, and later relationships are lived through these inner figures. The term does not mean the relationship among id, ego and superego, the use of defences, or the genital stage (Sections 4.2 and 4.3). The school's founder was Melanie Klein (1882 to 1960), who worked in Berlin and, from 1926, London.

Klein analysed very young children through her play technique, treating play with small toys as free association. At first, she concluded, the baby relates to part objects: the gratifying "good breast" and the frustrating "bad breast", felt as two different beings. In the paranoid-schizoid position of the first months, the infant protects the good by splitting it off from the bad, keeping love and hate apart, and projects its rage into the bad object, which then seems to persecute it. From about the middle of the first year, as the infant grasps that the loved and hated mother are one person, it enters the depressive position: it feels guilt over the harm its rage may have done and makes reparation through loving acts. Klein spoke of positions, not stages, because adults move between them all their lives (Table 4.17). In projective identification a person puts unwanted parts of the self into someone else and then treats that person as if they had them. Envy (Envy and Gratitude, 1957) is the infant's wish to spoil the good breast because it has what the infant lacks.

Winnicott and the later object-relations theorists

The English paediatrician Donald Winnicott (1896 to 1971) held that the infant needs not a perfect mother but a good-enough mother, who meets its needs almost completely at first and then fails it in small, bearable ways, so that it learns to tolerate frustration. She provides a holding environment of physical and emotional security. As the infant learns that the mother is separate, it adopts a transitional object, the teddy or blanket that is neither wholly "me" nor "not-me" and comforts it when she is away. Good-enough care lets a spontaneous true self develop; without it the infant builds a false self, a compliant shell that gives others what they expect.

Ronald Fairbairn (1889 to 1964) made the cleanest break with Freud: libido is object-seeking, not pleasure-seeking. Margaret Mahler (1897 to 1985) traced the infant's psychological birth through separation-individuation, from fusion with the mother to a separate self (Chapter 6). Otto Kernberg (born 1928) described borderline personality organisation: intense early frustration and aggression leave the person unable to unite good and bad images, so splitting persists into adulthood, identity is diffuse and others are all good or all bad, though reality testing is largely kept. John Bowlby (1907 to 1990), trained in this tradition, turned the infant's tie to its mother into attachment theory, an offshoot taught in Chapter 6.

Kohut's self psychology

Heinz Kohut (1913 to 1981) founded self psychology, which drew on object relations, in The Analysis of the Self (1971) and The Restoration of the Self (1977). Where Freud saw narcissism as love withdrawn from others, Kohut saw healthy narcissism as a normal line of development. The self grows through selfobjects, people experienced as part of the self because they perform functions the child cannot yet perform alone. Three selfobject needs must be met: mirroring, the gleam in the mother's eye that confirms the child's "Look at me!" and so sustains the grandiose self; idealising, merging with a calm, strong parent (the idealised parent imago); and twinship (alter ego), added later, feeling essentially alike with others. When parents respond well but imperfectly, each bearable failure leads the child to take over the selfobject's soothing function; this transmuting internalisation builds a cohesive self. When mirroring and idealising fail chronically, the self stays fragmented and enfeebled: empty and dependent on others to hold it together. Failed mirroring above all leaves the archaic grandiose self unintegrated, and it returns in adulthood as narcissistic pathology: grandiosity, a constant hunger for admiration and little empathy, as the person still seeks the mirroring that never came.

Exam trap

Grandiosity, a need for constant admiration and a lack of empathy trace to failed parental mirroring. Failed idealising leaves a person searching for someone perfect to admire; twinship (alter ego) is the need to feel alike; "confrontation" is a therapist's technique, not a selfobject need.

Clinical link

A client says she is "no one", feels "empty inside", dreads abandonment and has intense, unstable relationships. Self psychology reads this as a fragmented self from failed parental mirroring and idealising. It is not oral fixation or an unresolved Oedipus complex with castration anxiety (Freud's psychosexual explanations, Section 4.2), nor guilt from a harsh superego, a neurotic conflict rather than a deficit of self. The borderline disorder itself is in Chapter 8.

Lacan, the "French Freud"

Jacques Lacan (1901 to 1981), the Paris analyst called the "French Freud", led a return to Freud, a close rereading of Freud's own texts against ego psychology's taming of them. In the mirror stage, between about 6 and 18 months, the infant delights in its unified mirror image; the ego is born from this identification with an outside image, and so is partly an illusion. Drawing on structural linguistics, Lacan taught that the unconscious is structured like a language. Experience moves in three registers: the Imaginary (images and identifications), the Symbolic (language, law and social order) and the Real (whatever resists being put into words).

Exam trap

The other options are earlier French psychiatrists. Jean-Étienne Esquirol gave hallucination its modern meaning and described monomania. Jules Falret, with Charles Lasègue (1877), described folie à deux, a delusion shared by two people. Paul Briquet (1859) described the chronic, many-symptom hysteria called Briquet's syndrome, now somatisation (Chapter 8).

4.6 Traits I: Allport, Cattell and the Lexical SearchLower yield · 17 Qs

Ask a friend to describe you and you will hear adjectives: friendly, stubborn, punctual, moody. Each one claims that you behave in a characteristic way across situations and across years. Trait theorists set out to make a science of this everyday habit. This section follows two of them, who began from the same dictionary and went opposite ways: Gordon Allport, who studied the unique individual, and Raymond Cattell, who used statistics to find the dimensions on which everyone differs. Eysenck and the Big Five follow in Section 4.7.

The trait approach and the lexical hypothesis

The trait approach describes personality through specific psychological attributes along which individuals differ in consistent and stable ways. A trait is one such attribute: an enduring dimension, such as sociability, on which everyone has a position. The approach assumes stability over time (the talkative child becomes a talkative adult), consistency across situations (sociable at a wedding and in the canteen alike) and individual differences (people sit at different points on the same dimension, so they can be compared and their behaviour predicted).

Three neighbouring ideas must be kept apart. A type sorts people into broad, discrete categories, such as Sheldon's body types (Section 4.1), and so describes personality through broad patterns; typing shades into stereotyping, a set of expected behaviours based on similarities among members of a group. Situationism holds that characteristics of the situation, not of the person, largely determine behaviour (Walter Mischel's challenge, Section 4.7). A state is a temporary condition: Charles Spielberger (1927 to 2013) separated state anxiety, a passing tension, from trait anxiety, a stable proneness to anxiety, and measured both with his State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (1970).

Where should one look for the traits that matter? The lexical hypothesis answers: in language. Differences between people that matter in daily life get noticed and named, so the important traits are already encoded as words, and the more important a trait, the more words a language has for it. In 1936 Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert searched an unabridged English dictionary and culled 17,953 words that distinguish one person's behaviour from another's. Only about 4,500 named stable traits (honest, sociable); the rest described temporary states (flustered), evaluations (admirable), or physical features and abilities. This list fed the whole factor-analytic tradition (Figure 4.9).

Such a list can be used in two ways. The nomothetic approach (Greek nomos, law) studies many people on the same dimensions to find general laws. The idiographic approach (idios, one's own) studies one person intensively to grasp his or her unique pattern. The terms are Wilhelm Windelband's (Section 4.1). Cattell went nomothetic; Allport preferred the idiographic path.

Exam trap

The trait approach focuses on specific psychological attributes along which individuals differ in consistent and stable ways. "Broad patterns" describes the type approach; "expected behaviour based on similarities" describes stereotyping; "situational characteristics determine behaviour" is situationism, the critique of trait theory.

Allport: traits as real structures

Gordon Willard Allport (1897 to 1967) did more than anyone to make personality a recognised field. In 1920 he called on Sigmund Freud in Vienna and, to fill an awkward silence, described a small boy he had just seen on a tram who was terrified of dirt. Freud looked at his neat, meticulous visitor and asked, "Was that little boy you?" Allport decided that psychoanalysis digs too deep and that psychology should take conscious, present motives seriously. Back at Harvard, from 1924, he taught what was probably the first American college course on personality, and his Personality: A Psychological Interpretation (1937) established the field. It defined personality as "the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment" (Section 4.1).

For Allport a trait was not a convenient label but a real neuropsychic structure. It renders many stimuli functionally equivalent and guides consistent behaviour: a friendly man greets the stranger at the bus stop, the new colleague and the neighbour's guest with the same warmth. Traits therefore cause behaviour rather than merely summarising it. Allport distinguished common traits, shared in varying degrees by the members of a culture so that people can be compared on them, from individual traits, which he later renamed personal dispositions (Pattern and Growth in Personality, 1961): characteristics peculiar to one person.

Personal dispositions differ in how much of a life they govern, and Allport set them on three levels (Table 4.18). A cardinal trait is the most pervasive and powerful kind: a single ruling passion that dominates a whole life. Few people have one, and those who do often lend it a name: Machiavellian, narcissistic, Christlike. Central traits are the handful, five to ten, of outstanding traits that describe a person's behaviour, the words you would put in a reference letter. Secondary traits are the least important, displayed inconspicuously and inconsistently, such as a taste for filter coffee, and noticed only by close friends.

The idiographic approach

Because every personality is unique, Allport argued, its science must study the individual case and not only group averages, which describe nobody in particular. He therefore preferred the idiographic approach to the nomothetic search for laws, with its factorial designs (several variables crossed over many subjects) and group statistics such as chi-square (Chapter 9). Later he called his approach morphogenic, the study of how traits are patterned within one person. His main method was the personal-document technique: analysing diaries, letters and autobiographies for the traits they reveal.

Key study: Letters from Jenny (1965)

Over twelve years a woman Allport called Jenny, the mother of his college roommate, wrote more than 300 letters to Allport and his wife. Thirty-six judges read them and listed nearly 200 trait names, which Allport reduced to eight clusters, among them quarrelsome-suspicious, self-centred and dramatic-intense. A later computer count of her words by one of his students found much the same eight. Criticism: the method rests on judges' interpretations, and one case is hard to generalise.

Allport was not purely idiographic. With Philip Vernon (1931), and later with Gardner Lindzey, he built the Study of Values, a nomothetic self-report test of the relative strength of six values taken from Eduard Spranger's types: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political and religious. With Vernon he also showed (1933) that expressive behaviour, such as handwriting, gesture and voice, is consistent across tasks.

Exam trap

Allport preferred the idiographic approach. The nomothetic approach, factorial designs and chi-square are the group methods he criticised. Nor did he use factor analysis: that is the hallmark of Cattell and Eysenck.

Functional autonomy, the proprium and maturity

Freud explained adult motives by tracing them to childhood. Allport's reply was functional autonomy: the motives of a mature adult become independent of the experiences in which they began, as a grown tree no longer needs its seed. A girl made to practise the veena to escape scolding may, as a woman, play for hours for its own sake. Perseverative functional autonomy covers low-level routines that persist without reward, such as addictions and daily rituals. Propriate functional autonomy covers motives bound up with one's values, self-image and lifestyle, and is the key to adult motivation.

"Propriate" comes from the proprium, Allport's word for the self. It develops through seven aspects: (1) the bodily self, (2) self-identity and (3) self-esteem, in about the first three years; (4) self-extension ("my house, my school") and (5) the self-image, actual and ideal, at about four to six; (6) the self as a rational coper, from six to twelve; and (7) propriate striving, the long-range goals of adolescence. The self returns in Section 4.10.

If childhood needs for affection and security are met, the result is the mature adult, marked by six criteria: (1) extension of the sense of self beyond oneself; (2) warm relating to others; (3) emotional security and self-acceptance; (4) realistic perception, skills and commitment to work; (5) self-objectification, meaning insight together with humour; and (6) a unifying philosophy of life. In 1960 Allport described personality as an open system, taking in energy and information and growing, not merely reducing tension. Critics note that functional autonomy says that motives become independent but not how, that the sharp break between child and adult personality is doubtful, and that the theory produced little research.

Cattell: surface and source traits

Raymond Bernard Cattell (1905 to 1998) studied chemistry in London and then took a PhD in psychology in 1929 under Charles Spearman. Spearman had applied factor analysis to mental abilities; Cattell resolved to apply it to personality. He moved to the United States in 1937 and worked mostly at the University of Illinois. His goal was prediction: "Personality is that which permits a prediction of what a person will do in a given situation" (1950).

Factor analysis examines the correlations among many measures and finds the few underlying factors that account for them: if people rated talkative are also rated sociable and cheerful, one factor may lie beneath all three. Cattell took Allport and Odbert's 4,500 stable-trait words, merged synonyms to leave 171, found that ratings on these formed about 35 clusters, and factor-analysed ratings, questionnaires and tests down to the 16 factors of his Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire, the 16PF (Figure 4.9).

The clusters and the factors give Cattell's most examined distinction. Surface traits are clusters of behaviours that correlate and appear together; anxiety, indecision and irrational fear, for example, form a surface trait one might call neuroticism. Because they arise from several sources, they are less stable and less important. Source traits are the stable, unitary factors beneath them, revealed by factor analysis: the building blocks of personality, as atoms are of matter. By origin, source traits are either constitutional traits, rooted in biology, or environmental-mold traits, learned from social and physical surroundings.

Cattell also separated common traits, which everyone has in some degree, from unique traits belonging to one or a few people (a passion for old coins), and divided traits by function into ability traits (how efficiently we pursue goals), temperament traits (emotional style) and dynamic traits (the motivating forces). Dynamic traits come in two kinds. Ergs are innate drives, such as hunger, sex, fear, curiosity, gregariousness and self-assertion. Sentiments are learned patterns of attitudes centred on an important object, such as career, religion, family or the self; the self-sentiment integrates the rest. A specific attitude ("I want to revise tonight") serves a sentiment, which in turn serves ergs, a chain Cattell called subsidiation. The web of such links is the dynamic lattice (Figure 4.10).

Exam trap

Cattell's pair is surface and source traits. Cardinal and central traits are Allport's; openness and conscientiousness are Big Five factors (Section 4.7); id and ego are Freud's. A factor found in only one kind of data might be an artefact of method, so Cattell required source traits to reappear across three kinds (Table 4.19). T-data (objective test data) come from controlled test situations whose purpose is hidden, so responses are hard to fake. Heredity, development and the wider trait family

Comparing twins and ordinary siblings reared together and apart, Cattell concluded that about one third of personality is genetically based and two thirds environmental, though intelligence (factor B) and boldness (factor H) looked about 80 percent hereditary. He proposed six life-span stages: infancy (birth to 6, the major formative period), childhood (6 to 14), adolescence (14 to 23), maturity (23 to 50), late maturity (50 to 65) and old age. His specification equation predicts a response in a given situation as the sum of the person's trait scores, each weighted by its relevance to that situation. 4.7 Traits II: Eysenck, the Big Five and the Biology of PersonalityLower yield · 18 Qs

Section 4.6 ended with Raymond Cattell's sixteen factors. Hans Eysenck thought three were enough and looked for them in the brain; Paul Costa and Robert McCrae settled on five, the answer that now dominates. This section follows both, then asks how much of a trait is inherited and whether traits predict what you will actually do tomorrow.

Eysenck's three superfactors

Hans Jurgen Eysenck (1916 to 1997) left Berlin for London in 1934 and spent his career at the Maudsley Hospital and Institute of Psychiatry. Like Cattell he used factor analysis, but he distrusted purely statistical factors: a real dimension of personality must also show up in laboratory experiments and have a biological cause. Factor analysis joined to experiment and biology is his signature.

His hierarchical model has four levels (Figure 4.11). At the bottom are specific responses: today you chat with a stranger at the bus stop. Responses that recur become habitual responses: you chat at every bus stop. Correlated habits form traits (sociability, liveliness, activity), and correlated traits form a type or superfactor, here extraversion. An Eysenck type is a broad continuous dimension, not a box people fall into.

His early analyses (Dimensions of Personality, 1947) found two superfactors; a third was added in the 1970s, giving the PEN model:

  • E, extraversion versus introversion. Extraverts are sociable, lively, active, talkative, assertive and sensation seeking; they enjoy parties and large gatherings and look for stimulation. Introverts are quiet, reserved and reflective; they prefer a few close friends, plan before acting and, in the popular phrase, gain energy from solitude while crowds drain them. Introversion is not unfriendliness, aggression or even shyness (Eysenck listed shyness under N).
  • N, neuroticism versus emotional stability. High scorers are anxious, moody, tense, guilt-prone and emotionally reactive, with low self-esteem; low scorers are calm and even-tempered.
  • P, psychoticism versus impulse control. High scorers are aggressive, cold, egocentric, impersonal, impulsive, antisocial and tough-minded, though sometimes creative. P is not psychosis; the scale is closer to psychopathy.
Exam trap

Eysenck's three are E, N and P. "Extraversion, sensing-intuition, thinking-feeling" smuggles in the Myers-Briggs dichotomies from Carl Jung's types (Section 4.4); "psychoticism-conscientiousness" borrows a Big Five factor. His earliest model had only E and N: not openness and conscientiousness (Big Five), not inferiority and superiority striving (Alfred Adler, Section 4.4), not internal and external locus of control (Julian Rotter, Section 4.9). "Aggressive and dominant" never describes the introvert: dominance goes with E, aggression with P.

Arousal, the visceral brain and the four temperaments

Why should one student crave the roar of a cricket stadium while her roommate hides in the library? Eysenck's answer (The Biological Basis of Personality, 1967) was arousal. The ascending reticular activating system (ARAS) in the brainstem sets the level of cortical arousal. Introverts, he proposed, have a higher baseline arousal, so extra stimulation overloads them and they avoid it; extraverts sit below their comfortable level and seek stimulation to climb up to it. The laboratory bore him out: given a few drops of lemon juice on the tongue, introverts salivate more than extraverts, and they are more sensitive to faint stimuli, tolerate pain less and condition faster. Later work confirmed these differences more firmly than the arousal explanation.

Neuroticism rests on the visceral brain, an older name for the limbic system (hippocampus, amygdala, cingulum, septum, hypothalamus), which drives the sympathetic nervous system; in high-N people this alarm fires at mild stress and settles slowly. For P he suggested male hormones, since men score higher; this biology is the weakest.

Because E and N are independent, crossing them gives four quadrants, and Eysenck pointed out that these reproduce the four temperaments of Galen (Section 4.1): the stable extravert is sanguine, the unstable extravert choleric, the unstable introvert melancholic and the stable introvert phlegmatic (Figure 4.12).

Eysenck's questionnaires, many built with his wife Sybil B. G. Eysenck, are the Maudsley Personality Inventory (MPI, 1959; E and N), the Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI, 1964, or 1963 in some sources; E, N and a Lie scale), the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ, 1975), which added P and kept the Lie scale (L) that catches faking good, and the EPQ-R (1985) with an improved P scale. Twin studies supported his view that the dimensions are largely inherited, E and N more than P, and all three appear in over 35 nations. In Crime and Personality (1964) he argued that offenders score high on E and N, adding P in later editions: extraverts condition poorly, so they are slow to acquire the conditioned anxiety that serves as conscience. The P link is the most consistent; the full theory has only modest support.

The Big Five: discovery and the five factors

If Cattell found sixteen factors and Eysenck three, who was right? Researchers who reanalysed trait ratings, following the lexical hypothesis of Section 4.6, kept arriving at five. Donald Fiske (1949) found five factors in ratings on Cattell's variables; Ernest Tupes and Raymond Christal (1961) found five recurrent factors in US Air Force ratings; Warren Norman (1963) replicated them; and Lewis Goldberg named them the Big Five (1981), "big" meaning broad, not important. Meanwhile Paul Costa (born 1942) and Robert McCrae (born 1949), at the US National Institutes of Health in Baltimore, reached the same five through questionnaires. The two roads met in the Five-Factor Model (FFM).

The five are remembered as OCEAN (or CANOE): Openness to experience, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness and Neuroticism. Each is a continuous dimension with two poles, and most people fall near the middle. In the NEO-PI-R each factor, or domain, splits into six narrower facets (Table 4.23).

The Big Five across life and cultures

McCrae and Costa built the factors into five-factor theory (1996). The traits are basic tendencies: biologically based capacities that upbringing does not shape. What life shapes are characteristic adaptations: the habits, attitudes, skills, roles, relationships and self-concept through which a tendency is expressed in a particular culture. High E is a basic tendency; joining the college cultural committee in Mysuru is one adaptation of it.

The factors replicate in more than 50 nations, India among them, suggesting a common human structure. Critics note that most samples are urban and literate, that a remote Bolivian tribal group did not show the five clearly, and that lexical studies in some languages find six factors.

How stable are the factors? People keep their rank order, more so with age, but mean levels shift: from adolescence into middle age, conscientiousness and agreeableness rise while neuroticism falls, the maturity principle. Costa and McCrae borrowed William James's 1890 phrase that by about 30 character is "set like plaster"; later work shows the plaster can still be chipped. Twin studies put the heritability of each factor at about 40 to 50 percent.

The factors also predict outcomes. High conscientiousness and low neuroticism go with better health and longer life. Lewis Terman began following about 1,500 bright Californian children in 1921; when Howard Friedman and colleagues (1993) traced their deaths, those rated conscientious and prudent as children had lived longer. Conscientious people exercise, wear seat belts, avoid smoking and follow medical advice. High N predicts distress and is the broadest personality risk factor for anxiety and depressive disorders (Chapter 8). Conscientiousness also predicted job performance in every occupational group in the meta-analysis of Murray Barrick and Michael Mount (1991).

Exam trap

The health pair is high C plus low N. "Low agreeableness and low neuroticism" swaps in low A, which as hostility (the toxic core of the Type A pattern; Type A and Type D are in Section 1.11) predicts heart disease, not health. "Neuroticism exclusively" is backwards, since high N harms health, and "introversion exclusively" has no such link.

Beyond five: HEXACO and the Dark Triad

Not everyone accepts five. Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee reanalysed trait words across many languages (from 2004) and found six factors, the HEXACO model: Honesty-Humility (sincere, fair, modest versus greedy, boastful, manipulative), Emotionality (fearful, anxious, sentimental; close to N), eXtraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness and Openness to experience. Honesty-Humility, the genuinely new factor, is low in offenders; the 100-item HEXACO-PI-R measures all six.

Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams (2002) described the Dark Triad, three overlapping, socially aversive but subclinical traits: narcissism (grandiosity, entitlement, hunger for admiration), Machiavellianism (cold, cynical, strategic manipulation) and psychopathy (callousness, impulsive thrill seeking, lack of remorse). All three share low agreeableness and low honesty-humility. The Dark Tetrad adds everyday sadism, enjoying others' suffering (Paulhus and colleagues, 2013).

Genes, environments and evolution

How can genes be separated from upbringing? Behaviour genetics uses two natural experiments. Identical (monozygotic) twins share all their genes and fraternal (dizygotic) twins about half, so greater similarity in identical pairs points to genes. In adoption studies, resemblance to the biological parents points to genes, resemblance to the adoptive parents to family environment. The heritability of a trait is the proportion of its variation within a population that is due to genetic differences; it says nothing about how "genetic" one person's trait is.

Key study: the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart (from 1979)

Question: if identical twins grow up in different homes, how alike are they? Method: Thomas Bouchard and colleagues brought more than 100 sets of twins and triplets reared apart to the University of Minnesota for about a week of tests. Result (reported in 1990): identical twins reared apart were about as similar in personality as those reared together; heritability came to about 0.40 to 0.50 for personality and about 0.70 for IQ. Criticism: some pairs grew up in similar homes or had met before testing, and the sample was small and self-selected.

The same studies held a surprise: growing up in the same family adds almost nothing to personality similarity, and adoptive siblings reared together are scarcely more alike than strangers. Plomin and Denise Daniels (1987) asked why children in the same family are so different, and answered that what matters is the non-shared environment: different friends, teachers, illnesses, accidents and differential treatment by parents. The shared environment (the same home, parents, income and religion) has only a weak effect on adult personality.

Genes and environments are also entangled. Sandra Scarr and Kathleen McCartney (1983) described three kinds of gene-environment correlation. In the passive kind, parents give a child both genes and a matching home (musical parents, a house full of music); it is strongest in infancy. In the evocative kind, a child's inherited traits draw responses from others (a smiling baby is picked up more). In the active kind, or niche picking, people seek settings that suit them (the extravert joins the drama club); it grows with age. In gene-environment interaction, by contrast, a gene's effect depends on the environment: a vulnerable genotype may raise the risk of depression only after severe stress.

Why does variation persist at all? David Buss (born 1953) treats traits as adaptive individual differences: the Big Five summarise what our ancestors most needed to know about one another (who will lead, cooperate, work hard, panic or innovate), and different trait levels pay off in different environments. He is not Arnold Buss of the EAS model.

The person-situation debate

Traits assume consistency: an honest child should be honest in class, at play and at home. Hugh Hartshorne and Mark May tested this and found it largely missing (Key study below). Forty years later Walter Mischel (1930 to 2018) generalised the point in Personality and Assessment (1968). Trait measures, he found, correlated with behaviour in a given situation, and behaviour in one situation with behaviour in another, rarely above about 0.30, a ceiling he called the personality coefficient; it explains less than 10 percent of the variation in behaviour. His critique: situational factors often override traits in determining behaviour. So began the person-situation debate.

Key study: Hartshorne and May's honesty studies (1928)

Question: is honesty a consistent trait? Method: in the Character Education Inquiry, thousands of American schoolchildren were given secret chances to cheat, lie or keep money in classroom, party, athletic and home settings. Result: honesty in one setting predicted honesty in another only weakly; a child who copied answers might still return a coin. Conclusion: honesty looked situation-specific. Criticism: single acts are unreliable samples; summed over many tests, the scores showed much more consistency.

Trait theorists replied in three ways. First, aggregation: Seymour Epstein (1979) showed that a single act is as unreliable as a single test item; averaged over many occasions, behaviour becomes far more stable, so traits predict most of the people much of the time. Second, a strong situation (a red traffic light, a funeral, an exam hall) has clear rules that make everyone behave alike, while a weak situation (a party, a free afternoon) lets traits show. Third, interactionism (Norman Endler and David Magnusson, 1976): behaviour depends on the person, the situation and their interaction. Mischel himself rebuilt consistency as stable if-then patterns in his cognitive-affective personality system (Section 4.9). Traits predict behaviour averaged across time and situations well, single acts poorly.

Exam trap

Mischel's critique was that situations often override traits. He did not say that personality is entirely biological (closer to Eysenck), that traits outpredict situations (the opposite of his point), or that traits cannot be measured reliably: trait questionnaires were reliable; they simply predicted single behaviours poorly.

4.8 Humanistic and Existential Theories: Rogers, Maslow and MayLower yield · 16 Qs

Psychoanalysis pictured people driven by unconscious conflict; behaviourism pictured them shaped by reinforcement. By the 1950s a group of American psychologists objected that neither left room for what people experience most directly: choosing, hoping and trying to become someone. The humanists, Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow, asked what a person could grow into. The existentialists, from Heidegger to Rollo May and Viktor Frankl, asked how a person faces freedom, anxiety and death.

The humanistic image of the person

The humanistic approach to personality rests on four assumptions. People have free will and answer for their choices. Every person has an innate tendency to grow and develop, to reach his or her maximum potential. A person must be understood as a whole, not as a bundle of parts. What matters most is conscious, present experience, studied from the inside through the person's phenomenological field: the world as he or she perceives it, which may differ from the world as it is.

Table 4.25 sets this image beside its rivals. Humanism does not explain people by unconscious conflict (the psychodynamic view), conditioned responses (behaviourism) or information processing (the cognitive perspective), nor judge them against statistical norms, the "average is normal" yardstick of trait measurement and of one definition of abnormality (Chapter 8). Its founders were Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers, with Charlotte Buhler (1893 to 1974), who studied the goals that organise the whole life course, and Rollo May, who brought in existential ideas. How it became psychology's "third force" is told in Section 1.2.

The standard criticism is neglect of social and cultural context: "too much focus on cognition" fits the cognitive approach, "too much focus on past experience" fits psychoanalysis, and "neglecting personal experience" is the opposite of what humanists do.

Rogers: the self and its conditions of worth

Carl Ransom Rogers (1902 to 1987) built his theory from what clients told him about their own experience. Its single motive is the actualising tendency, the innate drive of every organism to maintain and enhance itself: it keeps the toddler walking despite falls, and later powers psychological growth. Once a self has formed, part of it becomes the self-actualising tendency, the push to actualise the self as perceived. From infancy, experience is judged by the organismic valuing process: what enhances the organism feels good and is sought, what hinders it feels bad and is avoided.

Each person lives in a private phenomenal field, also called the experiential world: all experience as the person perceives it, the only reality that counts for behaviour. Part of it is gradually set apart as "I" and "me": the self-concept, the organised picture of who one is. Beside it grows the ideal self, the self one would most like to be.

With the self comes a universal need for positive regard (warmth, love and acceptance from important others) and later a need for positive self-regard, approval from within. Unconditional positive regard values the child whatever he or she does: the act may be disapproved of, the child never is. Conditional positive regard is love with strings attached. The child then introjects (takes in as his or her own) the parents' standards as conditions of worth: "I am worthy only when I ...". These work much like Freud's superego, and experience is now valued not by the organismic valuing process but by whether it earns approval.

The result is incongruence: a split between what the organism actually experiences and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth, felt as tension, anxiety and confusion. Its opposite, congruence, is a close match between self-concept and experience. Threatening experience is kept out by distortion (admitting it in a changed form) or denial (keeping it out of awareness). Arjun, praised at home only when he tops the class in science, believes "I am the future doctor". When he finds he loves designing furniture and finds anatomy dull, he distorts it ("I am just tired") or denies it; tension builds and anxiety appears without a nameable cause. Exam trap

"A split between the organismic valuing process and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth, producing tension and confusion" is incongruence. Introjection is only how conditions of worth get in (and a defence mechanism, Section 4.3); conflict is a general term (id, ego and superego in Section 4.2, approach-avoidance in Section 1.10); the actualising tendency is the growth force that incongruence blocks.

The fully functioning person and the Q-sort

A child given unconditional positive regard stays congruent and moves toward what Rogers called the fully functioning person, "a direction, not a destination" (On Becoming a Person, 1961). Five features mark such a person: openness to experience, nothing distorted or denied; existential living, fully in each moment; organismic trusting, relying on one's own reactions; experiential freedom, a felt freedom to choose and responsibility for the choice; and creativity. Rogers stated the theory as 19 propositions in Client-Centered Therapy (1951), beginning with the claim that each person lives at the centre of a continually changing world of experience and reacts to it as perceived. Therapy restores congruence; its core conditions are taught in Chapter 7.

Rogers also made the self measurable. The Q-sort, devised by the British psychologist William Stephenson (The Study of Behavior, 1953) and adopted by Rogers's group at Chicago, has the person sort self-descriptive statements ("I feel helpless", "I am emotionally mature") from "most like me" to "least like me" into a forced, roughly normal distribution: a few cards at each extreme, most in the middle. Because the statements are ranked against one another for the same person, the Q-sort captures within-person rather than between-person variation. Sorting once for the real self and once for the ideal self gives a self-ideal correlation, an index of congruence.

Key study: the case of Mrs Oak (Rogers, 1954)

Does successful therapy bring the perceived self closer to the ideal? Mrs Oak's self-ideal correlation rose from +.36 before client-centred therapy to +.79 a year after it, and in 25 clients studied by Rogers's associates J. M. Butler and G. V. Haigh (1954) the average rose from .01 before therapy to +.31 after it. Criticism: Q-sorts are self-reports, open to the wish to please the therapist, and a rising correlation can come from a lowered ideal as well as a changed self.

Research supports Rogers in part: conditionally loved children show unstable self-esteem, and large self-ideal gaps go with anxiety and depression. Critics note his reliance on conscious self-report and an actualising tendency assumed rather than shown; his lasting gifts were recorded therapy sessions as research data and the self-concept as a variable.

Exam trap

The Q-sort was devised by Stephenson and used by Rogers to measure the self-concept and self-ideal congruence. It did not replace the MMPI (Chapter 2), and it is neither a test of infant reflexes nor a diagnostic test for schizophrenia.

Maslow: deficiency, being and the self-actualiser

Abraham Harold Maslow (1908 to 1970) asked what the healthiest people are like. His hierarchy of needs (physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem, self-actualisation, with lower needs stronger and prepotent) is taught in Section 1.10; here we follow what it implies for personality.

The four lower levels are deficiency needs (D-needs): a lack creates tension that drives us to fill it, and once filled they stop motivating. Behaviour driven by them is D-motivation. Once they are met, a person is moved differently. Maslow called this metamotivation (also B-motivation, from Being), the distinct motivation of self-actualisers: not making up a lack but growing and expressing oneself, even seeking tension. Its objects are being needs, the metaneeds or B-values, such as truth, goodness, beauty, justice, wholeness and meaningfulness. Frustrated metaneeds produce metapathology: cynicism, apathy and a sense of meaninglessness that the sufferer cannot trace to any lack (Table 4.26).

Self-actualisation is the striving to fulfil one's potential, to become everything one is capable of becoming. The neurologist Kurt Goldstein (1878 to 1965) coined the term in The Organism (German 1934, English 1939); Maslow made it the summit of his hierarchy. Finding almost none among students, he studied older people and historical figures, among them the later Abraham Lincoln, Thomas Jefferson, Albert Einstein and Eleanor Roosevelt, and estimated that 1 percent of people or fewer self-actualise. They showed efficient perception of reality; acceptance of self, others and nature; spontaneity; problem-centring (a mission outside themselves); a need for privacy; autonomy and resistance to enculturation; freshness of appreciation; peak experiences; social interest (Adler's term, Section 4.4); deep but few relationships; a democratic character; and creativity.

A peak experience is a brief, intense moment of ecstasy and awe in which the sense of self is transcended; late in life Maslow added the plateau experience, a calmer, longer-lasting and more voluntary serenity. Why do so few self-actualise? Besides unmet lower needs and poor upbringing, Maslow blamed the Jonah complex, the fear of one's own greatness, named after the prophet who ran from God's call. The Personal Orientation Inventory (POI) of Everett Shostrom (1964) later measured self-actualisation with 150 paired statements. Critics note that the sample was small and hand-picked by Maslow's own values, and that his terms are vague. The transpersonal "fourth force" he foresaw is in Section 1.2.

Exam trap

The distinct motivation of self-actualisers is metamotivation; D-motivation drives everyone else, and "E-motivation" and "F-motivation" do not exist. "The striving for fulfilment of one's potential" is self-actualisation, not the self-concept (one's picture of oneself), the ideal self (the self one wishes to be) or self-efficacy (Bandura's belief that one can perform a task, Section 4.9).

Existential roots: Dasein and the three worlds

Both schools reject determinism, but humanism assumes an inner nature, a seed of potential waiting to unfold, and existentialism denies even that. It grew from the Danish philosopher Soren Kierkegaard (1813 to 1855), who wrote of the anxiety of choosing oneself; the German Martin Heidegger (1889 to 1976), author of Being and Time (1927); and the French Jean-Paul Sartre (1905 to 1980), whose slogan was existence precedes essence: we exist first and then make ourselves by our choices. Nothing fixes the meaning of a life in advance, so each person must continually recreate himself or herself, and the meaning of existence keeps changing. This is existential psychology: not humanistic psychology, which assumes a potential to fulfil, nor experimental psychology, which seeks general laws by controlled experiment, nor philosophy alone, since it carries the philosophers' ideas into the study and treatment of persons.

Heidegger's key term was Dasein (German, "being there"): human existence as being-in-the-world. A person is not a mind looking out at a separate world but one unit with it, so Dasein expresses the basic unity of person and environment. Behaviourists, cognitivists and psychoanalysts all split person from world; existentialists refuse the split. The Swiss psychiatrists Ludwig Binswanger (1881 to 1966) and Medard Boss (1903 to 1990) applied Heidegger to patients as Daseinsanalysis, describing each patient's world rather than explaining symptoms.

Binswanger, and after him May, described three modes of being-in-the-world, lived at once (Figure 4.14). The Umwelt ("world around") is the biological and physical world: body, drives, sleep and death. The Mitwelt ("with-world") is the world of relationships with others. The Eigenwelt ("own world") is one's relationship with oneself. Modern people, May argued, are alienated in all three.

Exam trap

Dasein and being-in-the-world are existential, not behavioural, cognitive or psychoanalytic. Dasein is the unity of person and environment; Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt are its three modes, so none of them is "the basic unity". The modes are not id, ego and superego, not conscious, preconscious and unconscious (both Section 4.2), and not Piaget's sensorimotor, preoperational and concrete operational stages (Chapter 6).

Rollo May: anxiety, the daimonic and love

Rollo May (1909 to 1994) brought existential psychology to America. After months in a tuberculosis sanatorium, unsure whether he would live, he wrote The Meaning of Anxiety (1950). For May anxiety is ontological, part of being itself: it arises whenever our existence, or a value we identify with, might be destroyed, and its root is awareness of nonbeing, death but also loss of self in conformity. Normal anxiety is proportionate to the threat, involves no repression and can be faced and used creatively; there is no freedom without it. Neurotic anxiety is disproportionate, involves repression and is handled by blocking off awareness.

In Love and Will (1969) May added three ideas. Intentionality is the structure that gives meaning to experience and lets a person decide about the future; it underlies will. The daimonic is any natural function that can take over the whole person, such as sex, anger or the craving for power: directed, it fuels creativity; unintegrated, it destroys. Love takes four forms: sex (biological release), eros (the longing to create and unite), philia (intimate, non-sexual friendship) and agape (selfless concern for another's welfare). In Freedom and Destiny (1981) he argued that freedom has meaning only against destiny, the givens of body, history and death; healthy people accept their destiny and use their freedom within it.

Frankl and the existential constructs

The Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Emil Frankl (1905 to 1997) survived Auschwitz and observed that prisoners who held on to a reason to live were the likelier to endure. In Man's Search for Meaning (German original 1946) he argued that the primary human motive is neither the will to pleasure (Freud) nor the will to power (Adler) but the will to meaning. Frustrated, it leaves an existential vacuum of emptiness and boredom. If symptoms follow, the result is a noogenic neurosis (Greek noos, mind or spirit), arising from an existential problem such as loss of meaning or a clash of values rather than from conflict between drives; its anxiety is noogenic anxiety. Frankl's therapy, logotherapy, is taught in Chapter 7.

Across the existential writers five constructs recur: coming to terms with anxiety; confronting the universal fear of death; accepting freedom and the responsibility it brings; understanding our ultimate isolation, since no one can live or die for us; and facing meaninglessness by creating meaning. Irvin Yalom's four ultimate concerns restate the list for therapy (Chapter 7). Existentialists do not dig for unconscious fears and desires; that is the psychoanalytic aim. Critics find existential writing vague and hard to test.

Exam trap

Noogenic neurosis and noogenic anxiety are Frankl's and belong to the existential school, not the humanistic, Gestalt (the school of perception in Section 1.2, or Perls's Gestalt therapy in Chapter 7) or psychodynamic school. "Uncovering our unconscious fears and desires" is psychoanalytic, the one option that is not an existential construct.

4.9 Behavioural, Social-Cognitive and Cognitive TheoriesLower yield · 16 Qs

Why does one student freeze before every viva while his classmate, no cleverer, walks in calm? The theorists of this section ask what he has learned, what he expects and how he reads the situation. The story runs from Skinner, who denied any inner person, through the social learning theorists who put expectation and thought back in, to George Kelly, for whom personality is a way of construing the world.

Skinner: personality without a self

Burrhus Frederic Skinner (1904 to 1990) gave the most radical answer: personality is only a collection of learned behaviour patterns, each kept alive by its consequences. This is radical behaviourism: thoughts and feelings are behaviour obeying the same laws, and inner causes explain nothing ("he shouts because he is aggressive" is circular, since the only evidence of aggressiveness is the shouting).

Skinner distinguished two kinds of behaviour. Respondent behaviour is elicited by a specific stimulus: the knee-jerk to a tap, or salivation to Pavlov's bell after conditioning. Operant behaviour is emitted spontaneously or voluntarily and operates on the environment to change it: the rat presses a lever and food drops, the child cries and a parent comes. Most human behaviour is operant, so a personality is a history of reinforcement. Reinforcement and its schedules are taught in Section 1.6.

Shaping (successive approximation) builds a new response by reinforcing closer and closer approximations to it. Superstitious behaviour is behaviour strengthened by accidental reinforcement: pigeons fed every 15 seconds, whatever they did, kept repeating whatever they happened to be doing when food arrived, as a batsman clings to the bat that once scored a century. Self-control, for Skinner, is not willpower but control of the outside variables that control us, by four techniques: stimulus avoidance (studying away from the phone), self-administered satiation (eating sweets until sick of them), aversive stimulation (announcing a diet so that lapses bring ridicule) and self-reinforcement.

Walden Two (1948) is his novel of a utopian community run by positive reinforcement; Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971) argued that freedom is an illusion and that cultures should design their contingencies deliberately. The approach is precise, testable and the source of behaviour modification and token economies; critics say it ignores cognition, heredity and inner experience and generalises too freely from rats and pigeons.

Exam trap

"Behaviour emitted spontaneously or voluntarily that operates on the environment to change it" is operant behaviour (Skinner). The distractors are other theorists' terms: the opposition principle is Jung's (psychic energy arises from conflict between opposites; Section 4.4), behaviour potential is Rotter's (below) and personal dispositions are Allport's individual traits (Section 4.6).

Dollard and Miller: Freud in learning terms

Earlier, two Yale scholars had married psychoanalysis to learning theory. John Dollard (1900 to 1980) and Neal Elgar Miller (1909 to 2002) restated Freud's clinical insights in the drive-reduction terms of Clark Leonard Hull (1884 to 1952), in Social Learning and Imitation (1941) and Personality and Psychotherapy (1950): the early social learning theory. Learning needs four things ("want something, notice something, do something, get something"): a drive, a strong stimulus that impels action; a cue, which signals when, where and how to respond; a response; and reinforcement, the drive reduction that strengthens the cue-response link.

Their key idea was the learned drive. Fear is learned: Miller's rats, once shocked in a white box, learned to turn a wheel to escape it with no shock present, reinforced by fear reduction alone. Freud then translates easily. Repression is learned not-thinking: a forbidden thought arouses fear, dropping it reduces fear, and the relief reinforces not-thinking. Neurotic conflict is competing approach and avoidance tendencies. Both grow stronger nearer the goal, but the avoidance gradient is steeper, so in an approach-avoidance conflict a person approaches a mixed goal from afar, retreats near it and hovers where the two gradients cross (the other conflict types are in Section 1.10). With Yale colleagues they also proposed the frustration-aggression hypothesis (1939): frustration always leads to some form of aggression, and aggression always presupposes frustration (Chapter 3). Bandura: reciprocal determinism and self-regulation

Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) began where Dollard and Miller stopped. With Richard Walters he wrote Social Learning and Personality Development (1963): people learn much of what they do by watching models, without performing the act or being reinforced, which is observational or vicarious learning (its four processes and the Bobo doll studies are in Section 1.6). In Social Foundations of Thought and Action (1986) he renamed the approach social cognitive theory, stressing that thought, not reinforcement alone, guides action.

Its organising principle is reciprocal determinism (Figure 4.15): person (cognitive) factors such as beliefs and expectations, behaviour and the environment each influence the other two, in both directions. The master is not the environment (Skinner), the genes (biological theories) or the unconscious (psychodynamic theory). A student who believes she can master statistics (person) joins a study group (behaviour); its praise (environment) lifts her confidence, and she picks harder problems, changing the environment again.

Exam trap

Reciprocal determinism is central to social cognitive theory, not psychodynamic, humanistic or biological theory, and it means two-way influence, not control by environment alone or genes alone, nor a denial of social reinforcement. Bandura's constructs are reciprocal determinism and observational (vicarious) learning, not reciprocal inhibition (Joseph Wolpe: a response incompatible with anxiety, such as relaxation, suppresses it; the basis of systematic desensitisation), reciprocal altruism (Robert Trivers: helping those who will help in return) or the invented "reciprocal interpretation" and "reciprocal dualism".

Bandura noticed that most behaviour occurs with no external reward or punishment in sight: nobody pays a novelist per page. Most behaviour must therefore stem from self-regulation. People adopt standards, largely from models such as parents and teachers, and run three processes: self-observation (watching their own performance), judgement (comparing it with the standard) and self-response (pride when they meet it, self-criticism when they fall short). Rewarding or punishing oneself this way is self-reinforcement. It is neither negative reinforcement (removal of an unpleasant stimulus, still an external consequence) nor Skinner's behavioural determinism. The machinery can also be switched off: moral disengagement silences self-condemnation during cruelty through moral justification, euphemistic labels, displaced responsibility and dehumanising the victim.

Self-efficacy: believing you can

At the centre of Bandura's theory sits self-efficacy: a person's belief in his capability to organise and execute the actions needed to achieve a specific goal. Bandura introduced it in 1977 as a unifying theory of behavioural change, a cognitive-behavioural account of how people change in therapy (his concept, not Aaron Beck's, Albert Ellis's or Skinner's). It is the key motivational factor of social cognitive theory, ahead of external rewards, biological drives or goal difficulty (goal-setting theory, Section 1.10): people attempt what they believe they can do, aim higher and persist after failure.

Bandura separated two expectations (Figure 4.16). An outcome expectancy is the belief that a behaviour will produce an outcome ("steady revision gets a good rank"); an efficacy expectancy is the belief that one can perform it ("I can revise three hours a day"). Only the second is self-efficacy, and it is task-specific: confident at chess, helpless in a viva.

Efficacy beliefs come from four sources, the first being the strongest:

  1. Performance (enactive) mastery: one's own past successes; early failures weaken efficacy most.
  2. Vicarious experience: seeing someone similar succeed ("if she can, so can I").
  3. Verbal persuasion: being told "you can do it", which helps only if realistic.
  4. Physiological and emotional states: calm is read as readiness, a pounding heart as inability.
Key study: mastering snake phobia (1969)

Bandura, Edward Blanchard and B. Ritter treated adults with intense snake phobia by a film of models approaching snakes, by systematic desensitisation, or by participant modelling: watching a live model handle a snake, then being guided, at first in gloves, to touch and hold it. Participant modelling, which gives direct mastery, worked best, removing the phobia in almost everyone. In later studies (1977) clients' efficacy beliefs after treatment predicted how close they would approach a snake, whichever treatment they had received. Critics reply that efficacy ratings may be predictions of behaviour rather than causes.

Collective efficacy is a group's shared belief that together it can succeed, as in a cricket team or a village self-help group. Bandura later framed the theory around human agency: people are agents who plan, anticipate, regulate and reflect on themselves, not puppets. Table 4.28 separates self-efficacy from its look-alikes.

Rotter: expectancy, value and locus of control

Julian Bernard Rotter (1916 to 2014) built his own social learning theory in Social Learning and Clinical Psychology (1954), asking which of several possible behaviours a person will perform in a situation. Four concepts answer:

  • Behaviour potential: "the potentiality of any behaviour occurring in any given situation as calculated in relation to any single reinforcement or set of reinforcements".
  • Expectancy: the subjective probability that the behaviour will bring that reinforcement.
  • Reinforcement value: how much he prefers that reinforcement to others, expectancies being equal.
  • Psychological situation: the situation as the person perceives it, which shapes both expectancy and value.

Behaviour potential is a function of expectancy and reinforcement value, BP = f(E & RV), often read as a product: a student who prizes a seat at NIMHANS but believes no study will win it will not study. There is no "punishment value" in the theory. Expectancies carried across many situations are generalised expectancies, which give personality its consistency; the best known are locus of control and interpersonal trust (belief that others' word can be relied on; Interpersonal Trust Scale, 1967).

Locus of control is a generalised expectancy about whether one's outcomes are controlled by internal efforts or external forces. A person with an internal locus of control believes outcomes depend on his own effort, ability and preparation, like the student who credits his exam result to his own work. One with an external locus of control credits luck, fate, chance, powerful others or an easy examiner. Rotter's Internal-External (I-E) Scale (1966) has 29 forced-choice pairs, 23 scored and 6 fillers; higher scores mean more externality. Internals, on average, achieve more, seek information, look after their health and cope actively; externals report more anxiety. Warm, consistent parents who praise achievement and encourage independence tend to raise internals. Hanna Levenson (1973) later split it into three separate dimensions: internality, powerful others and chance (her IPC scale).

Exam trap

Locus of control has two poles, internal and external; there is no "neutral" or "situational" locus. It concerns external forces versus internal efforts, not cognitive schemas (Beck), genetic predisposition or social status. It is not self-efficacy: locus of control asks "do outcomes depend on what I do?", self-efficacy asks "can I do it?". Attribution theory, with its own locus dimension, is in Section 1.10.

Mischel's cognitive-affective personality system

Walter Mischel (1930 to 2018) is known for the person-situation critique of trait theory (Section 4.7): people behave less consistently across situations than traits predict. His positive answer, with Yuichi Shoda, was the cognitive-affective personality system (CAPS, 1995), a stable network of five kinds of units: encodings (how one construes self, others and situations), expectancies and beliefs, affects, goals and values, and competencies and self-regulatory plans. Different situations activate different units, so what is stable is not an average level of behaviour but a behavioural signature of if-then relations: Ravi is aggressive if teased by classmates and meek if scolded by a teacher, every time. His delay of gratification research shows self-regulatory competencies at work (the marshmallow test is in Chapter 6).

Kelly: the person as scientist

George Alexander Kelly (1905 to 1967) set out the most purely cognitive theory in The Psychology of Personal Constructs (1955). Ordinary people, he argued, behave like scientists: they form theories, predict events and revise the theories when predictions fail. The theories are personal constructs, bipolar ways of construing events, such as friendly versus hostile. A construct may be permeable (admitting new events, open to revision) or impermeable (rigid whatever the evidence, as in prejudice). Since no one is trapped in one construal, people can always re-construe their lives: constructive alternativism.

The theory has a fundamental postulate, "a person's processes are psychologically channelised by the ways in which he anticipates events", and eleven corollaries (Table 4.29). Since anticipation itself drives behaviour, Kelly needed no separate motivation theory, and he redefined emotions through constructs. Anxiety is recognising that events lie outside the range of one's constructs; threat is awareness of imminent comprehensive change in one's core constructs; fear is the same for an incidental change; guilt is perceiving oneself dislodged from one's core role; hostility is continued effort to extort evidence for a prediction that has already failed.

To find a person's constructs Kelly devised the Role Construct Repertory Test (Rep test). The client names people who fill role titles in his life (mother, father, a liked teacher, a disliked person), then takes them three at a time and says how two are alike and differ from the third; each answer yields a construct. Fixed-role therapy is Kelly's; Aaron Beck developed cognitive therapy, Albert Ellis rational emotive behaviour therapy and Fritz Perls Gestalt therapy (Chapter 7).

Cognitive styles

A cognitive style is a habitual way of perceiving and processing information, distinct from ability. Herman Witkin (1916 to 1979) described field dependence-independence. In the rod-and-frame test a person in a dark room sets a luminous rod to vertical inside a tilted luminous frame: field-dependent people tilt the rod towards the frame, field-independent people trust their bodily sense of upright and also find shapes hidden in complex designs faster on the Embedded Figures Test. Field-independent people are more analytic, field-dependent people more global and socially attentive. Jerome Kagan (1929 to 2021) described reflection-impulsivity: on the Matching Familiar Figures Test, where a child picks which of several near-identical pictures matches a standard, impulsive children answer fast with many errors, reflective children slowly and accurately.

The self: James, Cooley and Mead

The modern study of the self begins with William James (1842 to 1910), whose Principles of Psychology (1890) split it in two. The I is the self as knower, the subject doing the thinking; the me is the self as known, everything a person can call his own. James divided the me into three parts. The material self is the body, clothes, family, home and possessions, which is why losing a phone can feel like losing a piece of yourself. The social self is the recognition one gets from others; a person has as many social selves as there are groups whose opinion he cares about. The spiritual self is the inner world of dispositions, conscience and faculties, the most intimate and enduring part.

James also gave a formula: self-esteem = success / pretensions. A student who aimed only to pass is content with 55 percent; one who pretended to the first rank is crushed by the second (James's example was a man ashamed to be only the second-best boxer in the world). So self-esteem rises in two ways: succeed more, or lower the pretensions.

For Charles Horton Cooley (1864 to 1929), writing in Human Nature and the Social Order (1902), the self is social from the start. His looking-glass self is the self-concept we build by seeing ourselves in other people as in a mirror, in three steps (Figure 4.17): we imagine how we appear to the other person, we imagine that person's judgement of that appearance, and we develop a self-feeling, such as pride or shame, from that imagined judgement. Every step is imagined, so the mirror can distort.

George Herbert Mead (1863 to 1931), whose lectures appeared after his death as Mind, Self and Society (1934), explained how the mirror gets inside us. With Cooley he founded the tradition later called symbolic interactionism: the self emerges through social interaction and shared symbols, above all language. Mead's "I" is the spontaneous, creative, unpredictable response of the person; his "me" is the organised set of others' attitudes that the person has taken in, the socialised self. The child builds the me by taking roles. In the play stage the child takes the role of one particular other at a time, playing at being mother or policeman. In the game stage, organised games demand holding everyone's role at once: a fielder in cricket must anticipate bowler, batter and captain within shared rules. The organised attitudes of the whole group, and finally of the community, form the generalised other, the standpoint from which the person judges himself.

Exam trap

Looking-glass self = imagined appearance, imagined judgement, self-feeling. The distractors are other theories: innate, unconscious drives (Freud, Section 4.2); comparing ourselves with similar others (Leon Festinger's social comparison theory, 1954, Chapter 3); resolving psychosocial crises (Erik Erikson, Section 6.10); operant reinforcement of self-statements (Section 4.9); solitary introspection or genetic inheritance (Cooley's self is neither private nor inborn). James's I is the knower, Mead's the spontaneous actor; the generalised other is Mead's.

Self-concept, self-esteem and the self-system

Two terms that are often blurred need separating. The self-concept is the cognitive description of who one is, the organised set of beliefs about one's own attributes: "I am a Kannadiga, a fast bowler, shy, good at maths." Self-esteem is the evaluative and affective component, how one feels about that self: "and I am worthwhile", or "and I am a failure." Two students may share the self-concept "I am shy", one at peace with it and one ashamed of it. The standard measure of global self-esteem is the ten-item Self-Esteem Scale (1965) of Morris Rosenberg (1922 to 1992), with statements such as "On the whole, I am satisfied with myself" rated from strongly agree to strongly disagree.

Nor is self-esteem the same as self-efficacy (Albert Bandura), the belief that one can carry out a specific task such as a statistics problem (Section 4.9), or self-actualisation (Kurt Goldstein's term, made famous by Abraham Maslow), the striving to fulfil one's potential (Section 4.8). Later researchers treated the self as a system that stores and uses information about itself (Table 4.30).

Limited-domain traits: sensation seeking and explanatory style

A limited-domain theory studies one trait in depth rather than the whole person. Marvin Zuckerman (1928 to 2018) defined sensation seeking as the need for varied, novel, complex and intense sensations and experiences, and the willingness to take physical, social, legal and financial risks for them. His Sensation Seeking Scale (SSS; first form 1964, later the 40-item Form V) offers forced choices such as "I would like to try parachute jumping". Factor analysis yielded four components:

  • Thrill and adventure seeking: a desire for speed, danger and defying gravity, as in bungee jumping or scuba diving.
  • Experience seeking: novel experience through travel, music, art or a nonconformist lifestyle.
  • Disinhibition: release through wild parties, drinking and risky sex.
  • Boredom susceptibility: an aversion to routine, repetition and predictable people.

Sensation seeking is strongly heritable and belongs within Eysenck's extraversion, with its impulsive form also tied to psychoticism (Section 4.7). It rises in adolescence, falls with age and is higher in men. High sensation seekers have low levels of monoamine oxidase (MAO), the enzyme that breaks down dopamine and the other monoamines, which Zuckerman linked to a strong dopamine-driven approach system.

Martin E. P. Seligman (born 1942) reached optimism by way of learned helplessness (Section 1.6), in which dogs given inescapable shock later failed to escape avoidable shock. People, he found, differ in how far helplessness spreads, depending on how they explain bad events to themselves. This habitual way of explaining is the explanatory style, which Learned Optimism (1990) describes by three Ps. Permanence: is the cause lasting ("I will always fail") or temporary ("I was tired that day")? Pervasiveness: does it spoil everything ("I am useless") or one area ("I am weak in statistics")? Personalisation: is it me ("I am stupid") or outside me ("the paper was unusually hard")? The pessimist explains bad events as permanent, pervasive and personal, and good events as temporary, specific and lucky; the optimist does the reverse, and is less prone to depression, illness and giving up. The Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ; Christopher Peterson, Seligman and colleagues, 1982) measures it: the respondent names the main cause of imagined good and bad events and rates it as internal, stable and global. Being learned, the style can be retrained. Dispositional optimism and hardiness belong to stress (Section 1.11).

Well-being: hedonic and eudaimonic

Positive psychology (its founding, flow and PERMA are in Sections 1.2 and 1.10) asks what makes a life go well, and two traditions answer differently. Hedonic well-being equates the good life with pleasure: positive feelings, comfort, enjoyment and satisfaction with life. Ed Diener (1946 to 2021) measured it as subjective well-being (SWB): high life satisfaction, frequent positive affect and infrequent negative affect, with life satisfaction assessed by his Satisfaction With Life Scale (1985).

Eudaimonic well-being goes back to Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE), for whom eudaimonia, flourishing, came from living by virtue and realising one's best potential. Here well-being means cultivating personal strengths, meaning and growth and contributing to the greater good, whether or not it feels pleasant at the moment. Carol Ryff (1989) gave it six dimensions of psychological well-being: self-acceptance, personal growth, purpose in life, environmental mastery (managing one's surroundings and demands), autonomy (self-determination and independence from social pressure) and positive relations with others. A holiday of films and good food yields hedonic well-being; one spent teaching village children to read may leave you tired but richer in eudaimonic well-being. The two correlate but are distinct.

Exam trap

Eudaimonic = cultivating strengths, growth and contribution to the greater good. "Pleasure and relaxation" and "pleasure and happiness" describe hedonic well-being. "Income and material possessions" is a material yardstick that neither tradition accepts as well-being itself.

The same strengths define character in sport. Sportsmanship is playing hard within the rules and with respect for opponents: the true sportsman is persistent (perseverance), keeps self-control under provocation (self-regulation), stays optimistic after a defeat (hope) and plays fair (fairness). Focusing on winning at all costs, by cheating, injuring or gamesmanship, is its opposite: the batter who walks after edging the ball shows character; the bowler who tampers with the ball does not.

The triguna theory of personality

The triguna theory comes mainly from the Samkhya school, attributed to the sage Kapila, together with the Bhagavad Gita (chapters 14, 17 and 18), with earlier hints in the Upanishads. It does not come from Nyaya (the school of logic), Mimamsa (the school of Vedic ritual) or the materialist Charvaka. Samkhya divides reality into purusha, pure consciousness, the passive witness, and prakriti, primordial nature, from which body and mind evolve (Section 1.2 gives the full scheme, the inner instrument and the koshas). The three gunas (strands or qualities) belong to prakriti, not purusha; the Gita calls them "born of prakriti" (14.5). Because the mind evolves from prakriti, the gunas are the stuff of personality:

  • Sattva is clarity, goodness, light, calm, balance and knowledge; the sattvic person is truthful, self-controlled, content and wise.
  • Rajas is activity, passion, desire and restlessness; the rajasic person is ambitious, driven and attached to results, and tips into over-activity, greed, anger and anxiety.
  • Tamas is inertia, dullness, darkness, lethargy and ignorance; the tamasic person is heavy, careless and confused, sleeps too much and cannot bring himself to act.

Every jiva, every embodied individual self, has all three in shifting proportions. None is ever absent; the dominant guna colours the personality (Figure 4.18), and the Gita traces that colouring through daily conduct (Table 4.32).

Exam trap

The only gunas are sattva, rajas and tamas. Not gunas: ahimsa (non-violence, the first of the yamas); yama, pratyahara and samadhi (absorption, the eighth limb), all limbs of yoga; jnana (knowledge); samskara (the latent impression a past act leaves on the mind); ahamkara (the I-maker or ego, a product of prakriti); prakriti itself; vata (an Ayurvedic dosha). The gunas belong to prakriti, not to purusha, atman (the true self), Brahman (ultimate reality), ahamkara, samadhi or samskara. Distractor triads: prakriti, purusha and kshetra (the "field", the body that the self knows); atman, Brahman and maya (appearance), the Vedanta triad; karma, dharma and moksha (action, duty and liberation).

The gunas have been turned into questionnaires: David Wolf's Vedic Personality Inventory (VPI, 1998) and Indian instruments such as the Mysore Triguna Scale score sattva, rajas and tamas separately. Such studies tend to link sattva with mental health and life satisfaction, and tamas with depression and poor adjustment.

Ayurvedic constitution and Indian typologies

Ayurveda, India's classical medicine, adds a bodily typology. Ayurveda also describes a mental constitution in terms of the gunas, and calls rajas and tamas the two doshas of the mind.

The Boy on the Vienna Tram, Revisited

Return to Allport's tram story with the whole chapter behind you. Freud's question was psychoanalysis in one line: an innocent anecdote read as the surface of hidden conflict (Sections 4.2 and 4.3). Chapter summary

4.1 What Personality Is: Definitions, Approaches and Early Type Theories. Personality, from the Latin persona (the actor's mask), is the relatively enduring, distinctive pattern of thought, feeling and behaviour; Allport (1937) called it the dynamic organisation of psychophysical systems. Temperament is its biologically based, early-appearing emotional core; character is personality evaluated; traits are dimensions, types are categories. Theories describe, explain, predict and generate research, and differ on Schultz's six questions about human nature. Personality reflects heredity, environment and the situation in interaction, and is studied by clinical, experimental and correlational methods, nomothetically or idiographically (Windelband). The early type theories, the four humours, physiognomy, Kretschmer's body types and Sheldon's somatotypes, did not survive testing.

4.2 Freud's Psychoanalysis: Instincts, Structure and Psychosexual Stages. Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939), a Viennese neurologist, built psychoanalysis from hysteria: Charcot's hypnosis, Bernheim's suggestion and Breuer's Anna O. (catharsis; Studies on Hysteria, 1895). Free association, dream analysis and parapraxes open the unconscious. Instincts (Eros and its libido; the death instinct, 1920) supply energy invested by cathexis. The topographic model divides conscious, preconscious and unconscious; the structural model (1923) sets the id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle, the executive) and superego (conscience and ego-ideal) in conflict, and anxiety (reality, neurotic, moral) signals danger. Fixation at the oral, anal or phallic (Oedipus) stage shapes adult character; latency and the genital stage follow. Research supports unconscious processes, not the stages.

4.3 Defence Mechanisms: How the Ego Protects Itself. Defence mechanisms are the ego's unconscious, reality-distorting strategies against anxiety, described by Sigmund Freud and catalogued by Anna Freud in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936); they are adaptive in moderation, pathological when rigid, and never rational. Repression, the involuntary blocking of threatening material from awareness, is the primary defence; suppression is its conscious, mature counterpart. Displacement changes the target, sublimation the activity, projection the owner, reaction formation the direction and rationalisation the stated reason. George Vaillant (1977) ranked defences as psychotic, immature, neurotic and mature. Obsessive-compulsive disorder favours isolation, undoing and reaction formation; phobias, displacement; hysteria, repression; borderline personality disorder, splitting.

4.4 Jung's Analytical Psychology and Adler's Individual Psychology. Jung and Adler were the first neo-Freudians to break with Freud (Adler in 1911, Jung in 1913). Jung's analytical psychology widened libido into general psychic energy and placed, beneath the personal unconscious and its complexes (detected with the word association test), an inherited collective unconscious of archetypes: persona, shadow, anima, animus and self. His two attitudes and four functions give eight types, the basis of the MBTI, and individuation belongs to midlife. Adler's individual psychology sees behaviour as goal-directed: universal inferiority feelings drive compensation and striving for superiority, shaped by fictional finalism, the style of life, social interest and birth order.

4.5 Horney, Fromm, Sullivan and the Object-Relations Theorists. Karen Horney traced neurosis to basic anxiety, the feeling of being lonely and helpless in a hostile world, managed by ten neurotic needs grouped into moving toward, against and away from people; she added the idealised self, the tyranny of the shoulds and womb envy. Erich Fromm described mechanisms of escape from freedom, five existential needs and character orientations; Harry Stack Sullivan saw personality as recurrent interpersonal situations. Ego psychology (Anna Freud, Hartmann, Erikson) strengthened the ego. Object relations are internalised images of self and others: Klein's splitting and two positions, Winnicott's good-enough mother. Kohut's selfobjects and Lacan's mirror stage complete the section.

4.6 Traits I: Allport, Cattell and the Lexical Search. The trait approach describes personality through stable, consistent dimensions of individual difference, unlike types, states or situationism. The lexical hypothesis led Allport and Odbert (1936) to cull 17,953 trait words from a dictionary. Gordon Allport treated traits as real neuropsychic structures, separated common traits from personal dispositions (cardinal, central, secondary), preferred the idiographic study of single cases (Letters from Jenny), and proposed functional autonomy, the proprium and six marks of maturity. Raymond Cattell factor-analysed the lexicon into surface and source traits, described ergs and sentiments, required L-, Q- and T-data, and built the 16PF (1949).

4.7 Traits II: Eysenck, the Big Five and the Biology of Personality. Hans Eysenck joined factor analysis to experiment and biology: a hierarchy from specific responses to types, and three superfactors, extraversion (cortical arousal set by the ARAS), neuroticism (the visceral brain) and psychoticism; crossing E and N gives Galen's four temperaments. Gray, Cloninger, Kagan, and Buss and Plomin extended the biology of temperament. The Big Five (OCEAN) came from lexical and questionnaire research; Costa and McCrae's NEO-PI-R has 240 items and 30 facets. High conscientiousness and low neuroticism predict health. HEXACO adds honesty-humility. Heritability is about 40 to 50 percent, with the non-shared environment mattering most. Mischel (1968) argued that situations often override traits; aggregation and interactionism replied.

4.8 Humanistic and Existential Theories: Rogers, Maslow and May. The humanistic approach stresses free will, an innate tendency to grow toward one's maximum potential, the whole person and conscious experience; disorder is blocked growth, and critics call it vague and blind to culture. Rogers's actualising tendency, organismic valuing process, conditions of worth and incongruence explain how conditional love splits self from experience; the fully functioning person is the ideal, and the Q-sort measures self-ideal congruence. Maslow contrasted D-motivation with metamotivation and described self-actualisers, peak experiences and the Jonah complex. Existentialists (Heidegger, Sartre, Binswanger, May, Frankl) describe Dasein in the Umwelt, Mitwelt and Eigenwelt, normal and neurotic anxiety, the will to meaning and noogenic neurosis.

4.9 Behavioural, Social-Cognitive and Cognitive Theories. Skinner's radical behaviourism treats personality as learned operant behaviour (emitted, acting on the environment) with no inner causes. Dollard and Miller (1950) recast Freud in Hull's drive, cue, response and reinforcement: early social learning. Bandura (later social learning, with Walters) proposed reciprocal determinism among person, behaviour and environment, self-regulation and self-efficacy (1977), fed by mastery, vicarious experience, persuasion and arousal. Rotter: behaviour potential = f(expectancy, reinforcement value); internal versus external locus of control (I-E Scale, 1966). Mischel's CAPS gives if-then signatures. Kelly (1955): the person as scientist, bipolar constructs, the Rep test and fixed-role therapy. Witkin: field dependence; Kagan: reflection-impulsivity.

4.10 The Self, Positive Traits and Indian Approaches to Personality. William James (1890) split the self into the knowing I and the known me (material, social and spiritual selves) and defined self-esteem as success divided by pretensions. Cooley's looking-glass self (1902) builds the self-concept from imagined appearance, imagined judgement and self-feeling; Mead (1934) added the play and game stages and the generalised other. Self-concept is cognitive, self-esteem evaluative (Rosenberg, 1965). Zuckerman's sensation seeking and Seligman's explanatory style are limited-domain traits. Hedonic well-being is pleasure; eudaimonic well-being is strengths, growth and contribution (Ryff's six dimensions). The triguna theory (Samkhya and the Gita) describes sattva, rajas and tamas, strands of prakriti; Ayurveda adds vata, pitta and kapha.

    (a) Disposition (b) Character (c) Trait (d) Temperament

    Answer: (d) Temperament. Temperament is the biologically based, early-appearing emotional style. Character is personality evaluated morally and learned, a trait is a descriptive dimension not defined by its biological origin, and disposition is too loose a term.

  2. Sheldon's theory that people with different body types have different personalities has been: (RML 2020)

    (a) supported by research (b) discredited (c) shown to be accurate for thin people but not overweight people (d) shown to be accurate for women but not for men

    Answer: (b) discredited. Sheldon rated physique and temperament himself, so his expectations inflated the correlations, and independent studies found only weak links. It holds for no subgroup, and his subjects were men.

  3. Physiognomy, an early approach to personality study, was based on : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Brain localization of functions (b) Dreams and unconscious motives (c) Handwriting style analysis (d) Facial features as indicators of character

    Answer: (d) Facial features as indicators of character. Brain localisation read from skull bumps is phrenology (Gall), handwriting analysis is graphology, and dreams as clues to unconscious motives belong to psychoanalysis.

  4. According to Freud, fixation at which stage of development is associated with sarcasm, criticalness, overeating, and nail-biting in adults? (RML 2020)

    (a) Oral (b) Anal (c) Phallic (d) Latency

    Answer: (a) Oral. Overeating and nail-biting are oral-incorporative habits, and sarcasm and criticalness are oral-aggressive (biting) traits; both come from problems of feeding and weaning. Anal fixation gives orderliness, stinginess and obstinacy, or messiness and cruelty; latency is not a fixation point.

  5. According to Freud, which term refers to the part of the personality that mediates between desires and social rules? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Id (b) Superego (c) Ego (d) Self

    Answer: (c) Ego. Working on the reality principle, the ego is the executive that mediates between the id's desires, the superego and reality or social rules. The id is pure desire (pleasure principle), the superego is internalised morality, and the self is not one of Freud's structures.

  6. In psychoanalytic theory, what term describes the mental representation of psychic energy being invested or attached to a particular object, person, or idea? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Libido (b) Cathexis (c) Fixation (d) Sublimation

    Answer: (b) Cathexis. Cathexis is the investment of psychic energy in an object, person or idea. Libido is the energy itself, fixation is libido left stuck at a psychosexual stage, and sublimation channels it into socially valued activity.

  7. A woman harassed by her boss at work, initiates an argument with her husband. She is using which of the following ego's defense mechanisms: (RML 2024)

    (a) Projection (b) Identification (c) Sublimation (d) Displacement

    Answer: (d) Displacement. Anger at the boss, who could punish her, is redirected onto a safer target, her husband. Projection would attribute her anger to someone else, identification would take on another's qualities, and sublimation would channel the anger into a socially valued activity.

  8. Which type of defence mechanism is dissociation? (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Mature (b) Immature (c) Neurotic (d) Conscious

    Answer: (c) Neurotic. Vaillant (1977) placed dissociation at Level III with intellectualisation, isolation, repression, displacement and reaction formation. It is not immature (projection, acting out and the like), and defences are not conscious as a class; only suppression is deliberate.

  9. Which of the following defense mechanisms is not involved in obsessive-compulsive disorder? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Reaction formation (b) Repression (c) Isolation (d) Undoing

    Answer: (b) Repression. In obsessional neurosis the disturbing idea stays conscious: its feeling is isolated, rituals undo it and reaction formation turns it into its opposite. Repression, which pushes the idea itself out of awareness, is the hallmark of hysteria.

  10. Who believed that the universe of the collective unconscious could be accounted for by evolutionary theory, through the similarity of brain structure evident in all human races? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Freud (b) Jung (c) Horney (d) None of these

    Answer: (b) Jung. The collective unconscious is Jung's inherited, universal layer of the psyche, and he explained it by the same brain structure in all human races. Freud's unconscious was personal, and Horney stressed social and cultural forces.

  11. The basic assumption of Adler's individual psychology is (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) human behaviour is purposeful and goal directed. (b) an optimistic view of human life. (c) human beings are social beings who could be understood in terms of their relationship with others. (d) development is a lifelong process and ego identity is granted a central place in the process.

    Answer: (a) Human behaviour is purposeful and goal directed. Adler explained behaviour by future goals that pull us forward. Optimism and the social emphasis are features of his view, not its basic assumption, and lifelong development with ego identity at the centre is Erikson's.

  12. According to Adler, middle children tend to be: (RML 2022)

    (a) Overachieving (b) Competitive (c) Resentful of the freedom of the older child (d) Filled with feelings of inferiority

    Answer: (b) Competitive. With a pacesetter always ahead, the middle (second) child behaves as if in a race: ambitious and competitive. Such a child may also give up and underachieve, and inferiority feelings are universal, not the mark of the middle child.

  13. Karen Horney emphasized personality development as influenced primarily by (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Social and cultural factors. (b) Biological instincts exclusively. (c) Unconscious sexual conflicts exclusively. (d) Classical conditioning only.

    Answer: (a) Social and cultural factors. Horney broke with Freud's instinct theory: basic anxiety grows from parental indifference and the culture around the child. Instincts and sexual conflicts are Freud's emphasis, and conditioning belongs to behaviourism.

  14. In paranoia, the ego's defense mechanism as given by Melaine Klein is seen as: (RML 2024)

    (a) Reaction formation (b) Splitting (c) Suppression (d) Projection

    Answer: (b) Splitting. Klein's paranoid-schizoid position rests on splitting good from bad objects; projection then puts the bad part outside. Projection is Freud's classic mechanism of paranoia, while reaction formation and suppression are unrelated defences.

  15. A client tells you that they are "no one" and feels "empty inside." They report chronic fears of abandonment and have a history of intense, unstable relationships. According to psychoanalytic object relations theory, this pathology of the self is most likely a result of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Fixation at the oral stage of psychosexual development. (b) A failure in parental mirroring and idealizing, leading to a fragmented self. (c) Unresolved Oedipal complex and castration anxiety. (d) Harsh superego demands leading to constant guilt.

    Answer: (b) A failure in parental mirroring and idealizing, leading to a fragmented self. This is Kohut's self psychology: unmet selfobject needs leave an empty, fragmented self. Oral fixation, the Oedipus complex and superego guilt are classical Freudian conflict explanations, not deficits of the self.

  16. Cattlell (sic) distinguished between which two types of traits? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Openness and Conscientiousness (b) Cardinal and central traits (c) Surface traits and Source traits (d) Id and Ego

    Answer: (c) Surface traits and source traits. Surface traits are clusters of correlated behaviours; source traits are the stable underlying factors that factor analysis reveals. Cardinal and central traits are Allport's, openness and conscientiousness are Big Five factors, and id and ego are Freud's.

  17. Allport believed that traits could be organised into three levels: (RML 2020)

    (a) primary, secondary, tertiary (b) cognitive, emotional, physiological (c) id, ego, superego (d) cardinal, central, secondary

    Answer: (d) cardinal, central, secondary. A cardinal trait is a ruling passion found in few people, central traits are the five to ten that describe a person, and secondary traits are minor and inconsistent. Primary-secondary-tertiary is invented, and id-ego-superego is Freud's structure.

  18. According to Raymond Cattell's classification of personality data sources, which of the following is NOT recognized as a legitimate data type? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Q-data (Questionnaire data) (b) L-data (Life record data) (c) T-data (Objective test data) (d) P-data (Projective data)

    Answer: (d) P-data. Cattell used exactly three sources: L-data (life records), Q-data (questionnaires) and T-data (objective tests). He classed hard-to-fake tasks such as inkblots as T-data, and his P-technique is a research design, not a data type.

  19. Hans Eysenck proposed which three dimension of personality? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Extraversion-Introversion, Neuroticism-Stability, Psychoticism (b) Extraversion-Introversion, Sensing-Intuition, Thinking-Feeling (c) Openness, Extraversion, Judging, Perceiving (d) Extraversion-Introversion, Neuroticism-Stability, Psychoticism-Conscientiousness

    Answer: (a) Extraversion-Introversion, Neuroticism-Stability, Psychoticism. These are Eysenck's PEN superfactors. Sensing-intuition, thinking-feeling, judging and perceiving are Myers-Briggs dichotomies from Jung's types, and conscientiousness is a Big Five factor, not part of Eysenck's model.

  20. Which personality trait is linked to better health outcomes? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Low Agreeableness and Low Neuroticism (b) Neuroticism exclusively (c) Introversion exclusively (d) High conscientiousness and Low neuroticism

    Answer: (d) High conscientiousness and low neuroticism. Conscientious people look after their health and lived longer in Terman's sample, and low neuroticism means less distress. Low agreeableness (hostility) predicts heart disease, high neuroticism harms health, and introversion alone has no health advantage.

  21. Mischel's critique of personality traits mainly emphasized that : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Situational factors often override traits in determining behavior (b) Personality is entirely biologically based (c) Traits are more important than situations in predicting behavior (d) Traits cannot be measured reliably

    Answer: (a) Situational factors often override traits in determining behaviour. In Personality and Assessment (1968) Mischel found trait-behaviour correlations rarely above about 0.30. He did not claim personality is biological, (c) is the opposite of his point, and trait questionnaires were reliable; they simply predicted single acts poorly.

  22. In existential psychology, Dasein encompasses three fundamental psychological dimensions of human existence. Which of the following correctly identifies these three modes of being-in-the-world? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Id, Ego, and Superego (b) Umwelt, Mitwelt, and Eigenwelt (c) Conscious, Preconscious, and Unconscious (d) Sensorimotor, Preoperational, and Concrete Operational

    Answer: (b) Umwelt, Mitwelt, and Eigenwelt. Binswanger and May describe the biological and physical world (Umwelt), the world of relationships (Mitwelt) and one's relation to oneself (Eigenwelt). Id, ego and superego is Freud's structural model, conscious to unconscious his topographic model, and the last option Piaget's stages.

  23. Noogenic anxiety is described under: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Humanistic School (b) Gestalt school (c) Existential school (d) Psychodynamic school

    Answer: (c) Existential school. Viktor Frankl's noogenic neurosis and anxiety arise from a frustrated will to meaning, an existential problem, not from drive conflict (psychodynamic), blocked growth (humanistic) or Gestalt ideas.

  24. A split between the organismic valuing process and a self-concept burdened by conditions of worth which results in feelings of tension and confusion: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Conflict (b) Incongruence (c) Introjection (d) Actualizing tendency

    Answer: (b) Incongruence. Rogers's term for the gap between experience and a self-concept shaped by conditions of worth. Introjection is how those conditions are taken in, conflict is a general term, and the actualising tendency is the growth force that incongruence blocks.

  25. Which concept is central to Bandura's Personality Theory? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Self-efficacy (b) Inferiority complex (c) Collective unconscious (d) Congruence

    Answer: (a) Self-efficacy. Self-efficacy, the belief that one can organise and execute the actions needed for a specific goal, is the core of Bandura's social cognitive theory. The inferiority complex is Adler's, the collective unconscious Jung's and congruence Rogers's.

  26. Which of the following best describes the concept of 'reciprocal determinism' as proposed by Albert Bandura? (RML 2025)

    (a) Individual behavior is solely controlled by environmental factors. (b) Cognitive processes influence both behavior and the environment in a bidirectional manner. (c) Personality develops strictly from genetic inheritance and biological predispositions. (d) Social reinforcement has no impact on self-concept formation.

    Answer: (b). In reciprocal determinism person (cognitive) factors, behaviour and environment influence one another in both directions. Environment alone is Skinner's position, genes alone a biological one, and Bandura never denied the role of social reinforcement.

  27. Julian Rotter's concept of locus of control describes whether individuals believe that their outcomes are controlled by : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) External forces or internal efforts (b) Their cognitive schemas (c) Genetic predisposition (d) Social status

    Answer: (a) External forces or internal efforts. Locus of control is a generalised expectancy that outcomes depend either on one's own effort (internal) or on luck, fate, chance and powerful others (external). Cognitive schemas belong to Beck; genes and social status are not part of the construct.

  28. The "looking-glass self," a concept introduced by Charles Horton Cooley, posits that our self-concept is primarily formed by: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Our innate, unconscious drives. (b) Comparing ourselves to others who are similar. (c) Imagining how we appear to others and how they judge us. (d) Successfully resolving psychosocial crises at each life stage.

    Answer: (c) Imagining how we appear to others and how they judge us. Cooley's three steps are imagined appearance, imagined judgement and a self-feeling such as pride or shame. Unconscious drives are Freud's, comparison with similar others is Festinger's social comparison, and psychosocial crises are Erikson's.

  29. Which of the following best describes the concept of "self-esteem" in contrast to "self-concept"? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Self-esteem is the cognitive appraisal of one's traits, while self-concept is the evaluative and emotional component. (b) Self-esteem is the evaluative and affective component of the self (how one feels about oneself), while self-concept is the cognitive description of who one is. (c) Self-esteem and self-concept are identical and can be used interchangeably. (d) Self-esteem is a collective term for the self in relation to others, while self-concept is the individual self.

    Answer: (b) Self-esteem is evaluative and affective; self-concept is the cognitive description of who one is. Option (a) reverses the two, they are not identical, and the individual versus collective self is a different distinction altogether.

  30. Bavisha lost her job. Since then she is stressed, has stopped eating properly and cannot get herself to do anything. According to yogic philosophy, the main issue she is dealing with is: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Yama alone (b) Tamas alone (c) Both yama and tamas (d) Rajas alone

    Answer: (b) Tamas alone. Not eating, withdrawal and the inability to act are the inertia and lethargy of tamas. Rajas would show as restless over-activity and agitation, and yama is not a mental state at all but the first limb of Patanjali's yoga, the ethical restraints.
