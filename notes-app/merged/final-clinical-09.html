On the night of Saturday 28 November 1942, the Thanksgiving weekend, the Cocoanut Grove was the busiest nightclub in Boston. About a thousand people, more than twice the club's legal capacity, were packed into its rooms: soldiers and sailors on leave, young couples, families out to celebrate. A little after ten o'clock a flame appeared among the artificial palm trees and cloth hangings of the basement bar. Within minutes, fire and poisonous smoke had swept through the whole building. The main entrance was a single revolving door, and it jammed as the crowd pressed against it. Other doors were locked, hidden behind curtains, or opened inwards against the crush. By morning 492 people were dead. It remains one of the deadliest fires in American history.

The burned and the dying were carried to the city's hospitals, Massachusetts General among them. A psychiatrist on that hospital's staff, Erich Lindemann, began to sit with the survivors and with the families of the dead. He went on interviewing bereaved people for months, and in 1944 he published what he had seen in a paper called "Symptomatology and management of acute grief", based on 101 bereaved people. Grief, he wrote, is not a vague sadness. It is a definite syndrome, with bodily distress that comes in waves (tightness in the throat, choking, sighing, an empty feeling in the stomach, exhaustion), preoccupation with the image of the dead person, guilt, irritability towards others, and a loss of the ordinary patterns of daily life. With help, most of the bereaved worked their way through this "grief work" in a matter of weeks; eight to ten interviews over four to six weeks were ordinarily enough. Some did not. Their grief was delayed or distorted, and it surfaced months later as physical illness, agitation, hostility or depression.

Lindemann drew a bold conclusion. If the normal course of a reaction to a terrible event could be described, then the people who were going wrong could be spotted early, and a few well-timed conversations might stop a temporary crisis from hardening into a lasting disorder. In 1948 he set up one of the first community mental health projects, the Wellesley Human Relations Service near Boston, and he was later joined in this work by a younger psychiatrist, Gerald Caplan. Out of their collaboration came crisis theory, the language of prevention in mental health, and much of the thinking behind the community mental health centres that the United States began to build after 1963.

Notice where this story travels. A disaster in a nightclub led a psychiatrist out of his consulting room, onto the hospital wards, into the homes of the bereaved and finally into a whole town. That journey is the subject of this chapter. Clinical psychology is not only the fifty-minute psychotherapy hour. It is also helping a newly diagnosed diabetic take her medicine, helping a bus driver stop smoking, talking to a suicidal student on a helpline at two in the morning, training village health workers to deliver counselling, and helping a man with schizophrenia keep a job. Remember the Cocoanut Grove; you will return to it at the end of the chapter.

9.1 Health Psychology and Behavioural MedicineMedium yield · 2 Qs

A century ago most people in India died of infections: plague, cholera, smallpox, tuberculosis, malaria. Today about six in every ten deaths in the country are caused by non-communicable diseases such as heart disease, stroke, chronic lung disease, cancer and diabetes. Think about what causes those diseases. Tobacco, alcohol, too much food of the wrong kind, too little movement, unmanaged stress, and medicines that are prescribed but never taken. Every one of those causes is a behaviour. That simple observation is the reason health psychology exists: if the great killers of our time are partly behavioural, then the science of behaviour must have something to say about preventing and treating them.

Health as more than the absence of disease

For most of the twentieth century medicine worked within the biomedical model: the view that every illness is fully explained by a biological fault (a germ, a lesion, a chemical imbalance), that mind and body are separate, and that health is simply the absence of disease. The World Health Organization (WHO) challenged that view in its constitution (adopted in 1946 and in force from 1948), which defined health as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity". Critics have pointed out that "complete" well-being is a standard almost nobody meets, but the definition did its job: it made well-being, not just disease, the business of health care.

Psychologists then set about measuring well-being. The best-known measure comes from Edward Diener (1946 to 2021), who in 1984 defined subjective well-being as having three components: a cognitive judgement of life satisfaction (how well your life measures up to your own standards), the frequent presence of positive affect, and the relative absence of negative affect. This is often called Diener's tripartite model. A different approach came from the medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky (1923 to 1994). Instead of asking what makes people ill (pathogenesis), Antonovsky in 1979 asked what keeps people well, which he called salutogenesis. His answer was a sense of coherence, a general feeling that life is comprehensible (it makes sense), manageable (I have the resources to meet its demands) and meaningful (it is worth engaging with). People high in sense of coherence stay healthier under stress. The related idea of hardiness, and the theories of stress and coping themselves, are taught in the General Psychology book.

Exam trap

Diener's three components of subjective well-being are life satisfaction (the cognitive part), positive affect and low negative affect. Distractors replace "life satisfaction" with "physical wellness", "spiritual wellness" or "social wellness"; none of those is in the model. Do not confuse it with Carol D. Ryff's six-dimension model of psychological well-being (autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations, purpose in life, self-acceptance).

Defining the field: health psychology, behavioural medicine and behavioural health

Three overlapping labels appeared in the late 1970s, and examiners love to ask how they differ. The first is behavioural medicine. The term seems to have been used first in 1973, in the title of a book on biofeedback edited by Lee Birk. The field was formally defined at the Yale Conference on Behavioral Medicine in 1977, and the definition published by Gary E. Schwartz and Stephen M. Weiss in 1978 is the standard one: behavioural medicine is "the interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of behavioural and biomedical science knowledge and techniques relevant to health and illness, and the application of this knowledge and these techniques to prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation". The key word is interdisciplinary. Physicians, nurses, epidemiologists, sociologists and psychologists all belong to behavioural medicine; no single discipline owns it.

The second label is health psychology, and it belongs to psychology alone. The American Psychological Association created its Division of Health Psychology (Division 38) in 1978, and its first president, Joseph D. Matarazzo, wrote the definition that every textbook quotes. In 1980 he defined health psychology as "the aggregate of the specific educational, scientific, and professional contributions of the discipline of psychology to the promotion and maintenance of health, the prevention and treatment of illness, and the identification of etiologic and diagnostic correlates of health, illness, and related dysfunction"; in 1982 he added "the analysis and improvement of the health care system and health policy formation". Read the definition slowly and you will see four targets: promoting health, preventing and treating illness, finding the psychological causes and correlates of disease, and improving the health-care system itself.

The third label, behavioural health, was proposed by Matarazzo in the same 1980 paper for the part of the enterprise that deals with keeping currently healthy people healthy: prevention, health promotion and the maintenance of good habits. Two older terms complete the family. Psychosomatic medicine, the psychoanalytically flavoured study of how emotions cause bodily disease, came first (you will meet it in Section 9.3). Clinical health psychology is the applied, patient-facing branch of health psychology: the psychologist on the cardiac ward, in the pain clinic or in the diabetes outpatient department. The biopsychosocial model at the bedside

The theoretical engine of health psychology is the biopsychosocial model proposed by the American psychiatrist George L. Engel (1913 to 1999) in a 1977 paper in Science, "The need for a new medical model". Its history and its place among models of psychopathology are covered in Chapter 1; here the point is how it changes what happens to a patient with a physical illness. Engel argued that biological, psychological and social systems are nested inside one another, so a change at any level ripples into the others. A disease is something the body has; an illness is something a person experiences, inside a family and a society.

Push a case through the model and its value becomes obvious. Mr Raghavendra, a 52-year-old bank manager in Mysuru, is admitted with a heart attack. The biomedical account is complete in its own terms: a coronary artery blocked by atherosclerotic plaque, high cholesterol, a father who died of the same disease at 60. But look at the psychological level. He is impatient and quick to anger, he has been sleeping five hours a night, and since the heart attack he has become quietly hopeless, convinced that he is now an invalid. And the social level: his branch is short-staffed, the family's evening meal is fried snacks with tea, his colleagues smoke together at lunch, and the statin he has been prescribed costs money he resents spending. Each level suggests its own intervention: medication and perhaps a stent (biological), treatment of his post-heart-attack depression and help with anger (psychological), and work with his family on diet and with his employer on hours (social). Depression after a heart attack is not a side issue: it roughly doubles to triples the risk of dying in the following year, as you will see in Section 9.3. A purely biomedical treatment would have missed half of what will decide whether he lives.

Illness behaviour and the sick role

Two people notice the same chest pain. One ignores it for a week; the other is in the emergency department within the hour. The difference is not in their arteries but in their illness behaviour, a term introduced by the American medical sociologist David Mechanic in 1962 to describe "the ways in which given symptoms may be differentially perceived, evaluated, and acted (or not acted) upon by different kinds of persons". Mechanic listed the factors that decide what a person does with a symptom: how visible and recognisable it is, how serious it seems, how much it disrupts work and family life, how often and how persistently it appears, the person's tolerance for it, what they know and believe about it, their need to deny it, competing needs (a daily-wage labourer cannot easily take a day off to see a doctor), other possible explanations for it ("it's just gas"), and how available and affordable treatment is. Four years later, Stanislav V. Kasl and Sidney Cobb (1966) separated three kinds of behaviour: health behaviour, which is what a healthy person does to stay well (exercise, vaccination); illness behaviour, which is what a person with symptoms does to find out what is wrong; and sick-role behaviour, which is what a diagnosed person does to get better.

Much of what happens between noticing a symptom and seeing a professional happens inside the family. The sociologist Eliot Freidson called this the lay referral system: the chain of relatives, neighbours and friends who are consulted first, and who often suggest home remedies, a pharmacist, an astrologer, a temple or a faith healer long before a doctor or a psychologist. In India, where pathways to mental health care very often pass through traditional and religious healers first, understanding the lay referral system is a practical clinical skill, not a sociological curiosity.

Once a person is accepted as ill, society gives them a temporary role. The American sociologist Talcott Parsons (1902 to 1979) described this sick role in his book The Social System (1951). Parsons saw illness as a kind of socially permitted deviance, and the sick role as the set of rules that keeps the deviance from spreading. It carries two rights and two obligations:

  • The sick person is exempted from normal social roles and duties (no work, no school, no cooking), in proportion to how ill they are.
  • The sick person is not held responsible for being ill and cannot be expected to get better simply by an act of will.
  • The sick person must regard being ill as undesirable and must want to get well.
  • The sick person must seek technically competent help (in Parsons's day, a physician) and cooperate with it.

The model has been criticised from several directions. It fits short, acute illnesses (a fever, a broken leg) much better than chronic illnesses such as diabetes or schizophrenia, from which the person never fully "returns" to normal roles. It assumes that everyone can reach a doctor. And it fails badly for stigmatised conditions: people with HIV, alcohol dependence or mental illness are often blamed for their illness, which is precisely the right the sick role is supposed to grant. Still, the idea that being sick is a social role, with secondary benefits as well as costs, remains indispensable.

When a person's way of perceiving, evaluating and acting on their own health is persistently out of proportion to the medical findings, the Australian psychiatrist Issy Pilowsky (1969) spoke of abnormal illness behaviour. The concept covers the person who keeps seeking investigations after repeated reassurance, but also the person who denies a serious illness and refuses treatment. The specific disorders in this territory (illness anxiety disorder, somatic symptom disorder, conversion disorder, factitious disorder) are described in the Psychiatric Disorders book, but the sick role gives you the cleanest way to tell three of them apart.

Exam trap

The sick role is the key to separating three presentations. In malingering the symptoms are produced deliberately for an external incentive (money, avoiding duty or prosecution, drugs). In factitious disorder (including factitious disorder imposed on another, once called Munchausen by proxy) the symptoms are also produced deliberately, but there is no obvious external incentive: the motive is to occupy the sick role itself. In conversion disorder (functional neurological symptom disorder) the symptoms are not intentionally produced at all. Distractors such as "presence of dissociation", "onset only in adolescence" or "comorbid substance use" do not separate them.

9.2 Why People Do or Do Not Protect Their HealthMedium yield · 2 Qs

Here is a puzzle that health workers meet every day. A free vaccination camp is announced in a village; half the families stay away. A man is told that he has high blood pressure and is given tablets that cost almost nothing; a year later he is taking them on perhaps half the days. Everyone knows that tobacco kills, yet roughly 28.6 percent of Indian adults used some form of tobacco in the second Global Adult Tobacco Survey (2016 to 2017). Knowledge clearly is not enough. Something stands between knowing and doing, and the models of health behaviour are attempts to say what it is. Each one is a theory about which beliefs matter. As you read, notice that the models borrow from one another; by the end of the section you should be able to recognise any of them from a list of its constructs, which is exactly how the examiners test them.

The health belief model

The oldest and most famous model was born of a practical failure. In the early 1950s the United States Public Health Service sent mobile X-ray vans into city neighbourhoods to screen for tuberculosis, free of charge, and very few people came. A group of social psychologists in the service, among them Godfrey M. Hochbaum, Irwin M. Rosenstock and Stephen S. Kegeles, set out to find out why.

Key study: Hochbaum and the tuberculosis X-rays (1958)

Hochbaum interviewed more than a thousand adults in three American cities about their beliefs concerning tuberculosis and then checked who had actually gone for a voluntary chest X-ray. Two beliefs stood out. People who believed that they personally could have tuberculosis without knowing it (perceived susceptibility) and who believed that early detection would make a difference (perceived benefits) were far more likely to have been screened: about four in five of those who held both beliefs had had an X-ray, against about one in five of those who held neither. The conclusion was that health actions depend less on the objective facts of a disease than on a person's beliefs about it. The criticism, which applies to the whole model that grew from this study, is that the data were correlational and the beliefs were measured after the behaviour.

Rosenstock formalised these ideas in 1966 and 1974 as the health belief model, and Marshall H. Becker extended it to explain how well patients follow medical advice. In its mature form the model says that the likelihood of a health action depends on six beliefs (Figure 9.1):

  • Perceived susceptibility: my own estimate of how likely I am to get the disease ("diabetes runs in my family; I could get it").
  • Perceived severity: how serious I believe the disease and its consequences would be, medically and socially ("I would lose my legs; I could not work").
  • Perceived benefits: my belief that the recommended action would actually reduce the threat ("walking every day really does prevent diabetes").
  • Perceived barriers: the costs I see in the action: money, time, pain, embarrassment, side effects, inconvenience ("I have no time to walk; the road has no footpath").
  • Cues to action: the trigger that tips a ready person into acting: a symptom, a relative's illness, a doctor's advice, a reminder SMS, a television campaign.
  • Self-efficacy: my confidence that I can carry out the action. This was added in 1988 by Rosenstock, Victor J. Strecher and Becker, borrowing Albert Bandura's concept (next subsection), because the original model could not explain why people who believed in the benefits of quitting smoking still did not try.

Susceptibility and severity together make up the perceived threat; benefits minus barriers is, in effect, a cost-benefit judgement about the action. Demographic and psychological variables (age, gender, education, personality, knowledge) are treated as modifying factors that act on these beliefs.

The health belief model is simple, intuitive and still widely used to design campaigns. Its weaknesses are just as clear. It says nothing about how the beliefs combine (should threat and cost-benefit be added or multiplied?), it ignores social pressure, emotion and habit, and in reviews its constructs predict behaviour only weakly, with barriers usually the strongest single predictor and severity the weakest.

Social cognitive theory and self-efficacy

Several models in this section lean on the work of Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021). In social cognitive theory (set out in full in 1986), Bandura argued that behaviour, personal factors and the environment influence one another (reciprocal determinism), that people learn by observing others, and that two kinds of expectation steer action: outcome expectancies (will this behaviour lead to that result?) and self-efficacy (can I perform the behaviour?), a concept he introduced in 1977. Self-efficacy turns out to be one of the most powerful predictors of whether a person starts and keeps up a health behaviour, which is why later models kept adding it. Social cognitive theory has a broader scope than health, and its learning principles are covered in the General and Personality books; for this chapter, remember it as the source of self-efficacy.

The theories of reasoned action and planned behaviour

A second family of models came from social psychology's long struggle to explain why attitudes predict behaviour so poorly. Martin Fishbein (1936 to 2009) and Icek Ajzen (born 1942) proposed the theory of reasoned action in their 1975 book Belief, Attitude, Intention, and Behavior. Its central claim is that the immediate cause of a deliberate behaviour is the person's behavioural intention, and that intention has two sources:

  • The attitude toward the behaviour: my overall evaluation of doing it, built from my beliefs about its outcomes ("if I eat less rice I will lose weight") weighted by how much I value those outcomes ("losing weight matters to me").
  • The subjective norm: the social pressure I perceive, built from my beliefs about what important people want me to do ("my wife and my doctor think I should cut down") weighted by my motivation to comply with them.

The theory worked well for behaviours under full voluntary control but poorly for behaviours that need skill, resources or other people's cooperation. Wanting to quit smoking is not the same as being able to. Ajzen therefore added a third determinant in 1985 (the classic statement is his 1991 paper) and renamed the model the theory of planned behaviour. The new element is perceived behavioural control: how easy or difficult I think the behaviour will be for me. Perceived behavioural control does two things (Figure 9.2). It feeds intention like the other two components, and it can also predict behaviour directly, because people who feel in control are often actually in control.

The theory of planned behaviour is one of the best-supported models in social and health psychology: across many studies intention predicts a substantial share of behaviour, and attitude, norm and control predict a larger share of intention. Its great weakness is the intention-behaviour gap. Many people who sincerely intend to exercise, take their tablets or use condoms do not, and the model, which stops at intention, cannot say why. The stage models below were built to fill that gap.

Exam trap

The theory of planned behaviour has three determinants of intention: attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control. Some papers (for example RML 2023) key the answer as "attitude, subjective norms and self-efficacy". Accept it: Ajzen himself said perceived behavioural control is closely compatible with Bandura's self-efficacy, and options listing genetics, personality, social support or health literacy are plainly wrong. But in your own answers write "perceived behavioural control", and remember that the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) has only the first two. The theory of planned behaviour belongs to Ajzen alone.

Protection motivation theory

Public health campaigns often try to frighten people: cigarette packets in India carry graphic pictures of mouth cancer over most of their surface. Do fear appeals work? Ronald W. Rogers addressed exactly that question with protection motivation theory, first proposed in 1975 as a theory of fear appeals and revised in 1983. Rogers argued that a threatening message sets off two appraisals:

  • Threat appraisal: how severe the danger is, how vulnerable I am to it, set against the rewards of carrying on with the risky behaviour (the pleasure of the cigarette, the relief of stress).
  • Coping appraisal: whether the recommended action works (response efficacy), whether I can do it (self-efficacy), set against the costs of doing it (response costs).

The outcome of the two appraisals is protection motivation, which functions like an intention to act. The model's practical lesson is important: fear alone backfires. A high threat with low coping appraisal produces denial, avoidance and fatalism ("everyone dies of something"). Frightening messages work only when they also tell people what to do and convince them that they can do it. Notice the overlap with the health belief model (vulnerability and severity are susceptibility and severity under new names) and with social cognitive theory (self-efficacy).

Stage models: the transtheoretical model and the health action process approach

All the models so far are continuum models: they place everyone on one scale of likelihood to act. Stage models argue instead that people pass through qualitatively different phases, and that each phase needs a different kind of help. The best known is the transtheoretical model of James O. Prochaska and Carlo C. DiClemente (1983), with its stages of precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance; it is taught in full alongside motivational interviewing in Chapter 6, and it is the model most often used in smoking and alcohol programmes.

A more recent stage model was designed to explain the intention-behaviour gap directly. The German psychologist Ralf Schwarzer proposed the health action process approach (HAPA) in 1992. It divides change into two phases. In the motivational phase a person forms an intention, driven by risk perception, outcome expectancies and action self-efficacy (much as the earlier models describe). In the volitional phase the person must turn the intention into action and keep it going, and here three new ingredients matter: planning (action planning, which spells out when, where and how: "I will walk from 6 to 6.30 every morning on Chamundi Hill road"; and coping planning, which anticipates obstacles: "if it rains I will walk in the corridor"), maintenance self-efficacy (confidence that I can keep going when it gets boring or difficult) and recovery self-efficacy (confidence that I can get back on track after a lapse). Adherence and compliance

All the models above apply to one behaviour that matters more than almost any other in medicine: doing what the doctor recommended. The older term compliance was defined by the Canadian physician R. Brian Haynes (1979) as "the extent to which a person's behaviour (in terms of taking medications, following diets, or executing lifestyle changes) coincides with medical or health advice". The word was gradually replaced by adherence, because "compliance" suggests an obedient patient and a commanding doctor, whereas adherence implies that the patient has agreed to the plan. A further step is concordance, a term promoted in Britain in the late 1990s for a negotiated agreement between patient and clinician in which the patient's beliefs are treated as legitimate.

How big is the problem? A WHO report on adherence to long-term therapies (2003) concluded that in developed countries only about 50 percent of patients with chronic illnesses take their treatment as prescribed, and the figure is lower in poorer countries. Non-adherence comes in two forms. Unintentional non-adherence happens when people forget, misunderstand the instructions, cannot afford the drug or cannot manage a complicated regimen. Intentional non-adherence happens when people decide not to follow the advice: because the side effects are unpleasant, because they feel better and see no reason to continue (a common story with antihypertensives, which treat a condition that has no symptoms), or because they hold beliefs about the medicine ("English medicines are too heating"; "I will become addicted to these tablets").

The British psychologist Philip Ley showed in the 1970s and 1980s that many patients forget a large part of what they are told within minutes of leaving the consulting room, and that they remember best what they are told first. His cognitive hypothesis model proposed that adherence depends on the patient's understanding of the advice, memory for it and satisfaction with the consultation. The practical techniques for raising adherence follow almost directly from that model and from the theories above:

  • Give the most important instruction first, use simple words, be specific ("one tablet after breakfast and one after dinner", not "twice a day"), repeat it, and give it in writing or as pictures for patients who cannot read.
  • Simplify the regimen: fewer doses, combination tablets, timing linked to daily routines such as meals or prayer times.
  • Use reminders and cues: pill boxes with compartments, mobile phone alarms, SMS reminders, blister calendars.
  • Involve the family, who in India often hold the medicines and decide on spending.
  • Elicit and address the patient's beliefs and fears about the illness and the medicine, using the language of the health belief model (benefits, barriers) and, where the person is ambivalent, motivational interviewing.
  • Use behavioural methods: self-monitoring charts, behavioural contracts and reinforcement for attendance and adherence (Chapter 4).
  • Reduce cost and distance: free medicines at primary health centres, home delivery, directly observed therapy (as in tuberculosis programmes).
Exam trap

"Compliance" means two different things in two different books. In health psychology it is following medical advice (now usually called adherence). In social psychology, compliance is going along with a direct request from another person, which is different from conformity (changing behaviour to match group pressure without being asked) and obedience (following an order from an authority). If a question says "In social psychology", the answer is the request-based meaning.

The patient-practitioner relationship

The most important single influence on adherence is the quality of the relationship between the patient and the clinician. In 1956 the psychiatrists Thomas S. Szasz and Marc H. Hollender described three models of that relationship, each appropriate in a different situation:

  • Activity-passivity: the clinician acts on a patient who cannot participate, as with an unconscious patient in the emergency room. The prototype is a parent and an infant.
  • Guidance-cooperation: the clinician advises and the patient follows, as in an acute infection. The prototype is a parent and an adolescent.
  • Mutual participation: clinician and patient share decisions as near-equals, the model best suited to chronic illnesses that the patient must manage every day (diabetes, hypertension, asthma). The prototype is two adults.

Later research contrasted a doctor-centred style (closed questions, focus on the disease, the doctor controls the agenda) with a patient-centred communication style (open questions, interest in the patient's ideas, concerns and expectations, shared decision making). Patient-centred consultations produce more satisfied patients, better recall of information and better adherence. Common barriers include medical jargon, rushed consultations (a few minutes per patient in a crowded government outpatient department), differences in language and social class, and the clinician's failure to ask what the patient believes is wrong. When the patient feels heard, the barriers in the health belief model shrink.

9.3 Stress and Physical IllnessHigh yield · 4 Qs

The models in Section 9.2 explain how beliefs lead to behaviours that make people ill. But there is a second, more direct route from mind to body. Emotions are bodily events: fear speeds the heart, anger raises blood pressure, prolonged distress floods the body with cortisol. If such states are frequent and long-lasting, can they by themselves damage organs? That question has driven a century of research, from psychoanalytic speculation to modern immunology.

Stress in brief

The theory of stress is taught in the General Psychology book; what you need here is the vocabulary that health psychology borrows from it. A stressor is an event or situation that makes demands on a person; the stress response is the physiological and psychological reaction to it. Stressors are usually classified by their time course. An acute stressor is short-lived, has a clear beginning and end, and is usually over in minutes or hours: a pop quiz, a job interview, a near-miss on the Ring Road. A chronic stressor persists for months or years and often has no clear end: caring for a parent with dementia, a long-running marital conflict, poverty, living with HIV or with cancer. In their influential 2004 review of about 300 studies, Suzanne Segerstrom and Gregory Miller used a finer taxonomy (acute time-limited stressors such as a laboratory speech task, brief naturalistic stressors such as examinations, stressful event sequences such as bereavement, chronic stressors, and distant stressors such as childhood abuse) and found that the body's response depends heavily on duration: brief stressors tend to mobilise parts of the immune system, while chronic stressors suppress it.

The classic physiological account is the general adaptation syndrome of the Austrian-Canadian endocrinologist Hans Selye (1907 to 1982), first described in 1936 and popularised in The Stress of Life (1956). Selye found that rats exposed to very different noxious agents (cold, injury, toxins) showed the same nonspecific response in three stages: alarm (the sympathetic fight-or-flight reaction and the release of adrenal hormones), resistance (the body adapts and copes at a raised level of arousal) and exhaustion (resources run out; illness and, in Selye's rats, enlarged adrenals, shrunken thymus and bleeding stomach ulcers follow). The other classic approach measures stress by counting life changes. Thomas H. Holmes and Richard H. Rahe (1967) built the Social Readjustment Rating Scale, 43 life events weighted in life change units (death of a spouse 100, marriage 50, a minor violation of the law 11); higher totals predicted later illness, though only weakly. In 1981 Allen Kanner, Richard Lazarus and their colleagues showed that minor daily hassles predicted psychological symptoms better than major life events did, and a 1982 follow-up by Anita DeLongis and colleagues found the same for physical health. Lazarus's transactional model of appraisal and coping, which underlies most modern stress management, is in the General book.

Exam trap

A pop quiz is the textbook acute stressor (brief, time-limited). HIV disease, colon cancer and marital discord are chronic. "Alarm, resistance, exhaustion" is Selye's general adaptation syndrome; life events theory is Holmes and Rahe; the health belief model and protection motivation theory are models of health behaviour, not of stress.

Psychosomatic medicine and Alexander's "holy seven"

The first systematic attempt to link emotions to bodily disease came from psychoanalysis. In 1935 the American psychiatrist Helen Flanders Dunbar (1902 to 1959) published Emotions and Bodily Changes, and she went on to propose personality profiles for patients with particular diseases (a "coronary personality", for example). The most influential figure, however, was the Hungarian-born psychoanalyst Franz Alexander (1891 to 1964) of the Chicago Institute for Psychoanalysis, whose book Psychosomatic Medicine appeared in 1950. Alexander proposed a specificity hypothesis: each disease arises from a specific unconscious emotional conflict acting on an organ that is constitutionally vulnerable. Frustrated longings to be cared for and fed were linked to peptic ulcer; chronically suppressed hostility to essential hypertension; and a repressed wish to cry out for the mother, coupled with fear of separation from her, to bronchial asthma.

Alexander and his colleagues concentrated on seven conditions, which became known as the holy seven (sometimes the "Chicago seven") of the psychophysiological disorders, also called psychosomatic disorders: physical diseases with real tissue damage in which emotional factors were thought to play a major causal role. The specificity hypothesis did not survive. Its evidence came from psychoanalytic interviews with patients who already had the disease, so there was no way to tell whether the "conflict" caused the illness or the illness produced the emotions. The decisive blow came in the early 1980s, when the Australian physicians Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed that most peptic ulcers are caused by a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, and are cured by antibiotics; they received the Nobel Prize in 2005. Yet the broader idea, that psychological factors influence the onset and course of physical disease, has only grown stronger. What changed is that no one now thinks of a few diseases as "psychosomatic" and the rest as purely physical. As Barlow and Durand put it, biological, psychological and social factors are implicated in every disorder, mental and physical. The diagnostic manuals tracked this shift: the "psychophysiologic disorders" of DSM-I and DSM-II became "psychological factors affecting physical condition" in DSM-III and, in DSM-5, "psychological factors affecting other medical conditions" (the details are in the Psychiatric Disorders book).

Exam trap

Learn the seven exactly: peptic ulcer, essential hypertension, bronchial asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis, neurodermatitis, thyrotoxicosis. Diabetes, coronary heart disease, migraine and irritable bowel syndrome are often called psychosomatic in loose usage, but they are not in Alexander's list. The specificity hypothesis is Alexander's (1950); personality profiles are Dunbar's.

Coronary heart disease: Type A, hostility and Type D

In the 1950s two San Francisco cardiologists, Meyer Friedman (1910 to 2001) and Ray H. Rosenman (1920 to 2013), had their waiting-room chairs reupholstered. The upholsterer remarked that the chairs were worn out in an odd way: only along the front edge of the seat, as if their patients spent the whole wait perched, ready to spring up. Friedman later said that he did not appreciate the remark at the time, but that it captured exactly what he and Rosenman went on to describe in 1959: a Type A behaviour pattern, made up of a chronic sense of time urgency and impatience, intense competitiveness and achievement striving, and easily aroused hostility and aggressiveness. People with the opposite, relaxed and unhurried pattern were labelled Type B. Notice that Friedman and Rosenman called it a behaviour pattern, a way of reacting to challenges, rather than a fixed personality type.

Key study: the Western Collaborative Group Study (1960 to 1969)

Did Type A behaviour actually predict heart disease, or did heart patients simply look hurried? To find out, Rosenman and Friedman classified 3,154 healthy men aged 39 to 59 as Type A or Type B using a structured interview, and then followed them for about eight and a half years. The Type A men were about twice as likely to develop coronary heart disease as the Type B men, even after the standard risk factors (smoking, cholesterol, blood pressure) were taken into account. The Framingham Heart Study found a similar result in men and women. For a time, Type A behaviour was accepted as an independent risk factor. Then came the criticism: several large later studies, including the Multiple Risk Factor Intervention Trial in the 1980s, found no relationship at all.

Why did the global Type A pattern stop predicting heart disease? The answer that emerged was that Type A is a bundle, and only one part of it is toxic. When researchers took the pattern apart, time urgency and achievement striving predicted heart disease much less consistently; the component that carried most of the risk was hostility, especially cynical hostility (a mistrustful, contemptuous view of other people) combined with frequent anger. In a famous study, John Barefoot, Grant Dahlstrom and Redford B. Williams (1983) found that physicians who had scored high on the Cook-Medley Hostility scale of the MMPI while in medical school had several times the rate of coronary heart disease, and a much higher death rate, over the next 25 years. Anger causes surges of blood pressure and heart rate, promotes inflammation and clotting, and goes with smoking, drinking and poor social support. Treatment confirms the link: in the Recurrent Coronary Prevention Project (Friedman and colleagues, 1986), heart-attack survivors who received group counselling to reduce Type A behaviour, especially anger and impatience, had substantially fewer second heart attacks than those who received cardiac counselling alone.

A third construct came from the Belgian psychologist Johan Denollet in the 1990s. The Type D personality ("distressed") combines high negative affectivity (worry, gloom, irritability) with high social inhibition (keeping those feelings to oneself), and in Denollet's studies it predicted a poorer outcome in people who already had heart disease. Beyond these patterns, depression is now one of the best-established psychological risk factors: in Nancy Frasure-Smith's studies in Montreal, heart-attack patients who were depressed were about three times as likely to die in the following year as those who were not, whatever the severity of their heart disease. RML 2024 asked which factor is "most directly linked to a higher risk for coronary heart disease", with Type A, Type B, hostility and self-efficacy as options, and the key was Type A. Type B is protective, and self-efficacy is a belief about one's ability, not a coronary risk factor.

Cancer and the Type C debate

If personality could cause heart disease, could it cause cancer? In 1979 Steven Greer and his colleagues at King's College Hospital in London reported that women with early breast cancer who reacted with "fighting spirit" or denial were more likely to be alive and free of recurrence five years later than women who reacted with stoic acceptance or helplessness. In the 1980s the American psychologist Lydia Temoshok popularised the Type C personality: a cooperative, patient, unassertive person who suppresses anger and other negative emotions and complies with authority, said to be prone to cancer and to faster cancer progression. The evidence did not hold up well. A much larger study of breast cancer patients by Maggie Watson and colleagues (1999) found that fighting spirit made no difference to survival, though helplessness and hopelessness predicted a modestly worse outcome, and later systematic reviews concluded that coping style has little or no reliable effect on cancer survival. The point matters clinically. Telling patients that a positive attitude will cure them sets them up to blame themselves when the cancer progresses; Jimmie C. Holland (1928 to 2017), the founder of modern psycho-oncology, called this "the tyranny of positive thinking".

Key study: Spiegel's support groups for metastatic breast cancer (1989)

David Spiegel and his colleagues at Stanford randomly assigned 86 women with metastatic breast cancer to routine care or to routine care plus a weekly supportive-expressive group for a year, where they talked openly about their fears and death, supported one another and learned self-hypnosis for pain. The group members had less distress and pain, and, to everyone's surprise, a follow-up ten years later showed that they had lived on average about twice as long (roughly 37 months against 19). The finding was a sensation. But larger replications, including a Canadian trial of 235 women (Goodwin and colleagues, 2001) and Spiegel's own later study (2007), found that the groups improved mood and pain but did not lengthen survival. The safe conclusion is that psychological interventions reliably improve quality of life in cancer, not life span.

Where psychology clearly helps in cancer is with distress, pain, fatigue, insomnia and the side effects of treatment. A classic example is anticipatory nausea: after a few cycles of chemotherapy, a quarter or more of patients begin to feel sick, or even vomit, at the sight of the hospital, the nurse or the smell of the ward, before any drug is given. This is classical conditioning, with the hospital cues as conditioned stimuli, and it responds well to relaxation training and systematic desensitisation (Chapter 4).

HIV and AIDS

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, which leads without treatment to acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), is the clearest case of an illness whose spread is entirely behavioural: unprotected sex, shared needles, and transmission from mother to child. Until effective combination antiretroviral therapy arrived in the mid-1990s, behaviour change was the main weapon, and health psychology built specific models for it. The AIDS risk reduction model of Joseph Catania, Susan Kegeles and Thomas Coates (1990) describes three stages: labelling one's behaviour as risky, committing to change it, and enacting the change (which depends on skills such as negotiating condom use). The information-motivation-behavioural skills model of Jeffrey and William Fisher (1992) argues that all three ingredients in its name are needed; information alone changes little.

Antiretroviral therapy (ART), available free in India's public programme since 2004, has turned HIV into a manageable chronic illness, which has moved psychology's role to new places. Testing is preceded and followed by counselling (pre-test and post-test counselling at Integrated Counselling and Testing Centres under the National AIDS Control Organisation, set up in 1992). Disclosure of status to partners and family is a delicate counselling task. Adherence must be very high, because missed doses allow resistant virus to emerge. Depression is common and worsens adherence. And stigma remains severe enough that India passed the HIV and AIDS (Prevention and Control) Act in 2017 to prohibit discrimination in employment, education and health care. Psychological interventions such as Michael Antoni's cognitive-behavioural stress management reduce distress and improve adherence. The neurocognitive effects of HIV are described in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

Diabetes

Diabetes mellitus is a disorder of blood sugar regulation. In Type 1 diabetes, an autoimmune process destroys the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas, usually in childhood, and the person needs insulin for life. In Type 2 diabetes, which accounts for about nine in ten cases, the body's cells become resistant to insulin and the pancreas eventually cannot keep up. Genes contribute (South Asians develop Type 2 diabetes at lower body weights and younger ages than Europeans, partly because they carry more fat around the abdomen), but the factors most strongly associated with developing it are a poor diet and a sedentary lifestyle, acting largely through obesity. The ICMR-INDIAB survey reported in 2023 that more than 10 crore Indians have diabetes.

Key study: the Diabetes Prevention Program (2002)

If lifestyle causes Type 2 diabetes, changing lifestyle should prevent it. The Diabetes Prevention Program in the United States randomly assigned 3,234 overweight adults with raised blood sugar to a placebo, to the drug metformin, or to an intensive lifestyle programme aiming at 7 percent weight loss and 150 minutes of brisk walking a week, delivered with behavioural techniques (self-monitoring, goal setting, problem solving). Over about three years the lifestyle programme reduced the incidence of diabetes by 58 percent, against 31 percent for metformin. Behaviour change beat the drug. Similar trials in Finland, China and India (the Indian Diabetes Prevention Programme in Chennai) reached the same conclusion.

Once diabetes develops, it is almost entirely self-managed. The patient, not the doctor, must monitor blood glucose, take tablets or inject insulin, control diet, exercise and care for the feet, every day for life. Not surprisingly, adherence is a constant problem, depression is about twice as common as in the general population and worsens glucose control, and many patients experience "diabetes distress", the frustration and burnout of relentless self-management. Stress also raises blood glucose directly through cortisol and adrenaline. Psychological care therefore includes diabetes self-management education, motivational interviewing, problem solving, treatment of depression, and work with the family, who in Indian homes usually cook the meals.

Exam trap

"Most strongly associated with Type 2 diabetes" is keyed as poor diet and sedentary lifestyle. Genetics is a real risk factor (and a tempting option), but lifestyle factors are the strongest and most modifiable contributors in exam terms. Psychological stress and environmental toxins play smaller roles. Do not confuse with Type 1, which is autoimmune.

Chronic pain

Acute pain follows an injury and fades as the tissue heals. Chronic pain persists beyond the normal time of healing, usually defined as longer than three months, and often bears little relation to any remaining tissue damage. Low back pain, headache, arthritis and fibromyalgia are the commonest forms. The severity of the damage is a poor predictor of how disabled a person with chronic pain becomes; anxiety, depression, beliefs about the pain, social reinforcement and compensation claims predict disability better. The physiological basis for that observation is the gate control theory proposed by Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall in 1965 (taught in the Physiological Psychology book): a "gate" in the spinal cord, which descending signals from the brain can open or close, means that attention, emotion and expectation genuinely change how much pain reaches awareness.

The American psychologist Wilbert E. Fordyce, working at the University of Washington in Seattle, transformed pain treatment with his book Behavioral Methods for Chronic Pain and Illness (1976). Fordyce distinguished pain (the sensation), suffering (the emotional response) and pain behaviours: the observable things people in pain do, such as grimacing, limping, groaning, lying down, complaining, asking for medicine and staying away from work. Pain behaviours, he argued, are operants. They are reinforced by attention and sympathy from the family, by rest, by medication and by escape from unpleasant duties, and so they can persist long after the injury has healed. His operant approach to pain therefore changes the consequences: the family and staff are taught to give attention to "well behaviours" (walking, working, cheerful talk) and to withdraw it from pain behaviours; activity is increased by gradually rising quotas rather than "until it hurts"; and medication is given on a fixed time schedule rather than when the patient complains (pain-contingent medication rewards complaint). The goal is to reduce disability, not necessarily the sensation.

The cognitive-behavioural approach to pain, developed by Dennis Turk, Donald Meichenbaum and others in the late 1970s and 1980s, adds the patient's thoughts. Patients learn that pain is influenced by attention and mood, are taught coping skills (relaxation, distraction, pacing, problem solving) and learn to challenge pain catastrophising ("this pain means my spine is crumbling; it will only get worse"). Catastrophising feeds the fear-avoidance cycle described by Johan Vlaeyen and Steven Linton (2000): fear of pain leads to avoidance of movement, which leads to deconditioning, disability and depression, which make pain worse. Acceptance and commitment therapy (Chapter 5) offers a third route, helping patients pursue valued activities while accepting some pain. These treatments are usually delivered in multidisciplinary pain clinics, a model pioneered in the United States by the anaesthesiologist John J. Bonica (1917 to 1994), who brought physicians, psychologists, physiotherapists and others into one team; Fordyce worked in Bonica's Seattle programme.

Clinical link

A useful question for any patient with chronic pain is "What would you be doing if the pain were gone?" The answer reveals the life the pain has taken away and becomes the treatment target. Rehabilitation aims to give that life back, even if some pain remains.

Psychoneuroimmunology

For most of the twentieth century the immune system was thought to operate on its own, untouched by the brain. That belief was overturned by a laboratory accident.

Key study: Ader and Cohen's conditioned immunosuppression (1975)

The psychologist Robert Ader (1932 to 2011) at the University of Rochester was studying taste aversion. Rats drank saccharin-flavoured water and were then injected with cyclophosphamide, a drug that causes nausea. Later, when the rats were given saccharin water alone, an unexpected number of them died. Ader realised that cyclophosphamide is also an immunosuppressant, and reasoned that the rats might have learned to suppress their own immune systems in response to the taste. With the immunologist Nicholas Cohen he tested this directly: rats conditioned in this way and later given saccharin alone produced fewer antibodies to an injected antigen than control rats. The immune system could be classically conditioned, so it had to be connected to the brain. Ader named the new field psychoneuroimmunology and edited the first book under that title in 1981.

Research in humans soon followed. Janice K. Kiecolt-Glaser and her husband, the immunologist Ronald Glaser, showed in the 1980s that the activity of natural killer cells in medical students fell during examinations, and in 1995 that a small standard wound on the arm took about nine days (24 percent) longer to heal in women caring for a relative with dementia than in matched controls. In 1991 Sheldon Cohen, David Tyrrell and Andrew Smith gave nasal drops containing common cold viruses to 394 healthy volunteers who had first completed stress questionnaires, and then kept them in quarantine: the higher the stress score, the more likely a person was to become infected and develop a cold, in a dose-response fashion. The pathways run through the two stress systems you will meet in the Physiological book, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (cortisol suppresses many immune functions) and the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary system, and through behaviour, because stressed people sleep, eat and exercise worse and smoke and drink more. Psychological interventions can push in the other direction: in the expressive writing studies of James Pennebaker in the late 1980s, students who wrote about their deepest feelings concerning a trauma for a few days made fewer visits to the health centre and showed better immune responses than students who wrote about trivial topics.

Exam trap

Psychoneuroimmunology began with Ader and Cohen (1975), conditioned immunosuppression in rats using saccharin and cyclophosphamide. The term is Ader's. Do not attribute it to Selye (general adaptation syndrome) or to Cannon (fight or flight). Acute stress can briefly enhance some immune responses; it is chronic stress that suppresses immunity.

9.4 Behavioural Medicine Techniques and Lifestyle ChangeMedium yield · 3 Qs

Knowing that stress and behaviour cause illness is only half of the story; the other half is what a psychologist can actually do about it. This section looks at the main tools of behavioural medicine. Many of them were developed in behaviour therapy and cognitive therapy, and their mechanics are taught in Chapters 4 and 5; here the emphasis is on how they are applied to physical health.

Biofeedback and relaxation

Biofeedback is a procedure in which a bodily process that a person cannot normally sense (muscle tension, skin temperature, heart rate, brain waves) is measured by an instrument and fed back as a tone, a light or a graph on a screen, so that the person can learn to control it. Its scientific origin lies in experiments by the American psychologist Neal E. Miller (1909 to 2002) in the late 1960s, which appeared to show that rats paralysed with curare could learn, by operant conditioning, to raise or lower their heart rate and blood pressure. Miller himself later found that the dramatic animal results could not be replicated, but by then clinical biofeedback had taken off. The main types are electromyographic (EMG) biofeedback of muscle tension, used for tension headache and for retraining muscles after stroke; thermal biofeedback of finger temperature (learning to warm the hands), used for migraine and for Raynaud's disease; electrodermal biofeedback of skin conductance, used for anxiety; heart rate variability biofeedback; and EEG biofeedback or neurofeedback, used with debated results for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Biofeedback works well for some conditions, but for many others it does no better than simple relaxation training, which is cheaper.

Relaxation is the most widely used technique in behavioural medicine. The main methods are Edmund Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation (alternately tensing and releasing muscle groups), Johannes Schultz's autogenic training (self-suggestions of heaviness and warmth), slow diaphragmatic breathing, guided imagery, meditation, and yoga with pranayama, which has a long history in Indian clinical settings. The cardiologist Herbert Benson (1975) argued that all of these share a common relaxation response, the physiological opposite of the fight-or-flight response, which can be elicited by a quiet setting, a mental focus (a word or the breath), a passive attitude and a comfortable position. Relaxation lowers arousal, reduces blood pressure modestly, helps with headache, insomnia and pain, and is a component of almost every stress management package. The techniques are described step by step in Chapter 4, and mindfulness-based stress reduction in Chapter 5.

Stress management

Stress management is not a single technique but a package that teaches people to recognise and reduce their stress. A typical programme for, say, a group of software engineers in Bengaluru would run for six to eight weekly sessions and combine: education about the stress response and its effects on health; self-monitoring with a stress diary to identify triggers; relaxation and breathing training; cognitive restructuring of stress-producing thoughts ("if this release is late, I am finished"); problem-solving and time-management skills; assertiveness training for handling unreasonable demands; and advice on exercise, sleep and social support. The best-known structured version is Donald Meichenbaum's stress inoculation training (1985), with its three phases of conceptualisation, skills acquisition and rehearsal, and application and follow-through; it is taught in Chapter 5.

Insomnia: cognitive behavioural therapy and sleep hygiene

Chronic insomnia (difficulty falling or staying asleep on at least three nights a week for three months, with daytime impairment) is common, and sleeping pills lose effectiveness and carry risks. The first-line treatment recommended by major guidelines is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), a package of four or five components.

  • Stimulus control, introduced by Richard Bootzin in 1972, aims to make the bed a strong cue for sleep again. Go to bed only when sleepy; use the bed only for sleep and sex (no phone, television or work in bed); if you cannot sleep within about 20 minutes, get up, go to another room and return only when sleepy; get up at the same time every morning; avoid daytime naps.
  • Sleep restriction, introduced by Arthur Spielman and colleagues in 1987, limits the time in bed to the time actually spent asleep, which builds up sleep pressure and consolidates sleep. The window is then widened in small steps as sleep efficiency improves.
  • Cognitive therapy targets beliefs that keep insomnia going, such as "I must get eight hours or I will not be able to function" and catastrophic worry about the effects of a bad night.
  • Relaxation training reduces the physiological arousal that keeps people awake.
  • Sleep hygiene education covers lifestyle and environment: avoid caffeine after midday and nicotine and alcohol in the evening, avoid heavy meals and vigorous exercise close to bedtime, keep the bedroom dark, quiet and cool, reduce screen use before bed, and keep a regular schedule.

A worked example shows how sleep restriction works. Ms Kavya spends nine hours in bed (from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m.) but, by her sleep diary, sleeps only about six of them. Her sleep efficiency (total sleep time divided by time in bed, times 100) is 6 ÷ 9 × 100, or about 67 percent. The therapist prescribes a six-hour window, say midnight to 6 a.m., with a fixed rising time. For the first week she is sleepy, but she falls asleep faster and wakes less. When her sleep efficiency rises above about 85 to 90 percent for a week, the window is extended by 15 to 30 minutes, and so on until she is sleeping well and feels rested. The time in bed is never cut to less than about five hours.

Exam trap

Sleep hygiene is not the same as CBT-I. On its own, sleep hygiene education is not an effective treatment for chronic insomnia; the most active components of CBT-I are stimulus control (Bootzin) and sleep restriction (Spielman). "Use the bed only for sleep and sex" and "get up if you cannot sleep" are stimulus control rules, not sleep hygiene rules.

Smoking cessation

Tobacco is the largest preventable cause of death in the world, and in India it comes in many forms: cigarettes and bidis, and smokeless products such as gutka, khaini and zarda, which are used by more Indians than smoked tobacco. Most tobacco users want to quit and many try, but nicotine dependence makes success difficult; without help, only a small percentage of quit attempts succeed for a year. Effective help combines behavioural support with medication. On the medication side, nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gum, lozenges), bupropion and varenicline roughly double quit rates. On the behavioural side, the core techniques are setting a quit date, self-monitoring of smoking triggers, removing tobacco from the home and workplace, planning for high-risk situations, coping with cravings (delay, distract, deep breathing, drink water), enlisting social support, and relapse prevention (Section 9.9).

Even very brief advice from a doctor helps. In a classic 1979 study in London general practices, Michael Russell and colleagues showed that a few minutes of firm advice to stop, with a leaflet and a warning of follow-up, produced a small but real increase in the number of patients who quit; across millions of consultations, a small effect becomes a large public-health gain. Clinical guidelines now teach the five As for every consultation: Ask about tobacco use, Advise quitting clearly and personally, Assess willingness to quit, Assist the attempt (counselling and medication), and Arrange follow-up. For those not yet willing, the five Rs are used to build motivation: relevance, risks, rewards, roadblocks and repetition. India supports quitting through tobacco control law (the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act, COTPA, 2003), large pictorial warnings, a national tobacco quitline and an SMS-based cessation programme.

Alcohol: screening, the AUDIT and brief interventions

Most people whose drinking harms their health are not alcohol dependent; they are hazardous drinkers (their drinking pattern raises the risk of harm) or harmful drinkers (harm has already occurred). They will never walk into a de-addiction centre, but they visit doctors for other reasons, and that is where they can be found. The standard screening instrument is the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), developed by a World Health Organization collaborative project across six countries and published in 1989, with its validation study by John B. Saunders, Thomas F. Babor and colleagues in 1993. It has 10 items covering three domains: hazardous use (items 1 to 3: frequency, typical quantity, frequency of heavy drinking), dependence symptoms (items 4 to 6) and harmful use (items 7 to 10, including guilt, blackouts, injury and others' concern). Each item scores 0 to 4, for a total of 0 to 40, and a score of 8 or more usually indicates hazardous or harmful use. The first three items alone form the short AUDIT-C. The WHO manual links scores to four risk zones (Table 9.5). An older and shorter screen is the four-question CAGE (Ewing, 1984): have you felt you should Cut down, have people Annoyed you by criticising your drinking, have you felt Guilty about drinking, have you ever had an Eye-opener drink in the morning? Two or more "yes" answers suggest a problem.

Obesity, weight management and exercise

Obesity is usually defined by the body mass index (BMI), weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres. Because South Asians carry more body fat and metabolic risk at a given BMI, Indian consensus guidelines (2009) use lower cut-offs than the international ones: 23 to 24.9 for overweight and 25 or above for obesity, against 25 and 30 internationally. Obesity is multiply determined, by genes, the biology of appetite (the hypothalamic mechanisms are in the Physiological book), cheap calorie-dense food, sedentary work and screens, sleep loss, stress and medications. Binge-eating disorder, which is common among people seeking weight-loss treatment, is described in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

Behavioural weight management grew out of Richard Stuart's 1967 report on the behavioural control of overeating and now has a standard set of components: self-monitoring of food intake, activity and weight (the single most powerful technique); stimulus control (eat only at the table, keep tempting foods out of the house, use smaller plates, never eat while watching television); slowing the rate of eating; realistic goal setting (losing 5 to 10 percent of body weight, which already improves blood pressure and blood sugar); reinforcement for changes in behaviour rather than for kilograms lost; cognitive restructuring of all-or-nothing thinking ("I ate one sweet, so the diet is ruined"); problem solving; and family involvement. Such programmes typically produce weight losses of about 5 to 10 percent in six months. The hard problem is maintenance: most people regain much of the weight within a few years unless contact, self-monitoring and physical activity continue.

Physical activity protects against heart disease, diabetes, some cancers, depression and cognitive decline. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines recommend that adults do at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (brisk walking) or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days. Starting is easy; continuing is hard, and a commonly cited estimate is that about half of the people who begin an exercise programme drop out within six months. Adherence is improved by choosing enjoyable and convenient activities, exercising with others, setting specific plans (the action and coping planning of HAPA), self-monitoring with a pedometer or phone, and building self-efficacy through early success.

Motivational interviewing as a health tool

Almost every lifestyle change above runs into the same obstacle: ambivalence. The smoker wants to quit and wants to smoke. Motivational interviewing, developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick and taught in full in Chapter 6, is a collaborative, person-centred but goal-directed style of conversation for resolving that ambivalence. Its spirit is summarised as partnership, acceptance, compassion and evocation (drawing the person's own reasons for change out of them, rather than installing the clinician's). It was created in the addictions field, as a deliberate alternative to the confrontational style of older alcohol treatment, and it has since spread to almost every area of health behaviour.

How far has it spread, and does it work? In 2003 Brian Burke, Hal Arkowitz and Marisa Menchola published the first major meta-analysis of controlled trials of adaptations of motivational interviewing. Pooling 30 trials, they found that motivational interviewing was as effective as other active treatments, and better than no treatment or placebo, for problems involving alcohol, drugs and diet and exercise (the diet-and-exercise trials included work with obese women with Type 2 diabetes); the results did not support its efficacy for smoking or for HIV-risk behaviour. Later meta-analyses have added medication adherence and other health behaviours. Obsessive-compulsive disorder, by contrast, is treated with exposure and response prevention (Chapter 4), not with motivational interviewing as a primary treatment.

Exam trap

Three motivational interviewing questions recur. (1) Where it was developed and is mainly used: substance abuse (IHBAS 2020). It is also used to improve treatment adherence, so "non-compliance to treatment" is a tempting option, but the original and best-established application is addiction; it is not a treatment for anxious-avoidant personality disorder or bipolar disorder as such. (2) Burke, Arkowitz and Menchola (2003): alcohol, drugs, diet and exercise (diabetes-related trials included) were studied, smoking was studied but not supported, and OCD was not among the problems (RML 2022). (3) The spirit: partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation, the wording of the 2013 third edition, which is what papers key. In the fourth edition (2023) Miller and Rollnick widened evocation into empowerment, and "evoking" lives on as one of MI's four processes (engaging, focusing, evoking, planning); if an option lists either version of the four, it is the MI spirit. "Confrontation, persuasion, expert authority" is exactly what motivational interviewing rejects; "free association, transference, abstinence" is psychoanalysis; "shaping, contingency management" is operant behaviour therapy.

9.5 Chronic Illness, Dying and GriefLower yield · 1 Q

Look back over the illnesses in Section 9.3 and you will notice something they share. Hypertension, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, HIV infection, chronic pain and many cancers are rarely cured; they are managed, often for decades. Modern medicine has become so good at keeping people alive that the typical patient of the twenty-first century is not someone who will be cured next week or dead next week, but someone who will live with an illness for the rest of their life. That changes the psychological task. Living with a chronic illness means adjusting to a new body, a new identity and an uncertain future. When the illness finally cannot be held back, the task changes again: to dying as well as possible and, for the family left behind, to grieving. This section follows that arc, from diagnosis to bereavement.

Living with chronic illness

A chronic illness is a long-lasting condition, usually defined as one that persists for three months or more, that can be controlled but not cured. Think of what it asks of a person. Mrs Shobha, a 46-year-old schoolteacher in Mandya, is told that she has rheumatoid arthritis. Her hands are painful and stiff every morning, she needs tablets every day and an injection every week, her blood must be tested every month, and nobody can tell her whether she will still be able to teach in five years' time. She was the person who looked after everyone else; now she needs help to open a jar. None of this is a psychiatric disorder. It is the ordinary, heavy psychological work of being chronically ill.

The American psychologist Rudolf H. Moos and his colleague Vivien Tsu (1977) described the diagnosis of a serious physical illness as a crisis in exactly the sense you will meet in Section 9.7: a turning point that overwhelms a person's usual ways of coping. They listed seven adaptive tasks that a seriously ill person faces. Three are specific to illness:

  • The patient must deal with pain, incapacity and the other symptoms of the illness.
  • The patient must deal with the hospital environment and the special procedures of treatment, from injections to scans to surgery.
  • The patient must develop and keep adequate relationships with the doctors, nurses and other staff.

Four are general tasks that any major life crisis brings:

  • The patient must preserve a reasonable emotional balance, managing anxiety, anger and sadness.
  • The patient must preserve a satisfactory self-image and a sense of competence and mastery.
  • The patient must sustain relationships with family and friends.
  • The patient must prepare for an uncertain future.

The list is useful because it turns a vague impression ("she is not coping well") into specific targets. Mrs Shobha may be managing her pain well but quietly withdrawing from her friends because she is ashamed of her swollen fingers, and that is where a psychologist can help.

How a person copes depends heavily on what they believe the illness is. Howard Leventhal and his colleagues (from 1980) proposed the common-sense model of illness, also called the self-regulation model. Faced with symptoms or a diagnosis, people build their own lay theory of the illness, an illness representation, and then choose coping actions that make sense within it. Research has found five dimensions in these representations:

  • Identity: the label and the symptoms that go with it ("I have sugar; it makes me tired and thirsty").
  • Cause: what brought it on ("tension at work", "eating too many sweets", "karma", "the evil eye").
  • Timeline: whether it is acute, chronic or cyclical ("it will go once the tension is over").
  • Consequences: what it will do to the person's life ("I will lose my legs like my uncle did").
  • Cure or control: whether it can be cured or controlled, and by whom ("only insulin can control it now; nothing I do matters").

The model explains many puzzling cases of non-adherence. A man who holds an acute, symptom-driven timeline for his hypertension ("the pressure comes when I am tense") takes his tablets only on days when he feels tense, which is exactly the wrong way to treat a condition that has no symptoms. Illness representations are measured with the Illness Perception Questionnaire (John Weinman and colleagues, 1996), and, as the next study shows, they can be changed.

Key study: changing illness perceptions after a heart attack (2002)

Keith Petrie, John Weinman and their colleagues in Auckland, New Zealand, randomly assigned 65 patients admitted with a first heart attack either to usual care or to usual care plus three short sessions with a psychologist while they were in hospital. The sessions targeted the patients' own illness perceptions: what had caused the heart attack (many blamed stress alone and ignored smoking or diet), how long it would affect them and how much they could control it, and each patient left with a written plan for recovery. Compared with the controls, the intervention patients developed more positive views of their illness, felt better prepared to leave hospital, returned to work sooner and reported fewer angina symptoms at follow-up. The criticism is that the sample was small and the follow-up short. The lesson is still striking: three conversations about a patient's beliefs changed what happened to him after discharge.

Is it always better to face an illness squarely? Not necessarily. Denial sounds unhealthy, but it can protect people during the acute phase. In a well-known study of heart patients, Jerome Levine and his colleagues (1987) found that patients high in denial spent fewer days in intensive care and showed fewer signs of cardiac dysfunction while in hospital; in the year after discharge, however, the same patients followed medical advice less well and were rehospitalised more often. Denial helps in the first few days and hurts in the long run. More generally, chronic illness needs both of the kinds of coping that Lazarus and Folkman distinguished (their theory is in the General book): problem-focused coping for the parts of the illness that can be controlled (diet, exercise, medicines, appointments) and emotion-focused coping, including acceptance, for the parts that cannot (the diagnosis itself, an uncertain prognosis).

Some people do more than cope; they find something of value in the illness. The American social psychologist Shelley E. Taylor interviewed women with breast cancer and in 1983 proposed a theory of cognitive adaptation. People adjust to threatening events, she argued, through three themes. They search for meaning (why did it happen, and what does it mean for my life now?), try to regain a sense of mastery over the illness and over life in general, and restore their self-esteem, often by comparing themselves with others who are worse off. Taylor's striking observation was that many of the beliefs that helped were not strictly accurate: women who believed that they could control their cancer adjusted better, even when the belief had little medical basis. She called these beliefs positive illusions. Related to this is post-traumatic growth, a term introduced in the mid-1990s by Richard G. Tedeschi and Lawrence G. Calhoun for the positive changes some people report after a major life crisis, including serious illness: closer relationships, new possibilities and priorities, a sense of personal strength, a greater appreciation of life and spiritual change.

Not everyone adapts. Depression is two to three times more common in people with chronic illness than in the general population, and it worsens the illness, mostly by eroding self-care: depressed diabetics check their sugar less often, and depressed heart patients skip their walks and their tablets. Screening for depression is therefore part of good chronic care. The family carries much of the load. In India, where hospitals expect a relative to stay with the patient and where most care after discharge happens at home, the burden on family carers, usually women, is heavy, and their own health suffers. Helping the carer is helping the patient.

The most widely copied psychological programme for chronic illness was developed by Kate Lorig at Stanford University. Her Chronic Disease Self-Management Program, which grew out of an earlier programme for arthritis, runs for six weekly sessions of about two and a half hours and is led by trained lay people who themselves live with a chronic illness. It teaches action planning, problem solving, the management of symptoms, communication with doctors and the proper use of medicines, and its explicit aim is to raise self-efficacy (Section 9.2). Participants report better health behaviours and more confidence, and they spend fewer days in hospital. Notice the theme that runs through this whole section and the next: the patient, not the professional, does most of the work, so the professional's job is to build the patient's capacity.

Exam trap

Keep three frameworks apart. Moos and Tsu (1977) listed seven adaptive tasks of physical illness, three illness-related and four general. Leventhal's common-sense model describes illness representations: identity, cause, timeline, consequences, cure or control. Taylor's cognitive adaptation theory (1983) has three themes, meaning, mastery and self-enhancement, sustained by positive illusions. Post-traumatic growth is Tedeschi and Calhoun's term, not Taylor's.

Dying and palliative care

For most of human history people died at home, surrounded by their families. In the twentieth century, in the richer countries, dying moved into hospitals, and death became something that doctors felt they had failed at and families did not discuss. Two American sociologists, Barney G. Glaser and Anselm L. Strauss, spent several years observing dying patients and the people around them in hospitals in the San Francisco area, and in Awareness of Dying (1965) they described four awareness contexts, defined by who knows what about the patient's approaching death:

  • In closed awareness, the staff and the family know that the patient is dying, but the patient does not.
  • In suspected awareness, the patient suspects and tries to confirm or disprove the suspicion, while the others try to keep the secret.
  • In mutual pretence, everyone knows, including the patient, but everyone acts as if they did not.
  • In open awareness, everyone knows and acknowledges it, so the dying person can talk about death and plan for it.

If closed awareness sounds old-fashioned, it is not. In India it is common for a family to ask the doctor not to tell the patient that he has cancer, a practice clinicians call collusion. The family's motive is love, but the cost can be high: the patient cannot say goodbye, settle his affairs or make choices about his own care.

The person who did most to break the silence around dying was the Swiss-born American psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross (1926 to 2004). In the mid-1960s, at the University of Chicago, she began a seminar in which she interviewed dying patients, with their permission, in front of medical and theology students, simply asking them what it was like. Her book On Death and Dying (1969), drawn from interviews with about 200 patients, proposed that people who learn that they are dying tend to pass through five stages. The study of death and dying, which her book helped to make respectable, is called thanatology. Follow Mrs Lakshmi, a 52-year-old woman with advanced cancer, through the five stages of dying:

  1. Denial (and isolation): "No, not me. The reports must have been mixed up." Denial cushions the shock and is, Kübler-Ross argued, usually temporary.
  2. Anger: "Why me? I never smoked; I did everything right." The anger is often displaced onto the doctors, the nurses or the family.
  3. Bargaining: "God, let me live until my daughter's wedding in March, and I will walk up the hill to Tirupati." It is an attempt to postpone the inevitable through a deal, often a secret one with God.
  4. Depression: "What is the use of anything now?" Kübler-Ross separated reactive depression (sadness over what has already been lost: the job, the hair, the role in the family) from preparatory depression (grieving in advance for the losses to come).
  5. Acceptance: "I am tired; I am ready." This is not a happy stage, she wrote, but one almost empty of feeling, as if the struggle were over.

Kübler-Ross herself said that the stages could overlap, that not everyone passes through all of them, and that hope persists through every stage. Her readers were less careful. The stages hardened into a fixed sequence that nurses and relatives expected patients to follow, so that a dying man who stayed angry was said to be "stuck", and a woman who never accepted death was felt to have failed. The research does not support a fixed sequence. The model came from clinical impressions rather than systematic measurement; people move back and forth between the reactions, many never show some of them, and the reactions differ with personality, age, culture and the illness itself. Its lasting value is moral rather than scientific: it taught a generation of clinicians to sit down and listen to dying people. The American thanatologist Charles Corr (1992) offered a task-based alternative in which the dying person works on four areas of need (physical, psychological, social and spiritual) at their own pace and in their own order, which fits the evidence better.

While Kübler-Ross was listening to dying patients in Chicago, a British nurse who had become a medical social worker and then a doctor, Cicely Saunders (1918 to 2005), was changing how they were cared for. In 1967 she opened St Christopher's Hospice in south London, the first modern hospice, which combined expert control of pain and other symptoms with research and teaching. Saunders introduced the concept of total pain: the suffering of a dying person has physical, psychological, social and spiritual components, and all four must be treated. She also showed that morphine given regularly, by the clock and in adequate doses, controls cancer pain without the addiction and stupor that doctors feared, a close parallel to the time-contingent medication of Fordyce's operant approach in Section 9.3.

The movement she started is now called palliative care. The World Health Organization (2002) defines it as an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by early identification, assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual. Three features of the definition matter for psychologists. The unit of care is the patient and the family, including support for the family in bereavement. The aim is quality of life, not length of life. And palliative care is not only for the last days: it can begin at diagnosis, alongside treatment aimed at cure or control. Hospice care is the part of palliative care given in the final phase of life, in a hospice or at home.

Key study: early palliative care in lung cancer (2010)

Does it matter when palliative care begins? Jennifer Temel and her colleagues in Boston randomly assigned 151 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer to standard cancer care or to standard care plus early, regular visits from a palliative care team. The early palliative care group had a better quality of life and fewer depressive symptoms, chose less aggressive treatment at the end of life and, unexpectedly, lived longer (a median of 11.6 months against 8.9). The survival finding came from a single trial and needs replication, but the main lesson is now widely accepted: palliative care belongs early in a serious illness, not only at the very end.

What does a psychologist do in palliative care? Assessment comes first, because sadness is normal in dying and depression is not, and the two need to be told apart (a useful clue: the dying person who is sad can still enjoy a visit from the grandchildren; the depressed one cannot). A related state, demoralisation, a loss of hope and meaning with a feeling of being trapped, is common and responds to meaning-focused work. Brief psychotherapies have been designed for the dying. In dignity therapy, developed by the Canadian psychiatrist Harvey Max Chochinov (2005), the patient is interviewed about the things that matter most to them, what they are proudest of and what they want their family to know and remember, and the edited transcript becomes a document they can leave behind. Meaning-centred psychotherapy, developed by William Breitbart, helps patients draw on sources of meaning in the spirit of Frankl's logotherapy (Chapter 6). The psychologist also helps with communication. Breaking bad news well is a skill, and one widely taught protocol, SPIKES (Walter Baile, Robert Buckman and colleagues, 2000), sets out six steps: Setting up the interview; assessing the patient's Perception of the situation; obtaining the patient's Invitation (how much does the patient want to know?); giving Knowledge in small, clear pieces; responding to the patient's Emotions with empathy; and agreeing a Strategy, with a summary.

Indian context

Most Indians who need palliative care never receive it. Two obstacles stand out. The first is access to opioids: for decades the strict licensing rules under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act 1985 made morphine almost unobtainable in most hospitals, until a 2014 amendment created a simpler category of "essential narcotic drugs". The second is that hospital-based care cannot reach people dying at home in villages. Kerala showed an alternative: a community-based model, the Neighbourhood Network in Palliative Care, in which trained local volunteers find bedridden patients in their own area and support them at home, backed by doctors and nurses. In 2008 Kerala became the first Indian state to adopt a palliative care policy, and a large share of the country's palliative care services are still found there.

Exam trap

Kübler-Ross's five stages, in order: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance (DABDA), from On Death and Dying (1969). They describe dying patients facing their own death; they were later applied, much less successfully, to the bereaved. They are neither a fixed nor a universal sequence. The founder of the modern hospice movement is Cicely Saunders (St Christopher's Hospice, 1967), whose concept is total pain. "Awareness contexts" are Glaser and Strauss (1965). Dignity therapy is Chochinov's.

Grief, mourning and the models that describe them

When death comes, the focus of care moves to the people who are left. Three words are used carefully in this field. Bereavement is the objective situation of having lost someone significant through death. Grief is the emotional, cognitive, physical and behavioural response to that loss: the sorrow and yearning, the disbelief, anger and guilt, the sleeplessness and the tight throat that you read about in the chapter's opening story. Mourning is the process by which people adapt to the loss, including the public expressions of grief shaped by culture and religion: the cremation and the immersion of the ashes, the rites of the days after a death (about thirteen days in many Hindu families), the annual shraddha, the prayers for the dead in Muslim and Christian homes. Grief can begin even before a death. Erich Lindemann (1900 to 1974), whose study of the Cocoanut Grove bereaved opened this chapter, noticed it in the wives of soldiers who had gone to war and named it anticipatory grief: grieving in advance for a loss that is expected, as families of people with dementia or advanced cancer often do.

The first modern theory of grief came from Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939). In "Mourning and Melancholia" (written in 1915 and published in 1917), he argued that in mourning the bereaved person must gradually withdraw the emotional energy (libido) invested in the dead person, memory by memory, a painful process that takes time; when "the work of mourning is completed the ego becomes free and uninhibited again" and can invest in new relationships. Freud also drew a distinction that clinicians still use. "In mourning it is the world which has become poor and empty; in melancholia it is the ego itself": the depressed person, unlike the grieving one, loses self-esteem and is full of self-reproach. Lindemann's account of grief work (1944) continued the same idea. The bereaved, he wrote, must emancipate themselves from their bondage to the deceased, readjust to an environment in which the deceased is missing, and form new relationships. The assumption that grief must be "worked through" by confronting the loss, and that avoiding it stores up trouble for later, is known as the grief work hypothesis. As you will see, it has been seriously questioned.

Attachment theory gave grief a biological meaning. John Bowlby (1907 to 1990) saw grief as a form of separation distress: the attachment system, built to keep us close to the people we depend on, goes on searching for a person who will never return. Working with Colin Murray Parkes, whose studies of London widows were published in Bereavement (1972), Bowlby described four phases of grief, set out most fully in Loss (1980), the third volume of Attachment and Loss: numbing, a period of shock and disbelief lasting from a few hours to about a week; yearning and searching, with pining, restlessness, anger and preoccupation with the dead person; disorganisation and despair, when the reality of the loss sinks in and the person feels apathetic and aimless; and reorganisation, when the person builds a new life and a new identity. Parkes found that widows often had a vivid sense of their dead husband's presence in the first months, an experience that is common in grief and not a sign of psychosis.

Many people also apply Kübler-Ross's five stages to the bereaved, a use she herself encouraged in her last book, On Grief and Grieving (2005, with David Kessler). The best test came from the Yale Bereavement Study, in which Paul Maciejewski, Holly G. Prigerson and their colleagues (2007) assessed 233 people at different points up to two years after a death from natural causes. Disbelief, yearning, anger and depression did peak roughly in that order over the first six months, which gives the stage idea some support. But the most frequently reported reaction, from the very first month, was not denial but acceptance, and the strongest negative feeling throughout was yearning, not anger or depression. The stages are better thought of as overlapping reactions than as steps on a ladder.

A different kind of model came from the American psychologist J. William Worden, whose Grief Counseling and Grief Therapy first appeared in 1982 and has been revised several times since. Worden objected that stages and phases make the mourner passive, someone to whom grief simply happens. He described instead four tasks of mourning, things the bereaved person has to do, which can be revisited and worked on in any order. In their current wording they are:

  1. To accept the reality of the loss, intellectually and emotionally: the person really is dead and will not return. Seeing the body and taking part in the funeral rites help with this task.
  2. To process the pain of grief, rather than avoiding or numbing it with alcohol, constant busyness or a hasty move to another city.
  3. To adjust to a world without the deceased. This includes external adjustments (learning to manage the money, to cook, to raise the children alone), internal adjustments ("Who am I now, if I am no longer a wife?") and spiritual adjustments ("What do I now believe about the world, and about God?").
  4. To find an enduring connection with the deceased while embarking on a new life. In the first edition this task was to withdraw emotional energy from the deceased and reinvest it in another relationship, which was Freud's idea; Worden changed it as evidence grew that healthy mourners keep a bond with the dead.

Worden also listed the mediators of mourning that make grief easier or harder: who the person who died was (a child, a spouse, a parent); the nature of the attachment (how strong, how secure, how ambivalent); how the person died (suddenly, violently, by suicide, after a long illness); the mourner's history of losses and mental health; the mourner's personality; the social support available; and other stresses happening at the same time. And he drew a line that matters for the next subsection: grief counselling helps people with normal, uncomplicated grief to work through the tasks, whereas grief therapy is for people whose grief has become complicated.

The Dutch psychologists Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut (1999) noticed that the grief work hypothesis captured only half of what bereaved people actually do. In their dual process model, bereaved people face two kinds of stressor (Figure 9.3). Loss-oriented coping deals with the loss itself: crying, looking at photographs, yearning, ruminating about the death, the grief work of the older models. Restoration-oriented coping deals with the secondary consequences of the death: learning to do the things the dead person used to do, taking on new roles and a new identity, building new relationships, and also taking time off from grief through distraction. The heart of the model is oscillation: healthy grieving moves back and forth between confronting the loss and turning away from it, sometimes many times a day. A widow who weeps over her husband's photograph in the morning and goes to the bank in the afternoon to transfer the accounts is not in denial in the afternoon; she is doing what grief requires. Problems arise when a person is stuck at one pole, grieving all the time with no restoration (chronic grief), or keeping so busy that the loss is never faced (avoided grief).

Worden's revised fourth task and the dual process model both reflect a shift that took place in the 1990s. In Continuing Bonds (1996), Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman challenged the Freudian assumption that healthy grief ends by "letting go". Bereaved people, they showed, commonly keep an inner relationship with the dead: they talk to them, consult them in their minds before decisions, keep their belongings and feel watched over by them. These continuing bonds are usually healthy. In much of the world this was never news. An Indian family that garlands a parent's photograph, lights a lamp before it, performs the annual rites for the ancestors and tells the grandchildren what their grandfather would have said is maintaining a continuing bond, and nobody would think it pathological.

The grief work hypothesis was challenged from another direction as well. In "The myths of coping with loss" (1989), Camille B. Wortman and Roxane Cohen Silver examined the evidence for several beliefs widely held by professionals: that intense distress after a loss is inevitable; that its absence is a sign of trouble (the person is "in denial" and will break down later); that the loss must be worked through; and that recovery should be complete within a year or so. None, they concluded, was well supported. In 2002 George A. Bonanno and his colleagues studied older married people from before a spouse's death to 18 months after it and identified distinct trajectories of grief. The most common, shown by about half of the bereaved, was resilience: some sadness and disruption, but stable, healthy functioning throughout. Others showed recovery (marked distress that eased over a year or two) or chronic grief (distress that stayed high), while delayed grief, the outcome that the grief work hypothesis predicted for people who did not grieve openly, was rare. Resilience, Bonanno argued (2004), is the norm, not a sign of denial.

Finally, not every grief is permitted. Kenneth J. Doka (1989) coined the term disenfranchised grief for grief that is not openly acknowledged, socially sanctioned or publicly mourned. It arises when the relationship is not recognised (a lover in a secret affair, a same-sex partner, a former spouse), when the loss is not recognised (a miscarriage, an abortion, the death of a pet, the "living loss" of a parent with dementia), when the griever is not recognised (a young child or a person with intellectual disability, who is assumed not to understand), or when the death itself is stigmatised (suicide, AIDS, a drug overdose). Disenfranchised mourners lack the rituals and support that help other mourners, and their grief is more likely to become complicated. Table 9.6 brings the models of grief together.

Complicated grief and bereavement care

If most grief is normal and most bereaved people are resilient, who needs help? Lindemann already knew that some people's grief went wrong. He described morbid grief reactions of two kinds: delayed grief, postponed for weeks, months or even years, and distorted grief, in which grief appears in disguised forms such as overactivity without any sense of loss, the symptoms of the dead person's last illness, physical disease, furious hostility against particular people, or agitated depression. Over the following decades the problem acquired many names (pathological, abnormal, chronic, traumatic and complicated grief), and it was only in the twenty-first century that it was defined precisely.

The research of Prigerson and of M. Katherine Shear showed that a minority of bereaved people, roughly one in ten after a death from natural causes and more after a violent death, develop a distinct syndrome. Its core is persistent, intense yearning or longing for the dead person, or preoccupation with them, together with symptoms such as disbelief, intense emotional pain, a feeling that part of oneself has died, avoidance of reminders, numbness, loneliness and a sense that life is meaningless, all lasting well beyond the period that the person's culture would expect and seriously impairing their life. It responds poorly to the standard treatments for depression, and it is not the same thing as either depression or post-traumatic stress disorder. The diagnostic systems now recognise it as prolonged grief disorder:

  • ICD-11 (adopted in 2019) includes prolonged grief disorder, requiring that the grief persist for an atypically long period, at least six months after the loss, and clearly exceed the norms of the person's culture.
  • DSM-5 (2013) was more cautious. It placed a similar condition, persistent complex bereavement disorder, in Section III, "Conditions for Further Study", alongside other proposals such as non-suicidal self-injury (repeatedly cutting, burning, scratching or otherwise deliberately injuring one's own body without any intent to die, usually to relieve tension or distress), internet gaming disorder (persistent, impairing loss of control over gaming) and attenuated psychosis syndrome (mild, subthreshold psychotic symptoms in a person at raised risk of psychosis). A Section III condition is a proposed criteria set offered to encourage research, not an official diagnosis.
  • DSM-5-TR (2022) moved it into the main text as an official diagnosis, renamed prolonged grief disorder, in the chapter on trauma- and stressor-related disorders. The death must have occurred at least 12 months earlier (at least 6 months for children and adolescents), with intense yearning or preoccupation and at least three of eight further symptoms present nearly every day for at least the last month.

The full criteria, and the differences from major depression and post-traumatic stress disorder, belong to the Psychiatric Disorders book. What matters here is what to do about it.

Here the evidence gives a clear and perhaps surprising answer: offering counselling to everyone who is bereaved does little good. In a meta-analysis of 61 controlled studies, Joseph Currier, Robert Neimeyer and Jeffrey Berman (2008) found that universal interventions, offered to anyone who had lost someone, had no reliable effect, whereas interventions offered to people who were already struggling, especially those with complicated grief, did help. Some early bereavement interventions may even get in the way of natural recovery, just as psychological debriefing does after trauma (Section 9.7). The practical model that follows is a stepped one, set out for example in British guidance on supportive and palliative care for adults with cancer (2004):

  • All bereaved people should receive information about grief and about the support that is available; most will cope with the help of their family, friends and community.
  • Some will benefit from a more formal opportunity to talk about their loss, through trained volunteers, self-help groups, faith communities or bereavement services.
  • A few, those with complicated or prolonged grief, depression or post-traumatic stress, need specialist mental health intervention.

For that last group, specific treatments work. Shear and her colleagues developed complicated grief treatment, a 16-session therapy that combines elements of interpersonal psychotherapy and cognitive behavioural therapy. It works on both orientations of the dual process model: loss-focused work, including "imaginal revisiting" (telling the story of the death in detail, with exposure to its most painful moments) and imaginal conversations with the dead person; and restoration-focused work on personal goals and relationships. In a randomised trial published in 2005, 51 percent of patients responded to complicated grief treatment, against 28 percent of those given standard interpersonal psychotherapy. Interpersonal psychotherapy itself (Chapter 3) counts grief as one of its four problem areas, alongside interpersonal role disputes, role transitions and interpersonal deficits, and cognitive-behavioural treatments for prolonged grief also have good support. Specialist grief therapy of this kind is normally conducted one to one, because it must be paced to the individual's distress; groups, especially groups for people bereaved by suicide, are a valuable complement that reduces isolation and stigma.

One more distinction matters after disasters. When a death is sudden, violent or witnessed (a fire, a road accident, a murder, a suicide), the bereaved may develop traumatic grief, in which the symptoms of grief are entangled with those of trauma: intrusive images of how the person died, nightmares, avoidance of reminders of the death, hypervigilance. Traumatic grief needs attention to both the trauma and the loss. But the word "may" is important. Most people bereaved in a disaster grieve normally and recover with ordinary support; Lindemann himself, whose 101 bereaved included Cocoanut Grove families, found that an uncomplicated grief reaction could ordinarily be settled in four to six weeks with eight to ten interviews.

Exam trap

RML 2020 asked which approach to bereavement after a crisis should not be adopted. Three options are sound: bereavement is a normal process that will usually run its course; complicated grieving requires intervention; and bereavement counselling, when it is needed, is best conducted one to one. The false one is that crisis-related bereavement should always be considered traumatic grief: most people bereaved in a crisis grieve normally, and treating all of them as traumatised wastes help and may hinder recovery. "Always" and "never" are warning words in such options. Also: persistent complex bereavement disorder was a DSM-5 Section III proposal (2013); in DSM-5-TR (2022) it became the official prolonged grief disorder (12 months; 6 months in children), while ICD-11 requires 6 months. Non-suicidal self-injury remains a Section III proposal (RML 2022).

Exam trap

A 2025 paper asked what interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT, Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman) does not typically include, offering grief and bereavement work, role conflict resolution, role transition support, and "role playing and role reversal techniques"; the key is the last. The first three are IPT's own problem areas (grief, interpersonal role disputes, role transitions; the fourth is interpersonal deficits), so the answer follows by elimination. Role reversal, in which the client acts the part of the other person, belongs to Jacob Levy Moreno's psychodrama (Chapter 7) and to Gestalt-style enactment. Do not overlearn the key, though: IPT manuals do list brief role play, rehearsing a difficult conversation with the therapist, among IPT's behaviour change techniques. What IPT lacks is psychodrama's staged enactment and role reversal.

Clinical link

Telling grief from depression is a daily clinical task. In grief the painful feelings come in waves, or "pangs", triggered by reminders of the dead person, and they are mixed with warm memories and even humour; self-esteem is preserved, and thoughts of death are usually about joining the person who died. In depression the low mood and loss of pleasure are pervasive, and there are feelings of worthlessness and self-loathing: Freud's contrast between the world becoming empty and the self becoming empty. DSM-5 removed the old "bereavement exclusion", so major depression can be diagnosed after a loss when its full criteria are met (details in the Psychiatric Disorders book).

Everything in this chapter so far has been about one person at a time: one woman with rheumatoid arthritis, one smoker, one widow. Now do some arithmetic. India has well under one psychiatrist for every lakh of its people, far fewer clinical psychologists, and most of both work in cities. Suppose every one of them saw patients for fifty hours a week, fifty weeks a year. They would still reach only a small fraction of the people who, according to the National Mental Health Survey, need care. No amount of skill in the consulting room can close a gap of that size. The American psychologist George W. Albee (1921 to 2006) liked to put the point in the language of public health: no mass disorder afflicting humankind has ever been eliminated by treating affected individuals one at a time. Cholera was beaten by clean water and sewers, not by more doctors. Community mental health is the attempt to think about mental illness in the same way, at the level of whole populations, and to move care out of the institution and into the places where people live. This section follows that idea from its history to its four big strategies: prevention, consultation, empowerment and the sharing of tasks with non-specialists, and finally to the obstacle that runs through all of them, stigma.

From the asylum to the community

The nineteenth century answered the problem of severe mental illness with the asylum. The reformers who built the first ones, whose moral treatment is described in Chapter 1, meant them to be small, humane retreats. They did not stay small. By the middle of the twentieth century the state mental hospitals of the United States held about 559,000 patients (the peak, reached in 1955), many of them for decades, on wards of a hundred beds or more. Britain's county asylums and India's old mental hospitals at Ranchi, Agra and elsewhere ran on the same custodial lines: patients were kept, fed and controlled, and very few went home.

After the Second World War a series of critics argued that the institution itself produced some of the disability it was supposed to treat. The British psychiatrist Russell Barton described in 1959 an "institutional neurosis" of apathy, submissiveness, lack of initiative and loss of interest in the future, which he blamed not on the illness but on enforced idleness, loss of contact with the outside world and the authoritarian attitudes of staff. Two years later the Canadian-born sociologist Erving Goffman (1922 to 1982), who had spent a year doing fieldwork at St Elizabeths Hospital in Washington, published Asylums (1961). Goffman described the mental hospital as a total institution: a place, like a prison, a monastery or an army barracks, where large numbers of people sleep, work and spend their leisure in the same place, under one authority, on a fixed timetable, cut off from the wider society. On admission, he argued, the patient undergoes a "mortification of the self": personal clothes and possessions are taken away, privacy disappears and every action is supervised, until the person has learned to be the patient the institution expects. The most careful evidence came from John Wing and George Brown, whose Institutionalism and Schizophrenia (1970) followed patients in three English mental hospitals through the 1960s. The poorer the social environment of a hospital (fewer personal possessions, less contact with the outside, more hours spent doing nothing), the more its patients showed the negative symptoms of schizophrenia: social withdrawal, flat affect and poverty of speech. As the environments improved, so did the patients.

Several forces then came together. The first was pharmacological. Chlorpromazine, the first antipsychotic drug, was introduced into psychiatry in Paris in 1952 by Jean Delay and Pierre Deniker and reached American hospitals in 1954; for the first time many people with psychotic illness could be helped enough to leave hospital (the drugs themselves belong to the Physiological and Disorders books). The second was the experience of the war. Military psychiatrists had found that soldiers with combat stress recovered best when they were treated quickly and close to their units rather than sent to distant hospitals, and the sheer number of psychiatric casualties had made mental health a public concern. The third was money and conscience: the hospitals were expensive, and their conditions had become a scandal. In the United States the National Mental Health Act of 1946 led to the creation of the National Institute of Mental Health, and a Joint Commission on Mental Illness and Health recommended in its report Action for Mental Health (1961) that the huge state hospitals be replaced by smaller services based in the community.

The decisive step was taken by John F. Kennedy (1917 to 1963), whose sister Rosemary had an intellectual disability and had been left severely impaired by a lobotomy in 1941. On 5 February 1963 Kennedy sent Congress a special message on mental illness and mental retardation, promising that "reliance on the cold mercy of custodial isolation will be supplanted by the open warmth of community concern and capability". On 31 October 1963, a few weeks before his assassination, he signed the Mental Retardation Facilities and Community Mental Health Centers Construction Act, usually called the Community Mental Health Centers Act. This launched the community mental health movement as national policy. The Act funded the building of community mental health centres, each responsible for a defined geographical catchment area of roughly 75,000 to 200,000 people, and each expected to provide five essential services:

  • Inpatient care, for short admissions close to the patient's home.
  • Outpatient care, for individuals, groups and families.
  • Partial hospitalisation, in the form of day care (and night care for people who could work during the day).
  • Emergency services, available twenty-four hours a day.
  • Consultation and education for the community's other agencies: schools, courts, police, clergy and family doctors.

Notice the last item. The centres were meant not only to treat but to prevent, and to work through other people as well as directly with patients. Those two ideas, prevention and consultation, are the subjects of the next two subsections, and both came largely from Gerald Caplan.

The policy that went with the centres was deinstitutionalisation: the systematic discharge of long-stay patients from mental hospitals into the community, together with the shrinking or closure of the hospitals themselves. In numbers it succeeded. The resident population of American state hospitals fell by about three quarters over the next three decades. In human terms the results were mixed, and the movement learned several hard lessons:

  • The money did not follow the patients. Far fewer centres were built than had been planned, and many of those that opened drifted towards treating less disabled and more rewarding clients, leaving the discharged long-stay patients with little support.
  • Many discharged patients relapsed, were readmitted, were discharged again and were readmitted again, a cycle known as the revolving door phenomenon.
  • Some ended up homeless. Barlow and Durand note that about 30 percent of homeless people in the United States have a severe mental illness, although the rise in homelessness in the 1980s also had economic causes, such as unemployment and a shortage of cheap housing.
  • Others simply moved from one institution to another: to nursing homes, boarding homes and, increasingly, jails and prisons. This is transinstitutionalisation, and the drift of untreated people into the criminal justice system is called criminalisation.
  • Much of the burden of care moved, unpaid and unsupported, onto families.

Barlow and Durand's verdict is that deinstitutionalisation is largely considered a failure: not because care in the community was a bad idea, but because the community services needed to make it work were never adequately provided. The lesson, repeated in country after country, is that closing beds is easy and building community services is hard, and that doing the first without the second harms the very people it was meant to free.

Other countries ran the same experiment in their own ways. The most radical was Italy. The psychiatrist Franco Basaglia (1924 to 1980) unlocked the wards of the asylum at Gorizia in the 1960s and later dismantled the asylum at Trieste, replacing it with community mental health centres open twenty-four hours a day. His campaign led to Law 180 of 1978, often called the Basaglia Law, which stopped new admissions to Italy's psychiatric hospitals and moved inpatient care into small units in general hospitals. Trieste's network of community centres is still held up by the World Health Organization as a model of community care.

India's story is different, because India never had the enormous asylum population that the West had to empty. Its problem was too little care of any kind. A handful of old mental hospitals, many in poor condition (a National Human Rights Commission review at the end of the 1990s described overcrowding, locked cells and neglect), coexisted with vast rural areas that had no mental health service at all. Families, temples, dargahs and faith healers filled the gap, sometimes with terrible results. On 6 August 2001, twenty-eight people with mental illness who were chained at a faith-healing home in Erwadi, in Tamil Nadu, burned to death when a fire broke out, because no one could free them in time. The Supreme Court took up the matter on its own motion, and the tragedy became a turning point in the regulation of such places.

India's answer to the shortage of specialists was to put mental health into primary care. In the 1970s pilot projects at Raipur Rani in Haryana (run from the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research, Chandigarh, as one centre of a WHO study in several developing countries) and at Sakalwara near Bengaluru (run by NIMHANS) showed that doctors and health workers in primary health centres could be trained to recognise and treat common and severe mental disorders. That work fed into the National Mental Health Programme (1982) and the District Mental Health Programme (1996), which was modelled on a pilot in Bellary district of Karnataka. The details of these programmes, of the National Mental Health Survey (2015 to 2016) and of the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, which gives every person a right to access mental health care and a right to live in the community, are set out in Chapter 1.

Exam trap

Prevention: from three levels to three targets

Public health measures disease in two ways, and you need both terms before prevention makes sense. Incidence is the number of new cases of a disorder that arise in a population during a given period, usually a year. Prevalence is the total number of cases, old and new, present in the population at a given time. Picture a bathtub. Incidence is the water flowing in from the tap; prevalence is the water in the tub. You can lower the level in the tub by turning down the tap (fewer new cases) or by opening the drain (shorter illnesses, through earlier and better treatment).

In the 1950s two American professors of preventive medicine, Hugh R. Leavell and E. Gurney Clark, organised the work of public health into "levels of prevention" that ran from health promotion and specific protection (immunisation is the classic example) through early diagnosis and prompt treatment to the limitation of disability and rehabilitation, grouped into primary, secondary and tertiary prevention. Gerald Caplan (1917 to 2008), the psychiatrist whom you met in the chapter's opening story working with Erich Lindemann, carried the scheme into mental health in Principles of Preventive Psychiatry (1964), the book that made prevention part of the everyday vocabulary of psychiatry and psychology. Caplan's three levels are defined by what they reduce (Table 9.7):

  • Primary prevention aims to reduce the incidence of a disorder: to stop new cases arising at all, by counteracting harmful circumstances before they have had a chance to produce illness. Caplan argued that people need a steady flow of three kinds of "supplies" to stay mentally healthy: physical supplies (food, shelter, sensory stimulation, protection from injury), psychosocial supplies (love, recognition and interaction with the significant people in one's life) and sociocultural supplies (the customs, values and social structures that give a person a place in society). Primary prevention protects those supplies and helps people through the crises that threaten them, which is why crisis intervention (Section 9.7) began as a preventive strategy.
  • Secondary prevention aims to reduce the prevalence of a disorder by shortening its duration, through early case finding, screening, prompt diagnosis and effective treatment.
  • Tertiary prevention aims to reduce the disability that an established disorder leaves behind, and the risk of relapse, through rehabilitation (Sections 9.8 and 9.9).

Apply the scheme and most exam questions on it answer themselves. Promoting vaccination to prevent a disease is primary prevention, because it acts on healthy people before the disease exists. Treating a diagnosed illness is secondary prevention (or simply treatment). Rehabilitation after an injury, or surgery to limit the damage of a chronic disease, deals with a condition that is already established, which is tertiary territory. Newborn screening for phenylketonuria is a neat secondary-prevention example from mental health: the metabolic disorder is detected in the first days of life, before any damage is done, and a special diet prevents the intellectual disability that would otherwise follow. Public health textbooks add a further level, primordial prevention, a term introduced by the WHO cardiologist Tomas Strasser in 1978, which goes back one step more and tries to stop the risk factors themselves from appearing in a population: keeping children from ever starting to use tobacco, or building towns in which walking to school is safe.

Caplan's scheme had a weakness that critics soon pointed out. Only primary prevention is prevention in the everyday sense. Secondary prevention is really early treatment, and tertiary prevention is rehabilitation; calling them prevention stretches the word until almost everything a mental health service does counts as preventive. In 1983 the American public health physician Robert S. Gordon proposed a different classification, based not on the stage of the disorder but on who receives the intervention. This matters, he argued, because every intervention has costs and risks, and those must be weighed against the risk of the disorder in the people who receive it. In the United States the Institute of Medicine (IOM) adopted Gordon's terms in its 1994 report Reducing Risks for Mental Disorders, edited by Patricia Mrazek and Robert Haggerty, and reserved the word prevention for interventions delivered before a disorder begins:

  • Universal prevention is aimed at a whole population, or a whole group such as every child in a school, chosen without regard to individual risk. It must therefore be cheap, safe and acceptable to everyone. Examples are school-based life-skills education (the World Health Organization's programme lists ten core life skills: decision making, problem solving, creative thinking, critical thinking, effective communication, interpersonal relationship skills, self-awareness, empathy, coping with emotions and coping with stress), anti-bullying programmes and laws restricting access to lethal pesticides.
  • Selective prevention is aimed at subgroups whose risk is higher than average because of a known biological, psychological or social risk factor, although the individuals in them show no symptoms: children of parents with depression or alcohol dependence, bereaved children, refugees, or young first-time mothers living in poverty who receive regular home visits from a nurse.
  • Indicated prevention is aimed at high-risk individuals who already show minimal but detectable signs or symptoms foreshadowing a disorder, but who do not yet meet diagnostic criteria. Brief cognitive behavioural programmes for adolescents with mild depressive symptoms are one example; monitoring and support for young people at "clinical high risk" of psychosis, whose attenuated symptoms DSM-5 placed as a proposal in its Section III (attenuated psychosis syndrome), are another.

The IOM report placed prevention at the start of a wider mental health intervention spectrum, followed by treatment (case identification and standard treatment for known disorders) and maintenance (support for long-term treatment and aftercare, including rehabilitation). Figure 9.4 shows how the old and new vocabularies line up. Roughly, Caplan's primary prevention became the three IOM types of prevention, his secondary prevention became treatment, and his tertiary prevention became maintenance.

Prevention aims to reduce disorder. A related but distinct goal is mental health promotion, which aims to increase positive mental health in everyone, whether or not they are at risk. The World Health Organization (2004) defines mental health as "a state of well-being in which the individual realizes his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and is able to make a contribution to his or her community", and promotion works on the conditions that make this possible: secure early childhoods, good schools, decent work, safe neighbourhoods, social inclusion. Albee summarised the logic of both prevention and promotion in a well-known formula. The incidence of disorder, he wrote, rises with organic factors and stress (and, in later versions, exploitation), and falls with coping skills, self-esteem and support groups. A preventive programme can therefore work on either side of the fraction: reduce the stresses and injustices in the numerator, or strengthen the competence and support in the denominator.

Why spend money on everyone when only a few will ever become ill? The British epidemiologist Geoffrey Rose (1926 to 1993) answered with the prevention paradox (1981): a preventive measure that brings large benefits to the community offers little to each participating individual. Because most people are at modest risk, the many people at small risk produce more cases of a disease than the few at high risk, so shifting the whole population a little towards safety prevents more cases than treating the high-risk minority intensively. The paradox has a cost. The benefit to any one person of a universal programme is so small that its effects are hard to detect, and universal programmes in mental health often show small effects or none; Barlow and Durand note, for example, that school curricula on suicide aimed at all students have generally not been shown to work, whereas programmes targeted at people at risk do better (Section 9.7). Good prevention planning combines both: universal measures that are cheap and safe, and selective and indicated measures for those who need more.

Mental health consultation

In 1949, a few years before he joined Lindemann in Boston, Caplan was working in Jerusalem, where a small team of mental health workers was responsible for many thousands of immigrant children living in residential institutions. Children were referred faster than they could possibly be seen. Caplan noticed something about the referrals: often the problem lay less in the child than in the way the institution's caretakers understood the child. So instead of treating children, his team began to discuss them with the caretakers. Each such discussion helped not only that child but every child the caretaker would look after in the future. Out of this experience came mental health consultation, which Caplan described fully in The Theory and Practice of Mental Health Consultation (1970).

Caplan defined consultation as an interaction between two professionals: the consultant, a specialist, and the consultee, a professional in another field (a teacher, a nurse, a general practitioner, a police officer, a priest) who asks for help with a current work problem that the consultee has decided lies within the consultant's competence. Several features set it apart from other kinds of professional help:

  • The relationship is coordinate, not hierarchical. The consultant has no administrative authority over the consultee, and the consultee is free to accept or reject the consultant's suggestions.
  • Professional responsibility for the client stays with the consultee. The consultant does not take over the case.
  • The focus is the work problem, not the consultee's personal life. If the consultee's own feelings are getting in the way, the consultant works on them indirectly, through discussion of the case, and does not turn the meeting into psychotherapy.
  • The aim is twofold: to help with the current case and to increase the consultee's ability to handle similar cases in the future without help. Because one consultee may deal with hundreds of clients, consultation multiplies the reach of a scarce specialist, which is why Caplan saw it as a tool of primary prevention.

These features distinguish consultation from supervision, in which a senior member of the same profession has authority over the supervisee, evaluates his work and shares responsibility for his cases; from psychotherapy, whose subject is the client's own problems; from education, which follows a curriculum rather than the consultee's current problem; and from collaboration, in which the specialist joins the team and shares responsibility for the outcome, a model that Caplan and Ruth B. Caplan described in 1993 as an alternative for settings such as hospitals and schools where a mental health specialist works on site.

Caplan divided consultation into four types (Table 9.8), according to whether the focus is a particular case or an administrative programme, and whether the main target is the client (or programme) or the consultee. Follow one school through all four. Ms Rekha teaches Class 6 in a government school in Mysuru. One of her pupils, eleven-year-old Divya, has become silent, tearful and inattentive since her father died three months ago, and Ms Rekha is convinced that the girl will fail her examinations and drop out. She asks the psychologist who visits the school every fortnight for help.

  • In client-centred case consultation, the psychologist assesses Divya herself, perhaps with an interview and a few tests, and gives the teacher an expert opinion and specific recommendations for managing her. The main goal is to help this child; anything the teacher learns is a bonus.
  • In consultee-centred case consultation, the focus moves to the teacher's difficulty in handling the case. Caplan listed four reasons why a consultee may struggle: lack of knowledge (Ms Rekha does not know how grief shows itself in children), lack of skill (she knows but does not know how to talk with a grieving child), lack of self-confidence (she is newly appointed and doubts herself) and lack of professional objectivity, when the consultee's own feelings or experiences distort her view of the client. The main goal is to improve the consultee's work with this and future cases.
  • In programme-centred administrative consultation, the headmaster asks the psychologist to help design a school programme for children who have been bereaved or are in distress: how to identify them, what the teachers should do, when to refer. The focus is the programme.
  • In consultee-centred administrative consultation, the focus is the difficulties that the administrators themselves have in running the school's programmes: poor communication between staff and management, weak leadership, conflict between departments. The consultant works with the organisation's people rather than on a specific plan.

Caplan's most original idea concerns the fourth reason for difficulty, lack of objectivity, and especially the form he called theme interference. Suppose Ms Rekha lost her own father as a child and grew up being told that a fatherless girl would never amount to anything. That unresolved theme now takes the form of a hidden syllogism: an "initial category" (a girl whose father has died) leads to an "inevitable outcome" (she will fail). Divya fits the category, so the teacher sees the outcome as certain and, without meaning to, gives up on her. Caplan's advice was not to interpret the teacher's past to her, which would turn consultation into therapy and offend her, but to reduce the theme interference indirectly: by examining Divya's case carefully with the teacher until it becomes clear that, for this girl, the outcome is not inevitable at all. The teacher's fear is disconfirmed in the case, and her objectivity returns.

Community psychology and empowerment

The new community mental health centres needed psychologists, and in May 1965 a group of American psychologists met at Swampscott, Massachusetts, for a conference on how to train them. They came away dissatisfied with the idea that psychologists should simply do traditional clinical work in a new building. They wanted a new field, which they named community psychology: the study of how individuals are shaped by, and can shape, their social systems, with the community rather than the individual as the main level of analysis and intervention. The Swampscott participants described the community psychologist as a "participant-conceptualiser", someone who works inside the community and also studies it. The field's core values are still those set out in its early years: prevention rather than treatment; strengths and competence rather than deficits; an ecological view of people in their settings; respect for diversity; collaboration with communities rather than doing things to them; citizen participation; and social justice.

The ecological view came largely from James G. Kelly, who in 1966 borrowed four principles from biology to describe how human settings work. Interdependence: a change in one part of a social system (a new principal, a factory closure) ripples through all the others. Cycling of resources: every setting has resources (people's talents, time, money, goodwill) that can be identified and used. Adaptation: people adapt to the demands of their settings, and settings change people. Succession: settings change over time, so an intervention must fit the history of the place. A second key idea came from Seymour B. Sarason (1919 to 2010), who in 1974 argued that the psychological sense of community, the feeling of belonging to a network of mutual support that one can depend on, should be the central concept of the field. David W. McMillan and David M. Chavis (1986) later broke the sense of community into four elements: membership (the feeling of belonging), influence (mattering to the group, and the group mattering to you), integration and fulfilment of needs (being rewarded by membership) and shared emotional connection (a shared history, places and experiences).

Community psychology also asked uncomfortable questions about where problems are located. In Blaming the Victim (1971) the psychologist William Ryan showed how social problems such as poverty, poor schooling and ill health are routinely explained by the supposed defects of the people who suffer from them (their attitudes, their culture, their families) rather than by the conditions that produce them. This kind of victim blaming leads naturally to programmes that try to fix individuals and leave the conditions alone. A clinical psychologist who treats one farmer's depression after crop failure, while ignoring the debt trap that is producing depression in the whole village, is in danger of doing exactly this.

The most influential answer came from Julian Rappaport. In a 1981 paper titled "In praise of paradox: A social policy of empowerment over prevention", Rappaport argued that even prevention could be paternalistic: experts decide what people need and deliver it to them, treating them like children. The opposite, an advocacy model that defends people's rights, treats them as citizens but may leave their needs unmet. Rappaport's resolution of this paradox was empowerment, which he later (1987) defined as "a process: the mechanism by which people, organizations, and communities gain mastery over their affairs". Empowerment treats people as complete human beings with both rights and needs, and as the experts on their own lives. The professional's role changes from expert provider to collaborator and resource. Marc A. Zimmerman (1995) separated three components of psychological empowerment: an intrapersonal component (perceived control and self-efficacy in a domain of life), an interactional component (a critical understanding of one's social environment, the resources available and how to use them) and a behavioural component (actually taking part in actions that change one's situation, such as joining an organisation).

Empowerment has roots outside psychology too. The Brazilian educator Paulo Freire (1921 to 1997), in Pedagogy of the Oppressed (written in Portuguese in 1968, first published in English in 1970), described conscientisation: the process by which poor and oppressed people, through dialogue, come to understand the social causes of their situation and to act on them. In mental health, empowerment takes several concrete forms. Self-help and mutual-help groups, from Alcoholics Anonymous (founded in 1935) to groups of people who hear voices and associations of family carers, let people with an experience help one another without professional control. The user and survivor movement demands a say in how services are run. Peer support workers, themselves in recovery, are employed in services. And law can empower: the advance directives and nominated representatives of India's Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (Chapter 1) are attempts to keep decisions in the hands of the person with the illness, even when they are unwell.

Task-shifting and lay counsellors

The treatment gap is the proportion of people with a disorder who need treatment but do not receive it. In a much-cited review of studies from many countries, Robert Kohn, Shekhar Saxena, Itzhak Levav and Benedetto Saraceno (2004) found that the gap was large everywhere and largest in low-income countries; in India the National Mental Health Survey (Chapter 1) put it at between about 70 and 92 percent for different disorders. The obvious solution, training more specialists, would take generations. As early as 1975 a WHO expert committee recommended that basic mental health care be integrated into primary health care and delivered by non-specialist health workers, and the Alma-Ata Declaration of 1978, with its call for primary health care for all, strengthened the case. The Indian pilot projects of the 1970s described in Section 9.6.1 were early tests of the idea.

The strategy now has a name. Task-shifting, defined in WHO guidelines of 2008, is the rational redistribution of tasks from highly qualified health workers to workers with shorter training, so that scarce specialists are used where only they can help. Because the specialists do not disappear but move into training and supervising the non-specialists, many writers now prefer the term task-sharing. In the same year the WHO launched its Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP), and its Intervention Guide (2010) gave non-specialist health workers simple protocols for the priority conditions: depression, psychosis, epilepsy, dementia, alcohol and drug use, child and adolescent disorders, and self-harm and suicide. The most important question was whether people with no formal mental health training could deliver psychological treatments, not just medicines. A series of trials, several of them in India, answered it.

The central figure is the Indian psychiatrist Vikram Patel (born 1964). In 1996 he co-founded Sangath, a non-governmental organisation in Goa, and his manual Where There Is No Psychiatrist (2003) was written for community health workers. With colleagues at Sangath he designed brief psychological treatments that could be delivered by a lay health counsellor: a local person, often a woman with a school or college education, selected for her interpersonal skills, trained for a few weeks and closely supervised.

Key study: the MANAS trial in Goa (2010)

Could lay counsellors improve the outcome of common mental disorders (depression and anxiety) in ordinary primary care? Patel and his colleagues added a lay health counsellor to 12 of 24 primary care clinics in Goa, half of them government health centres and half private general practices; the other 12 gave enhanced usual care, and 2,796 adult patients with common mental disorders took part. (MANAS, from the Konkani for "project to promote mental health", also means "mind".) The counsellors screened patients, gave psychoeducation, delivered a brief psychological treatment based on interpersonal psychotherapy, supported adherence to antidepressants prescribed by the clinic doctor and referred severe cases to a visiting specialist, a model of collaborative stepped care. In the government health centres the results were striking: at six months about two thirds of the intervention patients had recovered, against about two fifths of those in enhanced usual care, and suicide attempts and plans were also reduced. In the private clinics there was no benefit, probably because their patients did well even with usual care. The conclusion was that lay counsellors could deliver effective care where public services are thinnest; the criticism was that the treatment was a package, so it was hard to know which ingredient did the work.

The next step was to test specific, manualised psychological treatments. In the PREMIUM programme Sangath developed two such treatments and tested them in Goa, with results published in 2017. The Healthy Activity Program, a brief form of behavioural activation (Chapter 4) delivered by lay counsellors in six to eight sessions, produced significantly more remission from moderately severe to severe depression than enhanced usual care. Counselling for Alcohol Problems, a brief treatment built on motivational interviewing (Chapter 6), was tested in men with harmful drinking and also improved outcomes. The PRIDE programme (PRemIum for aDolEscents) carried the approach into secondary schools in New Delhi and Goa. Its first step, a brief problem-solving intervention delivered by lay counsellors with the help of printed booklets, reduced the severity of psychosocial problems among distressed students in a randomised trial published in 2020. Patel summed up the lessons of this work in the acronym SUNDAR (Hindi for "beautiful"): Simplify the message (avoid jargon), UNpack the treatment into simple components, Deliver it where people are (at home, in the community), use Affordable and available human resources (lay health workers), and Reallocate specialists to training and supervision.

Key study: the Friendship Bench in Zimbabwe (2016)

In the early 2000s Dixon Chibanda was one of only a handful of psychiatrists in Zimbabwe, a country of more than twelve million people. After one of his patients, who could not afford the bus fare to his hospital, took her own life, he began to look for a way to bring help to people where they lived. In 2006 he trained older women who already worked as community health volunteers in Harare (the "grandmothers") to deliver a simple form of problem-solving therapy, on wooden benches placed in the grounds of primary care clinics. The therapy used Shona ideas: people describe kufungisisa, "thinking too much", and the sessions move through kuvhura pfungwa (opening up the mind), kusimudzira (uplifting) and kusimbisa (strengthening). In a cluster randomised trial in 24 clinics, published in JAMA in 2016, 573 adults with common mental disorders received either six sessions on the Friendship Bench or enhanced usual care. After six months the Friendship Bench group had markedly fewer symptoms of common mental disorders, including depression. The model has since spread to other countries, and it is the best-known example of how lay workers, embedded in their own culture and supervised by professionals, can close part of the treatment gap.

A third landmark came from Pakistan. In the Thinking Healthy Programme, Atif Rahman and his colleagues (2008) trained lady health workers, the government's village-based community health workers, to deliver a cognitive behavioural intervention for depressed pregnant women and new mothers in rural Rawalpindi, woven into their routine home visits. Six months after the birth, 23 percent of the mothers in the intervention group were depressed, against 53 percent in the control group. WHO later adopted the programme as one of its scalable psychological interventions.

Why do these programmes work, and where do they fail? The successful ones share several ingredients. The counsellors are carefully selected for warmth and good judgement, not just qualifications. The treatments are short, structured and built on a few common elements of evidence-based therapy (psychoeducation, behavioural activation, problem solving, motivational interviewing). Training is competence-based, and supervision, usually in peer groups led by a specialist, never stops. There is a clear stepped-care pathway, so that people who do not improve, or who are at risk of suicide, are referred up to specialists. The difficulties are equally clear: supervision is expensive and easily cut when money is short, counsellors leave, quality drifts when a trial becomes a routine programme, and lay counsellors cannot manage severe or complicated illness. Task-sharing extends specialist care; it does not replace it.

Indian context

India already has a huge non-specialist health workforce. The Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs), village women appointed under the National Rural Health Mission (2005), are increasingly trained to identify common and severe mental disorders and to refer and follow up patients, and the Health and Wellness Centres of the Ayushman Bharat scheme (2018) include mental health in the services they provide. Community programmes such as Atmiyata in Gujarat's Mehsana district train village volunteers to offer basic counselling and to link distressed people to welfare benefits and to care. Tele-MANAS, the national tele-mental health helpline launched in 2022, adds a telephone layer to the same stepped system (Section 9.7).

Stigma and anti-stigma work

Imagine two job applicants with the same qualifications. One mentions that he had typhoid last year; the other mentions that he spent a month in hospital with depression. Most people know at once which one is less likely to get the job, and that knowledge is the subject of this subsection. Goffman, whose Asylums you met in Section 9.6.1, followed it with Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity (1963). Stigma, in Goffman's words, is "an attribute that is deeply discrediting", which reduces the bearer "from a whole and usual person to a tainted, discounted one". He described three kinds: abominations of the body (physical deformities), blemishes of individual character (among which he listed mental disorder, imprisonment, addiction and unemployment) and tribal stigma (of race, nation or religion). He also noticed that stigma spreads to the people around the stigmatised person, which he called courtesy stigma and which is now often called associative or family stigma. In India it is the reason a family may hide a son's schizophrenia for fear that his sisters will not find husbands.

Later researchers took stigma apart. The sociologists Bruce G. Link and Jo C. Phelan (2001) described it as the coming together of several components: people label a human difference ("mentally ill"); the label is linked to negative stereotypes ("dangerous", "unpredictable", "weak"); the labelled people are separated into a "them" distinct from "us"; they suffer status loss and discrimination; and the whole process depends on unequal power, because only groups with social, economic and political power can make their labels stick. The British psychiatrist Graham Thornicroft offered a simpler three-part scheme that is useful for planning interventions: stigma involves problems of knowledge (ignorance), problems of attitudes (prejudice) and problems of behaviour (discrimination). Each needs a different remedy: information for ignorance, contact and persuasion for prejudice, and law and policy for discrimination.

The American psychologist Patrick W. Corrigan distinguished the levels at which stigma operates:

  • Public stigma is the prejudice and discrimination of the general population towards people with mental illness: the landlord who will not rent, the employer who will not hire, the neighbour who keeps her children away.
  • Self-stigma occurs when people with mental illness turn the public stereotypes on themselves. Corrigan described it as a progression: the person becomes aware of the stereotype, agrees with it, applies it to himself ("I am mentally ill, so I must be weak and incompetent") and suffers the harm of lowered self-esteem and self-efficacy. The result is what Corrigan called the "why try" effect: "Why try to get a job? Someone like me could not hold one."
  • Structural stigma lies in the rules, policies and practices of institutions: lower spending on mental than on physical health, insurance that excludes mental illness, laws that bar people with mental illness from certain roles.

Stigma matters clinically because it keeps people away from care. Label avoidance, the decision not to seek treatment so as not to be labelled, is one of the main reasons for the treatment gap described in Section 9.6.5. In many Indian families the first help sought is from a faith healer or a temple, partly because such help carries no psychiatric label, and illnesses are often described in bodily terms ("weakness", "tension", "gas") that are less shameful than "mental".

What reduces stigma? Corrigan and David Penn (1999) grouped anti-stigma strategies into three kinds (Table 9.9). Protest objects to stigmatising images, for example in films and newspapers. It can stop a particular offensive advertisement, but as a way of changing attitudes it tends to fail and may even backfire, because telling people not to think something can make the thought stronger. Education replaces myths with facts (most people with mental illness are not violent; mental illnesses are common and treatable). Contact brings members of the public into direct interaction with people who have lived experience of mental illness, ideally as equals, telling their own stories of illness and recovery. In a meta-analysis of outcome studies from many countries, Corrigan and his colleagues (2012) found that both education and contact reduced stigma, but that contact was the more effective for adults, whereas education did better with adolescents. Social psychology explains why contact works: the contact hypothesis (taught in the Social book) predicts that personal acquaintance under favourable conditions breaks down stereotypes better than any amount of information.

In India the Live Love Laugh Foundation, set up by the actor Deepika Padukone in 2015 after she spoke publicly about her own depression, is the best-known effort, and the public disclosures of well-known people have done much to make talking about mental illness acceptable. Several other measures help: responsible media reporting (using "a person with schizophrenia" rather than "a schizophrenic", and never using psychiatric words as insults), training for health workers, police and teachers, and legal protection against discrimination, such as the rights to equality and confidentiality in the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (Chapter 1). 9.7 Crisis InterventionMedium yield · 3 Qs

It is two in the morning in a college hostel in Mysuru. Arjun, a twenty-year-old engineering student, has just seen his results: he has failed three of his six papers. A week ago the girl he hoped to marry told him it was over. His father, a bus conductor who borrowed money to pay the fees, telephones every evening to ask how the studies are going. Arjun has not slept properly for days, he has told nobody, and tonight he has sent his closest friend a message that says only "I can't do this any more. Sorry for everything." Nothing in his history suggests a psychiatric disorder; until a month ago he was a cheerful, ordinary student. What he is in now is a crisis, and what happens in the next few hours and weeks may decide whether he comes out of it stronger, the same, or not at all. This section is about what a crisis is and what helpers can do in one, from a single conversation with a frightened student to the care of a whole town after a disaster.

What a crisis is

Crisis theory began with the observations described in this chapter's opening story. Lindemann (1944) noticed that the survivors and the bereaved of the Cocoanut Grove fire went through a recognisable sequence of reactions, that most came through it within weeks, and that those who were going wrong could be helped by a few well-timed conversations. Caplan, working with Lindemann on the Wellesley project and at Harvard, turned these observations into crisis theory. He started from the idea of homeostasis. Each of us normally lives in a state of emotional equilibrium, kept steady by habitual ways of solving problems. In his best-known definition (1961), "people are in a state of crisis when they face an obstacle to important life goals that is, for a time, insurmountable through the utilization of customary methods of problem solving". A period of disorganisation and upset follows, in which the person tries many solutions that do not work, and eventually some kind of adaptation is reached, which may or may not be in the person's best interests.

Four features of a crisis follow from this definition, and they shape everything that crisis workers do:

  • A crisis is the person's reaction to an event, not the event itself. The same failed examination is a disappointment for one student and a crisis for another; what decides the matter is how the event is perceived and what coping resources are available.
  • A crisis is not an illness. Healthy, well-adjusted people have crises, and the reactions in a crisis (anxiety, confusion, sleeplessness, tearfulness, a narrowing of attention onto the problem) are normal responses to an abnormal load.
  • A crisis is self-limiting. Caplan observed that the acute state usually resolves within about four to six weeks, one way or another, because nobody can stay in such disequilibrium for long.
  • A crisis is a turning point. The person may emerge with better coping than before, with the same coping, or with worse: a lasting disorder, a damaging habit such as heavy drinking, a broken relationship or a suicide attempt. And because old habits have failed and defences are loosened, people in crisis are unusually open to help. The social worker Lydia Rapoport put this in a sentence that crisis workers still learn: "a little help, rationally directed and purposefully focused at a strategic time, is more effective than more extensive help given at a period of less emotional accessibility".

Caplan (1964) described four phases through which a crisis develops. In the first, the stressor raises the person's tension, and he tries his habitual ways of solving problems. In the second, those ways fail; tension rises further, and the person feels upset, ineffective and disorganised, and tries one thing after another. In the third, the rising tension mobilises emergency resources: the person may find a new way of solving the problem, redefine it, ask for help or give up an unattainable goal, and many crises are resolved here. If they are not, the fourth phase arrives: tension reaches a breaking point and there is major disorganisation, with severe distress and sometimes a psychiatric disorder or self-harm. Arjun, sending his message at two in the morning, is somewhere between the third and fourth phases.

Why does one person tip into crisis when another, facing the same event, does not? The American nurses Donna C. Aguilera and Janice M. Messick, in their textbook Crisis Intervention: Theory and Methodology, gave the most widely taught answer. A stressful event upsets equilibrium and creates a need to restore it. Whether equilibrium is restored depends on three balancing factors: a realistic perception of the event, adequate situational support (people who can be relied on to help) and adequate coping mechanisms (ways of reducing tension that have worked before). If all three are present, the problem is solved and there is no crisis; if one or more is missing, disequilibrium continues and a crisis follows (Figure 9.5). The model tells the crisis worker exactly where to look. Arjun perceives his results as the end of his life (a distorted perception, since supplementary examinations exist), he has told no one (no situational support), and his usual way of coping, working harder, is no longer available. All three balancing factors are missing.

The family sociologist Reuben Hill (1958) had proposed the same logic for families in his ABC-X model: A, the stressor event, interacting with B, the family's resources for meeting it, and C, the meaning the family gives to the event, produces X, the crisis. In both models the event alone never explains the crisis. Textbooks often add that the Chinese word for crisis combines the characters for "danger" and "opportunity"; linguists point out that this is a popular misreading of the second character, but the idea it is used to illustrate, that a crisis is a turning point and not only a catastrophe, is sound.

Three neighbouring terms need to be kept apart. A psychiatric emergency is a situation that demands immediate action to prevent death or serious harm: an overdose, a violent patient, a person standing on a bridge. Some crises are emergencies; most are not. Stress is a continuing demand that the person may be coping with, well or badly; a crisis is the acute point at which coping breaks down. A traumatic event involves actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence; it may cause a crisis, but many crises (a failed examination, a broken engagement, a child leaving home) involve no trauma at all. And recall from Section 9.5 that Moos and Tsu (1977) described the diagnosis of a serious physical illness as a crisis in exactly this sense, which is why their adaptive tasks read like a list of balancing factors.

Exam trap

A crisis is defined by the person's perception and coping, not by the size of the event, and it is not a mental illness. The three balancing factors (perception, situational support, coping mechanisms) are Aguilera and Messick's; the ABC-X model of family crisis is Reuben Hill's.

Kinds of crisis

Caplan distinguished two broad kinds. A developmental crisis (also called a maturational or normative crisis) arises at the predictable transitions of the life cycle, when old ways of living no longer fit the new stage: starting school, puberty, leaving home for a hostel, marriage (for many Indian brides, moving into a husband's joint family), the birth of the first child, children leaving home, retirement. Developmental crises are the ones that Erik H. Erikson (1902 to 1994) placed at the heart of his psychosocial stages (Chapter 3). A situational crisis (Caplan's accidental crisis) is triggered by sudden, unexpected and usually unwanted events: a road accident, a sudden death, the loss of a job, a failed examination, a diagnosis of cancer, a rape, a divorce, a house fire. The American counsellors Richard K. James and Burl E. Gilliland, whose textbook Crisis Intervention Strategies is widely used, added two more (Table 9.10).

A finer classification, ordered by how much psychopathology is involved, was proposed by Bruce A. Baldwin in 1978. His six classes run from the least to the most severe:

  1. Dispositional crises: problems of a practical kind, such as a lack of information or of a service, which need information, advice or referral.
  2. Crises of anticipated life transitions: normal, expected changes over which the person may have little control, such as a planned move or retirement.
  3. Crises resulting from traumatic stress: sudden, unexpected events imposed from outside, such as an assault or the sudden death of a spouse.
  4. Maturational or developmental crises: struggles over recurring developmental issues such as dependence, intimacy, identity or power, which keep coming back at new life stages.
  5. Crises reflecting psychopathology: crises in which an existing disorder (a personality disorder, a psychosis) has precipitated the crisis or greatly complicates its resolution.
  6. Psychiatric emergencies: crises in which general functioning is severely impaired and the person is dangerous to self or others, such as an acute psychosis, a serious suicide attempt or a drug overdose.

A different distinction, common in the crisis literature and in examination questions, concerns the scale of the precipitating event. Large-scale events affect whole communities at once: natural disasters (floods, cyclones, earthquakes, tornadoes, tsunamis), industrial disasters such as the Bhopal gas leak of 1984, terrorist attacks, war and epidemics. They call for the community-wide response described in Section 9.7.5. Small-scale events strike one person or one family, though they can be just as devastating for those involved: violent events (child abuse, domestic violence, crime), crises triggered by psychopathology (a suicide attempt, a drug overdose) and personal catastrophes (a road accident, a life-threatening illness). Most of a clinical psychologist's crisis work deals with small-scale events.

A tornado or flood is a large-scale, community-wide event (an ecosystemic crisis), so it is the exception. The goals and steps of crisis intervention

Crisis intervention is brief, active, present-focused help for a person in crisis, aimed at resolving the immediate crisis and restoring at least the level of functioning that existed before it. Aguilera and Messick set out its goals in two tiers. The minimum goal is the psychological resolution of the immediate crisis and a return to the pre-crisis level of functioning. The maximum goal is improvement beyond the pre-crisis level, so that the person leaves the crisis with new coping skills and is better prepared for the next one. Crisis intervention is not a short version of psychotherapy. Its principles are distinctive:

  • Immediacy. Help is given at once, ideally within hours, because the period of openness that Rapoport described is short. Crisis services therefore work by telephone, drop-in and home visit rather than by appointment next month.
  • Brevity. Crisis intervention usually takes one to six contacts within about six weeks, matching Caplan's time course.
  • Focus. The work concentrates on the precipitating event and the present problem ("why now?"), not on personality or childhood.
  • Activity. The worker is active and, when necessary, directive: asking questions, giving information and advice, contacting other people, making arrangements.
  • Safety first. Risk to life is assessed at the start and throughout.
  • Support and self-reliance. The worker mobilises the person's own network and resources, and does things with the person rather than for him wherever possible, so that he regains a sense of control.
  • Hope. The worker conveys a realistic expectation that the crisis will pass and the person will cope. Military psychiatry, which has handled acute combat reactions since the First World War, summarises the same ideas as proximity (help close to where the person is), immediacy and expectancy (the expectation of recovery and return).

Which kind of therapy best fits this work? Insight-oriented therapies that explore defences and the past, and structured therapies that need weeks of homework, are poorly suited to the acute phase. What a person in crisis needs first is support: the techniques of supportive psychotherapy (taught in Section 3.10). The worker allows ventilation of feelings, offers realistic reassurance, gives information and guidance, uses environmental manipulation (arranging leave from college, a safe place to stay, a call to the family), strengthens the person's existing defences and coping rather than challenging them, and helps with practical problem solving. Cognitive behavioural techniques have a place once the person is stable, for example to challenge "I am a complete failure", and longer therapy may follow if a disorder emerges. The same is true of acceptance and commitment therapy (Chapter 5), which builds psychological flexibility over many sessions, and of the expressive therapies (art, music, dance and movement, and drama therapy), which use creative activity as a channel for feelings that are hard to put into words: both can help in the weeks after a crisis, but neither is the first response to it. But in the crisis itself, support comes first.

Because it is built for a short, normal reaction to an identifiable stressor, crisis intervention is the classic treatment of choice for adjustment disorders, which by definition arise within three months of a stressor and usually settle within six months once the stressor or its consequences end (the diagnosis is described in the Psychiatric Disorders book). It is also used for acute stress reactions, for people who have just attempted suicide, for victims of violence and for families facing a sudden illness or death. It is delivered in many settings: telephone helplines, walk-in crisis centres, emergency departments, mobile crisis teams that visit people at home, the crisis resolution and home treatment teams that offer an alternative to admission in Britain, and crisis houses where a person can stay for a few days.

Suicide prevention

Every year, according to the World Health Organization, more than 700,000 people die by suicide, and for every death there are many more attempts. In India the National Crime Records Bureau recorded 1,70,924 suicides in 2022 and 1,71,418 in 2023, the highest numbers in its records, and suicide is among the leading causes of death of young Indians. How to assess an individual's risk (the risk and protective factors, the SAD PERSONS mnemonic and the reasons why no checklist predicts suicide well) is taught in Section 2.7. This subsection is about prevention, both for the individual in front of you and for the population.

Organised suicide prevention began with two initiatives of the 1950s. In London in 1953 the Anglican priest Chad Varah (1911 to 2007) advertised a telephone number that people thinking of suicide could ring, and so founded the Samaritans, whose trained volunteers offer confidential, non-judgemental listening ("befriending"). In 1958 the psychologists Edwin S. Shneidman (1918 to 2009) and Norman L. Farberow founded the Los Angeles Suicide Prevention Center, the model for crisis centres across the United States. Shneidman went on to found the field he called suicidology, developed, with his colleagues, the psychological autopsy (a reconstruction of the state of mind of a person who has died by suicide, from interviews with relatives and records) and described the central experience of the suicidal person as "psychache", unbearable psychological pain. In India, Lakshmi Vijayakumar founded Sneha in Chennai in 1986, a volunteer befriending service on the Samaritans model.

The levels of prevention from Section 9.6.2 organise the modern evidence neatly. The WHO's guide to national suicide prevention, LIVE LIFE (2021), recommends four key interventions: limiting access to the means of suicide, working with the media for responsible reporting, fostering socio-emotional life skills in adolescents, and early identification, assessment, management and follow-up of anyone affected by suicidal behaviour. The first three are universal; the last is selective and indicated.

Means restriction. The most consistently effective single measure is means restriction: reducing access to the methods people use to kill themselves. It works because many suicidal crises are brief and the person is ambivalent; if the most lethal method is not at hand during the worst hours, the crisis often passes, and most people do not simply substitute another method. The classic evidence comes from Britain. Until the late 1950s, domestic cooking gas, made from coal, contained a high proportion of carbon monoxide, and putting one's head in the gas oven was the commonest method of suicide. As coal gas was replaced by non-toxic natural gas during the 1960s, the psychiatrist Norman Kreitman showed in 1976, suicides by gas almost disappeared and the total suicide rate in England and Wales fell by about a third, with no matching rise in other methods. Sri Lanka, where pesticide poisoning was the commonest method, banned the most toxic pesticides in the 1990s; its suicide rate roughly halved over the following decade. In India, where pesticides and hanging account for much of the toll, the equivalents are removing the most hazardous pesticides from sale, locked storage of pesticides on farms, smaller pack sizes for dangerous medicines and barriers at bridges, railway lines and tall buildings.

Key study: Seiden's Golden Gate Bridge follow-up (1978)

A common objection to means restriction is that a person determined to die will find another way. The psychologist Richard H. Seiden tested this with the most dramatic means available. San Francisco's Golden Gate Bridge was then described as the world's most popular site for suicide, and between 1937 and 1971 police, bridge workers and passers-by had restrained 515 people who were about to jump. Seiden traced what had happened to them, through death records, on average more than two decades later. If the determination hypothesis were true, most would have gone on to kill themselves somewhere else. In fact about 94 percent were still alive or had died of natural causes; only about 5 percent had gone on to die by suicide. The conclusion is that suicidal behaviour is often crisis-bound: get a person through the crisis, and the wish to die frequently passes. The study is still the standard answer to the objection, and it helped the argument for building a safety net under the bridge, a project finally completed in the 2020s.

The media. In 1974 the sociologist David P. Phillips showed that suicides in the United States and Britain rose in the month after a suicide was reported on the front pages of newspapers, and rose more the more publicity it received. He named this imitative effect the Werther effect, after the hero of Goethe's novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), which was blamed for a wave of copycat suicides among young men in Europe. The opposite has also been shown: stories of people who were suicidal but found a way through their crisis appear to reduce suicides, the Papageno effect (named by Thomas Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues in 2010, after the character in Mozart's The Magic Flute who is talked out of suicide). The WHO's guidelines for journalists, adopted in India by the Press Council, follow directly: do not describe the method or the location in detail, do not use sensational headlines or present suicide as a solution to a problem, do not repeat a story prominently, and do include information about where to get help.

Schools and gatekeepers. School curricula about suicide aimed at all students have a mixed record, but well-designed programmes that teach young people about mental health and coping can work: in a large European trial (2015), schools that ran the Youth Aware of Mental Health programme had about half as many new suicide attempts over the following year as control schools. A related selective strategy is gatekeeper training: teaching people who are in regular contact with those at risk (teachers, hostel wardens, police, pharmacists, priests, ASHAs) to recognise warning signs, ask about suicide directly and help the person to care. One widely used format, QPR, devised by the American psychologist Paul Quinnett in 1995, teaches three steps: Question, Persuade, Refer. Gatekeeper training works best as part of a whole-system effort. The United States Air Force programme, launched in the late 1990s, combined training of commanders and staff, reduced stigma for seeking help and changed policies, and was followed by a reduction of about a third in suicides. A systematic review of suicide prevention strategies by John Mann and colleagues (2005) concluded that the two approaches with the clearest evidence were training doctors to recognise and treat depression, and means restriction.

Helplines. Telephone and online crisis lines are the most visible form of suicide prevention. They are hard to evaluate, because callers are anonymous, but studies of callers in the United States have found that their distress and suicidal feelings fall during the course of the call. Barlow and Durand add an important caution: volunteers on helplines must be backed up by mental health professionals who can respond to callers at serious risk. India's national helpline, Tele-MANAS (14416), described in the Indian context box below, is designed to do exactly that by linking callers to counsellors and, when needed, to specialist services.

People who have already attempted suicide. A previous suicide attempt is the strongest single predictor of eventual suicide, and the weeks after an attempt, and after discharge from a psychiatric admission, are the period of highest risk. This is where indicated prevention matters most. A study by Jason Luoma and colleagues (2002) found that about 45 percent of people who died by suicide had been in contact with a primary care doctor in the month before their death, so these contacts are opportunities that are often missed. The interventions with good evidence are these:

  • The safety planning intervention of Barbara Stanley and Gregory K. Brown (2012), a brief collaborative exercise, often completed in a single session, in which the clinician and the patient write a personalised, step-by-step plan for the patient to follow when suicidal feelings return (Table 9.12). In a large study in the emergency departments of United States veterans' hospitals (Stanley and colleagues, 2018), patients who received a safety plan plus follow-up telephone calls showed substantially less suicidal behaviour over the next six months than those who received usual care (about 3 percent against about 5 percent, a reduction of nearly half).
  • Brief contact and follow-up. In a famous study the psychiatrist Jerome A. Motto sent short, personal, caring letters several times a year for five years to people who had been admitted to hospital in a depressed or suicidal state and had then refused further treatment; in the first two years after discharge they had a significantly lower suicide rate than a comparable group who received no letters. These caring contacts cost almost nothing.
  • Specific psychological treatments. Ten sessions of cognitive therapy for recent suicide attempters, developed by Gregory Brown, Aaron Beck and their colleagues (2005), cut the rate of repeat attempts by about half over the following eighteen months. Dialectical behaviour therapy (Chapter 5) reduces self-harm in people with borderline personality disorder. Lithium in mood disorders and clozapine in schizophrenia also reduce suicide (the Psychiatric Disorders book).

Remember

Four myths about suicide. (1) "Asking about suicide puts the idea into someone's head": it does not, and many people feel relief at being asked (Section 2.2). (2) "People who talk about suicide do not do it": most people who die by suicide have given some warning. (3) "Someone who has decided will not be stopped": most suicidal people are ambivalent, and most who survive a crisis do not later die by suicide (Seiden). (4) "When a severely depressed person suddenly improves, the danger is over": risk can rise as energy returns, and it is highest in the weeks after discharge from hospital.

Psychological first aid and disaster mental health

When a cyclone, an earthquake or a flood strikes, thousands of people are in crisis at once, and the methods of the counselling room cannot reach them. The first thing to understand about disasters is that most of the reactions that follow them are normal. Fear, grief, sleeplessness, irritability, startle and intrusive memories are common in the first days and weeks, and for most people they fade with time, safety and the support of family and community. As Section 9.5 showed for bereavement, resilience is the usual outcome; only a minority go on to develop post-traumatic stress disorder, depression or problem drinking. Disaster workers often describe a typical sequence in a community's response: a heroic phase of rescue in the first hours and days, a "honeymoon" phase of solidarity and outside help, a phase of disillusionment when the help is withdrawn and the bureaucracy of compensation begins, and a long phase of reconstruction. Psychological needs change with each.

The Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC), which coordinates the humanitarian agencies of the United Nations and others, set out the now-standard framework in its Guidelines on Mental Health and Psychosocial Support in Emergency Settings (2007). Support is arranged as a pyramid of four layers (Figure 9.6). Everyone needs the bottom layer, and progressively fewer people need each layer above it. The pyramid carries a lesson that surprises many psychologists: the most important mental health interventions after a disaster are not psychological at all. Safety, shelter, food, water, information about missing relatives, and the chance to carry out funeral rites and to resume daily routines do more for most people's mental health than any counsellor.

In the hours and days after a disaster, the recommended psychological response for distressed people is psychological first aid (PFA). The WHO, the War Trauma Foundation and World Vision International published Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers in 2011, defining PFA as a humane, supportive response to a fellow human being who is suffering and who may need support. Its action principles are usually summarised as look, listen and link, preceded by prepare:

  • Prepare: learn about the crisis event, the services and supports available, and the safety and security concerns before going in.
  • Look: check for safety; check for people with obvious urgent basic needs (the injured, the lost child); check for people with serious distress reactions.
  • Listen: approach people who may need support; ask about their needs and concerns; listen to them and help them feel calm.
  • Link: help people meet basic needs and reach services; help them cope with practical problems; give accurate information; connect them with loved ones and social support.

The guide is just as clear about what PFA is not. It is not something only professionals can do; it is not professional counselling; it is not psychological debriefing; it does not require a detailed discussion of the event; and it does not involve pressing people to analyse what happened or to talk about their feelings if they do not wish to. A volunteer from the village, a teacher or a police officer can learn PFA in a day. Its principles rest on a review by Stevan E. Hobfoll and a panel of trauma experts (2007), who identified five essential elements of immediate and mid-term intervention after mass trauma, each supported by research: promoting a sense of safety, calming, a sense of self-efficacy and community efficacy, connectedness and hope. An American version, the Psychological First Aid Field Operations Guide (2006) of the National Child Traumatic Stress Network and the National Center for PTSD, sets out eight core actions in the same spirit: contact and engagement, safety and comfort, stabilisation, information gathering on current needs, practical assistance, connection with social supports, information on coping, and linkage with collaborative services.

Psychological debriefing: a cautionary tale

For about twenty years from the early 1980s, the standard response to a traumatic event in much of the Western world was psychological debriefing. Its best-known form, critical incident stress debriefing (CISD), was developed by Jeffrey T. Mitchell, a former firefighter and paramedic, and described in 1983. It was designed for emergency workers (firefighters, ambulance crews, police) after a distressing incident: a single structured group meeting, usually held within a few days of the event and lasting a few hours, led by a mental health professional with trained peers. It moves through seven phases: introduction (purpose, rules and confidentiality), fact (what happened, from each person's point of view), thought (each person's first thoughts during the incident), reaction (the worst part of it for them, and the feelings it aroused), symptom (the physical and psychological reactions they have had since), teaching (education about normal stress reactions and ways of coping) and re-entry (questions, summing up and referral). CISD later became one part of a wider package, critical incident stress management (CISM), developed by Mitchell and George Everly, which also includes training before incidents, brief "defusing" sessions, individual crisis support, family support and follow-up.

The logic seemed obvious: talk about the event early, express the emotions, learn that your reactions are normal, and you will be less likely to develop post-traumatic stress disorder. Debriefing spread from emergency services to the survivors of accidents, robberies, shootings and disasters, often offered to individuals who had been directly harmed, and in some organisations attendance was compulsory. Participants usually said they found it helpful. Then the randomised trials arrived.

Key study: Bisson's debriefing trial with burn victims (1997)

Jonathan I. Bisson and his colleagues in Cardiff randomly assigned adults admitted to hospital with burns either to a single debriefing session, held individually or with a partner within about the first three weeks after the injury, or to no intervention. They then followed them up. At three months the two groups did not differ. At thirteen months, 26 percent of the debriefed patients had post-traumatic stress disorder, against 9 percent of those who had not been debriefed. Critics pointed out that the groups were not perfectly matched at the start, so the size of the harm is uncertain. But the trial showed, at the very least, that debriefing had not prevented PTSD, and it raised the possibility that it could make things worse.

Other trials told the same story. In a study of road accident survivors in Oxford, Richard Mayou, Anke Ehlers and Mark Hobbs (2000) found that three years later the patients who had been debriefed had a worse outcome than those who had not, especially those who had been most distressed at the start. A Cochrane systematic review by Suzanna Rose, Jonathan Bisson, Rachel Churchill and Simon Wessely (2002) concluded that single-session individual debriefing did not reduce distress or prevent PTSD, and that compulsory debriefing of trauma victims should cease. A meta-analysis by Arnold van Emmerik and his colleagues in The Lancet in the same year found that CISD did not improve on natural recovery. Guidelines followed. Britain's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE, 2005, reaffirmed in 2018) advised against offering psychologically focused debriefing to prevent or treat PTSD, and the WHO (2013) recommended that psychological debriefing should not be used for people exposed to a traumatic event in the previous month.

Why might a procedure that feels helpful fail, or even harm? Several explanations have been offered. Debriefing asks people to relive the most frightening moments of an event in vivid detail while they are still highly aroused, which may strengthen the traumatic memory; but it does so in a single session, far too short for the habituation and reprocessing that real exposure therapy provides over many sessions. It may teach people to expect symptoms and to read normal reactions as signs of disorder. It may interfere with natural recovery, replacing the support of family, friends and colleagues with a single professional encounter. And because it is offered to everyone, though most people recover without help, it cannot improve on natural recovery for the majority, and it may disrupt it for some. The story is a lesson in why clinical psychology insists on controlled trials: an intervention that was plausible, popular and sincerely delivered turned out not to work.

What should be done instead? In the first days, psychological first aid and practical help. Then watchful waiting, now usually called active monitoring: keeping in touch with people who were exposed, and checking again after about a month, because most reactions fade by then. Those who still have marked symptoms, or who develop acute stress disorder or PTSD, are offered trauma-focused psychological treatment, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy or eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), over several sessions (Chapters 4 and 5). Such targeted early treatment works. Barlow and Durand describe a trial by Ehlers and her colleagues (2003) of people who had been in frightening road accidents and were clearly at risk: after up to twelve weekly sessions of cognitive therapy only 11 percent developed PTSD, compared with 61 percent of those given a detailed self-help booklet and 55 percent of those who were simply assessed repeatedly. The difference between debriefing and these treatments is not that one talks about the trauma and the others do not; it is who receives the help (everyone, or only those who need it), when, and how much. Trauma-informed care

Debriefing taught clinicians to be careful about treating trauma too eagerly. A different line of research taught them that they were usually not thinking about trauma enough. The turning point was a study that began in an obesity clinic.

Key study: the Adverse Childhood Experiences study (1998)

In the 1980s Vincent J. Felitti, a physician running a weight-loss programme for the health organisation Kaiser Permanente in San Diego, was puzzled that some of his most successful patients dropped out just as they were losing weight fastest. Interviewing them, he found that many had been sexually abused as children, and that their weight protected them in ways they could not give up. With Robert F. Anda of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, he designed a much larger study. Adult members of the health plan who came for routine medical examinations in the mid-1990s were asked about seven categories of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs): psychological, physical and sexual abuse, and growing up with a household member who misused alcohol or drugs, was mentally ill, was imprisoned, or treated the mother violently. The first report, in 1998, covered 9,508 respondents. More than half reported at least one category, and about one in sixteen reported four or more. The relationship with adult health was graded, like a dose and its response: compared with people who had none, those with four or more categories had four to twelve times the risk of alcoholism, drug abuse, depression and suicide attempts, and higher rates of smoking, severe obesity, heart disease, lung disease and cancer. The criticisms are that the sample was mostly white, middle-class and insured, and that the childhood experiences were reported retrospectively. Even so, the study showed that childhood adversity is common, cumulative and one of the most powerful influences on adult health.

If a large share of the people who come to health and social services have histories of trauma, the services themselves need to change. Trauma-informed care is an approach to organising a whole service, not a particular therapy, on the assumption that many of its clients (and staff) have experienced trauma. Its pioneers in mental health, Maxine Harris and Roger D. Fallot (2001), argued that services designed without this awareness often re-enact the dynamics of the original abuse: a woman who was held down as a child is held down again in physical restraint; a man who was powerless is again given no say. The question at the heart of trauma-informed care shifts from "What is wrong with you?" to "What happened to you?"

The most widely used definition comes from the United States Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA, 2014). SAMHSA describes trauma in terms of three Es (an event or circumstances, the individual's experience of it, and its lasting adverse effects) and says that a trauma-informed programme follows four Rs: it realises the widespread impact of trauma and understands paths to recovery; it recognises the signs and symptoms of trauma in clients, families and staff; it responds by fully integrating knowledge about trauma into its policies, procedures and practices; and it seeks actively to resist re-traumatisation. SAMHSA lists six key principles:

  1. Safety: physical and emotional safety for clients and staff, in the setting and in every interaction.
  2. Trustworthiness and transparency: decisions are explained and made openly, and promises are kept.
  3. Peer support: people with their own lived experience of trauma are part of the service.
  4. Collaboration and mutuality: power differences between staff and clients are reduced, and decisions are shared.
  5. Empowerment, voice and choice: clients' strengths are built on and they are given real choices.
  6. Cultural, historical and gender issues: the service recognises and responds to cultural, historical and gender-based trauma and avoids stereotypes.

In practice, trauma-informed care changes many small things. Staff ask about trauma routinely and sensitively, but do not press for details. Procedures that involve touch, undressing or restraint are explained before they happen and kept to a minimum; women who have been sexually assaulted can ask to be examined by a woman. Clients are given choices wherever possible, routines are predictable, and seclusion and restraint are reduced. In India, the Mental Healthcare Act 2017's ban on chaining and its strict limits on physical restraint, the child-friendly procedures of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act 2012 and the One Stop Centres for women affected by violence (from 2015) all move in this direction. The approach also cares for staff, because people who work every day with traumatised clients are at risk of secondary traumatic stress and burnout. It is important to keep two terms apart. Trauma-informed care is a framework for a whole organisation and applies to every client, whatever their diagnosis. Trauma-focused (or trauma-specific) treatment, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, prolonged exposure or EMDR, is a therapy for individuals who have a trauma-related disorder. A good service needs both.

9.8 Psychosocial Rehabilitation of Severe Mental IllnessMedium yield · 2 Qs

Mr Prakash is 34. He lives with his parents in a small house in Hassan, and he was first admitted to hospital with schizophrenia at 22, in the final year of an engineering diploma. For the past three years a monthly depot injection has kept the voices quiet and the frightening ideas away. By every psychiatric measure he is better. Yet he has not worked for twelve years. He spends most of the day on a mat in front of the television, his school friends have married and moved away, his mother handles his money, and when relatives visit he stays in the back room. His father, recently retired, asks the question that families everywhere ask: "The voices have stopped. Why is he still like this?"

The honest answer is that medicine treats illness, and what Mr Prakash now lives with is mostly something else: a disability. Twelve years of illness have taken away his skills, his confidence, his friendships, his place in the family and his place in the town, and the injection cannot give those back. Helping people like him rebuild a life, in the community rather than in a hospital, is the work of psychosocial rehabilitation. It grew up alongside the community mental health movement (Section 9.6), which emptied the large mental hospitals and then had to answer the obvious next question: where would the people who left them live, what would they do all day, and who would help them?

From symptoms to functioning: what rehabilitation is

Rehabilitation is meant for people whose illness is long-lasting and disabling, a group described as having severe mental illness (older writing says "chronic mental illness" or "serious and persistent mental illness"). Service planners have defined the group, since the early 1980s, by three Ds: a diagnosis of a major mental disorder (most often schizophrenia or another psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe recurrent depression), a long duration of illness or treatment, usually two years or more, and significant disability in everyday life. The disorders themselves are described in the Psychiatric Disorders book. What matters here is the third D. Two people with the same diagnosis and the same symptom score can live very different lives, and it is the life, not the symptom score, that rehabilitation sets out to change.

The clearest statement of the idea comes from the American psychologist William A. Anthony and his colleagues at the Center for Psychiatric Rehabilitation, which he founded at Boston University in 1979. Psychiatric rehabilitation (today usually called psychosocial rehabilitation; the two terms mean the same thing) aims, in their definition, to help people with psychiatric disabilities increase their ability to function, so that they are successful and satisfied in the environment of their choice, with the least amount of ongoing professional help. Read that slowly and every phrase carries weight. The target is functioning, not symptoms. Success is judged in a particular environment (a hostel, a job, a family home, a college) that the person chooses. And the professional's aim is to become steadily less necessary.

Anthony's group pointed out that there are only two ways to improve a person's functioning in an environment. The first is to develop the person's skills: teach Mr Prakash to travel alone by bus, to cook a simple meal, to keep a conversation going, to manage his own money. The second is to develop environmental supports: change the setting so that the person can succeed with the skills he already has. His mother might be helped to give him his own pocket money and let him make small mistakes with it; an employer might allow him a later start because his medicine makes him slow in the morning; a neighbour might be asked to take him along to the temple committee. Every rehabilitation plan is some mixture of the two. To build such a plan, the clinician starts not with a diagnostic assessment (what is wrong with him?) but with a functional assessment: what does this person want to do, in which setting, what can he already do there, and what skills or supports are missing? The principles that follow from this way of thinking are now accepted across the field:

  • The person's own goals set the agenda. A plan built around the clinician's goals ("he should attend the day centre") rarely survives contact with a person who wants something else ("I want to earn money").
  • Hope is a treatment ingredient, not a decoration. People do not work at rebuilding a life they believe is already finished.
  • Skills are taught, and supports arranged, in the real setting where they will be used, because skills learned in a hospital often fail to transfer to a home or a workplace.
  • Rehabilitation builds on strengths (what the person still does well, enjoys and values) rather than cataloguing deficits.
  • The family and the person are partners in planning, and the help continues for as long as it is needed; severe mental illness is rarely a problem that is solved once.

A shared language for describing disability came from the World Health Organization. Its International Classification of Impairments, Disabilities and Handicaps (ICIDH, 1980) separated three levels: an impairment is a loss or abnormality of a psychological or bodily function (hallucinations, poor attention); a disability is the resulting restriction in the ability to perform an activity (being unable to concentrate long enough to read a newspaper); and a handicap is the social disadvantage that follows (losing a job, being left out of family decisions). The scheme was criticised for implying a one-way chain and for locating the whole problem inside the person, so in 2001 the WHO replaced it with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF). The ICF keeps three levels but renames them in neutral terms (Figure 9.7): body functions and structures, where problems are called impairments; activities, where problems are called activity limitations; and participation in life situations, where problems are called participation restrictions. It then adds contextual factors, both environmental (family attitudes, stigma, services, laws, the physical setting) and personal (age, gender, education, coping style), and it draws every arrow in both directions. In the ICF, disability is not something a person has; it is the outcome of an interaction between a health condition and a context.

Push Mr Prakash through Figure 9.7 and the plan almost writes itself. His impairments are residual negative symptoms (little drive, flat expression), slowed thinking and the sedation from his medication. His activity limitations are that he cannot manage money, travel alone or hold a conversation with strangers. His participation restrictions are that he has no job, no friends and no role in family decisions. And his contextual factors pull in both directions: an overprotective but devoted mother, a town where everyone knows he was "in the mental hospital", no rehabilitation service within 40 kilometres, and, on the personal side, a good memory for engineering and a lifelong love of cricket. The psychiatrist works mainly on the impairments; the rehabilitation team works on the other three boxes. In the language of prevention that Section 9.6 introduces, rehabilitation is tertiary prevention: it does not stop a disorder from occurring (primary prevention) or catch it early (secondary prevention), but reduces the disability that an established disorder leaves behind.

Exam trap

Know both WHO schemes. The ICIDH (1980) used impairment, disability and handicap; the ICF (2001) uses impairment (body functions and structures), activity limitation and participation restriction, plus environmental and personal contextual factors. "Handicap" is not an ICF term. Rehabilitation is tertiary prevention, so "providing rehabilitation after injury" is never the answer to a question asking for an example of primary prevention (immunisation, health education). And rehabilitation targets functioning; reducing symptoms with medication is treatment, not rehabilitation.

Recovery as the guiding vision

For most of the twentieth century, psychiatry expected very little of people like Mr Prakash. Emil Kraepelin had defined dementia praecox by its deteriorating course, and the textbooks taught that schizophrenia, once established, led downhill. Rehabilitation was therefore about "maintenance": keeping a chronic patient stable and occupied. Two kinds of evidence overturned that pessimism, and they changed the goal of the whole enterprise.

Key study: the Vermont longitudinal study (1987)

In the late 1950s, 269 of the most severely and chronically ill patients on the back wards of Vermont State Hospital, people whom everyone had given up on, took part in an unusually thorough rehabilitation programme and were then discharged into the community with continuing support: help with housing, work and daily life. Some twenty to twenty-five years later the psychologist Courtenay M. Harding and her colleagues traced almost all of them and interviewed the survivors. The result, published in 1987, astonished the field: between one-half and two-thirds of these "hopeless" patients had improved considerably or recovered, most were living in the community, and many needed no medication at all. A follow-up comparison with matched patients from the neighbouring state of Maine, which had offered ordinary aftercare without the rehabilitation programme, found that the Vermont group were more productive, had fewer symptoms and were better adjusted to community life. The conclusion was twofold: the long-term course of schizophrenia is far more varied and hopeful than Kraepelin believed, and what services do makes a difference to it. The criticism is that the study was not randomised and the diagnoses were made by the standards of the 1950s, so later investigators re-diagnosed the cases by modern criteria; the main finding held.

International research pointed the same way. The World Health Organization's International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (reported in 1973) and its Determinants of Outcome of Severe Mental Disorders study (reported in 1992) found, to general surprise, that people with schizophrenia in the developing countries, including those at the Indian centres in Agra and Chandigarh, tended to have a better course over two to five years than those in Europe and North America. The finding has been argued about ever since, and the explanations offered (close family involvement, less rigid expectations about work, less stigma, a higher share of acute-onset illness) remain debated. But for Indian clinicians it carries a practical message: the family and the community, not just the clinic, are where outcomes are decided.

The second kind of evidence came from the people with the illness themselves. In the 1980s and 1990s, former patients organised, wrote and spoke publicly about their experience. The American psychologist Patricia E. Deegan, who had been diagnosed with schizophrenia as a teenager, wrote in 1988 that recovery is not the same as being cured or returning to the person one was before; it is a process of discovering a new sense of self and purpose within and beyond the limits of the disability. In 1993 Anthony turned the idea into policy. In a paper subtitled "the guiding vision of the mental health service system in the 1990s", he described recovery as a deeply personal, unique process of changing one's attitudes, values, feelings, goals, skills and roles: a way of living a satisfying, hopeful and contributing life even with the limitations caused by illness.

Notice that there are now two meanings of the word. Clinical recovery is the professional's meaning: symptoms remit and functioning returns to a normal range, judged by rating scales. Personal recovery is the person's meaning: living a meaningful life, as judged by the person, whether or not symptoms persist. Mr Prakash may never be clinically recovered and still achieve a great deal of personal recovery. A systematic review led by Mary Leamy and Mike Slade (2011) found that the personal accounts share five processes, remembered as CHIME: Connectedness (relationships and belonging), Hope and optimism about the future, Identity (rebuilding a positive sense of self beyond "patient"), Meaning in life, and Empowerment (personal responsibility, control over one's life, a focus on strengths). Recovery-oriented services try to support all five, which is why the rest of this section keeps returning to choice, work, housing and belonging rather than to symptoms alone.

Exam trap

Recovery does not mean cure. In the recovery model it means living a satisfying, hopeful and contributing life even with continuing symptoms (Anthony, 1993). Distinguish clinical recovery (symptom remission, judged by professionals) from personal recovery (meaning and control, judged by the person). CHIME = connectedness, hope, identity, meaning, empowerment. Patricia Deegan (1988) wrote from lived experience; William Anthony (Boston University) gave the definition used in policy. The Vermont study (Harding, 1987) is the classic evidence that long-term outcome in schizophrenia is often good.

Where to live and where to spend the day

A person leaving a long hospital stay, or a person like Mr Prakash who has never left home, needs two things before anything else: somewhere to live and something to do with the day. Rehabilitation services grew up around those two needs, and they are easiest to understand if you keep them apart.

Start with the day. A day hospital (also called partial hospitalisation) provides active psychiatric treatment during the day, with the patient going home each evening. It is an alternative to inpatient admission for people who are acutely ill but have a safe home, and an early step down after discharge. The first day hospitals appeared in Moscow in the 1930s and in Montreal and London soon after the Second World War. A day care centre is different in purpose: it offers longer-term rehabilitation to people whose acute illness has settled but who are disabled, through a structured daily programme of occupational therapy, social skills groups, recreation, vocational training, psychoeducation and simply the company of others. In India many teaching hospitals, the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS) in Bengaluru among them, run day care centres where people with severe mental illness spend the day in occupational therapy, vocational training and group activities. A third, looser form is the drop-in centre, which people visit when they wish for a cup of tea, company and informal support.

Now the place to live. In the countries that closed their mental hospitals, a range of residential options appeared, differing in how much supervision they provide:

  • A halfway house is a transitional residence, halfway between hospital and independent life. A small group of residents live together with staff on hand, follow house rules, share the cooking and cleaning, and work on daily living skills, with the expectation that they will move on, usually within months to a year or two. Halfway houses are also widely used for people recovering from alcohol and drug dependence (Section 9.9).
  • Long-stay residences, group homes and hostels offer permanent, supervised accommodation for people who are unlikely to manage alone, sometimes with staff present around the clock.
  • In supported housing, the person lives in an ordinary flat or house, alone or with flatmates of their choosing, and the support comes to them through visiting staff, as much or as little as is needed at the time.
  • In family foster care (boarding out), a person with mental illness lives as a lodger with a family who are paid and supported to look after them.

The oldest example of the last option in the world is a small town in Belgium. In Geel, east of Antwerp, people with mental illness have lived as boarders in ordinary family homes for some seven hundred years. The custom began with the pilgrims who came to the shrine of St Dymphna, a seventh-century Irish princess who, according to legend, fled to Geel and was killed there by her father, and who became the patron saint of the mentally ill. When the church could not house all the pilgrims, local families took them in, and the practice became a tradition that continues, on a smaller scale, today. Geel is the standard reply to anyone who claims that people with severe mental illness cannot live among ordinary families.

In the 1970s and 1980s these options were usually arranged as a linear continuum (Figure 9.8): a person was expected to move from hospital to a halfway house to a group home to supported housing and finally to independence, earning each step by showing readiness for it. The same "train then place" logic shaped vocational services. Critics in the 1990s pointed out the flaws. Every move meant leaving friends and staff and starting again; many people stalled on a middle rung for years; and the skills learned in one setting often did not carry over to the next. The newer approach reverses the order: help the person into the setting they want first, and then provide support there. Its boldest form is Housing First, begun by the psychologist Sam Tsemberis at Pathways to Housing in New York in 1992 for homeless people with severe mental illness. Instead of requiring sobriety or treatment before housing, it offers an ordinary flat immediately, with an assertive support team (see below) that the person can use as they choose. Housing First programmes have kept most of their tenants stably housed, a result that the "earn your housing" model rarely achieved.

India's situation is different again, and it is worth being clear about why. Here the great majority of people with severe mental illness live with their families, who provide the housing, the supervision and much of the rehabilitation without any formal service at all. The gaps are at the two ends: people with no family, or whose families have abandoned them, and families who are exhausted. For the first group, India has relied on non-governmental organisations. The Banyan, founded in Chennai in 1993 by Vandana Gopikumar and Vaishnavi Jayakumar to care for homeless women with mental illness found on the streets, went on to develop community-based group homes for people who could not return to their families. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (Chapter 1) now turns such services into legal duties: section 18, the right to access mental healthcare, lists halfway homes, sheltered accommodation and supported accommodation among the services the government must provide, and section 19 gives every person with mental illness the right to community living, that is, the right to live in, be part of and not be segregated from society.

Exam trap

A day hospital gives active treatment by day as an alternative to admission; a day care centre gives longer-term rehabilitation and structured activity. A halfway house is a transitional residence between hospital and independent living (time-limited, with skills training); a sheltered workshop is a place of protected work, not a place to live. Geel (Belgium) is the classic example of family foster care. Housing First (Tsemberis, 1992) gives housing without preconditions; it is the housing version of "place then train".

Work: from sheltered workshops to supported employment

Ask people with severe mental illness what they want, and a large proportion say the same thing: a job. Work gives a day its structure, provides money and therefore choice, brings ordinary social contact, and answers the question that strangers always ask ("What do you do?") with something other than "I am a patient". In surveys most people with severe mental illness say they would like to work, yet only a small minority are in paid employment. Closing that gap is the task of vocational rehabilitation, and its history is a story of a field slowly discovering that its first methods were holding people back.

The traditional approach was "train then place". The person first received a vocational assessment and prevocational training in work habits (punctuality, grooming, following instructions, tolerating a full day), often through occupational therapy. The next step was a sheltered workshop: a protected workplace, run by a hospital or charity, where people with disabilities do simple paid work (making paper bags, envelopes, candles or incense sticks, assembling components on contract) at their own pace, usually for a small piece-rate wage, among others with disabilities. Sheltered workshops give structure, a small income and a sense of contributing, and many Indian hospitals still run them. Their weakness is that they were meant as a step towards open employment and rarely led there: people stayed in them for years.

A middle path was developed by the Clubhouse movement (Section 9.8.7). In transitional employment, the clubhouse negotiates real jobs with ordinary employers, part-time and for a fixed period (typically six to nine months), and places members in them one after another. If a member falls ill, a staff member or another member covers the shift, so the employer is guaranteed that the work gets done. Transitional employment gives people a real job on their record and a chance to test themselves, without the pressure of having to find and keep the job alone.

The decisive shift came with supported employment, which turned the old sequence around into "place then train": help the person find a real job in the open labour market as quickly as possible, and then train and support them on the job. The best-defined version is Individual Placement and Support (IPS), developed in the early 1990s by Deborah R. Becker and Robert E. Drake at Dartmouth in New Hampshire. IPS rests on eight principles:

  1. Zero exclusion: everyone who wants to work is eligible, whatever their diagnosis, symptoms, substance use or work history. Wanting to work is the only criterion.
  2. Competitive employment is the goal: ordinary jobs, at ordinary wages, that anyone could apply for, not jobs set aside for people with disabilities.
  3. Rapid job search: contact with employers starts within about a month, without lengthy prevocational training.
  4. Integration with mental health treatment: the employment specialist is a member of the clinical team, so work and treatment plans fit together.
  5. Attention to the person's preferences about the type of job, the hours and whether to disclose the illness to the employer.
  6. Time-unlimited, individualised support after the person starts work, for as long as it helps.
  7. Benefits counselling, so the person knows how earning will affect any pension or allowance.
  8. Systematic job development: specialists build relationships with local employers, learning what they need.
Key study: IPS across Europe, the EQOLISE trial (2007)

IPS had done well in American trials, but would it work in countries with different labour markets and welfare systems? Tom Burns and his colleagues randomly assigned 312 people with severe mental illness in six European cities, from London to Sofia, either to IPS or to the best vocational rehabilitation service available locally, usually a train-then-place programme. Over 18 months, 55 percent of the IPS group worked for at least one day in an open-market job, against 28 percent of the comparison group; the IPS group also worked more hours, were less likely to drop out of the service and were less likely to be readmitted to hospital. Supported employment did not make people ill; if anything, the reverse. The criticism is that many jobs were short and part-time, and the effect varied with local unemployment. IPS is now the best-evidenced form of vocational rehabilitation for severe mental illness.

Apply this to Mr Prakash. A train-then-place service would start him in the day care centre's candle unit and review his "readiness" in six months. An IPS specialist would ask what work he wants (he says he would like to do something with electronics, as he once studied), help him decide what to tell an employer, and within weeks approach a mobile phone repair shop in Hassan town whose owner needs an assistant for afternoons. The specialist would keep visiting, helping Mr Prakash with the bus journey at first, with the owner's instructions and with his anxiety on a bad day. The same logic underlies supported education, which helps people with severe mental illness enter and stay in college or vocational courses.

Exam trap

Sheltered workshops and prevocational training are train then place; supported employment and IPS are place then train. IPS was developed by Becker and Drake and its signature principles are zero exclusion, competitive employment, rapid job search and time-unlimited support. Transitional employment, with time-limited part-time placements guaranteed by the clubhouse, belongs to the Clubhouse model. Supported employment does not raise relapse rates.

Assertive community treatment

Everything so far assumes that the person will turn up: at the day centre, at the job interview, at the clinic. Many of the most severely ill people do not. They miss appointments, stop their medicine, relapse, are readmitted, discharged, and the cycle starts again, a pattern clinicians call the revolving door. In the early 1970s three clinicians in Wisconsin decided that if these patients would not come to the services, the services would go to them.

Key study: Stein and Test's Training in Community Living (1972 to 1980)

At Mendota State Hospital in Madison, Wisconsin, Leonard I. Stein and Mary Ann Test, working with Arnold Marx, noticed that patients who had learned to manage on the ward lost those skills within weeks of discharge. Their solution was radical: they moved the staff of an inpatient ward out into the community. In the trial they reported in 1980, 130 patients who were seeking admission were randomly assigned either to usual care (a short admission followed by referral to aftercare) or to the Training in Community Living programme, in which the team helped each person find housing, taught daily living skills in the person's own home and neighbourhood, helped them find work or sheltered employment, made sure they took their medicine, supported their families and was available around the clock. Over 14 months the programme's patients spent far less time in hospital, more time in employment, had fewer symptoms and were more satisfied with their lives; an economic analysis found that the programme cost somewhat more than usual care but that its benefits, including patients' earnings, outweighed the extra cost. Then came the lesson that shaped everything afterwards. When the programme ended and the patients were transferred to ordinary services, many of the gains faded. Support for severe mental illness, Stein and Test concluded, must be continuous, not time-limited.

The model they built became known as assertive community treatment (ACT), sometimes described as a "hospital without walls". Its defining features are these:

  • A multidisciplinary team (psychiatrist, nurses, social workers, psychologists, vocational and substance-use specialists, and often a peer worker with lived experience) shares one caseload, so every client knows several staff and no one is lost when a staff member is on leave.
  • The staff-to-client ratio is low, about one to ten.
  • Services are delivered in vivo, in the person's home, street and workplace, rather than in an office; most contacts happen in the community.
  • The team is available 24 hours a day, seven days a week, and handles crises itself.
  • Outreach is assertive: if a client misses an appointment, the team goes to find them, persistently and respectfully.
  • The team provides most services directly (medication, help with housing, money, shopping, work, family and substance use) rather than referring the person elsewhere.
  • Support is time-unlimited.

ACT has been tested in many trials. Reviews have consistently found that, compared with standard care, it reduces time spent in hospital, improves housing stability and keeps people in contact with services, and that clients like it; its effects on symptoms and on social functioning are smaller. The benefits depend on fidelity, how faithfully a team follows the model, which can be measured with fidelity scales such as the Dartmouth Assertive Community Treatment Scale. They also depend on what ACT is compared with. In London, where ordinary community mental health teams already visited patients at home, trials such as REACT (Helen Killaspy and colleagues, 2006) found that ACT engaged people better and satisfied them more but did not reduce hospital use. Because ACT is expensive, it is now targeted at the people who need it most: those caught in the revolving door, people who are homeless, and people with both severe mental illness and substance use problems. A Dutch adaptation, flexible ACT, switches individual clients between ordinary case management and intensive shared-caseload care as their needs change.

Exam trap

ACT was developed by Stein and Test (with Marx) in Madison, Wisconsin, in the 1970s (the Training in Community Living programme, reported 1980). Its signatures are a shared team caseload, a ratio of about 1:10, in vivo services, 24-hour availability, assertive outreach, direct service provision and time-unlimited support. Its best-established effect is less time in hospital. Do not confuse it with acceptance and commitment therapy (Chapter 5), which shares the abbreviation, or with brokerage case management, in which one worker links the client to other agencies instead of treating them.

Case management

When the American mental hospitals emptied in the 1960s and 1970s, the people who left found themselves dealing with a dozen separate agencies: one for medication, one for housing, one for welfare benefits, one for work, one for emergencies, each with its own forms, rules and waiting lists. Many fell through the gaps. In 1977 the National Institute of Mental Health launched its Community Support Program to stitch these services together, and the stitch it chose was the case manager. Case management is a way of coordinating services for a person with complex, long-term needs, so that one worker or one team takes responsibility for seeing that the person gets the right help from all the agencies involved, and keeps getting it. Its roots lie in the social casework of the American social worker Mary Ellen Richmond (1861 to 1928), whose Social Diagnosis (1917) taught that a client's difficulties must be understood and addressed in the context of their whole social situation.

Whatever the model, case management is usually said to involve five core functions. The case manager assesses the person's needs and strengths across every area of life; plans services with the person; links them to the services and resources they need (a psychiatrist, a disability pension, a hostel place, a job programme); monitors whether the services are actually delivered and are helping; and advocates for the person when an agency refuses, delays or treats them unfairly. The models differ in how much of the help the case manager gives personally, how large the caseload is, and, more subtly, in where they place the power between worker and client. The broker model is the simplest: the case manager is a kind of travel agent who books the services and leaves the person to use them. Dissatisfaction with it pushed the field in two directions. The American social worker Joel S. Kanter argued in 1989 for clinical case management, in which the case manager is a trained clinician who also offers psychotherapeutic help, uses the relationship as a vehicle of change, and attends to the person's inner world as well as their practical needs. At the University of Kansas, Charles A. Rapp and his colleagues developed the strengths model, which deliberately turns away from deficits: the client, not the worker, directs the process; the worker helps the person identify their talents, interests and aspirations; the relationship is primary; contact happens in the community; and the community is regarded not as an obstacle but as an oasis of resources (a neighbour, a temple, a tea-shop owner, a cricket club). Intensive case management cut caseloads to allow more direct help, and ACT went further still, replacing the individual case manager with a team.

Social work texts also describe models in terms of the relationship between worker and client, and one examination question has asked about the task or role performance model. This model applies structure-function theory (the structural functionalism of the sociologist Talcott Parsons, whose sick role you met in Section 9.1) at the level of the organisation. The agency is a structure with functions; the case manager occupies a formal role within it; and the client is expected to perform assigned tasks, such as attending appointments, taking medicine and applying for jobs. The worker therefore holds and uses real authority: to provide, deny or withdraw services depending on whether the client complies with the tasks. Because the worker controls resources that belong to the agency and the public, the case manager carries a fiduciary obligation (to use those resources responsibly, like a trustee) as well as a humanitarian obligation to the client's welfare. The contrasting empowerment or advocacy models reject this hierarchy. In them the worker sides with the client against the barriers the client faces, shares power and decisions with them and treats them as a partner or consumer: the worker forms what is sometimes called a power alliance with the client. Holding the two side by side makes the exam question easy. Authority, compliance, organisational roles and fiduciary duty describe the task or role performance model; a power alliance does not.

What should case management achieve? Not a cure. Case management is for people whose conditions are by definition long-term, and its desired outcomes are about living with the condition as well as possible: continuity of care (no one falling through the gaps between agencies), access to services and entitlements, community tenure (more time living in the community and less in hospital), skill training and acquisition, environmental accommodations (changes in the person's surroundings, such as a modified job, a supportive landlord or a family that has learned to lower its criticism, that let them function despite the condition), empowerment and a better quality of life. The treatment of the primary condition is the business of medicine and psychotherapy, and case management coordinates it; it does not claim to cure it.

The evidence is sobering for the simpler models. A Cochrane review by Max Marshall and his colleagues (1998) found that standard case management kept more people in contact with services but, if anything, increased hospital admissions (probably because case managers spotted relapses and arranged admission sooner), with no clear gains in symptoms, social functioning or quality of life. ACT and well-run intensive case management did better on hospital use and housing. The lesson is that coordination alone is not enough: people with severe mental illness need someone who coordinates and helps directly, continuously, where they live.

Exam trap

Two RML 2020 questions. (1) The task or role performance model rests on structure-function theory at the organisational level, on the worker's authority to provide, deny or withdraw services according to the client's compliance with assigned tasks, and on the case manager's fiduciary and humanitarian obligations. "Workers form a power alliance with the client" is the EXCEPTION: power-sharing belongs to the empowerment or advocacy models. (2) The desired outcomes of case management include environmental accommodations, skill training and acquisition and empowerment; "cure of a primary condition" is the EXCEPTION. Also know the five functions (assess, plan, link, monitor, advocate) and that the strengths model is Rapp's and clinical case management is Kanter's.

Skills, families, clubhouses and peers

Housing, work and coordinated care are the scaffolding of rehabilitation. Inside it, several specific interventions work on the person's skills and relationships. Most of them are taught in detail in other chapters, so here the emphasis is on how they fit together for severe mental illness.

The first systematic psychological treatment for long-stay patients was the token economy, an operant programme in which patients earn tokens (exchangeable for privileges, snacks or outings) for self-care, work and social behaviour. It was developed at Anna State Hospital in Illinois by Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan H. Azrin, whose book The Token Economy appeared in 1968; the method is explained in Chapter 4. Its most rigorous test was a study by Gordon Lee Paul and Robert J. Lentz (1977), who spent more than four years comparing three regimes for severely disabled long-stay patients at a mental health centre in Illinois: a social learning programme built on a token economy, milieu therapy (a therapeutic-community style of ward organised around group responsibility and expectations), and routine hospital care. The social learning programme was the clear winner. Its patients gained most in self-care, social and vocational skills, and far more of them were discharged and stayed in the community. Paul and Lentz showed that even very disabled people can learn the skills they need to live outside hospital. Token economies have since faded from use, partly because of concerns about coercion and the difficulty of keeping gains when the tokens stop, and partly because the long-stay wards they were designed for have closed.

Social skills training took the learning approach into the community. The American psychiatrist Robert Paul Liberman and his colleagues at the University of California, Los Angeles, packaged it from the 1980s into structured modules, the Social and Independent Living Skills programme, on topics such as medication management, symptom management (recognising one's personal warning signs of relapse and making an emergency plan), basic conversation, recreation and leisure, and avoiding alcohol and street drugs. Each skill is broken into small steps and taught by instruction, modelling, role play, feedback and homework in real settings, the techniques described in Chapter 4. Reviews find large gains on tests of the skills taught, smaller gains in everyday community functioning and only modest effects on relapse; the persistent problem is getting skills learned in a group room to appear in a bank or a bus queue.

Many people with schizophrenia also have lasting difficulties with attention, memory, processing speed and planning, and these predict everyday functioning better than hallucinations do. Two opposite strategies have been developed, and they foreshadow the distinction that Section 9.9 develops for brain injury. Cognitive remediation tries to improve the cognitive functions themselves through graded practice, usually on computerised exercises; meta-analyses, notably by Til Wykes and colleagues (2011), find moderate improvements in cognition, with gains in everyday functioning mainly when remediation is combined with other rehabilitation such as supported employment. Cognitive adaptation training, developed by Dawn I. Velligan and colleagues in Texas, does the reverse: it leaves the cognitive deficits alone and changes the person's surroundings to bypass them, with signs, checklists, alarms, labelled cupboards and pill containers placed where they will be seen. The first is a restorative approach and the second a compensatory one.

The family is the setting where most rehabilitation actually happens, especially in India. Research on expressed emotion (criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement in the family) showed that it predicts relapse, and family psychoeducation, which teaches families about the illness, its treatment and how to solve problems and communicate calmly, substantially reduces relapse rates. That story, from George Brown's studies to the modern programmes, is told in Chapter 7. Two related packages are often listed among the evidence-based practices for severe mental illness: illness management and recovery, which teaches people themselves to manage their condition (understanding the illness, using medication effectively, recognising early warning signs, coping with persistent symptoms, building social support) and to pursue personal recovery goals; and integrated dual-disorders treatment, in which the same team treats both the mental illness and a co-existing substance use problem rather than sending the person between two services that each blame the other.

Not all rehabilitation is done by professionals. In the 1940s a group of former patients of Rockland State Hospital in New York began meeting to support one another and called themselves WANA, for "We Are Not Alone". In 1948, with help from volunteers, they acquired a house on West 47th Street in Manhattan with a fountain in its garden, and named it Fountain House. Under the social worker John H. Beard, who became its director in 1955, Fountain House developed what is now called the Clubhouse model. Its principles are unusual. The people who attend are members, not patients or clients, and membership is voluntary and for life. The clubhouse is run through a work-ordered day: members and staff work side by side in units that keep the clubhouse going (the kitchen, the office, reception, the snack bar, the newsletter), and the number of staff is deliberately kept too small to run the house without the members, so that every member is genuinely needed. Through transitional employment (Section 9.8.4) the clubhouse opens doors to ordinary jobs, and it also supports members in housing, education and social life. There are now hundreds of clubhouses in many countries, working to shared international standards. Members' experience of being wanted, rather than treated, is the heart of the model, though research on its outcomes is harder to interpret because the people who choose to join may differ from those who do not.

The same insight lies behind peer support: people who have lived through severe mental illness and recovery are trained and employed to support others, in clubhouses, ACT teams, hospitals and self-help groups. A peer worker can say "I have been where you are" with an authority no professional has, and is living evidence of recovery. Table 9.15 brings the components of psychosocial rehabilitation together.

On a wet evening in July, Kiran, a 24-year-old software tester, was riding pillion without a helmet on the Bengaluru to Mysuru highway when the motorcycle skidded. He asks his mother the same question five times in an hour. He cannot follow the plot of a television serial. He starts making tea and leaves the gas on. Kiran's case opens the three topics of this final section. The first is how psychologists help people whose brains have been damaged. The second takes the same rehabilitation logic into addiction, where the problem is not a lost skill but a habit that keeps returning. The third is the legal machinery, disability certification, through which people like Kiran, Mr Prakash in Section 9.8 and a man recovering from alcohol dependence can claim the support the law promises them.

What brain-injury rehabilitation tries to do

Brain injury comes from many causes: traumatic brain injury (in India most often from road traffic crashes, frequently young men on two-wheelers), stroke, brain tumours and their surgery, encephalitis, and lack of oxygen after cardiac arrest or near-drowning. After the acute phase, most people improve for months on their own, fastest in the first six months, as swelling settles and the brain reorganises. Neuropsychological rehabilitation is what is added to that natural recovery: the planned effort to reduce the cognitive, emotional, behavioural and social consequences of brain injury and to help the person and family live as well as possible with whatever remains. The narrower term cognitive rehabilitation refers to the part that addresses thinking (attention, memory, language, perception, executive functions). The British clinical psychologist Barbara A. Wilson (born 1941), the most influential figure in the modern field, defines it broadly as any intervention that helps people with brain injury, and their families, to live with, manage, bypass, reduce or come to terms with the deficits the injury has caused. Notice that "reduce" is only one verb among five.

Modern neuropsychological rehabilitation was born in two world wars, which produced large numbers of young men with penetrating head wounds who survived and needed to work. During the First World War, the German neurologist Kurt Goldstein (1878 to 1965) set up an institute in Frankfurt in 1916 for research on the consequences of brain injury, which combined assessment, retraining and sheltered workshops for injured soldiers. Goldstein noticed that his patients lost the abstract attitude (the ability to think in categories, shift between ideas and consider the merely possible) and were left with a concrete one, and he described the catastrophic reaction: the sudden distress, agitation or tears of a brain-injured person confronted with a task they can no longer perform. A good rehabilitation programme, he taught, arranges the environment so that such failures are avoided.

During the Second World War, the Soviet neuropsychologist Alexander Romanovich Luria (1902 to 1977) ran a rehabilitation hospital for wounded soldiers. In Restoration of Function after Brain Injury (1948 in Russian; English translation 1963), he argued that complex psychological functions such as reading, writing or walking are not stored in one spot but produced by functional systems: chains of brain areas working together, each contributing one link. When one link is destroyed, the function can sometimes be rebuilt by recruiting a different chain, using the intact links in a new way. Luria called this functional reorganisation, and it is the theoretical ancestor of every compensatory technique you will meet below.

Key study: Luria's "man with a shattered world"

In 1943 a young Soviet officer, Lev Zasetsky, was shot in the head during the fighting near Smolensk. The bullet damaged the left parieto-occipital region of his brain. He lost half his field of vision, could not read, could not find words, could not tell left from right and could not hold a sentence in his mind long enough to understand it. Luria worked with him for some twenty-five years. Zasetsky's breakthrough came when Luria encouraged him to write without stopping to think about the letters: although he could not spell letter by letter, he could write fluently if he wrote quickly without lifting the pen, using the intact motor "melody" of writing that he had learned as a child. Over a quarter of a century he wrote a diary of about 3,000 pages, struggling sometimes for a whole day over a single paragraph. Luria published it with his own commentary as The Man with a Shattered World (1972). The case shows functional reorganisation in action: a damaged route to a function was bypassed by an intact one. It also shows the limits. Zasetsky never recovered the ability to read with ease, and his world stayed shattered.

In Britain the same war produced the Edinburgh brain injury unit, where the psychologist Oliver Louis Zangwill (1913 to 1987) worked with injured servicemen. In a paper published in 1947 he distinguished three approaches to retraining, and his classification is still the starting point of the field. Compensation means reorganising psychological function to minimise or get round a disability (for example, a man who can no longer remember appointments learns to rely on a notebook). Substitution means building up an entirely new method of response to replace one that has been irreparably damaged (for example, a person who cannot read by sight learns Braille, or a person with severe aphasia learns to communicate with gestures and a picture board). Direct retraining means training the damaged function itself, by practice, in the hope that it will improve. These three collapse into the two approaches that modern rehabilitation contrasts: restoring the function, or compensating for its loss.

The catastrophic reaction and the loss of the abstract attitude are Goldstein's. Cognitive rehabilitation is only one part of neuropsychological rehabilitation, which also covers emotion, behaviour, family and work.

Restorative and compensatory approaches

Every decision in cognitive rehabilitation sits somewhere between two strategies. The restorative approach (also called the remedial or retraining approach, and its drills cognitive retraining) aims to repair the damaged function itself, on the assumption that the brain is plastic and that repeated, graded exercise will strengthen the weakened networks, much as physiotherapy strengthens a weak muscle. The compensatory approach accepts that the function may not come back and teaches the person other ways of achieving the same goal, using intact abilities, strategies, external aids or a changed environment. Neither is always right. The choice depends on how severe the impairment is, how long ago the injury happened, how aware the person is of the problem and, above all, what the person is trying to do in daily life.

The best-known restorative programme for attention came from the American speech-language pathologist McKay Moore Sohlberg and the neuropsychologist Catherine A. Mateer. Their attention process training (APT), first evaluated in 1987, rests on a clinical model in which attention is a hierarchy of five levels, each building on the one below:

  1. Focused attention: responding at all to specific visual, auditory or touch stimuli, the level that returns first after coma.
  2. Sustained attention: keeping a consistent response going over a continuous activity (vigilance), including holding information in mind while working on it.
  3. Selective attention: maintaining a response in the face of distraction, such as listening for target words while a radio plays in the background.
  4. Alternating attention: shifting the focus flexibly between tasks with different demands, such as switching back and forth between adding and subtracting.
  5. Divided attention: responding to two tasks at the same time, such as sorting cards while listening for a target word.

Patients work through graded exercises at each level, moving up as they succeed. Attention training of this kind produces gains, especially when it is linked to everyday activities and combined with strategy training, but the gains on the exercises do not always show up in daily life.

That last caution applies to the restorative approach in general, and it has been tested on a large scale. In 2010 the British neuroscientist Adrian M. Owen and his colleagues, working with a BBC science programme, had 11,430 healthy viewers practise online tasks of reasoning, memory, planning and attention for six weeks. The participants improved steadily on the tasks they practised, but there was no transfer to untrained tasks, even ones that were closely related. Commercial "brain training" games make people better at the games. For people with brain injury, the lesson is that drill should target, or closely resemble, the real activity the person needs.

The clearest success of the restorative approach is in movement rather than thought. The American behavioural neuroscientist Edward Taub, working with monkeys whose sensory nerves to one arm had been cut, observed that they stopped using the arm even when it could move: every failed attempt was punished and using the good arm was rewarded, a pattern he called learned non-use. He reasoned that many stroke patients do the same. In constraint-induced movement therapy, the unaffected arm is restrained in a mitt or sling for most waking hours over about two weeks while the patient practises intensively with the affected arm, using shaping (Chapter 4) to reward small improvements. In the EXCITE trial (Steven Wolf and colleagues, 2006), stroke patients given this therapy three to nine months after their stroke regained significantly more arm function than controls, and the gains lasted at least a year. Here practice really did restore function, and brain imaging showed reorganisation of the motor cortex.

For memory, the evidence points the other way: in people with significant memory impairment, practice at remembering (memorising lists, playing memory games) does little to improve memory itself. Rehabilitation therefore relies mainly on compensation, of three kinds:

  • Internal strategies are mental techniques that make the most of the memory that remains: visual imagery (linking a name to a picture), first-letter mnemonics, chunking, and study methods such as PQRST (Preview, Question, Read, State, Test) for remembering written material. They require effort and some remaining memory, so they suit people with mild impairments.
  • External memory aids move the remembering outside the head: diaries and memory notebooks, wall calendars and whiteboards, alarms, pill organisers, and today the smartphone, with its calendar reminders, notes, photographs and location tracking. They are the most useful tools for people with moderate to severe impairment, but the person has to be taught to use them, which is harder than it sounds when the problem is memory.
  • Environmental modification changes the surroundings so less memory is needed: labels on cupboards and doors, a fixed place for keys, a written daily routine on the fridge, a quieter room for work, a family that gives one instruction at a time.

A neat demonstration of external aids was NeuroPage, a simple paging system that sent timed reminders ("Take your tablets now"; "Feed the dog") to people with memory and planning problems. In a randomised trial by Wilson and her colleagues (2001), the 143 participants, most with brain injury, carried out far more of their target everyday tasks while using the pager than during the baseline period, and for many the improvement persisted after the pager was returned. Today the same principle runs on every phone.

Executive problems (difficulty planning, organising, starting, monitoring and stopping) can also be helped with strategies. In goal management training, developed by the psychologist Ian H. Robertson in the 1990s and tested by Brian Levine and colleagues in 2000, people learn to interrupt automatic behaviour and run through a fixed sequence for any task: stop and ask "What am I doing?"; define the main goal; list the steps; learn the steps; and, while carrying them out, check "Am I doing what I planned?" For Kiran, that sequence written on a card beside the gas stove might prevent the forgotten kettle. Table 9.16 summarises the two approaches.

Teaching people who cannot remember: errorless learning and its relatives

There is a hidden trap in the way most of us learn. We guess, make mistakes, are corrected and try again. This works because explicit memory lets us remember that the first guess was wrong. Now imagine someone with severe amnesia, like Kiran at his worst. He still has implicit memory: skills and habits strengthen with repetition even when he cannot recall the learning episode. So every time he guesses wrongly, the wrong answer is strengthened implicitly, and he has no explicit memory to tell him it was the mistake. Trial and error, for him, means practising errors.

The solution came from an unexpected source. In 1963 the American psychologist Herbert S. Terrace showed that pigeons could learn a difficult colour discrimination without ever pecking the wrong key, if the incorrect stimulus was introduced so faintly and briefly at first that they never responded to it, and was then gradually strengthened. He called this errorless discrimination learning, and it was applied in the 1960s and 1970s to teaching people with intellectual disability. Thirty years later two British psychologists asked whether it could help people with amnesia.

Key study: Baddeley and Wilson on errorless learning in amnesia (1994)

Alan D. Baddeley and Barbara Wilson taught lists of five-letter words to 16 people with severe amnesia and to young and elderly control participants, in two conditions. In the errorful condition, participants were given the first two letters and invited to guess (for example, "I am thinking of a five-letter word beginning with QU; can you guess what it is?"), so they usually produced several wrong words before being told the right one. In the errorless condition they were simply told the word ("The word beginning with QU is QUOTE") and wrote it down, so they never made a mistake. When memory was later tested by giving the first two letters, the amnesic participants performed much better on the words they had learned errorlessly, and every one of the 16 amnesic patients showed the advantage. The explanation offered was that amnesic patients learn by implicit memory, which cannot separate correct responses from errors; only explicit memory can weed out a mistake. Wilson and her colleagues went on, in the same year, to apply the method to practical goals in single patients: learning the names of therapists, programming an electronic memory aid, recognising pictures of people. The criticism is that errorless learning helps most with specific facts and routines and with severe impairment; it produces rather rigid learning that may not generalise to new situations.

In practice, errorless learning means arranging teaching so that the person is prevented from making errors while learning: no guessing is allowed, the correct answer or action is supplied or modelled from the start, tasks are broken into small steps, and help is withdrawn only gradually as success is secure. It is usually combined with two related techniques:

  • The method of vanishing cues, introduced by Elizabeth L. Glisky, Daniel Schacter and Endel Tulving in 1986 to teach amnesic patients the vocabulary needed to use a computer. The person is given a definition and the whole word; on later trials letters are removed one at a time from the end ("SAV_", "SA__") until the person can produce the word from the definition alone. If the person cannot answer, a letter is added back.
  • Spaced retrieval, in which the person recalls a piece of information at gradually lengthening intervals (after 15 seconds, 30 seconds, a minute, two minutes and so on), with the interval shortened again after any failure. It has been used widely with people with dementia to teach a single useful fact, such as "My room is the one with the blue door".

Apply them to Kiran. To teach him to check the calendar on his phone, the therapist does not ask "What should you do when you wake up?" and wait for a wrong answer. She says "When you wake up, open the calendar. Watch me," shows him, has him do it with her hand guiding his, then asks him to show her after half a minute, then after two minutes, then after five, then the next morning, prompting immediately if he hesitates so that he never practises the wrong move.

Exam trap

Errorless learning means preventing errors during learning, because people with amnesia learn implicitly and cannot use the memory of an error to correct it. It comes from Terrace's errorless discrimination learning in pigeons (1963) and was applied to amnesia by Baddeley and Wilson (1994). It is not "learning from mistakes", not shaping by reinforcing successive approximations, and not trial and error. The method of vanishing cues is Glisky, Schacter and Tulving (1986); spaced retrieval lengthens the gap between successful recalls.

Holistic rehabilitation and the problem of awareness

Remember that Kiran believes he can return to work next week. That belief may be the biggest obstacle in his rehabilitation, because a person who sees no problem has no reason to use a strategy or a memory aid. Lack of awareness of one's own deficits after brain injury is called anosognosia, a term coined by the French neurologist Joseph Babinski in 1914 for stroke patients who denied that they were paralysed. It is not the same as psychological denial: it is a deficit of self-monitoring caused by the injury itself, typically involving the right hemisphere or the frontal lobes. Bruce Crosson and colleagues (1989) distinguished three levels of awareness, arranged as a pyramid. Intellectual awareness is knowing that a function is impaired ("my memory is poor since the accident"). Emergent awareness is recognising a problem at the moment it happens ("I have just lost track of this conversation"). Anticipatory awareness is foreseeing that a problem will arise and acting in advance ("I will forget the appointment, so I will set an alarm now"). Each level depends on the one below it, and compensatory strategies need the top one.

Brain injury also changes emotion and personality. Depression and anxiety are common in the year after injury; irritability, impulsivity, apathy and loss of social sensitivity, typical of frontal damage, are often harder on families than any cognitive problem. Relatives describe living with a stranger in a familiar body, and the burden on carers is heavy. These problems cannot be separated from the cognitive ones, which is why the most respected programmes treat the whole person.

Holistic neuropsychological rehabilitation was developed in the 1970s and 1980s, especially by Yehuda Ben-Yishay at the Rusk Institute of New York University, who began a day programme for brain-injured adults in 1978, and by George P. Prigatano, whose Neuropsychological Rehabilitation after Brain Injury (1986) put awareness, emotion and motivation at the centre of the work. In Britain Barbara Wilson founded the Oliver Zangwill Centre for Neuropsychological Rehabilitation at Ely in 1996, named after Zangwill. Holistic programmes share several features: an intensive programme of several months, usually as a day patient; a combination of group and individual work; a therapeutic milieu in which patients and staff form a community; explicit work on awareness and acceptance, often by having patients predict their own performance and then compare the prediction with the result; training in compensatory strategies; psychological therapy for mood and identity; involvement of families; and planned return to work or other meaningful activity. Goals are set with the patient in everyday terms and progress is often measured with goal attainment scaling, devised by Thomas Kiresuk and Robert Sherman in 1968, in which each personal goal is given a five-point scale from much less than expected (minus 2) to much more than expected (plus 2), with 0 as the expected outcome. Reviews have found that holistic programmes improve community participation, productivity and self-efficacy, and they are recommended for people with complex needs after traumatic brain injury.

Clinical link

Assessment comes before rehabilitation: a neuropsychological evaluation maps strengths and weaknesses (the tests and batteries are in the Assessment book, and neuropsychology as a specialty is described in Chapter 10). But a test profile is not a plan. A good rehabilitation goal for Kiran is not "improve memory score by 10 points" but "Kiran will use his phone calendar to attend all his physiotherapy appointments for four weeks without reminders from his mother". Goals of this kind are specific, measurable, agreed with the person and tied to everyday life.

Rehabilitation in addiction: from detoxification to aftercare

Rehabilitation after brain injury rebuilds lost abilities. Rehabilitation in addiction has a different problem. A man who has stopped drinking for three weeks in a de-addiction ward has not lost a skill; he has lost a habit, temporarily, and almost everything in his life (his friends, his evening routine, the stresses that drinking used to numb, the shop on his way home) is pulling it back. Most people treated for alcohol or drug dependence relapse at least once, most often within the first few months. Addiction rehabilitation is therefore organised less around getting people off a substance than around keeping them off it. The disorders themselves, and the pharmacology, are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book; here the focus is the sequence of psychological care.

The first step, for people who are physically dependent, is detoxification: the medically supervised management of withdrawal. For alcohol this usually means a reducing course of a long-acting benzodiazepine such as chlordiazepoxide over about a week, with thiamine to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy; for opioids it means a tapering dose of buprenorphine or methadone, or clonidine to ease the symptoms. Detoxification makes stopping safe and bearable, but on its own it does almost nothing to prevent relapse. It is the doorway to treatment, not treatment itself, and a programme that discharges people after detoxification has simply arranged their next admission.

After detoxification, rehabilitation usually moves through overlapping phases. Engagement and motivation come first, because ambivalence is the rule: motivational interviewing, born in the addictions field, is the main tool (Chapter 6; its use in health care is described in Section 9.4). Active treatment then teaches the person to live without the substance, using several evidence-based approaches:

  • Cognitive-behavioural coping skills training teaches the person to recognise triggers, refuse offers, manage cravings and negative moods, and solve the problems that used to lead to use.
  • Contingency management gives tangible rewards, such as vouchers, for verified abstinence (for example, drug-free urine samples); it is one of the most effective methods for stimulant use and is described in Chapter 4.
  • The community reinforcement approach, introduced by George Hunt and Nathan Azrin in 1973, rearranges the person's everyday life (work, family, recreation, social life) so that sober living is more rewarding than drinking. Its family version, community reinforcement and family training, teaches relatives how to encourage a reluctant drinker into treatment.
  • Family and couples therapy, and twelve-step facilitation, which encourages active involvement in Alcoholics Anonymous or Narcotics Anonymous (mutual-help groups are described in Chapter 7).
  • Medication to support abstinence: disulfiram (which makes drinking unpleasant), naltrexone and acamprosate (which reduce craving), and for opioid dependence long-term opioid substitution with methadone or buprenorphine, which is a harm-reduction strategy as much as a treatment.

Finally come relapse prevention (the next subsection) and aftercare: continuing outpatient contact, mutual-help groups, and sometimes a sober-living halfway house, for months or years. Does it matter which active treatment is chosen?

Key study: Project MATCH (1997)

By the late 1980s it seemed obvious that different alcoholics needed different treatments, and that matching clients to treatments by their characteristics would improve results. The United States National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism funded the largest psychotherapy trial ever conducted for alcohol problems to find out. The Project MATCH Research Group randomly assigned 1,726 people with alcohol problems, in an outpatient arm and an aftercare arm, to one of three twelve-week individual treatments: cognitive-behavioural coping skills therapy (twelve sessions), motivational enhancement therapy (only four sessions) and twelve-step facilitation (twelve sessions). Drinking fell sharply and similarly in all three groups and stayed down over the follow-up. And most of the predicted matching effects did not appear: client characteristics did little to predict who did best with which treatment, with a few exceptions (for example, clients high in anger did better with motivational enhancement). The conclusion was that well-delivered structured treatments work about equally well, so a four-session motivational treatment can be as effective as twelve sessions of other therapy. The criticism is that all the participants were volunteers who received unusually good care, there was no untreated control group, and matching might still matter for people with more severe or complex problems.

Indian context

The national survey Magnitude of Substance Use in India (2019), carried out by the National Drug Dependence Treatment Centre of AIIMS for the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, found that about 14.6 percent of Indians aged 10 to 75 used alcohol, about 2.8 percent cannabis and about 2.1 percent opioids, and that only a small fraction of those dependent had ever received treatment. Because the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 includes mental conditions associated with the use of alcohol and drugs within mental illness, people in such centres are entitled to the Act's protections, including its prohibition of chaining.

Relapse prevention

Earlier treatment models looked at relapse in one of two ways. In the disease model, relapse proved that the person was powerless over the substance, and a single drink was the first step of an inevitable slide. In ordinary moral thinking, relapse proved weak will. The American psychologist G. Alan Marlatt (1941 to 2011) of the University of Washington in Seattle proposed a third view: relapse is the result of a failure of coping, learned and therefore preventable, and it is a process that unfolds over time rather than a sudden event. With Judith R. Gordon, he set out the cognitive-behavioural model and its treatment in Relapse Prevention: Maintenance Strategies in the Treatment of Addictive Behaviors (1985). Relapse prevention is the resulting self-management programme for maintaining change, designed for alcohol and drugs but applied since to smoking, gambling, overeating and many other habits.

The model starts with a distinction that changes how both clients and clinicians think. A lapse is a single slip: one drink, one cigarette, one bet. A relapse is a return to the old pattern of use. A lapse is common and need not lead to relapse; what happens next depends on how the person interprets and handles it. Marlatt sometimes spoke of a lapse as a fork in the road, one path leading back to the old pattern and the other, a "prolapse", back towards the goal.

Where do lapses happen? Marlatt and his colleagues asked people who had relapsed to describe in detail the situation in which they first used again. In an analysis of 311 initial relapse episodes, reported by Cummings, Gordon and Marlatt in 1980, among people with problems with alcohol, smoking, heroin, gambling and overeating, three kinds of high-risk situation accounted for nearly three-quarters of the episodes. The commonest were negative emotional states such as anger, anxiety, boredom, frustration and depression (about 35 percent), followed by social pressure, direct or indirect, to use (about 20 percent) and interpersonal conflict, such as an argument with a spouse or employer (about 16 percent). Marlatt grouped the high-risk situations into intrapersonal determinants (negative emotional states, negative physical states, positive emotional states, testing personal control, urges and temptations) and interpersonal determinants (interpersonal conflict, social pressure, positive emotional states with others, such as a celebration).

The heart of the model is what happens in the high-risk situation (Figure 9.9). If the person has an effective coping response (leaving, refusing, phoning a sponsor, using a relaxation skill), the situation passes, the person's self-efficacy (Bandura's concept, Section 9.2) rises, and the probability of relapse falls; each success makes the next one easier. If the person has no coping response, or uses it badly, self-efficacy falls, and if they also hold positive outcome expectancies for the substance ("one drink will calm me down"; "I deserve this"), an initial use, the lapse, becomes likely. Then a second process takes over. Marlatt called it the abstinence violation effect, and it has two parts. The first is cognitive dissonance: the lapse clashes with the person's new self-image as an abstainer, and the discomfort is often resolved by abandoning the self-image ("I am an addict after all"). The second is a self-attribution of the lapse to internal, stable and global causes, such as a lack of willpower or a disease that cannot be controlled, rather than to a specific, changeable situation. The result is guilt, a sense of having lost control, and the classic thought "I have blown it now, so I might as well finish the bottle". The more intense the abstinence violation effect, the more likely a lapse is to become a relapse. Dieters know the same pattern from Section 9.4: "I ate one sweet, so the diet is ruined".

Marlatt added a further insight: many high-risk situations are not stumbled into by accident. People set them up, without admitting it to themselves, through a chain of covert antecedents. It often begins with lifestyle imbalance, a life with too many "shoulds" (obligations, pressures) and too few "wants" (pleasures, rest). Imbalance breeds a desire for indulgence ("I deserve a reward"), which feeds urges and cravings, which are supported by rationalisation and denial. The person then makes a series of apparently irrelevant decisions (later called seemingly irrelevant decisions): small choices that each look innocent but together lead straight into the high-risk situation.

A worked example shows the whole chain. Ramesh, a 41-year-old bus conductor in Mysuru, has been sober for four months after treatment for alcohol dependence. His wife's family are pressing him over a property dispute, and he has been working double shifts to pay the loan (lifestyle imbalance). On Saturday he tells himself he has earned a relaxing evening (desire for indulgence). He decides to attend an old friend's son's wedding reception "just to show his face" (an apparently irrelevant decision), takes the long route home past the arrack shop "to avoid traffic" (another), and arrives with Rs 500 in his pocket because the shops might be open late (another). At the reception, his friends press a glass on him (social pressure, a high-risk situation) and he has no prepared way of refusing. He drinks one glass (the lapse). On the way home he thinks, "Four months wasted; I am a hopeless drunkard; my father was the same" (the abstinence violation effect, with an internal, stable, global attribution), and he stops at the arrack shop. Relapse prevention would have offered him a way out at every step: rebalancing his week, recognising "just showing my face" as a decision that leads somewhere, rehearsing a refusal ("No thanks, I have the early shift"), carrying a reminder card for the moment after a lapse ("One drink is a slip, not a failure; call my sponsor; leave now"), and reframing any lapse as a specific mistake in a specific situation from which he can learn.

The techniques of relapse prevention fall into two groups. Specific intervention strategies deal with the immediate chain: self-monitoring to identify personal high-risk situations; coping skills training for them, including drink and drug refusal, assertiveness, relaxation and problem solving; enhancing self-efficacy through graded success; challenging positive outcome expectancies by recalling the full sequence of effects ("one drink calms me" versus the hangover, the fight, the lost wages); a decisional balance of the short- and long-term pros and cons of using; and lapse management, including a written plan and the cognitive restructuring of the abstinence violation effect. Global self-control strategies address the covert antecedents: restoring lifestyle balance, building "positive addictions" such as exercise or meditation, learning to recognise apparently irrelevant decisions, and coping with urges. Marlatt's best-known urge technique is urge surfing: instead of fighting a craving or giving in to it, the person observes it like a wave that rises, peaks and subsides on its own, riding it out without acting on it.

Does it work? A meta-analysis of 26 studies by Jennifer Irvin and her colleagues (1999) found that relapse prevention was generally effective, particularly for alcohol problems, and was more effective in improving psychosocial adjustment than in reducing substance use itself. In 2004 Katie Witkiewitz and Marlatt revised the model into a dynamic one, in which risk factors interact and small changes can tip a person suddenly into relapse, rather than a fixed sequence of steps. And Marlatt's interest in Buddhist meditation led his group, with Sarah Bowen and Neha Chawla, to develop mindfulness-based relapse prevention, which combines relapse prevention skills with mindfulness practice in the manner of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (Chapter 5); a 2014 trial found it better than standard relapse prevention at reducing heavy drinking and drug use a year later.

Exam trap

Relapse prevention is Marlatt and Gordon (1985), not Prochaska and DiClemente (stages of change) and not Miller and Rollnick (motivational interviewing). A lapse is a single slip; a relapse is a return to the old pattern. The abstinence violation effect has two parts, cognitive dissonance and an internal, stable, global self-attribution ("I have no willpower"); it turns lapses into relapses. The commonest high-risk situation is negative emotional states (about 35 percent), then social pressure and interpersonal conflict. Apparently irrelevant decisions and lifestyle imbalance are covert antecedents. Urge surfing is Marlatt's. A lapse increases relapse risk only when it is interpreted catastrophically.

Therapeutic communities for addiction

For people whose whole way of life is organised around drugs (whose friends use, who have no job, who may have been in prison, whose families have given up), a weekly outpatient session cannot compete with the street. The therapeutic community for addiction takes the person out of that world altogether, into a residential community where recovery is the purpose of daily life and other residents are the main agents of change.

The first was Synanon, founded in Santa Monica, California, in 1958 by Charles E. Dederich (1913 to 1997), a former alcoholic who had been through Alcoholics Anonymous. Dederich ran it without professionals: residents lived together, worked to keep the house going and confronted one another's excuses in intense group sessions known as "the game". Synanon's later history is a warning. In the 1970s it turned into an authoritarian cult under Dederich's control, and it was eventually dissolved after criminal convictions of its leaders and the loss of its tax exemption. But its methods were adopted, and moderated, by programmes such as Daytop Village (New York, 1963) and Phoenix House (New York, 1967), which brought in professional staff, and they spread through the world. The American psychologist George De Leon, the leading researcher on these programmes, summarised their principle as "community as method": the community itself, its structure, relationships and daily routines, is the treatment.

A traditional drug-free therapeutic community of this kind (sometimes called a concept-based or hierarchical therapeutic community) has recognisable features:

  • A long residential stay, traditionally twelve to twenty-four months, now often shorter, moving through phases of induction, primary treatment and re-entry into the outside community.
  • A strict structure of house rules (no drugs, no violence, no threats of violence) and a hierarchy of jobs, from cleaning to running departments, through which residents rise as they show responsibility, earning privileges and losing them for breaking rules.
  • Peers as change agents: senior residents and staff who are themselves in recovery act as role models, and residents are expected to hold one another accountable ("acting as if" they already hold the values of recovery).
  • Groups, including encounter groups in which residents confront one another's behaviour, alongside groups for support, education and skills.
  • Work as therapy, with every resident contributing to running the community.
  • A view of addiction as a disorder of the whole person, so the goal is a change of lifestyle and identity, not only abstinence.

The therapeutic community for addiction should not be confused with the older therapeutic community of psychiatry, developed by Maxwell Jones in British hospitals in the 1940s and 1950s and described in Chapter 7. Jones's community was democratic: staff and patients shared decisions in daily community meetings, roles were flattened, and the community was used to understand behaviour in the here and now. The addiction therapeutic community is hierarchical and highly structured, with privileges earned by moving up a ladder of responsibility. Both use the community as the agent of change, but in opposite ways.

What is the evidence? People who complete therapeutic community programmes do well, with large reductions in drug use and crime and better employment, and the longer they stay the better they do. The difficulty is that many residents leave early, often within the first few weeks, and comparisons with other forms of residential treatment show less clear advantages. The most convincing evidence comes from therapeutic communities in prisons, which reduce reoffending and return to drug use, especially when residents go on to community aftercare after release. In India, the residential model was developed by organisations such as the T. T. Ranganathan Clinical Research Foundation (TTK Hospital) in Chennai, founded in 1980, which built family involvement into its programme, and the Kripa Foundation in Mumbai, founded in 1981 by the priest Joseph Pereira, which combined the twelve-step programme of Alcoholics Anonymous with yoga and meditation.

Exam trap

The therapeutic community "pioneered by Maxwell Jones and characterised by the democratic involvement of staff and patients in decisions" is the psychiatric therapeutic community (Chapter 7). The addiction therapeutic community began with Synanon (Dederich, 1958) and is hierarchical, peer-led and long-term ("community as method", De Leon). Milieu therapy is the broader idea of using the whole ward environment therapeutically. Encounter groups belong to the humanistic group movement, though the addiction therapeutic community borrowed its confrontational style.

Disability certification in India

Kiran's family is told that he may be entitled to a disability certificate. Why would anyone want a certificate saying they are disabled? Because in Indian law almost every entitlement for people with disabilities, from a reserved government post to a railway concession, flows from a single document: a certificate, issued by a designated medical authority, stating the specified disability and its extent as a percentage. Disability certification is where the clinical psychologist's assessments turn into legal rights.

The legal framework is the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 (RPWD Act), which replaced the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995 and is taught in Chapter 1. Three points from it matter here. First, the Act lists 21 specified disabilities, including mental illness, intellectual disability, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder and chronic neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease. Second, a person with benchmark disability is one with not less than 40 percent of a specified disability, as certified, and most of the Act's special entitlements are reserved for this group. Third, the Act requires the government to notify official guidelines for assessing each disability; these were issued in 2018 and replaced by revised guidelines notified in March 2024, and certifying authorities must use them.

The process is straightforward in outline. The person or family applies, since 2021 online through the national Unique Disability ID (UDID) portal, which issues both the certificate and a single identity card valid throughout India. The applicant is assessed by a designated medical authority, usually a board of specialists at a government district or teaching hospital, using the notified method for that disability. The certificate states the disability and its percentage and says whether it is permanent or temporary; a temporary certificate, common for children and for conditions that may change, must be renewed after reassessment. A person who disagrees with the decision can appeal to an appellate authority designated by the state government. Each disability has its own assessment method, and four of them are the clinical psychologist's daily business.

For mental illness, the tool is the Indian Disability Evaluation and Assessment Scale (IDEAS), developed by the Rehabilitation Committee of the Indian Psychiatric Society and adopted by the government for certification in 2002 (in guidelines notified by the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment that February). Using an interview with the person and the family, a psychiatrist rates disability in four areas, each from 0 (no disability) to 4 (profound disability): self-care (hygiene, dressing, eating); interpersonal activities (social relationships); communication and understanding; and work (employment, housework or study, whichever applies). A fifth score, from 1 to 4, reflects the duration of illness, longer illness scoring higher (less than two years scores 1, two to five years 2, six to ten years 3 and more than ten years 4). The total, from 0 to 20, is the global disability score, and it is converted into a grade and a percentage (Table 9.17). Duration also limits what can be certified. Under the 2024 guidelines a permanent certificate for mental illness requires at least two years of illness; for a shorter illness the board normally issues a certificate valid only for a limited time, so that the temporary disability of an acute episode is not certified as lasting, and a person who has not yet received adequate treatment is also given only a time-limited certificate. IDEAS is used only for mental illness: intellectual disability, autism and specific learning disabilities have their own methods.

For intellectual disability, a clinical psychologist on the board measures intelligence and adaptive (social) functioning with standardised tests adapted for Indian children and adults (the tests are described in the Assessment book), and the level of intellectual disability fixes the percentage (under the 2024 guidelines the level is read from the social quotient on the Vineland Social Maturity Scale); a child under five is certified as having global developmental delay rather than intellectual disability. For specific learning disabilities, the guidelines require that the child be at least 8 years old, because before that age reading, writing and arithmetic are too new to judge, and the assessment compares the child's attainment with what is expected for their age and schooling after ruling out poor teaching, sensory impairment and intellectual disability. In the 2024 version, teachers screen children at eight years or in Class III, whichever comes first, and the diagnosis requires an IQ of 85 or more together with attainment at least three standard deviations below the child's current class on the NIMHANS battery for specific learning disability (or a score below 40 percent on the Grade Level Assessment Device). What does a certificate bring? For persons with benchmark disabilities, the RPWD Act reserves not less than 4 percent of vacancies in government establishments, of which one percentage point is shared among autism, intellectual disability, specific learning disability, mental illness and multiple disabilities; it reserves not less than 5 percent of seats in government and government-aided institutions of higher education; and it gives every child with a benchmark disability the right to free education from 6 to 18 years. Certificates also open the way to disability pensions, travel concessions, tax deductions and many state schemes. For people with autism, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability and multiple disabilities, the National Trust Act 1999 (Chapter 1) adds a system of legal guardianship through local level committees; it does not cover mental illness, for which the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 provides nominated representatives instead.

The clinical psychologist faces real dilemmas here. Under-certifying denies people their rights; over-certifying, or certifying during a temporary crisis, can attach a permanent label to a recoverable condition. Many families of people with mental illness refuse to apply at all, because a certificate stating "mental illness, 50 percent" feels like an announcement to the world, and the stigma described in Section 9.6 weighs more than the benefits. Explaining honestly what a certificate will and will not do is part of the work.

Finally, it helps to know which part of the government does what, because examiners like to ask. Disability and rehabilitation in India come under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, through its Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities. That ministry administers the RPWD Act, the National Trust and the national institutes for the various disabilities, and the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI), which began as a registered society in 1986 and became a statutory body under the RCI Act 1992 (in force from 1993). The RCI regulates the training of rehabilitation professionals, including clinical psychologists, and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register in which they must be registered to practise (Chapter 1). Mental health services, by contrast, come under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, which administers the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, the National Mental Health Programme and NIMHANS. A clinical psychologist in India therefore works under two ministries at once: registered by a council of the social justice ministry, and practising within a health system run by the health ministry.

The Indian tool for assessing and certifying disability in autism is ISAA, the Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism. Some IHBAS papers (2020 and 2024) key the answer as CARS (the Childhood Autism Rating Scale); CARS is an American rating scale (Schopler and colleagues, 1980), not the Indian certification instrument, so the fact to know is ISAA. The catch is the examiners: IHBAS keyed CARS both times it asked, in 2020 ("The instrument used for the assessment of disability in autism is") and in 2024 ("Name of the Indian tool to assess the severity and disability of children with autism", where ISAA was one of the options). The case of the Cocoanut Grove fire, revisited

Go back to Massachusetts General Hospital in the winter of 1942, where Erich Lindemann sat with the survivors and the bereaved of the Cocoanut Grove. You can now name almost everything he saw. The waves of bodily distress, the sighing and the exhaustion are grief as the whole body lives it, the meeting point of emotion and physiology that Section 9.3 explored. His "grief work" carried forward the idea of Freud's "Mourning and Melancholia" that a loss must be worked through, an idea later questioned by Wortman and Silver's "myths of coping with loss" and by Bonanno's finding that resilience is the commonest outcome; today it survives inside Stroebe and Schut's dual process model, in which healthy mourners oscillate between confronting the loss and rebuilding their lives. His delayed and distorted grief reactions are the complicated grief that Prigerson and Shear studied and that ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR now call prolonged grief disorder. And his practical finding, that most of the bereaved came through with a few interviews while a minority went wrong, is what the modern evidence on bereavement care and on psychological debriefing confirms: help offered to everyone does little, while help aimed at the people who are struggling does a great deal.

Lindemann's bolder conclusion was that a few well-timed conversations at a turning point could stop a temporary crisis from hardening into a lasting disorder. Gerald Caplan, who joined him on the Wellesley project, turned that conclusion into crisis theory (a crisis is the person's reaction rather than the event, it is not an illness, it resolves within about four to six weeks, and it is a time when people are unusually open to help), into the three levels of prevention of Principles of Preventive Psychiatry (1964) and into mental health consultation (1970). The same thinking stood behind the Community Mental Health Centers Act that Kennedy signed in 1963, whose centres were meant to prevent and consult as well as to treat. When a counsellor in Mysuru sits with a student like Arjun at two in the morning, assesses lethality, mobilises his perception, support and coping and plans follow-up, she is using tools whose history begins on those hospital wards. A nightclub fire led a psychiatrist out of the consulting room and into a whole town; this chapter has followed the same road, from one patient's beliefs about illness to the communities, crisis services and rehabilitation teams that carry clinical psychology beyond the fifty-minute hour.

Chapter summary

9.1 Health Psychology and Behavioural Medicine. Most deaths in India now come from non-communicable diseases whose causes are largely behavioural, which gives psychology a direct role in physical health. The biomedical model gave way to the WHO's definition of health as complete physical, mental and social well-being, to Diener's subjective well-being (life satisfaction, positive affect, low negative affect) and to Antonovsky's salutogenesis and sense of coherence. Behavioural medicine, defined by Schwartz and Weiss (1978) after the Yale Conference of 1977, is interdisciplinary; health psychology, defined by Matarazzo (1980), is psychology's own contribution to promotion, prevention, treatment, causes and the health-care system. Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977) shapes care at the bedside. Mechanic described illness behaviour (1962), Freidson the lay referral system, Parsons (1951) the sick role with its two rights and two obligations, and Pilowsky abnormal illness behaviour; the sick role separates malingering, factitious disorder and conversion.

9.2 Why People Do or Do Not Protect Their Health. Knowledge alone does not produce health behaviour; each model names the beliefs that fill the gap. The health belief model, born of Hochbaum's tuberculosis X-ray study, rests on perceived susceptibility and severity (together, threat), benefits, barriers, cues to action and, from 1988, Bandura's self-efficacy. Fishbein and Ajzen's theory of reasoned action (1975) makes intention the immediate cause of behaviour, fed by attitude and subjective norm; Ajzen's theory of planned behaviour adds perceived behavioural control. Rogers's protection motivation theory (1975) sets threat appraisal against coping appraisal, so fear works only with a credible way out. Stage models, Prochaska and DiClemente's transtheoretical model and Schwarzer's health action process approach (1992), tackle the intention-behaviour gap. Only about half of chronically ill patients adhere; clear instructions, simpler regimens and patient-centred communication help, and Szasz and Hollender's mutual participation suits chronic illness.

9.3 Stress and Physical Illness. Stressors are acute (a pop quiz) or chronic (HIV, caring for a parent with dementia). Selye's general adaptation syndrome runs through alarm, resistance and exhaustion, and Holmes and Rahe (1967) measured stress by life change. Dunbar and Alexander founded psychosomatic medicine, and Alexander's specificity hypothesis tied the holy seven (peptic ulcer, essential hypertension, bronchial asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis, neurodermatitis, thyrotoxicosis) to specific unconscious conflicts; it did not survive. Friedman and Rosenman's Type A pattern doubled coronary risk in the Western Collaborative Group Study, but hostility proved its toxic component; Type D and depression also worsen outcome. Type C and fighting spirit do not reliably affect cancer survival. HIV, Type 2 diabetes (most strongly linked to poor diet and inactivity) and chronic pain (Fordyce's operant approach, cognitive-behavioural treatment, Bonica's pain clinics) are behavioural problems too, and Ader and Cohen (1975) founded psychoneuroimmunology.

9.4 Behavioural Medicine Techniques and Lifestyle Change. Biofeedback, which grew from Neal Miller's experiments, feeds back muscle tension, skin temperature or heart rate so that they can be controlled, though for many conditions it does no better than relaxation, whose common element Benson called the relaxation response. Stress management packages combine education, relaxation, cognitive restructuring and skills. CBT for insomnia rests on Bootzin's stimulus control and Spielman's sleep restriction; sleep hygiene alone is not an effective treatment. Smoking cessation combines medication with behavioural support and the five As. The 10-item AUDIT screens drinking (8 or more suggests hazardous or harmful use), and positive screens lead to brief interventions built on FRAMES. Weight management relies on self-monitoring and stimulus control. Motivational interviewing (Miller and Rollnick) began in addiction work; Burke, Arkowitz and Menchola (2003) supported it for alcohol, drugs, and diet and exercise, but not for smoking.

9.5 Chronic Illness, Dying and Grief. Chronic illness is managed rather than cured. Moos and Tsu (1977) listed seven adaptive tasks, Leventhal's common-sense model describes illness representations (identity, cause, timeline, consequences, cure or control), Taylor's cognitive adaptation theory (1983) stresses meaning, mastery and self-esteem, and Tedeschi and Calhoun named post-traumatic growth. Glaser and Strauss (1965) described awareness contexts; Kübler-Ross (1969) proposed five stages of dying (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) that are not a fixed sequence; Cicely Saunders opened St Christopher's Hospice (1967) and introduced total pain, and palliative care belongs early. Grief has been described as grief work (Freud, Lindemann), phases (Bowlby and Parkes), tasks (Worden), oscillation (Stroebe and Schut's dual process model), continuing bonds and trajectories in which resilience is commonest (Bonanno). Doka named disenfranchised grief. A minority develop prolonged grief disorder, and help works best when targeted at them.

9.6 Community Mental Health. Albee argued that no mass disorder has been eliminated by treating individuals one at a time. Critiques of the asylum (Barton's institutional neurosis, Goffman's total institution), chlorpromazine and the cost of the hospitals led to Kennedy's Community Mental Health Centers Act of 1963 and to deinstitutionalisation, with its revolving door and transinstitutionalisation; Basaglia's Law 180 (1978) ended new admissions to Italy's asylums. Caplan (1964) brought Leavell and Clark's primary, secondary and tertiary prevention into psychiatry; Gordon (1983) and the IOM (1994) introduced universal, selective and indicated prevention, and Rose described the prevention paradox. Caplan's consultation (1970) has four types, theme interference arising in consultee-centred case consultation. Community psychology (Swampscott, 1965) and Rappaport's empowerment shift power towards communities. Task-sharing with lay counsellors (Patel's MANAS, Chibanda's Friendship Bench, Rahman's Thinking Healthy) narrows the treatment gap, and stigma (Goffman, Link and Phelan, Corrigan) is reduced most by contact.

9.7 Crisis Intervention. Crisis theory grew from Lindemann's Cocoanut Grove observations and Caplan's work: a crisis is a reaction to an obstacle that customary coping cannot overcome, not an illness, self-limiting within about four to six weeks and a turning point, and Aguilera and Messick's balancing factors (perception, support, coping) decide whether it occurs. Crises are developmental, situational, existential or ecosystemic, and small-scale (abuse, accidents, overdoses) or large-scale (floods, earthquakes). Crisis intervention is immediate, brief, focused and active; Roberts's seven stages begin with lethality assessment and end with follow-up, supportive therapy is its core, and it is the classic treatment for adjustment disorder. Suicide prevention relies on means restriction, responsible reporting (the Werther and Papageno effects), gatekeeper training, helplines and Stanley and Brown's safety plan. After disasters, WHO psychological first aid (look, listen, link) is recommended, Mitchell's critical incident stress debriefing is not, and trauma-informed care reshapes services.

9.8 Psychosocial Rehabilitation of Severe Mental Illness. Rehabilitation targets functioning, not symptoms. Anthony's psychiatric rehabilitation builds skills and supports for a setting the person chooses, and the WHO's ICF (2001) replaced impairment, disability and handicap with impairment, activity limitation and participation restriction. The Vermont study (Harding, 1987) and Deegan's account made recovery, a satisfying life with or without symptoms (CHIME), the guiding vision. Day hospitals treat, day care centres rehabilitate, halfway houses are transitional residences, and Housing First offers homes without preconditions. Sheltered workshops train then place; Becker and Drake's Individual Placement and Support places then trains and has the best evidence. Stein and Test's assertive community treatment reduces time in hospital. Case management assesses, plans, links, monitors and advocates, and its outcomes never include cure. Token economies (Ayllon and Azrin), social skills training (Liberman), family psychoeducation, the Clubhouse model (Fountain House, 1948) and peer support complete the picture.

9.9 Neuropsychological and Addiction Rehabilitation, and Disability Certification. Neuropsychological rehabilitation grew from Goldstein's and Luria's wartime work and from Zangwill's compensation, substitution and direct retraining (1947). Restorative methods (Sohlberg and Mateer's attention process training, Taub's constraint-induced movement therapy) repair function; compensatory methods (external memory aids, goal management training) bypass it, and errorless learning (Baddeley and Wilson, 1994) suits amnesia. Holistic programmes add work on awareness and emotion. In addiction, detoxification is only the doorway, and Project MATCH found three structured treatments roughly equal. Marlatt and Gordon's relapse prevention (1985) separates a lapse from a relapse, maps high-risk situations and targets the abstinence violation effect. Therapeutic communities for addiction (Synanon, 1958) are hierarchical, unlike Maxwell Jones's democratic ward. Under the RPWD Act 2016 benchmark disability is at least 40 percent; IDEAS certifies mental illness, ISAA autism, and the RCI sits under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment.

From the past papers

  1. Health psychology differs from behavioural medicine primarily in that (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Health psychology focuses on health promotion and prevention; behavioural medicine integrates behavioural and biomedical interventions (b) Health psychology only uses medication (c) Behavioural medicine does not consider behaviour (d) Health psychology is purely neurological

    Answer: (a). Behavioural medicine (Yale Conference 1977; Schwartz and Weiss, 1978) is an interdisciplinary field that integrates behavioural and biomedical knowledge and techniques, while health psychology (Matarazzo, 1980) is psychology's own contribution, with a strong emphasis on promotion and prevention. The other three options are false on their face: psychologists do not treat with medication, behaviour is in the very name of behavioural medicine, and health psychology is not a branch of neurology.

  2. Which model explains health behaviour by focussing on perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Health Belief Model (b) Theory of Planned Behaviour (c) Social Cognitive Theory (d) Transtheoretical Model

    Answer: (a) Health Belief Model. Susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers are the signature constructs of the model built by Hochbaum, Rosenstock and Kegeles from the tuberculosis screening failures of the 1950s. The theory of planned behaviour uses attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control; social cognitive theory centres on self-efficacy and outcome expectancies; the transtheoretical model describes stages of change from precontemplation to maintenance.

  3. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior, which of the following factors influences an individual's intention to engage in health behaviors? (RML 2023)

    (a) Attitude, subjective norms and self-efficacy (b) Genetics, personality traits and socio-economic status (c) Cultural beliefs, social support and health literacy (d) Physical environment, emotional intelligence and coping strategies

    Answer: (a), as keyed. Ajzen's three determinants of intention are attitude toward the behaviour, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control; the paper writes "self-efficacy" for the third, which Ajzen himself regarded as closely compatible with Bandura's concept, so the key can be accepted. In your own answers write "perceived behavioural control". None of the variables in (b), (c) or (d) is a construct of the theory.

  4. In social psychology, what is the difference between conformity and compliance? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Conformity involves a change in behaviour due to social pressure, while compliance involves following direct requests (b) Compliance involves peer pressure, while conformity does not (c) Both terms refer to obedience (d) Conformity is always involuntary, while compliance is voluntary

    Answer: (a). In social psychology, compliance is going along with a direct request, conformity is changing behaviour to match group pressure without being asked, and obedience is following an order from an authority, so (c) merges three different things and (b) reverses the facts. Do not carry over the health psychology meaning of compliance (following medical advice, now usually called adherence) when a question says "in social psychology".

  5. Which of the following would be considered an acute stressor? (RML 2024)

    (a) A pop quiz (b) HIV disease (c) Marital discord (d) Colon cancer

    Answer: (a) A pop quiz. An acute stressor is short-lived, with a clear beginning and end, usually over in minutes or hours. HIV disease, colon cancer and a long-running marital conflict persist for months or years with no clear end, which makes them chronic stressors.

  6. Alarm, resistance and exhaustion describe three stages represented in which model of stress? (RML 2020)

    (a) Life events theory (b) Protection motivation theory (c) Selye's general adaptation syndrome (d) Health belief model

    Answer: (c) Selye's general adaptation syndrome. Hans Selye described the same nonspecific three-stage response in rats exposed to very different noxious agents. Life events theory is Holmes and Rahe's counting of life change units (1967), and protection motivation theory and the health belief model are models of health behaviour, not of stress.

  7. Which of the following factors is most directly linked to a higher risk for coronary heart disease? (RML 2024)

    (a) Type A personality (b) Type B personality (c) Hostility (d) Self-efficacy

    Answer: (a) Type A personality, as keyed. This is the classic textbook answer: in the Western Collaborative Group Study, Friedman and Rosenman's Type A men had about twice the coronary risk of Type B men. Strictly, hostility is the component of Type A that carries most of the risk, so choose hostility if a question asks for the toxic component. Type B is the low-risk pattern, and self-efficacy is a belief about one's ability, not a coronary risk factor.

  8. Which of the following factors is most strongly associated with the development of Type 2 diabetes? (RML 2023)

    (a) Genetics (b) Poor diet and sedentary lifestyle (c) Psychological stress (d) Exposure to environmental toxins

    Answer: (b) Poor diet and sedentary lifestyle. These act largely through obesity, and the Diabetes Prevention Program showed that changing them cut the incidence of diabetes by 58 percent. Genes are a real and tempting contributor (South Asians develop the disease at lower body weights), but lifestyle is the strongest and most modifiable factor; stress and toxins play smaller roles.

  9. Which of the following is NOT listed by Burke, Arkowitz and Menchola (2003) as being treatable with motivational interviewing? (RML 2022)

    (a) Substance-related disorders (b) Smoking cessation (c) Diabetes mellitus (d) Obsessive-compulsive disorder

    Answer: (d) Obsessive-compulsive disorder. (The paper misspells the third author as "Mechola".) Their meta-analysis of 30 trials covered alcohol, drugs, diet and exercise (including work with obese women with Type 2 diabetes) and smoking, though for smoking the results did not support efficacy. OCD was not among the problems studied; it is treated with exposure and response prevention.

  10. Motivational interviewing is used in the management of: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Non-compliance to treatment (b) Substance abuse (c) Anxious-avoidant personality disorder (d) Bipolar disorder

    Answer: (b) Substance abuse. Miller and Rollnick created motivational interviewing in the addictions field, as an alternative to the confrontational style of older alcohol treatment, and substance use remains its original and best-established use. It is also used to improve adherence, which makes (a) tempting, but it is not a treatment for anxious-avoidant personality disorder or bipolar disorder as such.

  11. When crisis results in bereavement, all of the following approaches to bereavement should be considered EXCEPT: (RML 2020)

    (a) Bereavement is a normal process that will run its course (b) Complicated grieving requires intervention (c) Bereavement counselling, when needed, is best conducted one-by-one (d) Crisis related bereavement should always be considered traumatic grief

    Answer: (d). Most people bereaved in a crisis grieve normally and recover with ordinary support, so treating all of them as traumatised wastes help and may hinder recovery; "always" is the warning word. The other three are sound: grief usually runs its course, complicated grief responds to targeted treatment such as Shear's complicated grief treatment, and specialist grief work is paced to the individual, one to one.

  12. Which of the following is a proposed criterion set in the current DSM that involves cutting, burning, or otherwise intentionally hurting one's own body without the intent to kill oneself? (RML 2022)

    (a) Internet gaming disorder (b) Attenuated psychosis syndrome (c) Persistent complex bereavement (d) Non-suicidal self-injury

    Answer: (d) Non-suicidal self-injury. It is a Section III proposal: deliberate self-injury without intent to die, usually to relieve tension or distress. The other three were also Section III proposals in DSM-5 but describe different problems: impairing loss of control over gaming, mild subthreshold psychotic symptoms, and prolonged grief (which DSM-5-TR made an official diagnosis, prolonged grief disorder, in 2022).

  13. Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) does NOT typically include which of the following techniques or focus areas? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Role playing and role reversal techniques (b) Grief and bereavement work (c) Role conflict resolution (d) Role transition support

    Answer: (a), as keyed. Grief, interpersonal role disputes and role transitions are three of IPT's four problem areas (the fourth is interpersonal deficits), so the answer follows by elimination. Role reversal belongs to Moreno's psychodrama and Gestalt-style enactment. Do not overlearn the key: IPT manuals do list brief role play, rehearsing a difficult conversation, among their techniques; what IPT lacks is staged enactment and role reversal.

  14. Which of the following is an example of primary prevention in health psychology? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Promoting vaccination to prevent disease (b) Treating a diagnosed illness (c) Providing rehabilitation after injury (d) Surgery for chronic disease

    Answer: (a). Primary prevention acts on healthy people before a disorder exists and lowers its incidence (Leavell and Clark's scheme, brought into psychiatry by Caplan in 1964). Treating a diagnosed illness is secondary prevention, or simply treatment; rehabilitation after injury and surgery to limit the damage of a chronic disease deal with an established condition, which is tertiary prevention.

  15. There are small-scale events that can perpetuate a 'crisis'. All of the following are such events EXCEPT: (RML 2020)

    (a) tornado or flood (b) violent events such as child abuse, domestic abuse, crime (c) psychopathology that triggers a crisis, such as a suicide attempt or drug overdose (d) car accident, life-threatening illness

    Answer: (a) tornado or flood. Small-scale events strike one person or one family: violence, crises triggered by psychopathology and personal catastrophes such as accidents and serious illness. A tornado or flood affects a whole community at once, so it is a large-scale event (an ecosystemic crisis in James and Gilliland's terms) that calls for a community-wide disaster response.

  16. The treatment of choice for Adjustment disorder is: (RML 2024)

    (a) Psychoanalytic psychotherapy (b) Group psychotherapy (c) Cognitive behaviour therapy (d) Crisis intervention

    Answer: (d) Crisis intervention. Adjustment disorder arises within three months of an identifiable stressor and usually settles within six months, and crisis intervention is built for exactly this kind of short reaction to an identifiable stressor. Psychoanalytic and group psychotherapy are long and not focused on the precipitating event; brief problem-focused and cognitive behavioural therapies are also used, but crisis intervention is the classic answer.

  17. Which therapy is mostly needed in 'Crisis Management'? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Cognitive behavior therapy (b) Acceptance and commitment therapy (c) Expressive therapy (d) Supportive therapy

    Answer: (d) Supportive therapy. In the acute phase a person in crisis needs ventilation, realistic reassurance, information and guidance, environmental manipulation and support for existing coping, which are the techniques of supportive psychotherapy. CBT is useful once the person is stable, acceptance and commitment therapy builds flexibility over many sessions, and expressive therapies (art, music, dance and movement, drama) may help in the following weeks, but none is the first response.

  18. The task or role performance model of case management is based on all of the following premises EXCEPT: (RML 2020)

    (a) It is consistent with structure-function theory applied at the organisational level (b) Workers are expected to use their authority to provide, deny, or withdraw services based on a client's compliance with assigned tasks (c) Workers are expected to form a power alliance with the client (d) Case managers have both a fiduciary and a humanitarian obligation to clients

    Answer: (c). The task or role performance model applies structure-function theory (the structural functionalism of Talcott Parsons) to the agency, gives the worker real authority to provide, deny or withdraw services according to the client's compliance with tasks, and binds the case manager to fiduciary and humanitarian duties. Forming a power alliance with the client is the mark of the empowerment or advocacy models, which reject that hierarchy.

  19. The desired outcomes of case management consist of all of the following EXCEPT: (RML 2020)

    (a) Cure of a primary condition (b) Environmental accommodations (c) Skill training and acquisitions (d) Empowerment

    Answer: (a) Cure of a primary condition. Case management serves people with long-term conditions and aims at living with them as well as possible: continuity of care, access to services, community tenure, skill training, environmental accommodations, empowerment and quality of life. Treating the primary condition is the business of medicine and psychotherapy, which case management coordinates but does not claim to cure.
