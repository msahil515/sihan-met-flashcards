Southern France, January 1800. On a winter morning a boy walked out of the woods near the village of Saint-Sernin-sur-Rance, in the district of Aveyron, looking for food. He was naked but for the rags of a shirt, scarred, about twelve years old, and he could not speak. Local people had glimpsed a wild child in the forests for years, and more than once he had been caught and had escaped. This time he stayed. Within months he was in Paris, the most talked-about child in France, and learned men came to see what a human being is like when society has had no hand in shaping him.

The verdict of the greatest authority was harsh. Philippe Pinel, reformer of the Paris asylums, examined the boy and judged him an incurable idiot, perhaps abandoned because he was defective from birth. A 26-year-old physician at the Paris institution for deaf-mutes, Jean-Marc-Gaspard Itard, disagreed. The boy, he argued, was backward because he had grown up alone; give him the experiences he had missed and he would become fully human. Itard named him Victor and, with his carer Madame Guérin, worked with him daily for about five years, training his senses, his attention and his feelings, and trying above all to teach him language.

The results, reported in 1801 and 1806, were mixed. Victor came to recognise written words, follow instructions, show affection for Madame Guérin and protest when he was punished unjustly. But he never learned to speak more than a word or two, and Itard ended the lessons disappointed. Victor lived quietly with Madame Guérin in Paris until his death in 1828, aged about forty. Was his failure born or made, and had the teaching simply come too late? This chapter gives you the tools to answer.

6.1 Foundations: The Lifespan View, Key Issues and Ecological ContextsLower yield · 13 Qs

Before the story of how a fertilised cell becomes an ageing adult, you need its frame: what developmental change means, what drives it, the questions still argued over, and the contexts in which every child grows.

The science of change and stability

Developmental psychology is the scientific study of change and stability from conception to death. It takes the developmental perspective, the approach concerned with the changes that occur as people mature. The other perspectives of Section 1.2 ask other questions: the psychoanalytic about unconscious conflicts rooted in childhood, the biological about genes, brain and hormones, and the humanistic about free choice and self-actualisation.

Keep two kinds of change apart. Growth is quantitative change, an increase in size or amount: height, weight, vocabulary. Development is qualitative change in structure and organisation, an orderly, progressive series of changes towards maturity: the child does not just know more words but starts combining them into sentences. Change runs through three interacting domains: physical (body, brain, motor skills), cognitive (memory, language, thinking) and psychosocial, or socioemotional (emotions, personality, relationships).

The lifespan is divided into periods, but the divisions are a social construction, an idea a society shares rather than a fact of nature: adolescence became a recognised stage only with industrialisation. Indian courses follow Elizabeth Hurlock (1898 to 1988), whose Developmental Psychology: A Life-Span Approach marks ten periods (Table 6.1).

Older texts treated development as ending with adolescence. Paul Baltes (1939 to 2006) countered in 1987 with the lifespan perspective: development is lifelong (no age holds supremacy), multidimensional (physical, cognitive and social strands), multidirectional (some abilities rise while others fall), plastic (open to change at every age), contextual (embedded in history and culture) and multidisciplinary, and every age brings both gains and losses (an older adult loses speed but may gain wisdom). Baltes named three kinds of influence. Normative age-graded influences are tied to age and shared by most people (puberty, retirement). Normative history-graded influences are shared by a cohort, a generation, because of when it lived (Partition, the COVID-19 pandemic). Non-normative influences are unusual events in one life (a parent's early death). Longitudinal, cross-sectional and cross-sequential designs, which separate age changes from cohort effects, are in Chapter 9; infant research methods are in Section 6.3.

From philosophers to baby biographies

Two philosophers framed the debate. John Locke (1632 to 1704) held that the newborn mind is a tabula rasa, a blank slate written on by experience: the root of the nurture view. Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712 to 1778), in Émile (1762), saw the child as a noble savage, born good, whose natural plan of growth unfolds in stages: the root of the nature, maturation and stage views.

Scientific observation began with the baby biography, a detailed diary of one child. Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882) published one on his first son in 1877, and Wilhelm Preyer (1841 to 1897) followed with The Mind of the Child (1882), often taken as the start of scientific child psychology. G. Stanley Hall (1844 to 1924) led the child study movement with questionnaires given to large numbers of children; his Adolescence (1904) called the teens a time of storm and stress (Section 6.10) and proposed recapitulation, the idea that each child's development replays the evolutionary history of the species. Hall's student Arnold Gesell (1880 to 1961) took the normative approach, observing many children to find the typical age, or norm, of each behaviour. Against him stood the behaviourist John B. Watson (1878 to 1958), who boasted that he could train any healthy infant into any kind of specialist.

Maturation and learning

Gesell's lasting idea is maturation, the biological unfolding of genetic potential: changes that appear on a genetic timetable with little dependence on practice. Learning is change through experience and practice. Maturation supplies readiness; learning builds on it. Three terms that depend on experience are not maturation: socialisation, acquiring the values, norms and behaviour of one's society; accommodation, Jean Piaget's term for changing a mental scheme to fit new information (Section 6.4); and conditioning, learning by association (Section 1.6).

Key study: Gesell and Thompson's co-twin control (1929)

Can practice hurry an unready body? Gesell and Helen Thompson studied identical twin girls, T and C, holding heredity constant: the method of co-twin control. Twin T practised stair climbing daily for six weeks from 46 weeks of age. Twin C's training began only at 53 weeks, yet after two weeks she had caught up. Maturation, not early training, set the pace. Criticism: one pair of twins and a narrow set of skills.

Myrtle McGraw (1899 to 1988) refined the point in Growth: A Study of Johnny and Jimmy (1935). Intensive training from infancy did not hurry Johnny's phylogenetic skills, those common to the species (crawling, walking), but it advanced ontogenetic skills particular to the individual (swimming, roller skating) once he was ready; his twin Jimmy was left untrained. So a child's increasing skill at using his muscles is due mainly to maturation, not to the opportunity to exercise, pushing by parents or learning. Gesell turned such observations into age norms and developmental schedules for motor, adaptive, language and personal-social behaviour (the scales are in Chapter 2).

Exam trap

"The biological unfolding of genetic potential" is maturation (Gesell), never socialisation, accommodation or conditioning. A child's growing muscular skill is credited to maturation, not exercise, parental pushing or learning. Growth is quantitative (size, amount); development is qualitative (structure, organisation).

Early childhood tasks include walking, talking, taking solid food and controlling elimination; middle childhood brings the skills of ordinary games, getting along with age-mates, and reading, writing and arithmetic. Continuity versus discontinuity asks whether development is gradual, quantitative accumulation, a ramp, or a series of qualitatively different stages, a staircase.

Try a worked example. In one Native American tribe, mothers answer an infant's distress at once and hold the baby almost constantly for three years; for generations the children have grown up quiet, even-tempered, obedient and respectful. Is the calm inborn? No: it follows a child-rearing practice handed down in the culture, so it is learnt behaviour, acquired through experience. Innate behaviour appears without experience, as reflexes do. The calm is also overt behaviour (observable action), not covert behaviour (private thoughts and feelings), and "complex behaviour" describes how many parts an act has, not where it comes from.

Exam trap

Behaviour that follows a child-rearing practice handed down in a culture is learnt; stability across generations is not proof of heredity. Stage theorists (Freud, Piaget, Erikson) take the discontinuity side; learning and information-processing theorists take the continuity side.

Critical and sensitive periods

A critical period is a fixed window in which a particular experience must occur for normal development; if it passes without that experience, the effects are largely irreversible. The model case comes from ethology, the study of behaviour in its evolutionary setting: Konrad Lorenz (1903 to 1989) reported in 1935 that greylag goslings imprint on the first moving object they see after hatching, even Lorenz himself (Section 6.7). In human development the clearest critical periods are in the embryo: each organ forms within a brief window, so a teratogen such as thalidomide or rubella seriously damages an organ only if it strikes while that organ is forming, mostly in weeks 3 to 8 (Section 6.2).

A sensitive period is an optimal but more flexible window: the experience matters most then, but some development remains possible later (Figure 6.1). First language is the standard case. Eric Lenneberg (1967) proposed a critical period for language from about age two to puberty (Section 1.8); the evidence now favours a sensitive period. Genie, found in Los Angeles in 1970 at 13 after years of isolation, learned many words but never mastered grammar. Such lasting limits after early language deprivation support sensitive periods. They say nothing about semantics (word meaning) or syntax (rules of word order), which are levels of language, or about nativism, the view of Noam Chomsky (born 1928) that language ability is innate.

Can later experience repair a poor start? Ann Clarke and Alan Clarke argued that it often can in Early Experience: Myth and Evidence (1976). The strongest test is the English and Romanian Adoptees study led by Michael Rutter (1933 to 2021): children adopted into British homes from Romanian orphanages after 1989 caught up almost fully if adopted before about six months, and greatly, though some kept lasting difficulties, if adopted later (Section 6.7). Later experience compensating for earlier deficits contradicts the strict critical-period view. Sensitive periods allow late recovery by definition; continuity concerns gradual versus stepwise change, and stability whether traits persist.

Exam trap

RML 2024 asked for the strongest evidence for critical periods "in the case of human development" and keyed imprinting. Imprinting is the classic critical period, but it is animal evidence. Within human development the clearest critical periods are in embryonic development (teratogen timing); language shows a sensitive period, and vocational interests show none. Expect imprinting as the key; know why embryonic development is the better human answer.

Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems

Most theories put the child under a microscope; Urie Bronfenbrenner (1917 to 2005) widened the lens. His ecological systems theory, set out in The Ecology of Human Development (1979), places the child inside environmental systems nested like Russian dolls (Figure 6.2), each affecting the child and affected in turn.

  • The microsystem is the innermost layer: the settings in which the child takes part directly, face to face, such as family, school, peer group and neighbourhood. Influence is bidirectional: parents shape the child, and the child shapes the parents.
  • The mesosystem is the set of links between microsystems: home and school (a parent-teacher meeting), family and peers. It is a relationship between settings, not a setting.
  • The exosystem holds settings the child is not in that still affect the child: a parent's workplace (a night shift), the school board, the mass media, extended family.
  • The macrosystem is the overarching culture: values, laws, ideology and the economic system.
  • The chronosystem, added later, is time: transitions such as parental divorce and historical events such as a pandemic.

Later Bronfenbrenner gave biology more weight. In the bioecological model, developed with Stephen Ceci (1994) and later with Pamela Morris (1998), development is the product of Process-Person-Context-Time (PPCT). Its engine is the proximal process: regular, increasingly complex, two-way interaction between the child and nearby people, objects and symbols, such as a parent reading with a child every night. Person characteristics (temperament, abilities) shape these processes, context is the five systems, and time runs from moments to history. Bronfenbrenner also helped found Head Start (1965), the American preschool programme for poor children.

Exam trap

Family, school and peer group are all microsystem; the immediate family is never meso-, exo- or macrosystem. The mesosystem is a link (school-family, parent-teacher), not a setting. The exosystem does not contain the child (the parent's workplace). Culture is macrosystem; divorce is chronosystem. Nested systems are Bronfenbrenner's, not Skinner's (operant learning), Gesell's (maturation) or Hall's (child study).

6.2 Heredity, Prenatal Development, Birth and the NewbornLower yield · 7 Qs

Every life begins as one cell; nine months later a newborn arrives able to suck, startle and grip. This section follows that journey, from genes to the newborn's first reflexes.

Genes, chromosomes and conception

The nucleus of each body cell holds 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent. A chromosome is a long strand of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid); a gene is a segment of DNA that codes for a protein. Twenty-two pairs are autosomes; the twenty-third, the sex chromosomes, is XX in females and XY in males. Body cells multiply by mitosis into identical copies; sperm and ova form by meiosis, which halves the number to 23 and reshuffles genes, so siblings differ. At conception, usually in a fallopian tube, sperm and ovum unite into one cell, the zygote; since every ovum carries an X, the father's X or Y sperm decides the child's sex. A zygote that splits yields monozygotic twins (identical, sharing all their genes); two ova fertilised by two sperm yield dizygotic twins (fraternal, sharing about half, like any siblings).

Patterns of inheritance

Each gene comes in alternative forms called alleles. The alleles a person carries are the genotype; the observable traits, shaped also by the environment, are the phenotype. Two identical alleles make a person homozygous for that gene; two different ones, heterozygous.

In dominant-recessive inheritance, the pattern Gregor Mendel (1822 to 1884) reported from pea plants in 1866, a heterozygote shows only the dominant allele's trait; the recessive allele is masked but can be passed on, so the person is a carrier. Let blue eyes (D) be dominant and green (d) recessive: DD and Dd children have blue eyes, dd children green. Figure 6.3 shows why two blue-eyed Dd parents can have a green-eyed child.

Exam trap

Heterozygous does not mean blended. A Dd child shows the dominant phenotype (blue). Blue-green would need incomplete dominance, and "cannot be determined" is wrong whenever dominance is stated.

Two carriers thus face a one-in-four risk, in each pregnancy, of a child with a recessive disorder: phenylketonuria (PKU), cystic fibrosis (thick mucus clogs lungs and pancreas), sickle-cell anaemia or Tay-Sachs disease (fatal nervous system degeneration in early childhood). In PKU the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase is missing, so phenylalanine builds up and damages the brain; newborn screening and a low-phenylalanine diet prevent the intellectual disability. A dominant disorder needs one copy: Huntington's disease (chorea and dementia) appears in midlife, often after it has been passed on. Genetic counselling estimates such risks from family history and tests.

In incomplete dominance the heterozygote is intermediate (sickle-cell carriers sickle mildly when oxygen is low); in codominance both alleles are fully expressed (A and B alleles give blood group AB). In X-linked inheritance a recessive allele on the X is usually masked by a female's second X, but a male has none, so haemophilia, red-green colour blindness and Duchenne muscular dystrophy strike mainly males, through carrier mothers. Polygenic inheritance, many genes of small effect, underlies continuous traits such as height and intelligence. In genomic imprinting one parent's allele is chemically silenced, so the effect depends on which parent it came from. A mutation is a sudden, permanent change in DNA, spontaneous or caused by radiation or chemicals.

Chromosomal abnormalities

Whole chromosomes can be extra or missing, usually because a pair fails to separate in meiosis (nondisjunction), and parts can be lost (deletion). Down syndrome, described by John Langdon Down (1828 to 1896) in 1866, is usually trisomy 21, three copies of chromosome 21; its risk rises with maternal age. It is the commonest genetic cause of intellectual disability; fragile X is the commonest inherited one (Table 6.5).

Heredity and environment

How far is a trait inherited? (The nature-nurture debate itself is in Section 6.1.) Family studies are weak evidence, since relatives share homes as well as genes. Adoption studies ask whether adopted children resemble their biological or adoptive parents; twin studies, whether identical (MZ) twins are more alike than fraternal (DZ) twins. Most informative are MZ twins adopted by different parents: identical genes, different homes, so their similarities reflect heredity and their differences environment. MZ twins reared together share genes and home, confounding the two; DZ twins or siblings adopted apart share only about half their genes. Albert Stunkard and colleagues (1990) found identical twins reared apart nearly as alike in body-mass index as those reared together. Heritability, the proportion of variation in a trait within a population due to genetic differences, describes groups, not individuals (IQ figures: Section 1.9; behaviour genetics: Chapter 5).

Genes and environment also work together. Sandra Scarr and Kathleen McCartney (1983) described three kinds of gene-environment correlation: passive (parents supply both genes and a matching home: musical parents, a musical house), strongest in infancy; evocative (a child's traits evoke responses: a cheerful baby draws more play); and active, or niche-picking (children seek settings that suit them), which grows with age. In gene-environment interaction one environment affects genotypes differently: an ordinary diet harms only the child with PKU. Irving Gottesman's (1963) reaction range is the band of outcomes a genotype permits, the environment deciding where in it a person falls. Conrad Hal Waddington's (1905 to 1975) canalisation is heredity's channelling of some traits, such as early motor milestones, to the same outcome in almost any environment. Epigenetics studies how experience switches genes on or off (for example by DNA methylation) without changing the DNA sequence.

The three prenatal periods

Prenatal development lasts about 38 weeks from conception, in three periods (Figure 6.4). In the germinal period, about the first two weeks, the zygote divides (cleavage) as it travels down the fallopian tube, becoming a solid ball, the morula (about day 4), then a hollow blastocyst (about day 5), whose inner cell mass becomes the baby and outer layer the placenta. Implantation in the uterine wall ends the period.

The embryonic period (weeks 3 to 8) opens with gastrulation, when the inner cell mass forms three germ layers: ectoderm (nervous system, skin), mesoderm (muscle, bone, heart, blood) and endoderm (gut, lungs, glands). The nervous system begins here: the neural plate arises from ectoderm and folds into the neural tube, the future brain and spinal cord, by about week 4. Because every major organ forms now (organogenesis), this period is the most vulnerable to teratogens. The fetal period (week 9 to birth) brings growth and refinement: quickening, the first movements the mother feels, at about 16 to 20 weeks, and the age of viability, when a baby born early may survive with intensive care, at about 22 to 24 weeks.

Exam trap

The nervous system begins at the gastrula stage, not at the zygote, morula or blastocyst stage, whose cells have not yet formed germ layers. The embryonic, not the fetal, period is the most vulnerable.

Teratogens, maternal factors and prenatal tests

A teratogen is any environmental agent that harms the developing child. Birth and assessing the newborn

Labour has three stages: dilation of the cervix (the longest), delivery of the baby, and delivery of the placenta. Its main danger is anoxia, too little oxygen (as when the cord is squeezed), which can damage the brain and cause cerebral palsy. A preterm baby is born before 37 weeks, perhaps at a weight right for its age; a baby small for gestational age weighs less than expected for its age, even at term, and usually fares worse. Low birth weight means under 2,500 g. Kangaroo care, skin-to-skin holding on a parent's chest, steadies temperature, breathing and heart rate and improves survival.

The Apgar scale, devised in 1952 (published 1953) by the anaesthesiologist Virginia Apgar (1909 to 1974), rates five signs 0, 1 or 2 (total 10) at 1 and 5 minutes: appearance (colour), pulse, grimace (reflex irritability), activity (muscle tone) and respiration. A total of 7 or more is good; 3 or less is an emergency. The Neonatal Behavioral Assessment Scale (1973) of the paediatrician T. Berry Brazelton (1918 to 2018) assesses reflexes, muscle tone, state changes and responses to people.

Peter Wolff (1966) distinguished the newborn's states of arousal: regular (non-REM) sleep, irregular (REM) sleep, drowsiness, quiet alertness, and waking activity and crying. Newborns sleep about 16 to 18 hours a day, half of it REM sleep. Sudden infant death syndrome (unexplained death of a sleeping infant under one year) fell sharply after back-to-sleep campaigns taught parents to lay babies on their backs.

6.3 Infancy: Brain, Body, Motor Skills and PerceptionLower yield · 12 Qs

A newborn cannot hold up her own head. A year later she walks across the room and picks a grain of rice off the floor between thumb and finger. This section follows that change through brain, body, movement and the senses, and asks how we can know what a baby who cannot speak perceives and learns.

Physical growth in the first two years

Birth weight roughly doubles by about 5 months and triples by the first birthday. Growth has two directions. In the cephalocaudal trend (Latin, "head to tail") the head grows first and fastest; it is a quarter of body length at birth but an eighth in adulthood. In the proximodistal trend growth runs from the centre outward, trunk before arms and arms before hands. Breast milk is the ideal food. Severe malnutrition causes marasmus, wasting of the whole body from too little food of any kind, usually in the first year, or kwashiorkor, a protein deficiency after weaning in which belly and limbs swell with fluid.

The developing brain

Neurogenesis, the birth of new neurons, is largely prenatal. Why, then, does the brain grow so much after birth? Because existing neurons form connections and gain insulation. In synaptogenesis the infant brain forms far more synapses than it will keep, and synaptic pruning then removes the rarely used ones and keeps those used often: "use it or lose it". Pruning is a process of neuronal development and plasticity that fits circuits to the child's own experience, continuing in the prefrontal cortex through adolescence (Section 5.5). Myelination, the wrapping of axons in fatty myelin that speeds conduction, reaches the prefrontal cortex last, continuing into early adulthood. Together these take the brain from about 25 percent of adult weight at birth to about half by 6 months, 75 percent by age 2 and 90 percent by age 5 to 6 (Figure 6.5).

William Greenough and colleagues (1987) distinguished two kinds of plasticity. Experience-expectant plasticity depends on experiences every member of the species can count on, such as patterned light and speech; the brain overproduces synapses in readiness and keeps those the expected input uses. Hence sensitive periods: a cataract left untreated in an infant's eye leaves lasting poor vision. Experience-dependent plasticity builds connections from experiences unique to the individual and continues for life.

Exam trap

Synaptic pruning belongs to neuronal development and plasticity, not memory consolidation (stabilising a new memory, Section 1.7), neurotransmitter release (the moment-to-moment signal at a synapse) or long-term potentiation (lasting strengthening of a synapse by repeated stimulation, Chapter 5). Half of adult brain size is keyed at about 6 months, not birth, 1 year or 3 years; some texts imply nearer the first year, so treat these percentages as approximate.

Principles and milestones of motor development

Motor control follows the same two directions as growth (Figure 6.6). By the cephalocaudal principle it spreads from head to foot: the infant holds up her head before she sits, and sits before she stands. By the proximodistal principle it spreads from the centre of the body to the extremities: she swings her whole arm at a toy months before her fingers can pick it up. Development also runs from gross motor skills (large movements of trunk and limbs, such as walking) to fine motor skills (precise movements of hands and fingers), and from mass to specific: shown a toy, a young infant waves, kicks and wriggles all at once, and only later reaches with one hand. Finally, motor development shapes social and behavioural expectations: a child who walks and feeds herself is treated differently and expected to do more.

Fine motor skills: from reflex to pincer grasp

The hand tells the proximodistal story in miniature. The newborn's palmar grasp reflex closes her fingers round anything pressed into her palm. Her prereaching is poorly aimed swiping at objects in view, fading after about 2 months. At 3 to 4 months voluntary reaching brings the first voluntary grasp, the ulnar grasp: the fingers close clumsily against the palm, holding the object on the little-finger (ulnar) side. By 4 to 5 months she transfers objects from hand to hand. Control then shifts to the thumb side in radial grasps (the radius is the forearm bone on the thumb side), and by about 9 to 12 months the pincer grasp, thumb against forefinger, lifts that grain of rice.

Exam trap

The keyed first grasp is the ulnar grasp, the first voluntary one; the palmar grasp at birth is a reflex. Texts order the middle steps differently (one even puts a clumsy palmar grip before the ulnar grasp), but the pincer grasp always comes last.

Explaining motor development

Why do babies sit, crawl and walk in this order? Arnold Gesell (1880 to 1961) gave the maturational view: motor development unfolds a genetically programmed sequence as nerves and muscles mature, so practice adds little (his co-twin study is in Section 6.1). Experience matters more than he thought. Wayne Dennis and Marsena Dennis (1940) found that Hopi infants bound to cradleboards walked at about the same age as unbound Hopi infants, which seemed to favour maturation; but in Iranian orphanages, where babies lay on their backs with almost no chance to move, Wayne Dennis (1960) found many 3- and 4-year-olds who could not walk alone. Cultures that train sitting and walking, such as the Kipsigis of Kenya, see these skills earlier. Maturation is necessary but not sufficient.

The modern account is dynamic systems theory, applied to motor skills by Esther Thelen (1941 to 2004): a skill is assembled from the body's current size and strength, the task and the support the environment offers. Take the newborn stepping reflex, which seems to vanish at about 2 months. It vanishes, Thelen showed, because the legs grow heavier faster than the muscles grow stronger: with her legs held in water, the infant steps again.

Methods for studying infant perception

How can you know what a baby perceives? Watch where she looks, how hard she sucks and how her brain responds. Robert Fantz (1961) used preferential looking: two stimuli are shown side by side and an observer times the looking at each. Infants from a few days to 6 months old looked longest at a face, then at patterns such as newsprint and a bull's-eye, and least at plain discs: they prefer patterns, and faces most of all.

The habituation-dishabituation method needs no favourite. Show a picture again and again and looking falls (habituation, the simplest form of learning); show a new one, and if looking recovers (dishabituation) the infant has noticed the difference. It is the standard test of discrimination and categorisation before language: habituate a baby to many cats, then show a new cat and a dog, and extra looking at the dog shows a category "cat". In high-amplitude sucking, strong sucks on a recording dummy switch on a sound, and sucking revives when a new sound is noticed. In the violation-of-expectation method of Renée Baillargeon (1985), infants look longer at an impossible event than a possible one, revealing what they expect of objects (Section 6.4). Evoked potentials, electrical brain responses recorded from the scalp, show whether the brain registers a difference when behaviour shows nothing.

Exam trap

Habituation paradigms assess discrimination and categorisation before language, not long-term declarative memory (recognition here lasts minutes within one session), object permanence specifically (tested by violation of expectation) or moral reasoning (Section 6.9).

The infant's senses

Every sense works at birth, but vision is the least mature. Newborn visual acuity, the sharpness of sight, is poor (about 20/200 to 20/600, against the adult 20/20) but improves rapidly over the first year. Colour vision is present by about 2 to 3 months, and newborns already prefer faces.

Hearing works before birth. Anthony DeCasper and William Fifer (1980) found that newborns under 3 days old sucked in whichever pattern played their own mother's voice rather than another woman's. DeCasper and Melanie Spence (1986) had mothers read The Cat in the Hat aloud in late pregnancy; after birth the babies sucked to hear that story rather than a new one. Infants at first hear speech-sound contrasts their own language does not use, then lose them: perceptual narrowing. Janet Werker and Richard Tees (1984) found that most English-learning infants of 6 to 8 months heard the Hindi contrast between a dental and a retroflex "ta", but few did by 10 to 12 months.

Taste and smell are keen at birth: newborns relax at sweet tastes and grimace at bitter, and breastfed babies about a week old turn towards a pad their own mother has worn. Touch is well developed, and newborns feel pain. Intermodal perception, combining several senses into one event, appears early: Elizabeth Spelke (1976) showed 4-month-olds two films side by side with one soundtrack, and they looked longer at the film that matched the sound.

Depth, locomotion and affordances

Eleanor Gibson and Richard Walk (1960) built the visual cliff, a glass-topped table with a checkerboard just under the glass on the shallow side and far below it on the deep side (full study in Section 1.4). Infants of crawling age, about 6 months onward, crossed the shallow side to their mothers but refused the deep side. Depth perception is therefore present by the time infants crawl: not fully formed at birth, not delayed until age 2 and not taught in words. Chicks and young goats tested within a day of birth also avoided the drop, which Gibson and Walk read as evidence that depth perception is largely innate.

Is the crawler's fear inborn? Joseph Campos and colleagues measured heart rate on the cliff. Two-month-olds on the deep side showed a slowing heart rate, a sign of interest; crawlers showed a quickening one, a sign of fear, and the more weeks an infant had crawled, the warier she was. Locomotor experience brings wariness of heights.

The ecological view of James J. Gibson (1904 to 1979) and Eleanor Gibson explains why: we perceive the world in terms of what we can do in it. An affordance is an action possibility the environment offers a particular perceiver: a slope affords crawling down to an experienced crawler but a fall to a novice. Because affordances depend on the perceiver's abilities, the change that most transforms them is independent locomotion: once the infant crawls and walks, distant toys become reachable and drops dangerous. Starting to speak, the attachment relationship and new caretaking reshape her social world, but none changes what her surroundings afford her actions nearly as much.

Exam trap

The visual cliff tested depth perception, present by crawling age, not colour perception, object permanence (Section 6.4), language comprehension, imprinting (Section 6.7) or audio-visual integration (intermodal perception). Toddlers' affordances change most with independent locomotion.

Learning, imitation and the milestone sequence

Infants learn from birth. Newborns can be classically conditioned: stroke a newborn's forehead just before giving sweet water and soon the stroke alone sets off sucking. In Carolyn Rovee-Collier's operant studies a ribbon tied a 2- or 3-month-old's ankle to a mobile, so kicking made it move; days later infants kicked again at the sight of the same mobile, showing memory long before language.

6.4 Piaget's Theory of Cognitive DevelopmentHigh yield · 43 Qs

Pour a five-year-old's juice from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin one and she will say she now has more, although she watched every drop. She is not careless; she is reasoning with the tools she has. A Swiss biologist built the most influential theory of children's thinking on mistakes like hers. This section follows it from method and core concepts through four stages and beyond, then weighs the evidence.

Piaget and his method

Jean Piaget (1896 to 1980) was a biologist first, with a 1918 doctorate on molluscs. Around 1920, in the Paris laboratory of Théodore Simon, co-author with Alfred Binet (1857 to 1911) of the first intelligence scale (1905; Section 1.9), he scored children's answers to reasoning items. The wrong answers fascinated him: children of the same age made the same kinds of error, so younger children seemed not duller than older ones but to think differently.

In Geneva from 1921 he pursued one question for sixty years: how does knowledge grow? He called the field genetic epistemology, the study of the origins (genesis) of knowledge. His tool was the clinical method (méthode clinique), a flexible interview that follows each answer with further questions to uncover the reasoning behind it; for infancy he observed his own three children closely. His name-year books are The Language and Thought of the Child (1923), The Moral Judgment of the Child (1932; Section 6.9), The Origins of Intelligence in Children (1936) and The Construction of Reality in the Child (1937); his stages of play are in Section 6.8.

Piaget treated intelligence as biological adaptation: as a species adapts through bodily structures, a child adapts through ever better mental structures. In his constructivism the child is an active "little scientist" who builds knowledge by acting on objects, first physically and later mentally.

Exam trap

Piaget = intelligence as a biological adaptation, and the stages of cognitive development. Free association is Sigmund Freud's (1856 to 1939), operant conditioning Burrhus Frederic Skinner's (1904 to 1990) and aggression as learned social behaviour Albert Bandura's (1925 to 2021). Psychosocial stages belong to Erik Erikson (1902 to 1994) and moral stages to Lawrence Kohlberg (1927 to 1987).

Schemes, adaptation and equilibration

The unit of knowledge is the scheme (often called a schema), an organised pattern of action or thought for dealing with a class of experiences: a newborn's sucking and grasping, a ten-year-old's adding and classifying. Two functional invariants shape schemes at every age. Organisation combines simple schemes into larger systems, as looking and grasping merge into reaching for what one sees. Adaptation adjusts schemes to the environment through two complementary processes.

In assimilation the child fits new experience into an existing scheme: a two-year-old whose "doggie" scheme means "four-legged animal" calls a cow "doggie". In accommodation the child changes the scheme, that is, his way of thinking, to fit experience that will not fit: the cow moos, an adult says "cow", and the scheme splits in two. Assimilation keeps knowledge stable; accommodation makes it grow.

When schemes handle events, the child is in equilibrium. A misfit produces disequilibrium, an uncomfortable cognitive conflict, resolved by assimilating and accommodating until a new, more advanced balance is reached. This self-regulating process, equilibration (Figure 6.7), drives the child from stage to stage. Mature schemes become operations: internalised mental actions that are reversible (they can be undone in thought) and organised into logical systems; the stage names describe the child's relation to them.

Piaget named four factors in development: maturation of the nervous system; experience, both physical (properties of objects, such as weight) and logico-mathematical (relations one's own actions create, such as ten pebbles staying ten in any order of counting); social transmission (being taught); and equilibration, which coordinates the other three. Development thus comes from maturation and learning together, never from either alone, and not from social and cultural practice alone, which is nearer Vygotsky's emphasis (Section 6.5).

Exam trap

Piaget's two basic processes of learning and adaptation are assimilation and accommodation; encoding and retrieval are memory processes (Section 1.7), and attention and perception, or sensory input and motor output, are not his pair. Changing one's way of thinking to fit new experience is accommodation, not assimilation, conservation (below) or habituation, the decline in responding to a repeated stimulus (Section 6.3).

Piaget's stages are qualitatively different ways of thinking, not simply more knowledge. Every child passes through them in the same invariant sequence, none skipped or reversed, each built on the last. The ages are approximate; the order is what the theory fixes. Sensorimotor stage and object permanence

For the first two years, Piaget argued, infants think with senses and actions rather than mental symbols: the sensorimotor stage. Its motor is the circular reaction, an action the infant hits on by chance and repeats because the result is interesting; its target moves outward from her own body (primary) to objects (secondary) to deliberate experiment (tertiary), as Table 6.12 shows. By 8 to 12 months acts are intentional, one scheme serving as the means to another (pushing a cushion aside to reach a toy). To a 4-month-old, Piaget claimed, a toy covered by a cloth has ceased to be. Search for a fully hidden object begins in substage 4, about 8 to 12 months, with a revealing mistake. Adele Diamond (1985) showed that the A-not-B error grows with the delay between hiding and search, and later tied its disappearance to maturation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which supports working memory and inhibition of a prepotent (habitual, previously rewarded) reach.

Key study: Baillargeon's drawbridge (1985, 1987)

Renée Baillargeon used the violation-of-expectation method: infants look longer at events that surprise them. A screen rotated like a drawbridge towards a box placed behind it. In the possible event it stopped at the hidden box; in the impossible event it swung through the box's space. Five-month-olds (1985), and later some infants of 3.5 to 4.5 months (1987), looked longer at the impossible event, as if they knew the hidden box still existed. Criticism: longer looking may reflect novelty or perceptual preference rather than knowledge.

Exam trap

Object permanence is a sensorimotor achievement, never preoperational, concrete or formal, and not to be confused with conservation. Keyed ages: it begins about 8 to 12 months (substage 4, A-not-B error) and is fully established by 18 to 24 months; 2 months, 4 to 6 months, 8 to 10 months (onset only) or 3 years are wrong for full mastery. The A-not-B error reflects incomplete object permanence with a prepotent reach, not centration or failed reversibility.

Preoperational thought

Around the second birthday the symbolic function appears: the ability to make one thing (a word, image or gesture) stand for another, seen in language, pretend play (a banana becomes a telephone) and drawing. The preoperational stage (2 to 7) is named for what the child cannot yet do: perform operations. It has a symbolic function substage (2 to 4) and an intuitive thought substage (4 to 7), when children ask endless "why" questions and judge by intuition rather than logic. Judy DeLoache (1987) found that children under about 3 cannot use a scale model of a room to find a toy hidden in the real room: they lack dual representation, treating an object both as a thing and as a symbol for something else.

In the Piagetian view, then, preschoolers are unable to reason properly, and their thought shows a family of related limits:

  • Egocentrism: the inability to distinguish one's own visual or conceptual perspective from another person's; a cognitive limit, not selfishness.
  • Animism: attributing life and feelings to inanimate things ("the sun is following me").
  • Artificialism: believing natural things were made by people ("someone painted the sky").
  • Centration: focusing on one salient feature while ignoring the others. A 5-year-old who sorts a deck of cards by colour but cannot then re-sort the same deck by suit is centring on colour.
  • Irreversibility: the inability to run an action backwards in the mind, with a focus on states, not transformations: the child compares before and after and ignores the pouring between.
  • Transductive reasoning: reasoning from particular to particular with no general rule. Piaget's daughter, having missed her nap, announced, "It isn't afternoon."
  • Failure of class inclusion: shown wooden beads, mostly brown, and asked "More brown beads or more wooden beads?", the child says "more brown", comparing subclasses instead of a subclass with the whole.
  • Lack of conservation, the subject of the next subsection.
Key study: The three mountains task

Piaget and Bärbel Inhelder (1913 to 1997) seated children before a model of three mountains (one snow-capped, one with a house, one with a red cross) and asked them to pick the photograph showing the view of a doll sitting elsewhere. Four-year-olds usually picked their own view; correct choices became common only around 7 to 8. Piaget read this as egocentrism. Criticism: the task is unfamiliar and spatially hard; meaningful versions show perspective taking much earlier (Evaluating Piaget).

Exam trap

Centration = focusing on one salient feature while ignoring others (the card sorter; judging liquid by height), not egocentrism, animism, transductive reasoning, irreversibility or the reversible operation behind conservation. Coordinating schemes across senses is a sensorimotor achievement, not centration. "Preschoolers cannot reason properly" is the Piagetian view, not that of Arthur Robert Jensen (1923 to 2012, Level I and Level II abilities), Joy Paul Guilford (1897 to 1987, structure of intellect) or a "holistic" view (Section 1.9).

Conservation

Conservation is the understanding that a quantity stays the same despite changes in shape or arrangement, so long as nothing is added or taken away. Every task follows one script (Figure 6.8). The child agrees that two identical short, wide glasses hold equal water. One is then poured, as she watches, into a tall, thin glass, where the water stands higher (it does not stay at the same height; that is the point). The preoperational child now says the tall glass has more: her judgement is swayed by one dimension, height, and she ignores the narrower width.

The conserving child gives one of three justifications, exactly the operations the preoperational child lacks:

  • Identity: "It's the same water; you didn't add any or take any away." The principle of identity is the recognition that certain characteristics of an object remain fixed even when its appearance changes.
  • Compensation: "It's taller, but it's thinner." A change in one dimension is offset by a change in another.
  • Reversibility (negation): "If you pour it back, it will be the same as before."

Conservation is several achievements, mastered in a typical order (Table 6.13). A child who conserves substance at 7 or 8 may fail with weight until 9 or 10, though both need the same operation. Piaget called this horizontal décalage (French for "gap"): one operation, applied to different contents, mastered at different ages within a stage. Vertical décalage is the same achievement recurring at a higher level in a later stage: a toddler finds her way round the house by action but maps it only years later. Conservation of liquid, loosely called liquid volume, differs from volume by displacement, which comes last; all count as concrete operational achievements.

Concrete operational stage

At about 7 children begin to perform true operations on real objects and events: the concrete operational stage (7 to 11). Shown the clay ball rolled into a sausage, the child now says it is still the same amount, and can say why. The gains behind this are:

  • Decentration: attending to several features at once, such as height and width.
  • Reversibility: mentally undoing an action, which first appears in this stage.
  • Seriation: arranging items along a dimension (sticks from shortest to longest) systematically, not by trial and error.
  • Transitive inference (transitivity): if A is longer than B and B is longer than C, then A is longer than C, without comparing A and C directly.
  • Classification by more than one attribute, and success on class inclusion ("more wooden beads").
  • Reduced egocentrism in perception and conversation.

The limit is in the name: the concrete thinker reasons logically about real, picturable things but balks at the purely hypothetical. Told "If all dogs were pink and Bruno is a dog, what colour is Bruno?", she objects that dogs are not pink.

Exam trap

In the concrete stage reduced egocentrism, transitive inference and conservation of mass are present, object permanence is long established and deferred imitation dates from infancy. What the concrete operational child struggles with, and does not attain, is hypothetico-deductive reasoning.

Formal operations and postformal thought

From about 11 or 12 the formal operational stage frees thought from the concrete. Adolescents reason about abstractions (justice, infinity), about the possible as well as the real, and about propositions, statements whose conclusions follow from their form ("If A then B; A; therefore B"). The signature is hypothetico-deductive reasoning: deducing hypotheses from a general theory and testing them systematically, one variable at a time. With it come counterfactual reasoning (from premises contrary to fact: "If the moon were made of cheese..."), combinatorial reasoning (generating every possible combination of factors) and metacognitive reflection, thinking about one's own thinking.

Inhelder and Piaget's tasks appear in The Growth of Logical Thinking from Childhood to Adolescence (1958). In the pendulum problem the child must find what controls how fast a pendulum swings: string length, weight, height of release or force of push. The concrete thinker changes several things at once; the formal thinker varies one at a time and finds that only length matters. In the chemical combination task the adolescent mixes colourless liquids in every combination to find which turns yellow. The new power brings idealism (measuring the real world against ideal ones) and adolescent egocentrism (Section 6.10). Nor is the stage universal: many adults do not reason formally on unfamiliar content.

Is formal logic the end point? Several theorists proposed postformal thought, adult reasoning that accepts contradiction, uncertainty and context (Table 6.14). Its core is dialectical reasoning: seeing that opposing views each hold part of the truth and integrating them in a synthesis.

Evaluating Piaget

Piaget's broad picture survives: children actively construct knowledge, thinking changes in kind as well as amount, and his sequence recurs across cultures. His timing and stages have fared worse.

First, he underestimated infants and preschoolers. Baillargeon credited infants with object knowledge months early, and many preschool failures came from the task rather than the mind. Margaret Donaldson (1926 to 2020) drew the lesson in Children's Minds (1978): children reason well when a task makes "human sense" to them.

Key study: Policemen and a naughty teddy

Martin Hughes (1975) asked children of 3.5 to 5 to hide a boy doll from two policeman dolls among crossing walls; about 90 percent succeeded, though the task needs two perspectives at once, because hiding makes sense to them. James McGarrigle and Donaldson (1974) let a "naughty teddy" disarrange one row of the number-conservation task as if by accident; far more 4- to 6-year-olds conserved than when the adult changed the row on purpose. Criticism: some may have ignored the accidental change rather than truly conserved.

Second, development is less stage-like than claimed: décalage is the rule, and abilities grow gradually, domain by domain. Third, culture and schooling matter more than he allowed, the core of Lev Vygotsky's critique (Section 6.5). Fourth, he did not separate competence, the understanding a child has, from performance, what she shows on a task with its own language, memory and attention demands.

Neo-Piagetian theories keep the stages but explain them by growing processing capacity. Juan Pascual-Leone (1970) proposed M-space, the number of schemes a child can hold in mind at once, which grows with age. Robbie Case (1944 to 2000) traced stage progress to more efficient use of limited working memory as skills become automatic. Kurt Fischer (1943 to 2020) proposed skill theory (1980): skills develop through levels, separately in each domain, and run higher with support than alone.

True Piagetian statements: children construct knowledge by acting on objects physically or mentally; infant schemes are organised patterns of action; older children's operations let them see that quantities are conserved despite changes in appearance.

6.5 Vygotsky, Bruner, Information Processing and Theory of MindMedium yield · 25 Qs

Piaget's child (Section 6.4) works alone, a small scientist testing the world. Yet a real child learning to tie her shoelaces usually has an older sister kneeling beside her. Vygotsky and Bruner put that helper back into the picture; the information-processing approach treats the mind as a computer that gets faster and smarter; and research on theory of mind asks when children discover that other people have minds of their own.

Vygotsky's sociocultural theory

Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (1896 to 1934), a Russian psychologist who died of tuberculosis at 37, saw cognitive growth as a socially mediated activity: children acquire new ways of thinking through cooperative dialogue with more skilled members of their culture. This is his sociocultural theory, set out in Thinking and Speech (1934) and known to English readers mainly through Mind in Society (1978), an edited collection of his essays.

Vygotsky separated elementary mental functions, natural capacities we share with animals (simple attention, perception, involuntary memory), from higher mental functions such as voluntary attention, deliberate memory and verbal reasoning, which are human and cultural. What turns the first into the second is mediation: we act on the world, and on our own minds, through tools. Each culture supplies tools of intellectual adaptation, signs such as number systems, writing, maps and memory aids, that let children use their basic functions more adaptively; a knot tied in a handkerchief to remember an errand is a sign steering one's own memory. Language is the master tool.

How does a tool that exists between people get inside one head? The general genetic law of cultural development states that every higher function appears twice: first between people (interpsychological), then within the child (intrapsychological). This passage from outer to inner is internalisation. A mother who asks "Where did you last see your bag?" remembers together with her son; years later he asks himself silently. Joint work rests on intersubjectivity, the process by which two people who begin a task with different understandings arrive at a shared one. Inter-subjectivity therefore precedes intra-subjectivity.

Development, Vygotsky added, must be studied at four interrelated levels: microgenetic (change over minutes or days), ontogenetic (over a lifetime), phylogenetic (over evolution) and sociohistorical (change in the culture's values and technologies).

Exam trap

For Vygotsky, inter-subjectivity precedes intra-subjectivity. "Intra precedes inter" reverses the law, and "children do not engage in inter-subjectivity" contradicts it. "The two are related" is true but empty: the law is about their order.

The zone of proximal development and scaffolding

Vygotsky described two children who, working alone, both solve test problems set for eight-year-olds. Given hints, one solves problems set for twelve-year-olds, the other only those for nine-year-olds. The distance from eight to twelve, or from eight to nine, is what he called the zone of proximal development (ZPD): the gap between the child's actual developmental level, shown by independent problem solving, and her level of potential development, shown by problem solving under adult guidance or with more capable peers. That helper is now called the more knowledgeable other. As a range of tasks, the ZPD holds those too difficult to master alone but achievable with help (Figure 6.9). Teaching should aim inside it, at functions still ripening.

Teaching within the ZPD is now usually called scaffolding: support fitted to the learner's current level and withdrawn as competence grows. The term is not Vygotsky's: it comes from David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross (1976).

Key study: Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976)

A tutor helped 3- to 5-year-olds build a pyramid of interlocking wooden blocks, a task too hard for them alone. Good tutoring recruited interest, simplified the task, kept the goal in view, marked critical features, controlled frustration and demonstrated, stepping in only as far as the child needed. Criticism: the metaphor implies a fixed structure built by the adult, whereas real tutoring is a two-way exchange.

Barbara Rogoff (Apprenticeship in Thinking, 1990) described development as an apprenticeship built on guided participation: children learn by taking part, beside adults and peers, in their community's valued activities. In reciprocal teaching (Annemarie Palincsar and Ann Brown, 1984), teacher and pupils take turns leading discussion of a text through questioning, summarising, clarifying and predicting, the teacher fading as pupils take over. Dynamic assessment (test, teach, retest) measures how much a child gains from help, as in Reuven Feuerstein's Learning Potential Assessment Device (1979).

Exam trap

The ZPD is the gap between independent and assisted performance, a term coined by Vygotsky (not Piaget, Bruner, Kohlberg, Bronfenbrenner, Erikson, Skinner or Freud). Wrong options name something else: tasks done alone (the actual level), too simple, too hard even with help, or learned without instruction; innate abilities, structures or the language acquisition device (Section 1.8); a critical, optimal or language-learning period (Section 6.1) or the usual age for a milestone; a stage of logical operations or object permanence (Section 6.4); a fixed capacity or genetic ceiling; mental age minus chronological age (Section 1.9); a brain or adolescent growth spurt; a neural region; a peer group.

Thought, speech and concepts

On language the two theorists disagreed most clearly. For Piaget thought comes first, and language expresses concepts already built through action; the preschooler's talk to no one, his egocentric speech (The Language and Thought of the Child, 1923), mirrors egocentric thought and simply fades. For Vygotsky thought and language have separate roots: prelinguistic thought (a toddler using a stick to reach a toy) and preintellectual speech (babbling). At about age 2 they merge; thought becomes verbal, and from then on language shapes thought. So thought depends on language for Vygotsky, and language depends on thought for Piaget.

Speech then turns inward. Social speech, used to communicate, comes first. From about 3 children talk aloud to themselves as they work ("the red one goes... no, here"). Vygotsky saw this talk, now called private speech, as social speech turned to guiding one's own actions; at school age it goes underground as inner speech, silent verbal thought. Laura E. Berk's studies in the 1980s and 1990s supported him: children use more private speech on hard tasks and after errors, those who use it more tend to improve, and it fades to whispers before going inward.

Exam trap

Vygotsky: thought depends on language; Piaget: language depends on thought. The reversed option swaps the names, and "one studied early, the other later language" is false. Private speech guides and regulates behaviour and becomes inner speech. It is not a regression to "autistic" (undirected, wishful) thinking, a word Piaget used, nor mere imitation, a language disorder or egocentrism that fades with schooling.

Vygotsky traced concept formation with block-sorting tasks. Young children form heaps (syncretic groupings), objects lumped together by chance impression. Next come complexes, groups built on concrete, shifting links (a red block, then a red triangle, then any triangle). The most advanced complex, the pseudoconcept, looks like a true concept (every triangle gathered) but rests on visible likeness, not an abstract rule. True concepts mature in adolescence. Vygotsky linked them to the distinction between the acting "I" and the reflecting "I" (the self that does and the self that thinks about its doing), to the problems and developmental tasks society sets adolescents, and to participation in its institutions. Gender differences play no part.

Jerome Bruner: representation and discovery

Jerome Seymour Bruner (1915 to 2016), an American leader of the cognitive revolution, shared Vygotsky's interest in culture and teaching. In 1966 he proposed three modes of representation. The enactive mode represents knowledge through action (a baby knows a rattle as a shaking movement); the iconic mode, through images (picturing the route to school); the symbolic mode, through language, numbers and other arbitrary symbols. They appear roughly in that order from infancy to the school years, but unlike Piaget's stages none is left behind.

In The Process of Education (1960) Bruner argued that any subject can be taught in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage. Hence the spiral curriculum, which returns to the same big ideas at rising levels of complexity, and discovery learning, in which learners find principles for themselves by working on problems. His language acquisition support system (LASS) is covered in Section 1.8.

Information processing: the mind as a computer

The information-processing approach likens the mind to a computer: information comes in, is held and transformed, and leaves as an answer. Development means better hardware (the brain and nervous system) and better software (attention, memory strategies and problem-solving routines), and it is continuous: many small quantitative gains, not qualitative stages.

The hardware speeds up. Robert V. Kail (1991) showed that processing speed rises steeply in childhood and more slowly in adolescence, at a similar rate across very different tasks, which suggests a general biological cause such as myelination. Attention grows more selective, working memory holds more, and the executive functions of the prefrontal cortex mature: inhibitory control (stopping a dominant response), working memory (holding and updating information) and cognitive flexibility (switching rules or viewpoints).

The software improves too. Memory strategies appear in order: rehearsal (repeating items) from about 7, organisation (grouping items by meaning) in middle childhood and elaboration (linking items through an image or sentence) mostly in adolescence. A child with a production deficiency, John H. Flavell's term, can use a strategy when told but does not produce it spontaneously; one with a utilisation deficiency (Patricia H. Miller) produces it but at first gains little from it.

Flavell (1979) also made metacognition, knowledge and control of one's own thinking, central; its memory part is metamemory, such as knowing that a long list needs more study. Knowledge matters as much as capacity: Michelene Chi (1978) found that child chess experts recalled board positions better than adult novices, who still recalled digit lists better. Robert S. Siegler's overlapping waves model (1996) holds that children always have several strategies (for 3 + 5: counting on fingers, counting on from the larger number, simply retrieving 8) whose frequencies shift gradually, like overlapping waves, rather than in stages. In fuzzy-trace theory (Charles Brainerd and Valerie Reyna, 1990) we store separate verbatim and gist traces and rely more on gist with age.

Theory of mind

A theory of mind is the understanding that people have mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions) that guide their actions and can differ from one's own and from reality. The term comes from David Premack and Guy Woodruff (1978), who asked whether a chimpanzee has one. Its precursors appear late in the first year: joint attention (following another's gaze or point to a shared object), social referencing (Section 6.6) and intention reading (treating a reach as aimed at a goal).

Gyorgy Gergely and Gergely Csibra (from 1995) call this early framework the teleological stance: around the end of the first year infants read an action as efficient and goal-directed, expecting an agent to take the most direct route its situation allows. In the philosopher John Searle's (1983) terms, toddlers understand intentions in action, the goal visible in an ongoing act. They have moved beyond mere contingencies (this event follows that one) but not yet to prior intentions (plans formed before acting) or mental states such as belief and desire.

Henry Wellman (1990) traced the next steps: a desire psychology at about 2, explaining actions by wants, then a belief-desire psychology at 3 to 4, in which actions follow from what people want and what they believe, even falsely. The test is the false-belief task: the child must predict the action of someone whose belief the child knows is wrong. In Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner's original (1983), Maxi puts his chocolate in one cupboard and goes out; his mother moves it. Where will Maxi look? In the Smarties (unexpected-contents) task of Perner, Susan Leekam and Wimmer (1987), a child who expected sweets in a Smarties tube is shown pencils and asked what a friend will think is inside. Three-year-olds answer from reality; most children pass at about 4, in the preoperational years. Flavell's appearance-reality distinction comes at a similar age: shown a sponge painted like a rock, a 3-year-old says it looks like a sponge, while a 5-year-old says it looks like a rock but is really a sponge.

Key study: Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985)

Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie and Uta Frith acted out the Sally-Anne task with dolls (Figure 6.10). About 85 percent of typically developing preschoolers and 86 percent of children with Down syndrome answered "the basket", but 80 percent of autistic children, though of higher verbal mental age than the Down syndrome group, pointed to the box. The authors proposed a specific theory-of-mind deficit in autism, later called mindblindness (Chapter 8). Criticism: the task also loads on language and memory, and some autistic children pass it.

Exam trap

False-belief tasks (Maxi, Sally-Anne, Smarties) assess theory of mind and are passed at about age 4, in the preoperational period: not at 12 months (only the teleological stance), age 8 (concrete operations) or adolescence. They are not the A-not-B task (object permanence), a conservation task (both Section 6.4), the Strange Situation (attachment, Section 6.7) or a working memory test.

Recursive thought and social perspective taking

Passing a false-belief task is first-order reasoning: "Sally thinks the marble is in the basket." A second-order theory of mind nests one belief inside another ("John thinks that Mary thinks the ice-cream van is in the park"); Perner and Wimmer (1985) found it at about 6 to 7. The ability to nest perspectives is recursive thought, a form of perspective taking that views a situation from at least two perspectives, reasoning simultaneously about what two or more people are thinking ("I think that you think that I think..."). Patricia Miller, Frank Kessel and Flavell (1970) showed it growing through the school years. It is not referential style, Katherine Nelson's term for toddlers whose first words mostly name objects; not reversibility, Piaget's mental undoing of an operation (Section 6.4); and not reconstruction, the rebuilding of memories (Section 1.7).

Robert L. Selman (1976, 1980) studied social perspective taking, understanding another person's thoughts and feelings, through dilemmas. In the best known, Holly has promised her father not to climb trees, then finds a friend's kitten stuck in one. 6.6 Emotional Development, Temperament and Self-RegulationLower yield · 19 Qs

Follow one baby through her first three years: at two months she smiles at your face, at eight months she cries when a stranger reaches for her, and at two years she hides her face in embarrassment. Alongside this shared timetable runs an individual style, temperament, and the growing capacity to manage it, self-regulation.

The first emotions

What can a newborn feel? In the differentiation theory of Katharine Banham Bridges (1932), built from observing infants of different ages, the newborn has only a diffuse general excitement. Within about three months this splits into two poles, distress and delight, which are therefore the first emotions infants express. Each pole then divides again, distress into anger, disgust and fear, delight into elation and affection, until by two years most adult emotions can be seen (Figure 6.11).

Two rival views differ. John Broadus Watson (1878 to 1958) held that only fear, rage and love are innate and all other emotions are conditioned onto them. Carroll Izard (1923 to 2017), in his differential emotions theory, held that each basic emotion is a separate innate system with its own facial expression, switched on by maturation (Section 1.11). Pairs such as "pain and anger" or "fear and euphoria" belong to no one's scheme.

Modern research, much of it by Michael Lewis, places the primary emotions (joy, interest, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger and fear) within the first six months; none needs a sense of self. Is the first smile joy? Not yet. The reflexive smile (endogenous smile) of the first weeks comes from internal states, mostly in sleep. The social smile, a smile in response to a human face or voice, appears at about 6 to 10 weeks, and laughter at about 3 to 4 months (Figure 6.12).

Self-awareness and the self-conscious emotions

A second family of emotions must wait for a self. Embarrassment, pride, shame, guilt, envy and empathy are self-conscious emotions (secondary or self-evaluative emotions): each involves judging yourself through others' eyes or against a standard, so each needs self-awareness and appears only after 12 months, from the second half of the second year. Lewis places embarrassment, envy and empathy at about 18 to 24 months, once the child recognises himself, and pride, shame and guilt at about two and a half to three years, once he has rules to judge himself by. Joseph Campos (2009) and Jerome Kagan (1929 to 2021; 2010) agree on this timing.

Key study: the rouge test (Lewis and Brooks-Gunn, 1979)

Michael Lewis and Jeanne Brooks-Gunn secretly dabbed rouge on infants' noses and placed them before a mirror. A baby who touches her own nose, not the reflection's, knows the face is hers. Infants under a year treated the image as another baby; nose-touching was typical by 18 to 24 months. This mirror self-recognition marks the arrival of the self, just as embarrassment appears.

Exam trap

Self-conscious emotions appear after 12 months (from the second half of the second year), never at 3 months, 6 to 9 months or "before 9 months". Those early ages belong to the primary emotions.

Strangers, separations and the still face

In the second half of the first year emotion turns sharply social. Stranger anxiety, wariness or crying when an unfamiliar adult approaches, emerges at about 6 to 8 months; René Spitz (1887 to 1974) called it eight-month anxiety (Section 6.7). Separation protest (separation anxiety), crying when the caregiver leaves, follows; Kagan found the same curve in several very different cultures, rising from about 8 months, peaking at about 13 to 15 months and then declining. Both fears therefore peak within roughly 8 to 15 months, because the baby now has a specific attachment figure and enough memory to notice an unfamiliar face or an absent caregiver. Excessive distress far beyond this age is separation anxiety disorder (Chapter 8).

Exam trap

Stranger anxiety and separation anxiety peak at about 8 to 15 months, not at 1 to 2 months (the age of the social smile), 3 years or 6 years.

Infants also learn to use others' emotions as information. In social referencing, from about 8 to 12 months, an infant facing an uncertain situation reads a trusted adult's face and lets it guide her. James Sorce, Campos and colleagues (1985) placed 12-month-olds on a visual cliff (Section 6.3) with a drop just deep enough to make them hesitate: when the mother looked happy, about three in four crossed; when she looked afraid, none did.

Key study: the still-face paradigm (Tronick and colleagues, 1978)

Are young infants simply soaked in their caregivers' mood, or partners in the exchange? Edward Tronick and colleagues filmed mothers with infants a few months old in three episodes: normal face-to-face play; a still face, in which the mother keeps looking at the baby with a blank, unresponsive face; and reunion. Faced with the still face, babies first tried to restart the exchange with smiles, coos and reaching, then turned their gaze away, slumped and became distressed and disorganised. The still-face paradigm showed that even very young infants actively expect and seek affective reciprocity (emotional give-and-take); it is now used to study the effects of maternal depression.

Exam trap

The still face shows infants as active partners who expect reciprocity, distressed and disorganised when it stops. They are not passive recipients of caregiver mood and do not habituate at once; the paradigm says nothing about fathers versus mothers, preferring strangers, or face recognition before 12 months.

From soothing to self-regulation

Caregivers first manage a baby's feelings by rocking and feeding. Emotion regulation, the processes by which we monitor and adjust the intensity and timing of our emotional reactions, develops as a handover to the child: by a few months babies avert their gaze from overstimulation or suck a thumb. Claire Kopp (1982) described three steps: control (about 12 to 18 months: complying with caregivers' demands), self-control (from about 2 years: complying without being watched) and self-regulation (from about 3 years: flexibly guiding one's own behaviour). Children also learn display rules, a culture's norms for when, where and how much emotion may be shown (Section 1.11): in many Indian homes a child learns early to hide anger before elders.

What temperament is

Some babies take a new face in their stride; others scream. Temperament names these differences. In Mary Rothbart's definition, it is constitutionally based individual differences in reactivity (how quickly and strongly a child is aroused) and self-regulation (how she modulates that arousal). It appears early, within the first months. It is moderately stable: a fearful infant often becomes a cautious child, but many change. And it is generally considered biologically and genetically based: identical twins are more alike in temperament than fraternal twins. The environment does not create temperament but shapes its expression, the idea of goodness of fit (below). Temperament is the raw material of adult personality (Chapter 4).

Exam trap

Asked what temperament is "generally considered to be", choose genetically (biologically) based. "Learned behaviour", "a result of parenting" and "environmentally influenced" are the distractors: the environment modulates temperament but is not its origin.

  • The difficult child (about 10 percent) has irregular biological rhythms, a generally negative mood and intense reactions, withdraws from new stimuli and is slow to adapt.
  • The slow-to-warm-up child (about 15 percent) is low in activity, withdraws from new stimuli at first with mild rather than intense negativity, and adapts slowly but gradually with repeated exposure.

The remaining 35 percent or so showed mixed patterns: they are unclassified, not a fourth named type. Picture the first week at a Mysuru playschool. Ananya plays at once and naps on schedule: easy. Kabir screams, flings his snack and still protests a fortnight later: difficult. Meera clings quietly to her mother's dupatta, watches from the edge and joins in by the second week: slow-to-warm-up, because her withdrawal is mild and gives way.

The difficult child syndrome means that some infants are difficult even in the first few weeks after birth, before parenting could have produced it. It is a temperament, not a later behaviour problem (though such children run a higher risk of one), and has nothing to do with auditory discrimination (turning the head to one sound and not another) or a delay in voluntary control.

Why do some difficult children do well? Thomas and Chess's answer was goodness of fit: the match between a child's temperament and the demands and expectations of caregivers and the wider environment. A difficult child whose parents stay patient and consistent often thrives; the same child with impatient, punitive parents tends to develop problems.

Exam trap

Thomas and Chess are known for temperament, not human sexuality, needs and drives or marital therapy. Their three types are easy, difficult and slow-to-warm-up. Distractors: sanguine, choleric, melancholic and phlegmatic (Hippocrates' humours, Galen's temperaments; Chapter 4); Type A, B and C (behaviour patterns linked to heart disease and cancer; Section 1.11); "inconsistent", "sensitive, resilient, withdrawn" and "inhibited only" (inhibition is Kagan's construct, below); and "insecurely attached", an attachment category (Section 6.7). Goodness of fit is never IQ differences between twins, the number of siblings or parent-child genetic concordance.

Rothbart and Bates, Kagan, Buss and Plomin

Later researchers condensed the overlapping nine dimensions. Mary Rothbart, who built the parent-report Infant Behavior Questionnaire (1981), and John Bates proposed a structural model of temperament (1998) with exactly three broad dimensions. Surgency, or extraversion, covers approach, activity, positive anticipation, smiling and sociability. Negative affectivity covers fear, frustration, sadness, discomfort and difficulty being soothed. Effortful control is the capacity to focus and shift attention voluntarily and to inhibit a dominant response; it is the self-regulatory side of temperament and matures in the preschool years.

Kagan studied reaction to the unfamiliar. Shown novel mobiles, voices and smells, about one 4-month-old in five thrashed and cried. These high-reactive infants tended to become inhibited, shy and wary children, while low-reactive infants tended to become uninhibited, bold and sociable. Behavioural inhibition, the tendency to withdraw from and be distressed by the unfamiliar, is Kagan's construct; he traced it to an easily excited amygdala. Its stability is moderate: many inhibited toddlers grow less shy. Arnold Buss and Robert Plomin (1984) proposed the EAS model of three heritable temperaments: emotionality (how easily a child becomes distressed), activity (tempo and vigour) and sociability (preference for company). Effortful control and the marshmallow test

Effortful control is the core of self-regulation, the ability to manage attention, emotion and action in pursuit of a goal; its most famous test asks a child to wait.

Key study: the marshmallow test (Mischel, from the late 1960s)

Walter Mischel (1930 to 2018) tested preschoolers at the Bing Nursery School at Stanford University. A child could eat one treat now or have two if she waited, up to 15 minutes, for the experimenter to return; ringing a bell brought him back at once, but then she got only one. The marshmallow test measures delay of gratification: giving up a smaller immediate reward for a larger later one. Follow-ups found that longer waiters were rated more competent as adolescents and scored higher on the SAT, the American college admission test (Yuichi Shoda, Mischel and Philip Peake, 1990). A larger, more diverse replication by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan (2018) found the link about half as strong, and weaker still once family background was controlled.

Waiting turned out to be a skill of attention. Children waited longer when the treats were covered or when they distracted themselves by singing. Dwelling on the treat's hot features (sweet, chewy) made waiting almost impossible; thinking of its cool, abstract features ("puffy, like a cloud") made it easier. Mischel later described a hot, emotional "go" system and a cool, cognitive "know" system that matures with age.

Keep delay of gratification apart from three neighbours. Sustained attention is keeping focus on one task over time. Resistance to temptation is obeying a prohibition when no one is watching, studied with the forbidden-toy paradigm: the child is told not to touch an attractive toy and left alone with it, with no bigger reward for complying. Impulse control in social settings is holding back an unacceptable act such as grabbing or interrupting. The marshmallow task draws on all three, but its defining feature is the choice between a smaller-sooner and a larger-later reward. Mischel's personality theory is in Chapter 4.

Exam trap

The marshmallow test primarily measures delay of gratification, not sustained attention, resistance to temptation (the forbidden toy) or impulse control in social settings.

6.7 Attachment: Imprinting, Bowlby, Ainsworth and BeyondMedium yield · 37 Qs

Leave a one-year-old in a strange room and watch the door. She cries when her mother walks out, crawls to her the moment she returns and, within a minute, is back among the toys. That small drama is attachment, the first close relationship of life. This section asks why the bond forms, how it unfolds over the first three years, how its quality is measured, what its absence does and how it echoes into adulthood.

What attachment is: four theories

An attachment is a strong, enduring emotional bond to a specific person who cannot be swapped for anyone else. Four behaviours reveal it: the infant keeps close to the person (proximity seeking), explores from her as a secure base, runs back to her as a safe haven when frightened, and shows separation distress when she leaves. A baby who smiles at everyone is sociable; one who protests when one particular person leaves is attached.

Why does the bond form? Four answers compete (Table 6.19). For Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) it was feeding: the mother gratifies oral needs, so the infant loves her; Erik Erikson (1902 to 1994) added that responsive care builds basic trust (Section 6.10). The learning theorists John Dollard and Neal Miller (1950) and Robert Sears said the same in behavioural terms: the mother, paired again and again with hunger reduction, becomes a secondary reinforcer, so attachment is a learned secondary drive, nicknamed 'cupboard love'. The cognitive-developmental view adds a precondition: a baby must tell familiar people from strangers and know that people go on existing when out of sight, an achievement called person permanence (Section 6.4). The ethological theory of John Bowlby holds that attachment is an evolved behavioural system that kept helpless infants near a protector and so alive. Geese and monkeys, as the next two subsections show, undermined the feeding theories, and the ethological theory is the predominantly accepted account today.

Lorenz and imprinting

Key study: Lorenz's goslings (1935)

Konrad Lorenz (1903 to 1989), an Austrian zoologist, split a clutch of greylag goose eggs. Half hatched under their mother; half hatched in an incubator, where the first moving thing the goslings saw was Lorenz. The incubator goslings followed him everywhere, and when the two broods were mixed and released, each gosling ran to its own 'mother', goose or man.

Lorenz called this imprinting: a rapid, largely irreversible following response to the first moving object, formed only within a narrow critical period after hatching (Section 6.1). It needs no practice and no food reward. It also lasts: birds imprinted on humans later courted humans, a long-term effect called sexual imprinting. Eckhard Hess (1916 to 1986) timed the window in mallard ducklings: imprinting peaked at about 13 to 16 hours after hatching and then fell away sharply. Later work found the window more flexible than Lorenz thought, so many now say sensitive period. Lorenz shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Nikolaas Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch. Human babies do not imprint, but Lorenz gave Bowlby two ideas: early bonds are biologically prepared, and they have a critical period.

Exam trap

Imprinting is rapid, largely irreversible and limited to a critical period: not slow, reversible lifelong learning, operant conditioning with food or a purely visual system unrelated to attachment. Nor is it habituation (Section 6.3), newborn reflexive smiling (Section 6.6) or same-sex playmate preference at 5 (Section 6.8). Lorenz, not Ainsworth, Bowlby or Erikson, argued that newborn creatures imprint during a critical period.

Harlow's surrogate mothers

If feeding builds the bond, an infant should love whoever feeds it. Harry Harlow (1905 to 1981) tested this at the University of Wisconsin with his wife and colleague Margaret Harlow.

Key study: 'The Nature of Love' (Harlow, 1958)

Infant rhesus monkeys, taken from their mothers at birth, were raised with two surrogate mothers: a bare wire-mesh figure and one covered in terry cloth. For some infants only the wire mother held a feeding bottle. Whichever fed them, they spent most of the day clinging to the cloth mother and visited the wire one only to feed. Frightened by a mechanical toy, they fled to the cloth mother, calmed, then turned to explore; in a strange room they explored only when she was present. Conclusion: contact comfort, not feeding, is the primary basis of attachment, and the cloth mother served as safe haven and secure base. Criticism: the work is ethically troubling, and monkeys are not children.

Monkeys reared in total isolation for six months to a year grew up rocking, clasping themselves and unable to play or mate normally; isolate females who later had infants became 'motherless mothers', neglecting or abusing them. Even a cloth mother could not produce normal social development. Yet Stephen Suomi and Harlow (1972) found that housing isolates with younger 'therapist' monkeys, which clung and played without threat, restored much social behaviour. Early deprivation is serious but partly reversible, a theme the human studies below repeat.

Exam trap

Harlow showed contact comfort, not feeding, as the basis of attachment: not conditioned reinforcement, operant shaping of clinging, classical conditioning of hunger or innate fear of strangers. He did not show imprinting in primates, critical periods for language or genetic determinism, and his isolates show that maternal absence harms even when nutrition is met.

Bowlby's attachment theory

John Bowlby (1907 to 1990), a British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, wove these threads into attachment theory. In Forty-Four Juvenile Thieves (1944) he found that 14 of the young thieves at his London clinic showed affectionless psychopathy (no guilt, no affection for others), and 12 of those 14 had suffered prolonged early separation from their mothers. His 1951 World Health Organization report, Maternal Care and Mental Health, set out the maternal deprivation hypothesis: an infant needs a warm, continuous relationship with the mother or a permanent substitute, and its prolonged disruption in the early years does lasting harm. The trilogy Attachment (1969), Separation (1973) and Loss (1980) completed the theory.

Its claims are these. Attachment is an evolved behavioural system: crying, smiling, clinging and following bring a caregiver close; the system switches on under threat or separation, and when the infant feels safe it explores from its secure base. Monotropy means that one bond, usually with the mother, is special and qualitatively different from the rest. The bond must form within a critical period, roughly the first two and a half years. And the infant builds an internal working model: a mental representation of the self (am I lovable?), of attachment figures (will they come?) and of relationships, built from early experience, that serves as a prototype, a schema, for later relationships yet can be revised by new experience.

What does a broken bond look like? Bowlby's colleague James Robertson filmed A Two-Year-Old Goes to Hospital (1952) and later, with Joyce Robertson, other brief separations. Young children passed through a protest-despair-detachment sequence: protest (crying, searching), despair (listless misery) and detachment (seeming recovery, but coolness to the mother on her return). Bowlby applied a similar sequence to grief (Section 6.11).

Exam trap

Attachment theory is Bowlby's, not Freud's, Rogers's or Bandura's. The internal working model is a revisable representation of self, others and relationships. It is not the proximity-seeking mechanism or the attachment system itself (which the model guides), a 'bio-social relationship model' (not Bowlby's term), Tolman's cognitive map (Chapter 1), a hippocampus-amygdala circuit, a genetic fear template, a reflex repertoire, a language substrate or object permanence.

Phases of attachment: Bowlby, Schaffer and Emerson, Mahler

Attachment assembles itself over the first two years. Bowlby described four phases (Figure 6.13):

  • Pre-attachment (birth to 6 weeks): signals bring adults close; the baby knows her mother's smell and voice but does not mind strangers.
  • Attachment-in-the-making (6 weeks to 6 to 8 months): she prefers familiar people but does not yet protest at separation.
  • Clear-cut attachment (6 to 8 months to 18 months to 2 years): clear preference for the primary caregiver, separation protest, stranger anxiety (Section 6.6) and secure-base behaviour.
  • Formation of a reciprocal relationship, the goal-corrected partnership (from 18 months to 2 years onwards): language lets the toddler understand and predict the parent's comings and goings, so protest gives way to negotiation.

Rudolph Schaffer and Peggy Emerson (1964) followed 60 Glasgow infants at home, monthly through the first year and again at 18 months, recording protest at everyday separations and fear of strangers. They found four stages: asocial (0 to 6 weeks), indiscriminate (6 weeks to about 7 months, accepting comfort from anyone), specific attachment (from about 7 months, with separation protest and stranger fear) and multiple attachments (soon after; most infants had several by 18 months). Infants attached most strongly to whoever responded most sensitively to them, not necessarily the person who fed them.

Margaret Mahler (1897 to 1985), a psychoanalyst, traced how the child separates psychologically within the bond (The Psychological Birth of the Human Infant, 1975, with Fred Pine and Anni Bergman). A normal autistic phase (the first weeks) and a symbiotic phase (to about 5 months, mother and infant felt as one) give way to separation-individuation in four subphases: differentiation or hatching (about 5 to 10 months: turning outward, first stranger reactions); practising (about 10 to 16 months: delighted exploration on new legs); rapprochement (about 15 to 24 months: aware now of being separate, the toddler alternates independent exploration with returns to the caregiver for emotional refuelling, and the clash of autonomy and closeness brings the clinging and tantrums of the rapprochement crisis); and consolidation (about 24 to 36 months), when object constancy, a stable inner image of the mother, lets the child bear her absence. Her developmental theory of schizophrenia and childhood psychosis belongs to Chapter 8.

Exam trap

Clear preference, separation protest and stranger anxiety mark Bowlby's clear-cut phase (6 to 8 months to 2 years; '6 months to 2 years' in keys), not pre-attachment, attachment-in-the-making or the goal-corrected partnership. Schaffer and Emerson's specific attachment starts at about 7 months. Rapprochement (15 to 24 months) is exploration alternating with refuelling: not symbiotic merger, the first stranger reactions (differentiation) or full object constancy.

Ainsworth and the Strange Situation

Bowlby's theory said every infant attaches; it did not say how well. That measure came from Mary Ainsworth (1913 to 1999), who worked with Bowlby in London and then watched mothers and babies at home in Uganda and Baltimore. To capture differences in security in one short session she devised the Strange Situation (Ainsworth and Barbara Wittig, 1969; reported in full in Patterns of Attachment, 1978, with Mary Blehar, Everett Waters and Sally Wall). Over eight episodes of about three minutes each, a 12- to 18-month-old explores a playroom with the caregiver, meets a stranger, is left with the stranger, is reunited, is left alone, is joined by the stranger and is reunited again. Observers score exploration, separation distress, reaction to the stranger and, above all, reunion behaviour, which best reveals what the infant expects of the caregiver.

Ainsworth's maternal sensitivity hypothesis explains the differences: security grows from noticing the baby's signals, reading them correctly and answering them promptly and appropriately. In Baltimore, mothers who answered crying promptly in the early months had securely attached one-year-olds; later studies confirm a real but more modest link.

Exam trap

Ainsworth (not Bowlby, Harlow, Lorenz, Piaget or Erikson) devised the Strange Situation, and she is known for attachment, not cognitive, moral or language development. Bowlby is to attachment as Ainsworth is to the Strange Situation; the Bobo doll is Albert Bandura's, Little Albert is John Watson and Rosalie Rayner's, and the visual cliff is Eleanor Gibson and Richard Walk's (Section 6.3).

Four patterns of attachment, and culture

Ainsworth's infants fell into three groups, A, B and C; Mary Main and Judith Solomon (1986, 1990) added a fourth, D (Table 6.20). Secure attachment (B) is the commonest, about 60 to 70 percent of samples. Insecure-avoidant attachment (A, about 20 percent) follows rejecting care: an infant whose bids for comfort are rebuffed learns to suppress them. Insecure-resistant attachment (C), or ambivalent (about 10 to 15 percent), follows inconsistent care: never sure that comfort will come, the infant clings and protests. Disorganised attachment (D), or disoriented, abounds among maltreated infants and those with a frightened or frightening caregiver, who is both the source of fear and its only refuge.

Northern Germany's excess of type A is cultural (an emphasis on early independence), not genetic, a modified protocol or a leap into adult attachment.

Deprivation and privation

René Spitz (1887 to 1974) compared babies in a foundling home, fed and clean but rarely held, with babies cared for by their own mothers in a prison nursery. The foundling babies fell behind, sickened and many died, a syndrome he called hospitalism (1945). He also described anaclitic depression (1946): infants separated from their mothers in the second half of the first year turned weepy, then withdrawn, lost weight and stopped developing, recovering if the mother returned within a few months.

Michael Rutter (1933 to 2021), in Maternal Deprivation Reassessed (1972), distinguished deprivation (losing a bond already formed) from privation (never forming one), the more harmful of the two. Barbara Tizard and Jill Hodges followed children raised in London residential nurseries by many changing carers: those later adopted formed close bonds with their new parents, closer than children returned to birth families, yet at 16 still had more difficulty with peers. Rutter's English and Romanian Adoptees study found that Romanian orphans adopted into British homes before about 6 months caught up almost fully, while later adoptees more often kept cognitive deficits and disinhibited attachment. Privation harms, but early rescue repairs much. Donald Winnicott (1896 to 1971) added the good-enough mother, reliably responsive rather than perfect, and the transitional object, a blanket or soft toy that comforts the child on the way to independence.

Clinical link

Severe early neglect can produce reactive attachment disorder (a withdrawn child who rarely seeks comfort) or disinhibited social engagement disorder (indiscriminate, overfamiliar approach to strangers); both are taught in Chapter 8.

Exam trap

Hospitalism and anaclitic depression are Spitz's, not Piaget's, Skinner's or Eysenck's. Deprivation is losing a bond; privation is never having one.

Attachment beyond infancy

Mary Main's Adult Attachment Interview classifies adults by how coherently they discuss their childhood: secure-autonomous (balanced), dismissing (idealises parents, recalls little), preoccupied (still angrily entangled) and unresolved (incoherent about loss or abuse). These parallel infant types B, A, C and D, and a parent's classification predicts her infant's well above chance. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) found secure, avoidant and anxious-ambivalent styles in adult romantic love, in proportions resembling the infant ones. Kim Bartholomew and Leonard Horowitz (1991) crossed positive or negative models of self and of others into four adult styles: secure, preoccupied, dismissing and fearful.

Monotropy, finally, has softened. Fathers are attachment figures in their own right, and most infants have several attachments by 18 months, as in any Indian joint family; responsiveness, not feeding, decides to whom. On day care, Jay Belsky (1986) warned that over 20 hours a week in the first year raises the risk of insecurity, but the large Study of Early Child Care run by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) found that day care alone does not; risk rises only when poor or very long care meets insensitive mothering.

6.8 Parenting, Family, Play and Gender in ChildhoodLower yield · 12 Qs

Watch a four-year-old for an afternoon. A parent sets her limits, siblings and grandparents crowd round, she plays beside and then with other children, and in almost everything she is learning what it means to be a girl. Baumrind's parenting styles

Why do some children grow up confident and self-controlled while others become anxious or unruly? In the 1960s Diana Baumrind (1927 to 2018) observed preschoolers at nursery school and at home and interviewed their parents. In papers from 1966 to 1971 she described three patterns of parenting style, the overall emotional climate in which a parent raises a child.

Authoritative parenting combines warmth and emotional support with clear boundaries: rules are explained, discipline is reasoned, children are held accountable for their behaviour and their growing autonomy is encouraged. Authoritarian parenting makes high demands with little warmth: strict obedience, rules without reasons ("because I said so") and often harsh punishment. Permissive parenting, also called indulgent, is warm and accepting but makes few demands and sets few rules.

In 1983 Eleanor Maccoby (1917 to 2018) and John Martin recast the styles as combinations of two independent dimensions: demandingness (control: expecting mature behaviour, supervising, disciplining) and responsiveness (warmth: acceptance and attention to the child's needs). The cell low on both became a fourth style, neglectful parenting (uninvolved): detached parents who do little beyond meeting basic needs. Figure 6.14 shows the four styles. Children of authoritative parents are the most self-reliant, competent and achievement-oriented; those of authoritarian parents tend to be anxious, withdrawn and unhappy, and those of permissive parents impulsive, with poor self-control; children of neglectful parents fare worst, with low self-esteem, poor schoolwork and later delinquency.

Culture shifts the picture. Ruth Chao (1994) showed that Chinese-American parents score as authoritarian on Western scales, yet their children do well at school: their strictness expresses the Chinese idea of training (chiao shun), devoted involvement that children read as care. Many Indian families show a similar warm control, firm expectations of obedience inside close affection. Control harms mainly when it comes without warmth.

Exam trap

Authoritative is the best style; authoritarian is strict and cold. "Emotional support, clear boundaries and accountability", "high demandingness and high responsiveness" and "reasoned discipline with autonomy support" all mean authoritative. Permissive is low demandingness with high responsiveness; neglectful is low on both and was added by Maccoby and Martin, not Baumrind.

New labels, discipline and the family

Helicopter parenting is over-involved parenting: parents hover, monitor and step in to solve problems the child could handle alone. The image comes from Haim Ginott (1922 to 1973), whose 1969 book Between Parent and Teenager quotes a teenager whose mother hovers over him like a helicopter. It has several roots at once: parental anxiety, parents' unfulfilled wishes lived out through the child, and parents' own personality traits such as perfectionism. Tiger parenting (after Amy Chua's 2011 memoir) is strict and achievement-driven; free-range parenting grants age-appropriate independence with little supervision. Martin Hoffman's (1967) three kinds of discipline, induction (explaining how the behaviour affects others), power assertion (force, threats) and love withdrawal (withholding affection), are compared in Section 6.9.

Parents are one part of a family system in which every member affects every other. Siblings are companions, teachers and rivals; the claim that birth order shapes personality belongs to Alfred Adler (Chapter 4). Only children, despite the "spoilt" stereotype, match others in achievement and adjustment. E. Mavis Hetherington, following families for decades (2002), found that most children adjust to divorce within two to three years, though about 25 percent show serious problems against about 10 percent from intact families; remarriage brings a second adjustment. Maternal employment does not in itself harm children, and day care matters through its quality.

Indian context

In a joint family several generations share a home and its childcare, so the child has many attachment figures, more adults to obey and a strong sense of interdependence. Urbanisation has made the nuclear family (parents and children only) the commonest household, with day care or grandparents filling the gap.

Child maltreatment and neglect

In 1962 the paediatrician C. Henry Kempe (1922 to 1984) and colleagues described the battered child syndrome: young children with fractures at different stages of healing that their parents' accounts did not explain. Child maltreatment has four types: physical abuse (non-accidental injury), sexual abuse (any sexual activity with a child), emotional abuse (persistent rejecting, belittling or terrorising) and neglect, failure to meet a child's basic needs and the most often reported form. Neglect has five categories: physical (failure to provide proper nutrition, housing and clothing), supervisory (children left unattended), health or medical (failure to immunise, seek proper care or follow medical directions), educational (not enrolling the child, chronic absence) and emotional (withholding affection and attention).

Exam trap

Using licensed day care is not neglect: it is approved, supervised care, the odd one out beside nutrition, supervision and immunisation.

Risk factors include parents who were themselves maltreated (about a third repeat it), young, depressed or misusing substances; premature, disabled or difficult children; and poverty, domestic violence and isolation. Effects include disorganised attachment (Section 6.7), aggression, school failure, depression and post-traumatic stress. Management and India's Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012 are in Chapter 7.

Peers: status, friendship and bullying

Sociometric status is measured by asking classmates whom they like most and least. In 1982 John Coie, Kenneth Dodge and a colleague identified five groups: popular (many "like most"), rejected (many "like least"), neglected (few nominations of either kind), controversial (many of both) and average. Rejected children are most at risk of loneliness and later problems; neglected children usually do well once they find a friend. Friendship deepens from shared play to mutual trust and, in adolescence, intimacy. Bullying, as defined by the Norwegian psychologist Dan Olweus (1931 to 2020), is repeated, intentional aggression against someone less powerful; his whole-school programme roughly halved it in Norway.

Gender concepts and gender socialisation

Gender research needs exact terms. Sex is biological: chromosomes, hormones and anatomy. Gender is the psychological and social meaning a culture attaches to being male or female. Gender identity is one's own sense of being male, female or another gender; a gender role is the behaviour a culture expects of each sex. A gender stereotype is a rigid, oversimplified belief about what males and females are like ("boys don't cry"); gender stereotyping applies it to individuals. Gender typing is the process by which children adopt the preferences and behaviours their culture considers right for their sex.

Gender socialisation is the process by which children learn this "gender map" from the people and institutions around them. Parents come first: pink or blue, dolls or trucks, indoor or outdoor chores, much of it taught unconsciously because their own gender orientations are so deeply embedded. Peers, school and media reinforce the map. Gender construction, by contrast, is the sociological view that a society creates gender categories and their meanings, and gender structuralisation is not a standard developmental term. From about three, children prefer same-sex playmates; this gender segregation peaks in middle childhood, so that, as Maccoby put it, boys (large groups, rough play) and girls (small groups, talk) grow up in two cultures.

Exam trap

Parents passing on the "gender map" (pink and blue, toys, chores), consciously or unconsciously, is gender socialisation. Gender stereotyping is a belief applied to people, not a learning process; gender construction and gender structuralisation are distractors.

Theories of gender development

The biological view points to prenatal hormones: girls with congenital adrenal hyperplasia, exposed to high androgen levels before birth, prefer boys' toys and rough play more than other girls, although raised as girls. In the psychoanalytic view the child of three to six identifies with the same-sex parent (Chapter 4). Social learning theory, applied to gender by Walter Mischel (1930 to 2018) in 1966, holds that children are reinforced for gender-appropriate behaviour and imitate same-sex models. Kay Bussey and Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) extended this in 1999 into a social cognitive theory: reinforcement and modelling build internal gender standards by which children regulate themselves. Either way, shaping from outside comes first.

Lawrence Kohlberg (1927 to 1987) reversed the order in his cognitive-developmental theory of 1966: "I am a boy, therefore I want to do boy things." Understanding comes first, and children then socialise themselves. It grows in three steps (Figure 6.15): basic gender identity (about two to three: labelling oneself and others), gender stability (about three to five: a boy will become a man) and gender constancy or consistency (about five to seven: a boy in a frock is still a boy). These are his only stages; "gender-role achievement" is not one. Ronald Slaby and Karin Frey (1975) supported him: watching a split-screen film of a man and a woman doing the same things, children high in constancy looked longer at the same-sex adult. Yet children prefer gender-typed toys by about two, long before constancy.

Gender schema theory, proposed in 1981 by Sandra Bem (1944 to 2014) and independently by Carol Martin and Charles Halverson, explains this. A gender schema is an organised set of beliefs about what goes with each sex. As soon as children have basic gender identity, they sort toys, activities and people into "for boys" and "for girls" and attend to and remember what fits their own group: shown a girl sawing wood, many later recall a boy doing it (Martin and Halverson, 1983). Earlier, Bem (1974) had treated masculinity and femininity as separate dimensions, so a person can be high on both: androgyny, measured by the Bem Sex Role Inventory (Chapter 2).

Exam trap

IHBAS 2020 keyed basic gender identity as the starting point of self-socialisation in Kohlberg's theory: it is the first link in his chain. Many textbooks are stricter: for Kohlberg, children become fully committed self-socialisers only at gender consistency (constancy), and it is gender schema theory that starts self-socialisation at basic gender identity. How large are the resulting differences? In 1974 Eleanor Maccoby and Carol Jacklin found only four well established: girls' greater verbal ability and boys' greater visual-spatial ability, mathematical ability and aggression. Janet Shibley Hyde's gender similarities hypothesis (2005) went further: across 46 meta-analyses, 78 percent of differences were small or close to zero.

6.9 Moral Development: Piaget, Kohlberg, Gilligan and BeyondLower yield · 20 Qs

Read two stories to a six-year-old. John, called to dinner, opens a door and accidentally smashes fifteen cups standing on a tray behind it. Henry, reaching for jam while his mother is out, breaks one cup. Who is naughtier? The six-year-old usually says John, since fifteen cups are worse than one; a ten-year-old says Henry, who was doing wrong. What has changed is not knowledge about cups but reasoning about right and wrong. This section follows that change through Piaget, Kohlberg and Gilligan, then meets the researchers who widened the picture.

Three sides of morality

Moral development is the growth of the standards by which a person judges right and wrong, and of the feelings and actions that go with them. Morality has three sides, and each tradition studied one. The emotional side is guilt, shame and empathy (their emergence in toddlers is traced in Section 6.6). Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) placed it in the superego, the conscience formed at about five or six when the child resolves the Oedipus complex by identifying with the same-sex parent; it punishes misdeeds with guilt (Chapter 4). The behavioural side is what people do when tempted. Social learning theorists led by Albert Bandura (1925 to 2021) held that moral conduct is learned through reinforcement, punishment and above all modelling; in 1963 Bandura and Frederick McDonald even shifted children's judgments between consequences and intentions simply by letting them hear adults judge the other way. The cognitive side, moral reasoning, is how a person thinks a moral problem through; it belongs to Piaget and Kohlberg. The study below shows why reasoning and conduct need separate study.

Key study: the Character Education Inquiry (1928 to 1930)

Hugh Hartshorne and Mark May asked whether children have a general trait of honesty. They gave more than 10,000 schoolchildren rigged chances to lie, cheat and steal at school, at home, at parties and in games. Most children cheated in some situations and not in others, and cheating in one setting only weakly predicted cheating in another. Honesty, they concluded, is largely situation-specific, not a fixed trait, and moral knowledge barely predicted honest conduct.

Piaget: from moral realism to autonomy

Jean Piaget (1896 to 1980) turned from logic (Section 6.4) to morality in The Moral Judgment of the Child (1932). He asked Swiss boys playing marbles where the rules came from and whether they could be changed, and he read children paired stories like John and Henry's, in which one child does more damage by accident and another less damage while misbehaving. He described a premoral period, before about age 5, when children pay little attention to rules, followed by two stages (Table 6.24).

In heteronomous morality (Greek for "ruled by another"), also called moral realism, from about 5 to 10 (some books say 9), rules come from adults and are sacred and unchangeable: a new marble rule is cheating even if every player agrees. Acts are judged by their consequences, not intentions, which Piaget called objective responsibility, so John is naughtier. The child believes in immanent justice, that the world itself punishes wrongdoing (a boy who stole apples falls through a rotten bridge "because he stole"), and favours expiatory punishment, severe and unrelated to the offence.

From about 10, cooperation among equal peers brings autonomous morality, or moral relativism. Rules become agreements that players may change by consent; intentions outweigh consequences, so Henry is naughtier; fairness means equality; and reciprocal punishment should fit the misdeed and repair it (the child who broke a window pays for it). Piaget underestimated preschoolers, who do use intentions when a story makes them clear, but his idea that moral reasoning grows with cognition and peer experience became Kohlberg's starting point.

Kohlberg's method: the Heinz dilemma

Lawrence Kohlberg (1927 to 1987) carried Piaget's approach into adolescence. For his 1958 doctoral study he interviewed 72 Chicago boys aged 10, 13 and 16, and re-interviewed many of them every three or four years for about 20 years. His method was to pose hypothetical moral dilemmas, stories in which two moral values collide, to children and adolescents of different ages. The most famous is the Heinz dilemma:

A woman is dying of cancer. A druggist has a drug that might save her, but he charges $2,000 for a dose that cost him $200 to make. Her husband, Heinz, can raise only about $1,000, and the druggist will not sell it cheaper or let him pay later. Heinz breaks in and steals the drug. Should he have done that? Why?

The decisive word is "why". Kohlberg scored the reasoning, not the choice: "steal" and "don't steal" can come from the same stage, and the same answer from different stages. His interview and scoring system became the Moral Judgment Interview. The theory describes moral reasoning, not moral behaviour.

Exam trap

Kohlberg studied morality by posing moral dilemmas to children of different ages, not through daily journals, court records of delinquents, or making people perform legal and illegal acts (watching real cheating was Hartshorne and May's method). His stages concern moral reasoning; language acquisition (Chapter 1), motor skills (Section 6.3) and psychosexual conflict (Freud, Chapter 4) belong to other theories.

Three levels, six stages

Kohlberg grouped the reasons into three levels of two stages each (Table 6.25, Figure 6.16), named by the person's relation to conventions, society's rules and expectations.

At the preconventional level (Level I), typical of children under about 9, morality is under external control: the child obeys rules to avoid punishment or gain rewards, so rewarded acts are right and punished acts wrong. Stage 1, the punishment-and-obedience orientation, judges an act by the punishment it brings. Stage 2, instrumental purpose and exchange (naive hedonism), counts as right whatever serves one's own interest: what is in it for me?

At the conventional level (Level II), reached by most adolescents and where most adults stay, the person conforms to social rules to be a good group member and maintain the social order. Stage 3, the good boy-nice girl orientation, counts as right whatever wins the approval of close others; intentions matter, and the golden rule guides relationships. Stage 4, the law-and-order orientation, takes the whole society's view: do your duty, respect authority and obey laws, because society would collapse if everyone broke them.

At the postconventional level (Level III), reached by a minority of adults and rarely before 20, principles can stand above a particular law. Stage 5, the social-contract orientation, sees laws as instruments that express the will of the majority and further human welfare, to be changed by democratic agreement when they fail. Stage 6, universal ethical principles, follows self-chosen principles of justice and human dignity, even against an unjust law.

How sound is Kohlberg's theory?

Kohlberg held that the stages form an invariant sequence: everyone climbs them in the same order, without skipping, and regression is rare; the 20-year Chicago data largely agreed. Each stage needs a cognitive base that is necessary but not sufficient: postconventional reasoning needs formal operations (Section 6.4), yet many formal-operational adults never reach it. It also needs role-taking, seeing a problem from other people's viewpoints (Robert Selman's levels of social perspective taking, Section 6.5), which grows through discussion and education. Stage 6 proved so rare that Kohlberg later dropped it from his scoring manual. Because his interview needs trained scorers, James Rest built the Defining Issues Test in the 1970s, a questionnaire in which people rate and rank prepared statements about each dilemma.

Three criticisms dominate. The first is cultural bias. John Snarey (1985) reviewed 45 studies from 27 cultural areas: stages 1 to 4 appeared in order almost everywhere, but stage 5 mainly in urban, educated groups and hardly ever in village societies, whose communal reasoning the scoring missed. Richard Shweder, studying Hindu families in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, argued that the scale measures only an ethic of autonomy (harm, rights, justice) and misses an ethic of community (duty, hierarchy) and an ethic of divinity (purity, sanctity). The second is the gap between reasoning and behaviour: higher-stage reasoners act morally only somewhat more often. The third is gender bias: the founding sample was all male.

Indian context

Shweder's Oriya Brahmin informants judged it a serious moral wrong for an eldest son to get a haircut and eat chicken the day after his father's death; Chicago informants saw a personal choice. Joan Miller, comparing Hindu Indians in Mysore with Americans, found that Indians treated helping family, friends and even strangers as moral obligations, where Americans saw many of the same acts as personal decisions. A scale built around individual rights scores such duty-based reasoning as merely conventional.

Gilligan: the ethic of care

Carol Gilligan (born 1936), who had worked with Kohlberg at Harvard, noticed that women often scored at stage 3, apparently below men. In In a Different Voice (1982) she argued that his scale rests on a male ethic of justice, reasoning from rights, rules and fairness, and misses the ethic of care more often heard in women, reasoning from relationships and responsibility, wanting no one hurt. Her 11-year-old Jake solved Heinz like "a math problem with humans": life outweighs property. Amy worried that if Heinz went to jail his wife would have no one, and wanted them to talk it out.

From interviews with 29 women deciding about abortion, Gilligan described three levels of care: an orientation to individual survival (caring only for oneself), goodness as self-sacrifice (caring for others at one's own expense) and the morality of nonviolence (caring for self and others, hurting no one).

Is there a sex difference? Lawrence Walker's 1984 review found little difference in Kohlberg scores once education and occupation were allowed for, and later meta-analyses found only small differences in care and justice orientation: both sexes use both voices.

Exam trap

The pairing is fixed: females, ethic of care; males, ethic of justice. Options that reverse it are wrong, and "males have an ethic of justice", though part of her argument, is what Kohlberg already measured; her departure is the female ethic of care. Gilligan added a voice; she did not replace Kohlberg.

Beyond the stage theories

Elliot Turiel's social domain theory (1983) holds that children build separate kinds of social knowledge from early on: the moral domain (harm, fairness, rights), the social-conventional domain (agreed customs such as dress codes or forms of address) and the personal domain (choices such as friends or hairstyle). Even preschoolers say hitting would be wrong in a school with no rule against it, but calling the teacher by her first name would then be fine: they already separate moral from conventional rules.

Nancy Eisenberg studied prosocial moral reasoning, about helping at a cost to oneself (should Mary, on her way to a party, stop to help a girl who has fallen?). Children move through five levels: hedonistic, needs-oriented, approval-oriented, empathic and finally internalised reasons.

Martin Hoffman saw empathy as the main motive of moral action and traced four stages: global empathy in the first year (an infant cries when another cries), egocentric empathy in the second (a toddler fetches his own mother to comfort a crying friend), empathy for another's feelings from about 2 or 3, and in late childhood empathy for another's life condition (for the poor as a group). He also compared three kinds of discipline. Power assertion (force, threats, removing privileges) and love withdrawal (coldness, ignoring the child) produce compliance out of fear or anxiety. Induction, explaining how the child's act affects others ("Riya is crying because you snatched her book"), recruits empathy and guilt and so best fosters internalisation, behaving well when no one is watching (parenting styles are in Section 6.8).

Finally, Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model (2001) reverses the cognitive tradition: moral judgments are fast gut intuitions, and reasoning mostly follows to justify them.

6.10 Erikson's Psychosocial Stages, Identity and AdolescenceMedium yield · 40 Qs

Ask a nineteen-year-old in a Mysuru hostel who she is, and what she will make of her life, and you may get a long pause. Erik Erikson put that question at the centre of a theory that runs from the first feed to the last years, and he gave it a name now used everywhere: the identity crisis. His eight stages come first; then identity is followed into adolescence itself, with its identity statuses, its egocentrism, its changing body and brain, and the old argument over storm and stress.

Erikson and the psychosocial approach

Erik Homburger Erikson (1902 to 1994) lived his own theory. Born in Frankfurt to a Danish mother who never told him who his father was, he carried his stepfather's surname, Homburger, and wandered Europe as an artist in his late teens. At 25 he began teaching at a small school in Vienna, was analysed by Anna Freud (1895 to 1982), and became a psychoanalyst without any university degree. He moved to the United States in 1933 and later treated Second World War veterans whose trouble seemed to be a confused sense of who they were rather than repressed conflict. His key books are Childhood and Society (1950), which set out the eight stages, Young Man Luther (1958), Identity: Youth and Crisis (1968) and Gandhi's Truth (1969).

Erikson belonged to ego psychology, which treats the ego as a partly independent, adaptive part of the personality rather than a servant of the id. Development follows the epigenetic principle: like an embryo whose organs appear on a fixed timetable (epi means "upon"), the personality unfolds through a built-in sequence of stages, while society decides how each turns out. Each stage poses a psychosocial crisis, a turning point between two opposed tendencies set off by new social demands; it is a choice of direction, not a catastrophe. A favourable resolution leaves the ego with a basic strength, or virtue. Favourable means a balance tilted towards the positive pole, not the absence of the negative: a child who trusts everyone is gullible. An unresolved crisis makes later ones harder but not hopeless, because development continues across the whole lifespan.

Erikson coined the term identity crisis for the adolescent struggle to know who one is. His own labels for the eight stages of the life cycle run from the oral-sensory stage through the muscular-anal, locomotor-genital, latency, puberty and adolescence, young adulthood and adulthood stages to the stage of maturity. The first four echo Freud's oral, anal, phallic and latency stages; the adult stages are Erikson's own.

Exam trap

The identity crisis, and the eight stages from the "oral-sensory stage" to the "stage of maturity", are Erikson's. Freud described five psychosexual stages, Harry Stack Sullivan developmental epochs from infancy to adulthood, and Melanie Klein the paranoid-schizoid and depressive positions of infancy (all Chapter 4). John Bowlby is attachment (Section 6.7); Eric Berne is transactional analysis (Chapter 7).

Indian context

Gandhi's Truth (1969) is a psychohistory, a great life read through Erikson's stages and psychoanalytic ideas. Centred on the 1918 Ahmedabad textile mill strike, it asks how one man's identity struggle became a nation's method of nonviolent protest, as Young Man Luther had asked of Martin Luther. It won the Pulitzer Prize.

1. Trust versus mistrust. The helpless infant learns whether the world is dependable. Reliable feeding, comfort and contact build basic trust, an expectation of "consistency, continuity and sameness"; neglect or inconsistency breeds suspicion. The virtue is hope, a lasting confidence that wishes can be met.

2. Autonomy versus shame and doubt. The walking, talking toddler wants to do things alone. Erikson's key acts are holding on and letting go, and the classic battleground is toilet training. Patient parents leave the child with will, the determination to choose and to restrain oneself; harsh or overprotective ones leave shame and self-doubt.

3. Initiative versus guilt. The preschooler plans, explores, asks endless questions and acts out adult roles in play. Encouraged, the child gains purpose, the courage to pursue goals; punished or mocked for each venture, the child learns guilt.

4. Industry versus inferiority. School brings tools, tasks and classmates to compare oneself with. Finishing tasks and earning recognition build industry and competence; repeated failure, or being made to feel behind one's peers, brings inferiority.

5. Identity versus role confusion. With puberty and new choices, the young person must fuse childhood identifications, skills, values and future roles into one answer to "Who am I?" Success brings fidelity, loyalty to freely chosen values and people; failure brings role confusion (see Identity, moratorium and Marcia's statuses).

6. Intimacy versus isolation. With an identity in hand, the young adult can risk merging it with another's, in friendship and love, without losing it. The virtue is love, mutual devotion; failure brings isolation and self-absorption.

7. Generativity versus stagnation. Erikson's generativity is concern for establishing and guiding the next generation: raising children, mentoring juniors, teaching, building work that lasts (parenthood is neither necessary nor sufficient). Its failure is stagnation, a sense of having achieved little and having little to offer the next generation, with boredom and self-indulgence. The virtue is care.

8. Ego integrity versus despair. Looking back, the older person either accepts the one life lived, failures included, as meaningful (ego integrity), or feels regret, bitterness and fear of death because there is no time to start again (despair). The virtue is wisdom, a detached concern with life itself in the face of death.

Some personality texts put the adult boundaries at 35 and 55. The first four outcomes depend mainly on what others do to the child; in the last four the person increasingly chooses.

Applying the stages, and how they go wrong

The stages are easiest to see in people. Mrs Rao, 52, finds her deepest satisfaction in training young engineers: generativity. Her colleague of the same age, who feels he has achieved little and has nothing to pass on, is stagnating. Her father, 78, would live his life the same way again, mistakes and all: integrity. His neighbour broods over wasted chances and dreads death: despair. Her niece in Class 4, measuring herself against classmates while mastering long division, is in industry versus inferiority.

Exam trap

Erikson's term for the mid-life feeling of having achieved little and having little to offer the next generation is stagnation; generativity is the healthy pole. "Mid-life crisis" is a popular term, not Erikson's (Section 6.11), and presbyopia is the age-related loss of near focusing in the eye's lens.

Identity, moratorium and Marcia's statuses

For Erikson adolescence is the hinge of the life cycle. Childhood identifications (my father's son, a good student, a cricketer) must be sorted, kept or dropped, and fused with new abilities, sexual maturity and choices of work, beliefs and love into an ego identity: a sense of being the same person across time and situations, recognised by the people who count. Society helps by granting a psychosocial moratorium, a socially sanctioned delay of adult commitments in which the young can try out courses, friendships and causes before choosing; college years and Erikson's own wandering years are examples.

When the work fails the result is identity confusion (role confusion in Erikson's earlier wording; also identity diffusion): inner fragmentation, little or no sense of where one's life is headed, and an inability to gain the support of a social role or vocation. Some young people instead seize a negative identity, built from the roles their family or community holds up as most undesirable, such as the delinquent, on the logic that it is better to be somebody bad than nobody. Adolescence, then, is a true developmental stage and a moratorium, the fifth of eight and not the last.

Exam trap

"Inner fragmentation, no sense of direction, no support from a social role or vocation" is identity confusion. Incongruence is Carl Rogers's gap between self-concept and experience (Chapter 4); dissociation is a break in the integration of memory, identity and consciousness (Chapter 8); "conflict" is too general.

Key study: Marcia's identity status interview (1966)

James E. Marcia asked whether Erikson's identity could be measured. He interviewed male college students about occupation, religion and politics and scored two dimensions: crisis or exploration, a period of actively weighing alternatives, and commitment, a firm investment in a choice. Crossing them gave four identity statuses (Figure 6.17) that predicted behaviour: achieved students coped best with a stressful task, foreclosed students endorsed authoritarian values most. Criticism: an all-male first sample, and a person can hold different statuses in different areas of life.

  • Neither exploration nor commitment is identity diffusion. The young person drifts; adjustment is poorest.
  • Commitment without exploration is foreclosure. Choices are taken over ready-made, often from parents, like the son who becomes a doctor because the family always assumed he would; such youth tend to be rigid and conforming.
  • Exploration without commitment is moratorium. The person is in the middle of the crisis, anxious and actively trying options.
  • Exploration followed by commitment is identity achievement, the most mature status, with the highest self-esteem.

Statuses are not fixed stages. Achievement grows through late adolescence and young adulthood, and adults reopen settled questions after a divorce, a job loss or migration and then close them again: the MAMA cycles (moratorium, achievement, moratorium, achievement) described by Marcia and colleagues in 1992. Jean Phinney applied the same logic to ethnic identity, from unexamined through exploration to achieved; a secure ethnic identity goes with higher self-esteem in minority youth.

Exam trap

Foreclosure is commitment WITHOUT exploration; moratorium is exploration WITHOUT commitment; achievement has both; diffusion neither. Marcia's dimensions are crisis (exploration) and commitment, not IQ and emotional intelligence, locus of control (Chapter 4) or approach and avoidance motivation (Chapter 1).

Adolescent egocentrism

Formal operational thought (Section 6.4) lets adolescents think about thinking, including other people's. David Elkind argued in 1967 that this power brings a new adolescent egocentrism: the teenager fails to separate what others are thinking about from his or her own preoccupations. It shows in two mental constructions. The imaginary audience is the belief that everyone is watching and judging: the boy who will not enter class with a pimple on his chin, sure that all forty classmates will notice. The personal fable is the belief that one's experiences and feelings are unique, that no one, least of all a parent, could understand them ("You don't know what it's like to be me"), and with it a sense of invulnerability ("It won't happen to me") that feeds reckless riding. Some notes list the invulnerability strand separately as the "mythological fable". Both are strongest in early adolescence and fade by the late teens.

Exam trap

Adolescent egocentrism = imaginary audience ("everyone is looking at me") plus personal fable ("my experiences are unique"). Conservation and reversibility are concrete operational (Section 6.4), inner speech and scaffolding are Vygotsky's (Section 6.5), and object permanence and stranger anxiety belong to infancy (Sections 6.4 and 6.6).

Puberty and the adolescent brain

Adolescence begins with puberty, the biological changes that bring sexual maturity; adolescence itself is the wider psychological and social transition. The hormones come in two waves. In adrenarche, from about 6 to 8, the adrenal glands raise their output of weak androgens. In gonadarche, from about 9 or 10, the hypothalamus and pituitary switch on the ovaries and testes, which pour out oestrogens and testosterone (Chapter 5). The visible result is the growth spurt, a rapid gain in height and weight that starts about two years earlier in girls (around 10) than in boys (around 12), so that for a while many girls are taller than boys of their age. The primary sex characteristics are the reproductive organs themselves (ovaries, uterus and vagina; testes, penis and scrotum); secondary sex characteristics are the other visible signs (breasts, body and facial hair, a deeper voice, wider hips or shoulders). In girls, menarche, the first menstrual period, comes late in the sequence, at about 12 or 13; spermarche, the first ejaculation, at about 13 or 14. Better nutrition and health have pulled menarche earlier, from about 16 or 17 in mid-nineteenth-century Europe to about 12 or 13 today, and made adults taller: the secular trend.

Timing matters too. Early-maturing boys tend to be confident and popular, though they try alcohol and delinquency earlier; late-maturing boys are often more anxious. Early-maturing girls carry the greatest risk: looking older than they feel, they attract older friends and show more depression, poor body image, eating problems and substance use.

Inside the skull, regions mature at different speeds. Laurence Steinberg's dual systems model (2008) pairs a reward system in the limbic system and ventral striatum, which becomes sharply more responsive around puberty, with a cognitive control system centred on the prefrontal cortex, which matures slowly into the mid-twenties (Figure 6.18). In mid-adolescence the accelerator is pressed while the brakes are still being fitted, so risk-taking peaks, above all among peers.

Key study: risk with friends watching (Gardner and Steinberg, 2005)

Margo Gardner and Steinberg had adolescents (13 to 16), college-age youths and adults play a video driving game in which they could brake at an amber light or gamble on getting through, alone or with two friends watching. Friends' presence roughly doubled the adolescents' risk-taking, raised the youths' by about half and left the adults' unchanged. Criticism: a laboratory game, though it matches teen drivers' higher crash rates with teen passengers.

Storm and stress, tasks, parents and peers

Must adolescence be turbulent? G. Stanley Hall (1844 to 1924), in Adolescence (1904), said yes: the teens were a biologically driven period of storm and stress, of mood swings, conflict with parents and risk-taking. Margaret Mead (1901 to 1978) answered from the field. In Coming of Age in Samoa (1928) she described Samoan girls passing smoothly into adulthood in a relaxed culture with many caring adults, and concluded that turmoil is made by culture, not biology. Derek Freeman (1983) later attacked her fieldwork, but modern surveys support a middle view: most adolescents cope well and share their parents' basic values, while a minority run into serious trouble. Conflict and moodiness do rise in early adolescence; storm and stress is real but not universal.

Robert J. Havighurst (1900 to 1991) listed the developmental tasks of adolescence (the idea itself is in Section 6.1): new and more mature relations with age-mates of both sexes; a masculine or feminine social role; accepting one's body; emotional independence from parents and other adults; preparing for marriage and family life; preparing for a career; a set of values and an ethical system to guide behaviour; and socially responsible behaviour. Emotional separation from parents, strong peer identification and heterosexual peer interest are therefore all tasks of adolescence.

Relations with parents are renegotiated, not cut. The older psychoanalytic view of Anna Freud and Peter Blos held that healthy adolescents must detach from their parents. Research now shows that autonomy grows best inside continued connection: teenagers who stay securely attached explore more confidently and adjust better, so it can be healthy for interdependence to continue. Detachment does not typically happen earlier, in middle childhood; peers do not simply replace parents, who keep their influence on values, education and career while peers sway dress, music and leisure; and parents' goals differ across families and cultures, many Indian families valuing lasting interdependence.

Dexter Dunphy (1963), observing teenagers in Sydney, distinguished cliques, small groups of close friends, from crowds, larger groups formed when several cliques join for parties and outings. He traced five stages: isolated same-sex cliques; same-sex cliques beginning to interact; their leaders forming the first mixed-sex clique; a full crowd of mixed-sex cliques; and the crowd loosening into groups of couples. Conformity to peers rises in early adolescence, peaks at about 14 and then declines as autonomy matures.

Exam trap

Today's view of adolescents and parents: continued interdependence can be healthy. Not that detachment comes earlier in middle childhood, that the peer group matters more than parents, or that all parents share the same goals.

6.11 Adulthood, Ageing, Death and DyingLower yield · 2 Qs

Development does not stop at twenty. A Mysuru engineer of 24 unsure of his career, his mother of 50 nursing an ailing father, and his grandfather of 80 trimming his circle of friends are all still developing.

Emerging adulthood and adult development

In 2000 Jeffrey Jensen Arnett named the years from about 18 to 25 emerging adulthood, a period created in industrialised societies by later marriage, parenthood and settled work. Its five features are identity exploration (in love and work), instability, self-focus, feeling in-between (neither adolescent nor adult) and possibilities (life can still go many ways). Its Eriksonian task, intimacy versus isolation, is in Section 6.10.

Daniel Levinson (1920 to 1994) drew the best-known map of what follows. In The Seasons of a Man's Life (1978), built on interviews with 40 men aged 35 to 45, each man has a life structure, the underlying pattern of his life at a given time, built mainly around work and family. Overlapping eras alternate stable periods, when a structure is built, with transitions of about five years, when it is questioned and remade (Figure 6.19): the early adult transition (17 to 22) leaves adolescence behind, and the age-30 transition (28 to 33) revises the first adult structure. Indian context

Most Indian marriages are still arranged by families, now usually with the couple's consent; love is expected to grow after the wedding, and the marriage joins two families.

Elsewhere more adults cohabit, divorce is commoner, and a first child usually lowers marital satisfaction for a while. Work is the other pillar. Eli Ginzberg (1911 to 2002; Occupational Choice, 1951) described three periods of career choice: fantasy (to about 11), wishing without regard to ability; tentative (about 11 to 17), weighing interests, capacities and values; and realistic (17 to the early twenties), exploring, crystallising and specifying one choice. Donald Super's career stages are in Chapter 7.

Middle adulthood: the climacteric and midlife

From about 40 the body changes slowly. Near vision blurs as the lens stiffens (presbyopia), hearing for high pitches fades (presbycusis), bone mass falls (especially in women after menopause), hair recedes and greys, muscle strength and agility decline, and reaction time and manual dexterity slow. All of this is general physical ageing.

The climacteric is narrower: the end of ovulation and termination of reproductive capacity. In women it culminates in menopause, the last menstrual period (confirmed after 12 months without one), as ovarian oestrogen falls. The average age is about 51 in Western samples and about 46 to 47 in Indian surveys. The male climacteric, or andropause, is a slow decline in testosterone; most men stay fertile into old age.

Exam trap

Climacteric means the end of ovulation and termination of reproductive capacity (JEMAS 2025). Lost strength, receding or greying hair, and slower reactions and dexterity are general ageing. Menopause is the event within the climacteric, not a synonym.

Elliott Jaques (1917 to 2003) coined midlife crisis in 1965, from creative artists confronting mortality in their mid-thirties. Large surveys find that only a minority report one, usually after illness or job loss. Midlife is more often a peak of competence and generativity (Section 6.10). The empty nest, when the last child leaves, more often brings relief than depression. The sandwich generation are middle-aged adults, usually women, caring for children and ageing parents at once. K. Warner Schaie's Seattle Longitudinal Study (begun 1956) found that most mental abilities peak in middle adulthood, while perceptual speed declines from early adulthood.

Late adulthood and the biology of ageing

Gerontology studies the biological, psychological and social sides of ageing; geriatrics is the medicine of old age. Robert Butler (1927 to 2010) coined ageism, prejudice against people because they are old, in 1969. Older adults are divided into the young-old (65 to 74), old-old (75 to 84) and oldest-old (85 and over). Life span is the maximum possible length of life, about 120 years (Jeanne Calment lived 122); life expectancy is the average number of years a person born in a given year can expect to live, about 70 in India.

Primary ageing is gradual, inevitable and universal bodily decline. Secondary ageing comes from disease, disuse and lifestyle, so it is partly preventable. Tertiary ageing is the rapid loss shortly before death; its cognitive form, a sharp fall in test scores, is the terminal drop (Robert Kleemeier, 1962).

Programmed theories say the genes set ageing's timetable. Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead (1961) found that human cells in culture divide only about 40 to 60 times (some textbooks say 75 to 80), the Hayflick limit, apparently because telomeres, the caps on chromosome ends, shorten with each division. Damage, or variable-rate, theories blame accumulated random injury instead (Table 6.32).

Cognition, retirement and elder care

Fluid intelligence declines from early adulthood while crystallised intelligence holds or rises into old age (Section 1.9). Timothy Salthouse's processing-speed theory (1996) traces much of the loss to a general slowing of processing. Episodic and working memory decline; semantic and procedural memory hold. Paul Baltes's Berlin wisdom paradigm defines wisdom as expert knowledge about the fundamental pragmatics of life, judged by factual and procedural knowledge, lifespan contextualism, value relativism and managing uncertainty; it reflects experience more than age. Dementia is disease, not normal ageing (Chapters 5 and 8).

Retirement is a process: Atchley described a honeymoon, then possible disenchantment, reorientation and stability; it goes best when chosen, healthy and funded. Widowhood falls mostly on women, who live longer and usually marry older men.

Indian context

As joint families shrink, the law has stepped in. The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007 obliges children (or, for the childless, heirs) to maintain parents and senior citizens (60 and over). Tribunals can order up to Rs 10,000 a month, and abandoning a senior citizen is punishable by up to three months in prison, a Rs 5,000 fine, or both.

Death, dying and grief

Death was once the stopping of heart and breathing. Life support broke that link, so India (Transplantation of Human Organs Act 1994) and most countries also recognise brain death: irreversible loss of all brain function or, in Indian law, of all brainstem function. In a persistent vegetative state the brainstem still works.

Maria Nagy (1948) questioned 378 Budapest children aged 3 to 10. Under about 5, death was a departure or sleep, and reversible; from 5 to 9 it was final but personified, a "death-man" who could be escaped; after 9 it was universal and inevitable. Mark Speece and Sandor Brent (1984) named three parts of a mature concept, irreversibility, universality and nonfunctionality (all functions stop), grasped by most children at about 5 to 7.

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's five stages of dying (On Death and Dying, 1969) are denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance (Section 7.9). John Bowlby (1907 to 1990) saw mourning as a broken attachment (Section 6.7) with four phases: numbness, yearning and searching, disorganisation and despair, and reorganisation. Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut's dual process model (1999) replaces fixed stages with oscillation between loss-oriented coping (grief work, yearning) and restoration-oriented coping (new tasks and roles, time off from grief).

Cicely Saunders (1918 to 2005) opened St Christopher's Hospice, London, in 1967; hospices give palliative care, aiming at comfort and dignity rather than cure (Section 7.9). Euthanasia is active when a lethal act ends life and passive euthanasia when life support is withheld or withdrawn; India permits only the passive kind. In Aruna Shanbaug v Union of India (2011), about a Mumbai nurse left vegetative by a 1973 assault, the Supreme Court refused the plea but allowed passive euthanasia with High Court approval. Common Cause v Union of India (2018) held that dying with dignity is part of the right to life (Article 21) and upheld the living will, an advance directive refusing life support.

Exam trap

Kübler-Ross's stages describe the dying; Bowlby's phases, the bereaved; Stroebe and Schut's model is oscillation, not stages. Shanbaug (2011) allowed passive euthanasia; Common Cause (2018) added living wills.

The Wild Boy of Aveyron, Revisited

Look at Victor again with this chapter behind you. Pinel and Itard were arguing nature versus nurture (Section 6.1), and the modern answer is that each may have been partly right: Victor may have had a developmental disability, perhaps autism, before he was abandoned (Sections 6.2 and 6.3). His failure to speak after years of teaching fits a sensitive period for language that had largely closed (Section 6.1), while his growing problem solving and his bond with Madame Guérin show cognition and attachment still developing (Sections 6.4 to 6.7). His protest at unjust punishment touches moral development (Section 6.9), the turmoil puberty brought him touches adolescence (Section 6.10), and his quiet life to about forty is a reminder that development runs across the whole lifespan (Section 6.11).

Chapter summary

6.1 Foundations: The Lifespan View, Key Issues and Ecological Contexts. Developmental psychology studies change and stability from conception to death. Growth is quantitative, development qualitative, and maturation is the biological unfolding of genetic potential (Gesell; co-twin control, 1929). Hurlock divides life into ten periods; Baltes's lifespan perspective (1987) sees development as lifelong, multidirectional and plastic, shaped by age-graded, history-graded and non-normative influences. Development runs cephalocaudally and proximodistally, with Havighurst's developmental tasks at each period. Core issues include nature versus nurture and continuity versus discontinuity. A critical period is fixed and irreversible (imprinting, embryonic organ formation); a sensitive period is flexible (language). Bronfenbrenner (1979) nests the child in micro-, meso-, exo- and macrosystems, with the chronosystem added later.

6.2 Heredity, Prenatal Development, Birth and the Newborn. Each body cell carries 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs; meiosis halves them in sperm and ovum, which unite as a zygote. In dominant-recessive inheritance a heterozygote shows the dominant trait, while incomplete dominance, codominance, X-linked, polygenic inheritance and genomic imprinting vary the pattern. Chromosomal errors include Down (trisomy 21), Klinefelter (47,XXY), Turner (45,X) and Prader-Willi (paternal 15q11-q13 deletion). MZ twins reared apart best separate heredity from environment, which also correlate and interact. Prenatal life runs through germinal, embryonic (gastrulation, neural tube, greatest vulnerability to teratogens) and fetal periods. Newborns are scored on the Apgar scale and show primitive reflexes such as the Moro and Babinski.

6.3 Infancy: Brain, Body, Motor Skills and Perception. Birth weight doubles by about 5 months and triples by 1 year, and growth and motor control run cephalocaudal and proximodistal (never distal to proximal). Neurons are mostly made before birth; synaptogenesis, synaptic pruning and myelination take the brain from about 25 percent of adult weight to half by about 6 months. The first voluntary grasp is ulnar; the pincer grasp follows by 9 to 12 months. Gesell stressed maturation, Dennis experience and Thelen dynamic systems. Looking methods reveal face preference and perceptual narrowing; the visual cliff shows depth perception by crawling age, when independent locomotion transforms affordances. Newborns imitate (Meltzoff and Moore, 1977), and comprehension leads production.

6.4 Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development. Jean Piaget (1896 to 1980) saw intelligence as biological adaptation: the child, an active little scientist, builds schemes and adapts them by assimilation (fitting experience into existing schemes) and accommodation (changing schemes), driven by equilibration. Four stages follow in invariant order: sensorimotor (birth to 2; object permanence begins at 8 to 12 months with the A-not-B error and is complete by 18 to 24), preoperational (2 to 7; egocentrism, centration, no conservation), concrete operational (7 to 11; conservation, reversibility, transitivity) and formal operational (11 or 12 onward; hypothetico-deductive reasoning). Dialectical reasoning is postformal. Baillargeon, Donaldson and the neo-Piagetians showed he underestimated young children.

6.5 Vygotsky, Bruner, Information Processing and Theory of Mind. Lev Vygotsky's sociocultural theory holds that higher mental functions arise in social interaction, mediated by cultural tools (language the master tool): every function appears first between people, then within the child. The zone of proximal development is the gap between what a learner can do alone and with a more knowledgeable other; support there is scaffolding (Wood, Bruner and Ross, 1976). For Vygotsky language shapes thought and private speech becomes inner speech. Bruner described enactive, iconic and symbolic representation. Information-processing research tracks speed, strategies and metacognition. False-belief tasks are passed at about 4, and Selman traced perspective taking from egocentric to societal.

6.6 Emotional Development, Temperament and Self-Regulation. Bridges (1932) held that the newborn's general excitement differentiates first into distress and delight; primary emotions are present within six months, the social smile at 6 to 10 weeks, and the self-conscious emotions only after 12 months. Stranger and separation anxiety peak at about 8 to 15 months, and Tronick's still face shows that infants expect affective reciprocity. Temperament is early, moderately stable and biologically based: Thomas and Chess gave nine dimensions, the easy, difficult and slow-to-warm-up types and goodness of fit; Rothbart and Bates reduced it to surgency, negative affectivity and effortful control; Kagan studied behavioural inhibition. Mischel's marshmallow test measures delay of gratification.

6.7 Attachment: Imprinting, Bowlby, Ainsworth and Beyond. Attachment is an enduring bond shown by proximity seeking, secure-base and safe-haven behaviour and separation distress. Lorenz's imprinting and Harlow's contact comfort undermined the feeding-based psychoanalytic and secondary-drive theories, leaving Bowlby's ethological theory (an evolved system, monotropy, a critical period, the internal working model) predominant. Bowlby's four phases, Schaffer and Emerson's stages and Mahler's separation-individuation chart the first three years. Ainsworth's Strange Situation sorts infants by reunion behaviour into secure (B), avoidant (A) and resistant (C), plus Main and Solomon's disorganised (D). Spitz, Rutter and the adoption studies show privation harms but is partly reversible.

6.8 Parenting, Family, Play and Gender in Childhood. Diana Baumrind described authoritative, authoritarian and permissive parenting; Maccoby and Martin (1983) crossed demandingness with responsiveness and added the neglectful style. Authoritative parenting, high on both, gives the best outcomes, though warm control also works in Chinese and Indian families. Helicopter parenting has several roots, and the family acts as a system. Neglect is physical, supervisory, medical, educational or emotional; licensed day care is not neglect. Piaget's play runs from functional to symbolic play to games with rules, Smilansky added constructive play, and Parten traced solitary to cooperative play. Children learn the gender map through socialisation; Kohlberg and gender schema theory explain how they then socialise themselves.

6.9 Moral Development: Piaget, Kohlberg, Gilligan and Beyond. Morality has emotional (guilt, empathy, the superego), behavioural (social learning; Hartshorne and May found honesty situation-specific) and cognitive sides. Piaget (1932) traced a shift from heteronomous morality (fixed rules, judging by consequences, immanent justice, expiatory punishment) to autonomous morality (rules as agreements, intentions, reciprocal punishment). Kohlberg posed dilemmas such as Heinz's and scored the reasoning: preconventional (punishment, exchange), conventional (approval, law and order) and postconventional (social contract, universal principles). Critics cite cultural bias (Snarey, Shweder, Miller), the reasoning and behaviour gap and gender bias; Gilligan added the female ethic of care. Turiel, Eisenberg, Hoffman and Haidt widened the field.

6.10 Erikson's Psychosocial Stages, Identity and Adolescence. Erik Erikson (1902 to 1994), an ego psychologist, proposed eight psychosocial stages governed by the epigenetic principle, each a crisis whose favourable resolution yields a virtue: trust (hope), autonomy (will), initiative (purpose), industry (competence), identity (fidelity), intimacy (love), generativity (care) and integrity (wisdom). One-sided resolution gives maladaptive or malignant tendencies. Adolescence is a psychosocial moratorium whose failure is identity confusion. James Marcia (1966) crossed exploration with commitment to give diffusion, foreclosure, moratorium and achievement. David Elkind described the imaginary audience and personal fable. Puberty, Steinberg's dual systems model and modern research temper Hall's storm and stress: connection with parents stays healthy.

6.11 Adulthood, Ageing, Death and Dying. Arnett's emerging adulthood (about 18 to 25) leads into Levinson's life structures, eras, transitions and Dream, joined by Havighurst's developmental tasks, Vaillant, Gould, Neugarten's social clock and Peck. Hurlock's marriages are traditional, companionship or collegial (comradeship with different roles). The climacteric is the end of ovulation and reproductive capacity, culminating in menopause; the midlife crisis (Jaques, 1965) is not universal. Ageing is explained by programmed theories (Hayflick limit, telomeres) and damage theories (free radicals), and adapted to through disengagement, activity, continuity, socioemotional selectivity or SOC. Speed and fluid abilities fall; crystallised knowledge holds. Grief follows Bowlby's phases or Stroebe and Schut's dual process; India allows passive euthanasia and living wills.

  1. Which term refers to the biological unfolding of genetic potential? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Maturation (b) Socialization (c) Accommodation (d) Conditioning

    Answer: (a) Maturation. Maturation (Arnold Gesell) is change on a genetic timetable, largely independent of practice. Socialisation, accommodation (Piaget's adjustment of a scheme) and conditioning all depend on experience.

  2. The fact that people can use later experiences to compensate for earlier deficits is a contradiction of which element of earlier developmental psychology? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Critical periods (b) Continuity (c) Sensitive periods (d) Stability

    Answer: (a) Critical periods. A strict critical period makes a missed experience irreversible, so recovery through later experience (Clarke and Clarke, 1976; the English and Romanian Adoptees study) contradicts it. Sensitive periods allow late recovery by definition; continuity and stability concern other questions.

  3. In the case of human development, the strongest evidence for the critical period hypothesis is found in: (RML 2024)

    (a) Imprinting (b) Language acquisition (c) Embryonic development (d) Development of vocational interests

    Answer keyed: (a) Imprinting. Imprinting (Lorenz) is the textbook critical period, but it is animal evidence; within human development the clearest critical periods are in embryonic development, where teratogen timing decides which organ is damaged. Language shows a sensitive period; vocational interests show none.

  4. If a blue eye allele (D) is dominant and a green eye allele (d) is recessive, what is the phenotype of a heterozygous (Dd) child? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Green eyes (b) Blue eyes (c) Blue-green eyes (d) Cannot be determined

    Answer: (b) Blue eyes. In dominant-recessive inheritance a heterozygote shows the dominant allele's trait. Green eyes need the dd genotype, a blue-green blend would need incomplete dominance, and the phenotype can be determined because dominance is stated.

  5. During embryonic development, the nervous system begins forming at the : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Zygote stage (b) Morula stage (c) Gastrula stage (d) Blastocyst stage

    Answer: (c) Gastrula stage. At gastrulation, in week 3, the three germ layers form and the neural plate arises from ectoderm, folding into the neural tube by about week 4. The zygote, morula and blastocyst are earlier stages with no germ layers yet.

  6. A child with hyperphagia, obesity, short stature, and hypotonia is found to have a deletion on chromosome 15 (15q11-q13). This syndrome is : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Fragile X syndrome (b) Turner syndrome (c) Prader-Willi syndrome (d) Klinefelter syndrome

    Answer: (c) Prader-Willi syndrome. Loss of the paternal 15q11-q13 region gives hypotonia, hyperphagia, obesity and short stature; loss of the maternal copy gives Angelman syndrome. Fragile X is an FMR1 disorder on the X chromosome, Turner is 45,X and Klinefelter 47,XXY.

  7. Human motor development is governed by certain basic principles. Which of the following is Not a basic principle of motor development? (RML 2021)

    (a) Maturation of the motor system proceeds from gross motor to fine motor behaviours. (b) Motor development is from cephalo-to-caudal or from head to feet. (c) Motor development proceeds from the distal to the proximal or from the extremities to the central part of the body. (d) Motor development plays a major role in social and behavioural expectations.

    Answer: (c) Distal to proximal. The proximodistal principle runs from the centre of the body to the extremities, so (c) reverses it. Gross to fine, cephalocaudal and the social role of motor development are all genuine principles.

  8. What is the principal factor that changes the affordances available to the toddler, compared with the infant? (RML 2022)

    (a) Independent locomotion (b) Starting to speak (c) The attachment relationship (d) New kinds of caretaking

    Answer: (a) Independent locomotion. Affordances are the action possibilities the environment offers a perceiver, so they change most when the child's own action abilities change: crawling and walking make toys reachable and drops dangerous. Speech, attachment and caretaking change the social world, not what the surroundings afford action.

  9. The process of synaptic pruning is most closely associated with : (RML 2023)

    (a) Memory consolidation (b) Neuronal development and plasticity (c) Neurotransmitter release (d) Long-term potentiation

    Answer: (b) Neuronal development and plasticity. The young brain overproduces synapses and prunes the little-used ones, fitting circuits to experience. Memory consolidation, neurotransmitter release and long-term potentiation describe how memories and signals work at existing synapses, not the developmental sculpting of circuits.

  10. Which is not attained in Piaget's concrete operational stage of cognitive development? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Reduced egocentrism (b) Deferred imitation (c) Hypothetico deductive reasoning (d) Transitive inference

    Answer: (c) Hypothetico deductive reasoning. It is the hallmark of the formal operational stage (11 or 12 onward). Reduced egocentrism and transitive inference are concrete operational gains, and deferred imitation was attained earlier, at 18 to 24 months in the sensorimotor stage, so it is present rather than missing.

  11. A 5-year-old child is asked to sort a deck of cards. The cards can be sorted by color (red/black) or by suit (hearts, spades, etc.). The child successfully sorts by color but cannot then re-sort the same deck by suit. This inflexibility is an example of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Egocentrism (b) Animism (c) Centration (d) Transductive reasoning

    Answer: (c) Centration. The preoperational child focuses on one salient feature (colour) and ignores others (suit). Egocentrism is failure to take another's perspective, animism is giving life to objects, and transductive reasoning runs from particular to particular.

  12. Formal operational reasoning involves all the following EXCEPT: (RML 2022)

    (a) hypothetical reasoning (b) counterfactual reasoning (c) dialectical reasoning (d) metacognitive reasoning

    Answer: (c) Dialectical reasoning. Integrating opposing views is postformal thought (Riegel 1973, Basseches 1984). Hypothetical, counterfactual and metacognitive reasoning all belong to Piaget's formal operational stage.

  13. Vygotsky's concept of the 'zone of proximal development' (ZPD) refers to: (RML 2025)

    (a) The difference between what a learner can do independently and what they can achieve with guidance. (b) A measure of a person's fixed learning capacity across cognitive domains. (c) A developmental stage characterised by logical reasoning capability. (d) The optimal period during which specific cognitive skills must develop.

    Answer: (a) The difference between what a learner can do independently and what they can achieve with guidance. The ZPD runs from the actual developmental level (independent problem solving) to the level reached with a more knowledgeable other, and it moves as skills are internalised. It is not a fixed capacity, not a stage of logical operations and not a critical or optimal period.

  14. What is the primary difference between Piaget and Vygotsky's theories of language development? (RML 2021)

    (a) Piaget argued thought depends on language while Vygotsky argued language depends on thought (b) Vygotsky argued thought depends on language while Piaget argued language depends on thought (c) Vygotsky was concerned with early language development while Piaget was concerned with later language development. (d) None of the above

    Answer: (b). For Piaget language expresses concepts already built through action, so it depends on thought; for Vygotsky thought and speech have separate roots that merge at about 2, after which language shapes thought. Option (a) swaps the names, and (c) is false: both studied the speech of young children (egocentric speech for Piaget, private speech for Vygotsky).

  15. In the 'teleological stance', toddlers can understand: (RML 2022)

    (a) Contingencies (b) Intentions in action (c) Prior Intentions (d) Mental states such as belief and desire

    Answer: (b) Intentions in action. In Gergely and Csibra's teleological stance, infants near the end of the first year read an action as efficient and goal-directed, so they grasp the goal visible in an ongoing act. They have moved beyond mere contingencies but do not yet represent prior intentions (plans formed before acting) or beliefs and desires, which come with desire psychology at about 2 and belief-desire psychology at 3 to 4.

  16. Which of the following is a key characteristic of the "slow-to-warm-up" temperament category in the Thomas and Chess model? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Positive approach to new stimuli and high regularity of biological functions. (b) Negative withdrawal from new stimuli, slow adaptability, and mild intensity of reactions. (c) Intense and frequent negative emotional reactions with irregular routines. (d) A mix of easy and difficult characteristics with no clear pattern.

    Answer: (b) Negative withdrawal from new stimuli, slow adaptability, and mild intensity of reactions. The slow-to-warm-up child withdraws at first but mildly, and adapts gradually. Option (a) describes the easy child, (c) the difficult child, and (d) the roughly 35 percent who fitted no single type.

  17. Which among the following is not included within the classification of structure of temperament given by Rothbart and Bates (RML 2022)

    (a) Extraversion (b) Inhibition to the unfamiliar (c) Negative affectivity (d) Effortful control

    Answer: (b) Inhibition to the unfamiliar. Rothbart and Bates have exactly three dimensions: surgency (extraversion), negative affectivity and effortful control. Inhibition to the unfamiliar is Jerome Kagan's behavioural inhibition.

  18. According to experts on socioemotional development like Campos (2009) and Kagan (2010), self-conscious emotions appear at which age? (RML 2022)

    (a) 6-9 months (b) Before 9 months (c) After 12 months (d) 3 months

    Answer: (c) After 12 months. Embarrassment, pride, shame, guilt, envy and empathy need self-awareness, which arrives in the second half of the second year (mirror self-recognition at 18 to 24 months). The earlier ages belong to the primary emotions.

  19. Children, who are distressed by their parent's absence, seek interaction but also reject it when offered. How can the reaction be categorised? (RML 2021)

    (a) Insecure-avoidant (b) Insecure-resistant (c) Insecure-disorganized (d) Secure-resistant

    Answer: (b) Insecure-resistant. Ainsworth's type C (ambivalent) infants are very distressed at separation and on reunion seek contact yet resist it. Avoidant (A) infants show little distress and ignore the caregiver, disorganised (D) infants show contradictory or freezing behaviour, and 'secure-resistant' is not a type.

  20. According to the schema theory, which hypothetical construct provides a prototype for subsequent attachments and can be adapted through more experience? (RML 2021)

    (a) Proximity-seeking mechanism (b) Attachment system (c) Bio-social relationship model (d) Internal working model

    Answer: (d) Internal working model. Bowlby's internal working model is a schema of self, attachment figures and relationships, built from early experience, that acts as a prototype for later relationships and can be revised. Proximity seeking is a behaviour the attachment system produces, the attachment system is the evolved control system itself, and 'bio-social relationship model' is not Bowlby's term.

  21. Which theory of attachment is currently considered the predominantly accepted theory by developmental psychologists? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Psychoanalytic (b) Learning (c) Cognitive-developmental (d) Ethological

    Answer: (d) Ethological. Bowlby's ethological theory treats attachment as an evolved behavioural system that keeps the infant near a protector. The psychoanalytic and learning (secondary drive, 'cupboard love') theories rest on feeding, which Harlow's contact comfort findings undermined, and the cognitive-developmental view explains only the timing of attachment.

  22. Which parenting style is characterized by the combination of giving emotional support, setting clear boundaries, and maintaining accountability for children's behavior? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Authoritarian parenting (b) Authoritative parenting (c) Permissive parenting (d) Neglectful parenting

    Answer: (b) Authoritative parenting. Warmth (emotional support) plus firm, reasoned control (boundaries, accountability) is Baumrind's authoritative style, high on both demandingness and responsiveness. Authoritarian is high control without warmth, permissive is warmth without control, and neglectful lacks both.

  23. According to Jean Piaget's theory of play development, what is the first stage of child play that emerges during infancy? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Symbolic play (b) Functional play (c) Games with rules (d) Constructive play

    Answer: (b) Functional play. Functional (practice or sensorimotor) play, repeating actions for the pleasure of exercising sensorimotor schemes, comes first in infancy. Symbolic play needs the preoperational symbolic function, games with rules belong to the concrete operational years, and constructive play was added later by Smilansky.

  24. The starting point for self-socialization, according to Kohlberg's cognitive-developmental theory of gender-role development, is: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Gender consistency (b) Basic gender identity (c) Gender stability (d) Gender-role achievement

    Answer: (b) Basic gender identity (as keyed). Labelling oneself a boy or girl is the first link in Kohlberg's chain. Many textbooks say Kohlberg's children become full self-socialisers only at gender consistency, and that it is gender schema theory that starts self-socialisation at basic gender identity. Gender-role achievement is not one of his stages.

  25. What is the focus of Kohlberg's preconventional level of moral development? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Social contract (b) Avoiding punishment and gaining rewards (c) Obeying laws (d) Ethical principles

    Answer: (b) Avoiding punishment and gaining rewards. At Level I morality is under external control: stage 1 obeys to avoid punishment and stage 2 seeks rewards and fair deals. Obeying laws is stage 4 (conventional); social contract and ethical principles are stages 5 and 6 (postconventional).

  26. Jean Piaget's theory of moral development suggests that children in the stage of 'heteronomous morality' (typically ages 5-10) base their moral judgments on: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Intentions and equality (b) Rules as being unchangeable and the consequences of actions (c) Social contracts and individual rights (d) Abstract principles of justice

    Answer: (b) Rules as being unchangeable and the consequences of actions. Heteronomous children (moral realism) treat adult rules as sacred and judge acts by the damage done (objective responsibility). Intentions and equality belong to Piaget's autonomous stage; social contracts and abstract justice are Kohlberg's stages 5 and 6.

  27. Carol Gilligan departs from Kohlberg's theory on moral development by highlighting which of these differences? (RML 2022)

    (a) Males have ethics of care (b) Males have ethics of justice (c) Females have ethics care (d) Females have ethics of justice

    Answer: (c) Females have an ethic of care. In In a Different Voice (1982) Gilligan argued that Kohlberg's scale captures a male ethic of justice and misses the ethic of care, reasoning from relationships and responsibility, more often heard in women. Options (a) and (d) reverse the pairing; (b) is what Kohlberg already measured.

  28. According to Erik Erikson's psychosocial development theory, a child who is 1.5 years old (18 months) would be in which developmental stage? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Trust vs. Mistrust (b) Autonomy vs. Shame and Doubt (c) Initiative vs. Guilt (d) Industry vs. Inferiority

    Answer: (b) Autonomy vs. Shame and Doubt. Eighteen months is the boundary between the first two stages, and the keyed answer places the toddler, now walking and facing toilet training, in autonomy versus shame and doubt. Initiative versus guilt begins at about 3 and industry versus inferiority at school age.

  29. According to Erikson, the state of ______ involves feelings of inner fragmentation, little or no sense of where one's life is headed, and an inability to gain the support of a social role or vocation. (Amity 2023)

    (a) Conflict (b) Identity confusion (c) Incongruence (d) Dissociation

    Answer: (b) Identity confusion. It is the negative pole of Erikson's fifth stage, identity versus role confusion. Incongruence is Carl Rogers's gap between self-concept and experience, dissociation is a break in the integration of memory and identity, and conflict is too general.

  30. Which term was used by Erikson to refer to the feeling experienced by some individuals in mid-life that they have achieved very little or nothing and have little to offer to the next generation? (RML 2020)

    (a) Stagnation (b) Generativity (c) Presbyopia (d) Mid-life crisis

    Answer: (a) Stagnation. It is the negative pole of generativity versus stagnation in middle adulthood. Generativity is the healthy pole (guiding the next generation), presbyopia is age-related loss of near focusing in the lens, and mid-life crisis is a popular term, not Erikson's.

  31. In adulthood climacteric refers to (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) loss of muscular strength and agility. (b) receding hairlines and greying of hair in adults. (c) the end of ovulation and termination of reproductive capacity. (d) gradual decline in reaction time and manual dexterity.

    Answer: (c) the end of ovulation and termination of reproductive capacity. The climacteric is the midlife winding down of reproductive capacity, culminating in menopause in women. Lost strength and agility, receding and greying hair, and slower reaction time and dexterity are real midlife changes, but they are general physical ageing, not the climacteric.

  32. The type of marriage in which comradeship and sharing are emphasized, but husband and wife assume responsibility for different roles is known as (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Traditional marriage. (b) Collegial marriage. (c) Companionship marriage. (d) Satisfactory marriage.

    Answer: (b) Collegial marriage. In Hurlock's typology the collegial marriage stresses comradeship and sharing while each spouse takes responsibility for different, complementary roles. A companionship marriage shares the same roles, a traditional marriage separates roles without stressing comradeship, and satisfactory marriage is not a type.
