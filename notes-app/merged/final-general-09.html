In 1904 the French Ministry of Public Instruction had a problem that any Indian school inspector would recognise. The education laws of the early 1880s had made primary schooling free and compulsory, so every child in Paris now sat in a classroom, and some of them could not keep up. Which of these children were slow because they lacked the capacity to learn at the ordinary pace, and which were simply badly taught, mischievous, often absent or disliked by their teachers? Teachers' opinions were no help: the same child could be called "backward" by one teacher and "lazy" by the next. The ministry set up a commission to study the education of what it called "abnormal" children, and one of its members was a 47-year-old psychologist named Alfred Binet.

Binet had spent years watching children think, including his own two young daughters, and he had become convinced that the measures then in fashion (reaction times, the sharpness of hearing, the size of the head) told you almost nothing about a child's mind. Intelligence, he argued, shows itself in judgement: in understanding a question, keeping a goal in mind and noticing your own mistakes. So, with a young physician named Théodore Simon, he did something disarmingly simple. They collected dozens of small tasks, tried them on children in Paris schools and institutions, and kept the ones that older children passed more often than younger children. The result, published in 1905, was a ladder of 30 tasks. At the bottom, a child had only to follow a lighted match with the eyes; near the top, the child had to explain how two abstract words differed or build a sentence around three given words. How far up the ladder a child climbed, compared with other children of the same age, was the measure.

Binet was proud of the scale and wary of it. It was, he insisted, a practical tool for picking out children who needed special help, not a ruler that measured some fixed quantity inside the head. He prescribed "mental orthopaedics", classroom exercises in attention and self-control, to help slow children improve, and in 1909 he protested against "the brutal pessimism" of those who claimed that a person's intelligence is a fixed quantity that cannot be increased.

Binet died in 1911. Within a decade his ladder had crossed the Atlantic and been turned into something he had warned against. In the United States it was used to label newly arrived immigrants as "feeble-minded", to rank well over a million army recruits, and to argue that intelligence is simply inherited and that whole nations were born less able. Remember Binet's two claims: that intelligence is complex and a test can only sample it, and that it can be raised. By the end of this chapter you will be able to judge, with a century of evidence in hand, how right he was.

9.1 What Is Intelligence?Lower yield · 3 Qs

Everyone uses the word, and everyone believes they recognise intelligence when they see it: the classmate who solves a puzzle in seconds, the grandmother who keeps the household accounts in her head, the cricket captain who reads a match two overs ahead of everyone else. Yet when psychologists have tried to pin the word down, they have disagreed for more than a century. It is worth starting with the disagreements, because they shape every theory and every test in this chapter, and because examiners love to ask who said what.

Definitions from Binet to the Experts

Alfred Binet (1857 to 1911) put judgement at the centre: "to judge well, to comprehend well, to reason well, these are the essential activities of intelligence." He also called it good sense, practical sense, initiative and the faculty of adapting oneself to circumstances, and in 1909 he compressed it into four words: comprehension, invention, direction (holding a goal in mind) and censorship (criticising one's own attempts). Binet's view is sometimes classified as a unifactor theory, the idea that intelligence is one general, undivided capacity, because his scale produced a single overall level; the label is also attached to Lewis Terman and William Stern, whom you will meet in Section 9.2.

In 1921 the Journal of Educational Psychology asked more than a dozen leading American psychologists to define intelligence. The replies varied revealingly. Lewis Madison Terman said it was the ability to carry on abstract thinking. Edward Lee Thorndike called it "the power of good responses from the point of view of truth or fact." Others stressed the capacity to learn or to adapt to relatively new situations. Two years later, exasperated, the Harvard historian of psychology Edwin Garrigues Boring (1886 to 1968) wrote that intelligence is "what the tests test." He was making a serious point about method. Until psychologists agree on a theory, the only definition anyone can check is an operational definition, one that defines a concept by the procedure used to measure it.

The definition most often quoted in Indian examinations is that of David Wechsler (1896 to 1981): intelligence is "the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally and to deal effectively with his environment." It is usually dated 1944, from the third edition of his book The Measurement of Adult Intelligence; almost the same words appear in the 1939 first edition, so you may see either year. Notice the two key words. Aggregate means that intelligence is made of many distinct abilities; global means that they work together as a whole. That double view, many parts but one whole, is exactly how his tests are built: separate subtests whose scores are summed into one full-scale score.

Sixty-five years after the 1921 symposium, Robert Jeffrey Sternberg and Douglas Detterman repeated the exercise, putting the same question to about two dozen experts and publishing the replies as What Is Intelligence? (1986). The overlap with 1921 was considerable: higher-level abstract reasoning, problem solving and the capacity to learn appeared in both. But the 1986 experts gave more weight to metacognition (knowing about, and controlling, one's own thinking) and to the culture that decides which abilities count as intelligent. Sternberg's own definition makes the cultural point explicit: intelligence is mental activity directed toward purposive adaptation to, and selection and shaping of, real-world environments relevant to one's life. Hold on to the three verbs adapt, select and shape; they return as the "contextual" part of his triarchic theory in Section 9.5.

Finally, after the storm over the book The Bell Curve (Section 9.7), Linda Gottfredson drafted a statement signed by 52 researchers and published in December 1994. Its definition is the working definition most researchers use today: "a very general mental capability that, among other things, involves the ability to reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, comprehend complex ideas, learn quickly and learn from experience." It is, the statement added, "not merely book learning, a narrow academic skill, or test-taking smarts."

What do these definitions share? Three themes recur: the capacity to learn from experience, the capacity to reason abstractly, and the capacity to adapt to the environment. Where they part company is on two questions. Is intelligence one thing or many? And how much of it can a test capture? Those two disagreements organise the rest of this chapter. Intelligence, Aptitude and Achievement

Three words are often used loosely, and examiners exploit the looseness. Achievement is what a person has already learned through a specific course of instruction; an achievement test, such as a Class 10 board examination in mathematics, looks backwards at the effects of known teaching. An aptitude is a capacity to acquire, with training, some specific knowledge or skill. Walter Van Dyke Bingham (1880 to 1952) defined it in 1937 as a condition or set of characteristics regarded as symptomatic of a person's ability to acquire, with training, some knowledge, skill or set of responses, such as the ability to speak a language or produce music. An aptitude test therefore looks forwards: it predicts how well someone will learn to fly, to type or to study engineering before the training begins. The classic battery is the Differential Aptitude Tests of George Bennett, Harold Seashore and Alexander Wesman (1947). Because they predict how well a student will do in different courses and careers, aptitude tests are central tools of educational and vocational guidance.

Intelligence tests sit between the two. They sample broad abilities that schooling influences but does not directly teach, and they predict learning and problem solving in general rather than in one field. The boundary is fuzzy (a vocabulary subtest is partly an achievement measure), so the difference is best understood as one of breadth and purpose, as Table 9.2 shows.

The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) is an intelligence test, not an aptitude, achievement or attitude test. An attitude test measures a person's evaluative stance towards an object (the Social book). One paper (RML 2022) keyed the aptitude test as "the heart of the guidance programme". The standard phrase in guidance textbooks is that counselling is the heart of the guidance programme, so if counselling is offered, choose it; when counselling is not among the options, as in that paper, the aptitude test is the expected answer, because guidance decisions about courses and careers rest on it. The other usual options are guidance tools of a different kind: the case study is an intensive study of one person's history and circumstances, the interview is a face-to-face conversation to gather information and build rapport, and the cumulative record is a running file of a student's marks, test results, health and activities kept across the school years.

Implicit Theories: What Ordinary People and Cultures Believe

The definitions above are explicit theories, formal theories built by psychologists and tested against data. Ordinary people carry implicit theories of intelligence, beliefs about what intelligence is that guide how they judge themselves and others. In 1981 Sternberg and his colleagues simply asked people in a railway station, a supermarket and a college library to list the behaviours of an intelligent person. Three clusters emerged: practical problem-solving ability (reasons logically, sees all sides of a problem), verbal ability (speaks clearly, reads widely) and social competence (accepts others for what they are, admits mistakes). Laypeople, in other words, already included the social and practical abilities that the psychometric tests left out.

Implicit theories also differ in a way that changes behaviour. Carol Susan Dweck (born 1946) distinguished an entity theory of intelligence, the belief that intelligence is a fixed quantity you either have or lack, from an incremental theory, the belief that it can be developed through effort and good strategies. In her popular book Mindset (2006) these became the fixed mindset and the growth mindset. Children who hold an entity theory tend to avoid challenges and give up after failure, because failure seems to reveal a permanent lack; children with an incremental theory treat failure as information and persist. Praise can push a child towards one theory or the other. In a well-known series of experiments with American fifth-graders, Claudia Mueller and Dweck (1998) praised some children for being clever ("you must be smart at this") and others for working hard; after a later failure, the children praised for ability chose easier tasks, enjoyed the work less and performed worse than those praised for effort. The mindset research therefore recommends praising effort and strategy, not innate ability. Large school studies of brief mindset interventions have found real but small average effects on grades, with larger effects for low-achieving students, so the idea is useful but not magic.

Indian context

The Indian-born psychologist J. P. Das (Section 9.5) has pointed out that the closest Indian word, buddhi, is broader than the Western test-based idea. It includes discrimination between right and wrong, self-knowledge, determination and feeling, not only speed and accuracy of reasoning. Studies of everyday Indian conceptions likewise give a large place to social competence, responsibility and wisdom. This is one reason a test built on one culture's implicit theory can misjudge people from another.

Exam trap

"Incremental" belongs to Dweck's entity versus incremental theories, not to Sternberg's triarchic theory, whose three parts are componential, experiential and contextual. Options such as "incremental" and "experimental" in a Sternberg question are bait. A "growth mindset" is the belief that intelligence is malleable and can be developed through effort.

9.2 From Galton to Binet: The Birth of Mental TestingMedium yield · 5 Qs

Binet's 1905 scale is usually called the first intelligence test, and it was. But it was a reaction against an earlier attempt that had failed, and you cannot understand why Binet succeeded without knowing why his predecessors did not.

Francis Galton and the Anthropometric Laboratory

Francis Galton (1822 to 1911), a half-cousin of Charles Darwin, was fascinated by the fact that eminent men seemed to come from a small number of families. In Hereditary Genius (1869) he traced the family trees of judges, statesmen, scientists and poets and concluded that "natural ability" was inherited. (The obvious objection, that eminent families also pass on wealth, schooling and connections, did not trouble him much.) In 1874 he popularised the phrase "nature and nurture", in 1875 he proposed comparing twins as a way of separating the two, and in 1883 he coined the word eugenics for the programme of improving the human stock by selective breeding, an idea that later did great harm.

How was natural ability to be measured? Galton followed the empiricist logic that all knowledge enters through the senses, so the people with the keenest senses and quickest reactions should be the most intelligent. At the International Health Exhibition in London in 1884 he opened an anthropometric laboratory (anthropometry is the measurement of the human body) where, for a small fee, about 9,000 visitors had their head size, grip strength, reaction time, visual acuity and highest audible pitch measured. To analyse such data he developed the ideas of regression and correlation, later put into mathematical form by his protégé Karl Pearson. For this work Galton is often called the father of differential psychology, the study of individual differences, and of psychometrics. He had, however, measured the wrong things.

James McKeen Cattell and the "Mental Test"

The American James McKeen Cattell (1860 to 1944) took his doctorate under Wilhelm Wundt in Leipzig and then worked with Galton in England. In an 1890 paper in the journal Mind, "Mental Tests and Measurements", he coined the term mental test and proposed a battery of ten: grip pressure, rate of hand movement, two-point touch threshold, the pressure needed to cause pain, the smallest noticeable difference in weight, reaction time to sound, time to name colours, bisection of a 50-centimetre line, judgement of a ten-second interval, and the number of letters remembered after one hearing. At Columbia University he tested entering students every year.

The test of these tests came in 1901. Cattell's own student Clark Wissler correlated the scores of Columbia students with their college grades and with one another. The correlations with grades were close to zero, and the tests barely correlated with each other. If reaction time and pitch discrimination were measures of one underlying intelligence, they should have hung together and predicted academic success; they did neither. The sensory approach collapsed, leaving the field open for someone who would measure higher mental processes directly.

Binet and Simon: Measuring Judgement

That someone was Binet, working with the physician Théodore Simon (1873 to 1961; a few sources give 1872). Their approach differed from Galton's in two decisive ways. First, they measured complex functions (comprehension, reasoning, memory, judgement) rather than simple sensations. Second, they chose items empirically: a task stayed in the scale only if older children passed it more often than younger children, so success on the scale tracked mental development.

Key study: The Binet-Simon scales (1905, 1908, 1911)

Question. Could an objective test pick out schoolchildren who needed special education, without relying on teachers' impressions? Method. In 1905 Binet and Simon published 30 items arranged in order of increasing difficulty, from following a lighted match with the eyes to defining abstract words. In the 1908 revision they grouped the items by the age at which most normal children passed them (ages 3 to 13). A child's mental age was the age level of the items the child could pass (Binet's own term was "mental level"). A 7-year-old who passed the items typical of 9-year-olds had a mental age of 9. The 1911 revision, published the year Binet died, extended the scale into the adult range. Result. For the first time a single, standardised procedure could rank children's intellectual development. Conclusion and criticism. Binet regarded the score as a practical sample of performance, not a measure of fixed inborn capacity, and he warned against using it to label children for life. Those warnings were largely ignored once the test reached America.

There is a footnote to this story that examiners enjoy. Around 1920 a young Swiss biologist named Jean Piaget worked in Simon's laboratory in Paris, standardising reasoning tests on Parisian children. He became less interested in whether the children got the answers right than in the systematic ways they got them wrong, and that curiosity grew into his theory of cognitive development (the Developmental book). So Piaget came out of the Binet tradition, but he did not create the first intelligence test.

Across the Atlantic: Goddard, Stern and Terman

Henry Herbert Goddard (1866 to 1957), research director of the Vineland Training School for "feeble-minded" children in New Jersey, translated the Binet-Simon scale into English in 1908. He grouped people with intellectual disability into idiots (the most severe), imbeciles, and a new category he named in 1910: the moron, from a Greek word for foolish, for people with mental ages of roughly 8 to 12. His book The Kallikak Family (1912) claimed to trace "feeble-mindedness" down two branches of one family, and from 1913 his team tested immigrants arriving at Ellis Island in New York, classifying many as feeble-minded on the strength of a translated test given through interpreters to frightened, exhausted travellers. These terms are now purely historical, but they appear as options in exam questions.

Meanwhile, in Germany, William Stern (1871 to 1938) saw a problem with mental age. A child who is two years behind at age 4 is much more retarded in development than one who is two years behind at age 14. In 1912 he proposed dividing mental age by chronological age to give a "mental quotient", in German the Intelligenzquotient. Stern therefore coined the term and the ratio of the intelligence quotient (IQ).

It was Lewis Madison Terman (1877 to 1956) of Stanford University who made the IQ famous. His 1916 American revision of the Binet-Simon scale, the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale, multiplied Stern's quotient by 100 to remove the decimal and reported the result as the IQ:

IQ = (mental age ÷ chronological age) × 100

This is the ratio IQ. Work two cases through. A 10-year-old with a mental age of 8 has an IQ of (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80. A 10-year-old with a mental age of 12 has an IQ of 120. A child performing exactly at age level always scores 100, whatever the age, which is what made the number so appealing. Terman's Stanford-Binet became the standard American test for decades, and Terman, unlike Binet, believed it measured an inherited capacity.

Testing Goes to War: Army Alpha and Army Beta

When the United States entered the First World War in 1917, Robert Mearns Yerkes (1876 to 1956), then president of the American Psychological Association (and the same Yerkes as the Yerkes-Dodson law of Chapter 10), led a committee, with Terman and Goddard among its members, that built the first large-scale group intelligence tests. The Army Alpha was a written, verbal test for recruits who could read English. The Army Beta was a non-verbal, pictorial test, given by demonstration and mime, for recruits who were illiterate or did not speak English. About 1.75 million men were tested. Group testing had arrived: one examiner could now test hundreds of people at once.

The aftermath is a lesson in the misuse of data. In A Study of American Intelligence (1923) the psychologist Carl Brigham used the army results to rank immigrant groups, with northern Europeans on top, ignoring the obvious fact that recent immigrants scored lower the fewer years they had lived in the United States, that is, the less English and American schooling they had. Such arguments were cited in the debates that led to the restrictive Immigration Act of 1924. Brigham publicly retracted his conclusions in 1930. Clinical link

The Ellis Island and army episodes are the origin of three rules every clinical psychologist follows: test a person in a language and format they understand, interpret the score against norms from a comparable population, and never treat a single score as a verdict on a person's worth or future. In India, with its many languages and very unequal schooling, these rules decide which test you choose (Section 9.3).

9.3 The IQ and the Modern TestsHigh yield · 32 Qs

The ratio IQ had a fatal flaw, and the way it was fixed gave us the IQ we use today. To see the flaw, push an adult through the formula.

From Ratio IQ to Deviation IQ

Mental age grows quickly in childhood, slows in adolescence and stops growing in the mid to late teens. Chronological age keeps climbing. Take a perfectly ordinary 40-year-old whose mental age, like that of most adults, levelled off at about 16: the ratio formula gives (16 ÷ 40) × 100 = 40, a score that would label an average adult as severely impaired. Terman patched the problem by treating every adult as if he or she were about 16 years old, but the patch hid a second flaw. Even in childhood, the spread of ratio IQs differs from age to age, so an IQ of 115 might put a 6-year-old in a different position relative to his age-mates than a 12-year-old with the same number. A score that means different things at different ages is a poor score.

David Wechsler's solution, introduced with the Wechsler-Bellevue Intelligence Scale in 1939, was to abandon mental age altogether. A person's raw score is compared with the scores of a large, representative sample of people of the same age (the norms), and the IQ simply reports how far above or below the average of that age group the person falls. This is the deviation IQ; because it is a standard score, some books call it the "standard IQ". The mean of every age group is set at 100 and the standard deviation (SD), the typical distance of scores from the mean, is set at 15. In formula form, if z is the number of standard deviations a person scores above or below the age-group mean (a z score):

deviation IQ = 100 + 15z

A 40-year-old who scores exactly at the mean of 40-year-olds gets 100; one who scores one standard deviation above them gets 115. The meaning of 115 is now the same at every age: better than about 84 per cent of one's age-mates. Study guides sometimes describe Wechsler's method as "the actual score divided by the expected mean score for the person's age"; that is a loose way of saying the same thing, that the person is compared with the expected performance of his or her own age group, and if you meet that wording as an option, it is the one intended. Other standard scores work the same way with different numbers: the T score used by many personality and neuropsychological tests has a mean of 50 and an SD of 10, and the Wechsler subtest scaled scores have a mean of 10 and an SD of 3. A percentile rank is different again; it is not a standard score but simply the percentage of the norm group scoring below a person.

Because IQ scores are approximately normally distributed, the familiar properties of the normal curve let you translate any IQ into a percentile, the percentage of people scoring below it. As Figure 9.1 shows, about 68 per cent of people score between 85 and 115 (within one SD of the mean) and about 95 per cent between 70 and 130 (within two SDs).

Work two examples. On a Wechsler scale an IQ of 85 is one SD below the mean, so 16 per cent of people score below it (the 16th percentile); an IQ of 100, the mean, is by definition the 50th percentile. Now an older Stanford-Binet style question: on a test with a mean of 100 and an SD of 16, a person scores 136. Then z = (136 − 100) ÷ 16 = 2.25, and the normal-curve table gives about 0.987 of the area below z = 2.25, so about 98.7 per cent of people score lower. The SD matters. The Stanford-Binet adopted the deviation IQ in its 1960 revision (Form L-M) with an SD of 16, kept 16 in the fourth edition (1986), and moved to 15, in line with the Wechsler scales, only in the fifth edition (2003). Always check which SD a question gives you.

Exam trap

Ratio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100, from Stern (1912) and Terman (1916); the original Stanford-Binet used it. Deviation IQ is a standard score with mean 100 and SD 15, comparing a person with same-age peers; Wechsler introduced it in 1939, and modern tests (including the SB5) use it. The deviation IQ is not "only for adults", it does not use raw scores without standardisation, and the ratio IQ is not the more reliable of the two (its meaning shifts from age to age, which is why it was abandoned). The old Stanford-Binet SD was 16, not 15.

The Wechsler Scales

Wechsler, a Romanian-born American who had tested army recruits in 1918, was chief psychologist at Bellevue Psychiatric Hospital in New York when he built his first scale. He set out to correct several faults he saw in the Stanford-Binet: it was designed for children, so its items had little appeal for adults; it leaned heavily on verbal, word-manipulation tasks; it placed too much weight on speed; and it rested on the concept of mental age, which made no sense for adults. His answer had four features that still define the Wechsler tests. They are point scales rather than age scales: items are grouped by type into subtests, and points are earned within each subtest, rather than items being grouped by the age at which they are passed, as in Binet's ladder. They combine verbal subtests with non-verbal performance tests (block designs, picture puzzles) that require little or no language. They yield subtest and part scores as well as a total, so a clinician can see a profile of strengths and weaknesses. And they use the deviation IQ.

The family has grown with every generation (Table 9.4). Until the 2000s each test produced a Verbal IQ, a Performance IQ and a Full Scale IQ (FSIQ). The newer editions replaced the verbal-performance split with index scores built on factor-analytic research (Section 9.4).

An option listing "Verbal IQ, Performance IQ, Full Scale IQ and Memory Quotient" therefore mixes the old WAIS with the old WMS and is not the WAIS-IV.

Exam trap

The WAIS-IV has 3 core verbal (VCI) and 3 core perceptual (PRI) subtests, plus 2 core working-memory and 2 core processing-speed subtests: 10 core in all. The Working Memory Index, built from Digit Span and Arithmetic, is the one that reflects attention, concentration and mental control. One paper asked which subtest is "core in the WAIS-IV but not found in the WISC-V" and keyed Letter-Number Sequencing. Strictly, Letter-Number Sequencing is a supplemental subtest in the WAIS-IV (it was part of the Working Memory Index in the WAIS-III) and a secondary subtest in the WISC-V; but it is still the best of the options offered, because Digit Span, Symbol Search and Matrix Reasoning are core in both tests. The WISC-V covers 6 years to 16 years 11 months.

The Stanford-Binet Today, and Other Individual Tests

The Stanford-Binet was revised in 1937 (by Terman and Maud Merrill), in 1960, in 1986 and most recently as the Stanford-Binet Fifth Edition (SB5) (in full, the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales, Fifth Edition), developed by Gale Roid and published in 2003. The SB5 covers ages 2 to 85 and over and is organised around five factors drawn from the hierarchical Cattell-Horn-Carroll model of Section 9.4, with the working-memory factor also drawing on Alan Baddeley's working-memory model (Chapter 7): Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing and Working Memory. Each factor is measured once in a verbal and once in a non-verbal form, giving 10 subtests and a Full Scale IQ, a Verbal IQ and a Nonverbal IQ, all with mean 100 and SD 15. Like the earlier Stanford-Binets, it uses a basal level (the level at which the person passes all items, so easier items need not be given) and a ceiling level (the level at which the person fails all or most items, so testing stops).

Several other individual tests appear in questions. Alan and Nadeen Kaufman's Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (K-ABC, 1983) was one of the first major tests built on Luria's neuropsychology (Section 9.5); their Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test, second edition (KBIT-2, 2004), is a 20-minute screening test with just two scales, a Verbal or crystallised scale and a Nonverbal or fluid scale. For infants, intelligence in the adult sense cannot yet be measured, so developmental scales are used instead: the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (Nancy Bayley; the current edition covers roughly the first 42 months) assess cognitive, language and motor development, and the McCarthy Scales of Children's Abilities (Dorothea McCarthy, 1972) cover about 2½ to 8½ years. Developmental scales express results as a developmental quotient (DQ), developmental age divided by chronological age, times 100, a ratio exactly like Stern's. Detailed administration and scoring belong to the Assessment book.

Culture-Fair Tests

A vocabulary item asks, in effect, "Have you met this word?", and whether you have met it depends on your home, school and language. Psychologists have therefore tried to build tests whose content is equally familiar, or equally unfamiliar, to everyone. The early hope was a culture-free test, one entirely free of cultural influence; that turned out to be impossible, because even the habit of sitting quietly, working against a clock and treating abstract shapes as a meaningful problem is learned. The realistic goal is a culture-fair test (also called culture-reduced), one that minimises language, reading, specific knowledge and speed so that people from different backgrounds are on a more equal footing.

The most famous is Raven's Progressive Matrices, devised by the British psychologist John Carlyle Raven (1902 to 1970), a student of Spearman. Each item shows a pattern of figures arranged in a matrix with one piece missing, and the examinee chooses the piece that completes it. Solving an item requires working out the rule that governs the rows and columns, which is what Spearman called the eduction of relations (Section 9.4). The Standard Progressive Matrices (SPM), published in 1938 (Raven had drafted it by 1936, so some books give that year), has 60 items in five sets of 12 that grow progressively harder, hence the name. The Coloured Progressive Matrices (36 items) are for children and older or impaired adults, and the Advanced Progressive Matrices (48 items) for high-ability adults. Raven's matrices are the classic measure of fluid intelligence, the ability to reason with novel material (Section 9.4), and they are also the best single marker of Spearman's general factor.

Raymond Bernard Cattell's Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT), first issued around 1940 as a "culture-free" test and soon renamed, is a group test in three scales for different ages and abilities. Its subtests (series completion, classification, matrices and "conditions", a topological task) use only figures. Other non-verbal and performance tests that reduce the role of language include Florence Goodenough's Draw-a-Man test (1926), the Leiter International Performance Scale, the Porteus Maze Test, Kohs block designs and Alexander's Pass-Along test; the Army Beta was an early example of the same idea.

Exam trap

Raven's Progressive Matrices measure fluid intelligence; choose "fluid" over "crystallised", "verbal" or "social". If "general intelligence (g)" is offered alongside "fluid", the expected answer is still fluid, although Raven's is also the best single measure of g, because Gf and g are very closely related. Raven's is called culture-fair because it is non-verbal, with minimal language demands; it is not culture-free, and schooling still affects scores.

Are IQ Tests Reliable and Valid, and What Do They Predict?

A test is useful only if it is reliable (it gives consistent scores) and valid (it measures what it claims to measure). The major individual IQ tests are among the most reliable instruments in psychology: the internal-consistency reliability of the WAIS-IV Full Scale IQ is about .98, and retesting after a few weeks typically gives correlations around .9. Individual subtests are much less reliable than the total, which is why clinicians interpret composite scores first.

Validity comes in several kinds (the Assessment and Research Methodology books treat them fully). Content validity asks whether the items sample the domain adequately. Criterion-related validity asks whether scores relate to an outside criterion; it is called concurrent validity when test and criterion are measured at about the same time, and predictive validity when the criterion comes later. Construct validity asks whether the test behaves as the theory of the construct predicts, for example correlating with other measures of reasoning but not with unrelated traits. So if a new intelligence test is given alongside a well-established one, and the two sets of scores are correlated, the researcher is checking concurrent validity.

What does IQ predict? A great deal, though far from everything. IQ correlates about .5 with school grades and about .55 with the number of years of education completed. In a famous 1998 review of 85 years of research, Frank Schmidt and John Hunter found that general mental ability predicts job performance with a validity of about .5, higher for complex jobs than for simple ones, which makes it the single best predictor of job performance across occupations. Childhood IQ even predicts health and lifespan: in Scotland, people who had been tested at age 11 in the Scottish Mental Survey of 1932 were more likely to be alive decades later if they had scored higher. But a correlation of .5 means IQ accounts for only about a quarter of the variation in grades (.5 squared is .25); motivation, conscientiousness, teaching, opportunity and luck account for the rest.

Clinical link

Clinical psychologists use intelligence tests to diagnose intellectual disability, to identify specific learning disabilities (where achievement falls well below what ability predicts), to document cognitive decline after brain injury or in dementia, to plan rehabilitation and education, and to establish eligibility for benefits such as a disability allowance or disability certificate. For adults the WAIS is used; for children with special educational needs, the WISC or an Indian equivalent. Every such use rests on current, local norms: a test normed decades earlier inflates scores (the Flynn effect, Section 9.7), which can wrongly deny someone a diagnosis or support.

9.4 The Structure of Intelligence: Factor TheoriesMedium yield · 9 Qs

Once there were tests, a new question could be asked with data rather than opinion. If intelligence is one thing, people who do well on one test should do well on all of them. If it is many things, a person could be strong in one and weak in another. The tool for deciding between these possibilities was invented for the purpose, and it shaped the next hundred years of theory.

Factor Analysis in Plain Words

Suppose 200 Class 9 students take tests in English, Hindi, mathematics, physics and drawing. You correlate every test with every other and lay out the correlations in a table, a correlation matrix. You notice that English and Hindi correlate highly with each other, mathematics and physics correlate highly with each other, and every test correlates at least a little with every other. Factor analysis is a family of statistical methods that asks how few underlying variables, called factors, are needed to explain such a pattern of correlations. Each test's correlation with a factor is its factor loading. Here you might find one factor on which all five tests load (a general ability), plus a "language" factor and a "mathematics-science" factor. The method does not tell you what the factors mean; the researcher names them by looking at which tests load on them, and different methods of extracting and rotating factors can give different-looking answers from the same data. That is why factor analysts have argued for a century.

Spearman's Two-Factor Theory

The British psychologist Charles Edward Spearman (1863 to 1945) invented the first form of factor analysis. In 1904, in a paper titled "General Intelligence, Objectively Determined and Measured", he analysed the school marks and sensory-discrimination scores of English schoolchildren (in classics, French, English, mathematics, pitch discrimination and music). He found that all the measures correlated positively with one another, a pattern now called the positive manifold. Moreover, the correlations fell into a neat hierarchy, a regularity Spearman later expressed as "tetrad differences" close to zero, which is exactly what you would expect if a single common factor ran through every measure.

He called this common factor general intelligence (g). Every test, he proposed, measures g plus a specific factor (s) peculiar to that test: the ability to do arithmetic depends partly on g and partly on a specific arithmetical skill. Because it has exactly two kinds of factor, general and specific, this is called the two-factor theory (Figure 9.2). Tests differ in how heavily they are "saturated" with g; Spearman found that tasks requiring abstract reasoning were the most saturated, and simple sensory tasks the least. He interpreted g as a kind of general mental energy, and in 1923 he described three "noegenetic" (knowledge-creating) principles that he thought defined it: the apprehension of experience, the eduction of relations (seeing the relationship between two things) and the eduction of correlates (applying a relationship to generate a new item). Raven's matrices, built by his student, are a direct test of eduction. In The Abilities of Man (1927) Spearman admitted that some tests also share narrower group factors, such as verbal or mechanical ability, but g remained the centre of his theory.

Exam trap

Spearman's g is a general factor underlying performance on all cognitive tasks; s is the specific factor of each task. Neither is "group factors" (that is Thurstone, Burt and Vernon) nor "genetic predisposition". The two-factor theory is not the triarchic model (Sternberg), multiple intelligences (Gardner), seven primary abilities (Thurstone) or fluid and crystallised intelligence (Cattell). The same Spearman gave us the rank-order correlation coefficient (rho) and, with William Brown, the Spearman-Brown formula used in reliability.

Thorndike's Multifactor Theory

Edward Lee Thorndike (1874 to 1949), the learning theorist of Chapter 6, rejected g. For him intelligence was the product of a vast number of separate stimulus-response connections, and a person was more intelligent simply by having more of them. This multifactor theory is sometimes called the "anarchic" theory, because it allows no governing general factor. Thorndike distinguished three broad kinds of intelligence: abstract intelligence (dealing with ideas and symbols), mechanical intelligence (also called concrete intelligence: dealing with things and tools) and social intelligence (dealing with people). In 1920 he defined social intelligence as the ability to understand and manage men and women, boys and girls, and to act wisely in human relations. His later test, the CAVD (1927), combined four kinds of item, sentence Completion, Arithmetic, Vocabulary and following Directions, and he described intelligence along four dimensions: level (the difficulty of tasks one can solve), range (the number of tasks one can solve at a given level), area (the total of the ranges across levels) and speed.

A related idea came from the English-born psychologist Godfrey Hilton Thomson (1881 to 1955), who spent most of his career at Newcastle and then Edinburgh. His sampling theory (first proposed in 1916 and developed in The Factorial Analysis of Human Ability, 1939) showed that the positive manifold does not prove that a single g exists. Suppose the mind contains a huge number of small, independent "bonds" or elementary abilities, and every test calls on a random sample of them. Any two tests will then share some bonds by chance, and so will correlate, even though nothing general exists. Correlation among tests, Thomson pointed out, could come from overlapping samples rather than from one common cause. Versions of this argument are still debated today.

Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities

Louis Leon Thurstone (1887 to 1955) of the University of Chicago developed a more powerful method, multiple factor analysis, designed to find several factors at once. In Primary Mental Abilities (1938) he gave 56 tests to 240 university students and extracted seven factors that he called the primary mental abilities: verbal comprehension (V), word fluency (W), number (N), space (S), associative memory (M), perceptual speed (P) and reasoning, also called induction (R or I). Thurstone argued that these seven were distinct abilities, not reflections of g, and he built the Primary Mental Abilities tests (1941, with his wife Thelma Gwinn Thurstone) to give each person a profile across them. Because it replaces one ruler with a small number of group factors, his view is called a group-factor theory (sometimes "oligarchic", rule by a few).

The story has a twist. When the Thurstones tested schoolchildren, whose abilities are less differentiated than those of university students, the seven factors correlated substantially with one another. And a set of correlated factors can itself be factor-analysed, yielding a "second-order" general factor. Spearman and Thurstone were, in the end, both partly right: there are several distinct broad abilities, and there is something general that they share. The hierarchical models below formalise that compromise.

Exam trap

Count carefully. Thurstone: seven primary mental abilities (1938). Gardner: seven intelligences in 1983, eight after the naturalist was added in 1999. Do not mix the lists: "spatial" appears in both, but "word fluency", "perceptual speed" and "associative memory" are Thurstone's, while "musical", "bodily-kinaesthetic" and "intrapersonal" are Gardner's. Thurstone is also the Thurstone of attitude scaling (the method of equal-appearing intervals) in the Social book. In the Indian textbook classification, the unifactor or "monarchic" theory is Binet's (one general ability), the "oligarchic" theory is Thurstone's (a few group factors) and the "anarchic" theory is Thorndike's (many independent abilities).

Guilford's Structure of Intellect

Joy Paul Guilford (1897 to 1987), who had worked on the selection of aircrew during the Second World War, went furthest in the direction of many abilities. His structure of intellect (SOI) model, set out fully in The Nature of Human Intelligence (1967), classifies every intellectual task along three dimensions, pictured as a cube (Figure 9.3):

  • Operations, the kind of mental process involved (five): cognition, memory, divergent production (generating many possible answers to an open problem, the basis of creativity), convergent production (arriving at the one correct answer) and evaluation.
  • Contents, the kind of material the process works on (four): figural, symbolic, semantic and behavioural.
  • Products, the form the information takes (six): units, classes, relations, systems, transformations and implications.

Every combination of one operation, one content and one product defines a separate ability, so the model contains 5 × 4 × 6 = 120 abilities. For example, knowing what words mean is the cognition of semantic units; thinking up as many uses as possible for a brick is the divergent production of semantic units. Guilford later split the figural content into visual and auditory, giving 5 × 5 × 6 = 150 abilities (sources date this to 1977 or 1982), and in 1988 he split memory into memory recording and memory retention, giving 6 × 5 × 6 = 180.

Guilford's lasting legacy is the distinction between convergent and divergent thinking, which launched the psychometric study of creativity (Chapter 8). The model itself fared less well. Critics showed that his factor-analytic method could produce "evidence" for almost any structure the researcher had in mind, and that many of the supposedly independent abilities in fact correlate positively, as Spearman would have predicted.

Exam trap

Guilford's model has three dimensions (operations, contents, products) but 120 abilities (1967), later 150 and then 180. Strictly, then, the model has three dimensions and 120 abilities. One paper (RML 2021) asked "how many dimensions of intelligence" with both "three" and "120" among the options and keyed 120, using "dimensions" loosely to mean abilities. If that exact question returns, 120 is the answer its setters expect; but if a question asks you to name the dimensions, faces or parameters of the cube, the answer is three (operations, contents, products), and if it asks for abilities or factors, it is 120 (or 150 or 180 if the question names the later version). The number of products is six, of operations five, of contents four (later five).

Hierarchical Models: Burt and Vernon

British psychologists reconciled g with group factors by arranging abilities in a hierarchy, like the branches of a tree. Cyril Lodowic Burt (1883 to 1971) proposed one such model in 1949, and Philip Ewart Vernon (1905 to 1987) produced the best-known version in The Structure of Human Abilities (1950). In Vernon's hierarchical model, g sits at the top. Below it are two major group factors: v:ed (verbal-educational ability) and k:m (spatial, mechanical and practical ability). Below these come minor group factors: verbal fluency and numerical ability under v:ed; spatial, mechanical-information and psychomotor abilities under k:m. At the bottom are the specific factors of individual tests. Vernon's model is essentially Spearman's g and s with group factors inserted between them.

Fluid and Crystallised Intelligence

Raymond Bernard Cattell (1905 to 1998), a British-born student of Spearman who spent his career in the United States, divided g itself into two. He first sketched the idea in the early 1940s and set it out fully in 1963; his student John Leonard Horn (1928 to 2006) developed it with him from 1966. Fluid intelligence (Gf) is the capacity to reason, see relationships and solve novel problems independently of previously acquired knowledge; it is what you use the first time you meet a puzzle. Crystallised intelligence (Gc) is the repertoire of information, cognitive skills and strategies acquired through education and experience: vocabulary, general knowledge, learned methods. Cattell's investment theory (1971) linked the two: Gf is "invested" in learning experiences, and the knowledge that results "crystallises" as Gc, rather as water freezes into ice.

The two can be told apart by how they are measured and by how they change across life (Figure 9.4). Gf is measured by Raven's matrices, matrix reasoning, series completion and other novel-problem tests; Gc by vocabulary, information and comprehension subtests. Gf rises through childhood, peaks in the early twenties and then declines gradually. Gc goes on rising, or at least holds steady, well into the sixties, because people continue to learn. (Note that block design and coding, sometimes offered as options, are measures of visual-spatial ability and processing speed, not of Gc.)

Horn extended the model to nine or ten broad abilities, adding to Gf and Gc visual processing (Gv), auditory processing (Ga), short-term memory (Gsm), long-term storage and retrieval (Glr), processing speed (Gs), decision or reaction speed, quantitative knowledge (Gq) and reading-writing (Grw). Unlike Spearman, Horn did not believe in a single g above them.

Exam trap

Crystallised = a repertoire of information, cognitive skills and strategies; accumulated knowledge and verbal skill; measured best by vocabulary and information. Fluid = the ability to deal with new problems; measured by matrices. "Practical" and "successful" intelligence are Sternberg's terms; "speed of neural processing" and "working memory capacity" are related to, but not the definition of, either. The Gf and Gc theory belongs to Raymond Cattell (with Horn), not James McKeen Cattell.

Carroll's Three Strata and the CHC Model

The most thorough factor-analytic study ever made was the work of John Bissell Carroll (1916 to 2003). In Human Cognitive Abilities (1993) he re-analysed more than 460 data sets collected over most of the twentieth century. He concluded that abilities form three layers, or strata (Figure 9.5). Stratum III is a single general factor, g. Stratum II contains eight broad abilities: fluid intelligence, crystallised intelligence, general memory and learning, broad visual perception, broad auditory perception, broad retrieval ability, broad cognitive speediness and processing (decision) speed. Stratum I contains about 70 narrow abilities, such as induction, lexical knowledge, memory span and visualisation. This is the three-stratum theory.

Because Carroll's broad abilities matched Cattell and Horn's so closely, the psychologist Kevin McGrew merged the two models in the late 1990s into the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory. CHC keeps Carroll's g at the top (while noting Horn's doubts about it), recognises roughly ten broad abilities in the middle, and many narrow abilities at the bottom. It is now the dominant framework for test construction: the Woodcock-Johnson batteries, the SB5, the Kaufman tests and the newer Wechsler indices are all organised, more or less explicitly, around CHC abilities.

Jensen's Level I and Level II Abilities

The American Arthur Robert Jensen (1923 to 2012), a champion of g, proposed a simpler two-level scheme. Level I abilities are associative: rote learning, memory span (repeating digits) and simple association, with little transformation of the input. Level II abilities are conceptual: abstract reasoning, problem solving and the manipulation of concepts, essentially g. Jensen reported that Level I abilities were fairly evenly distributed across social classes and ethnic groups, while groups differed more on Level II tasks.

The scheme is famous because of where he published it. His 1969 article in the Harvard Educational Review, "How Much Can We Boost IQ and Scholastic Achievement?", opened with the sentence "Compensatory education has been tried and it apparently has failed." He went on to argue that the heritability of IQ was high and that genetic factors might be partly responsible for the average difference in IQ scores between Black and White Americans. The article provoked a storm of protest (the word "Jensenism" was coined for his position) and one of the longest-running debates in psychology. You will see in Section 9.7 why the step from "heritable within a group" to "genetic differences between groups" is a logical error, and why later research on compensatory education is more encouraging than Jensen claimed. 9.5 Broader Views: Multiple, Triarchic, PASS and Emotional IntelligenceMedium yield · 5 Qs

All the theories of Section 9.4 were built the same way: give people a pile of tests, correlate the scores and factor-analyse the correlations. That method has a built-in limit. It can only find structure among the abilities that someone thought to test, and almost everyone tested the abilities that school rewards. From the 1980s onwards a group of theorists asked what a theory of intelligence would look like if it started somewhere else: from the brain, from the study of experts and prodigies, from how people cope with real life, or from the emotions.

Gardner's Multiple Intelligences

Howard Gardner (born 1943), a Harvard developmental psychologist, spent years in two very different settings: studying artistic development in children with Harvard's Project Zero, and working with adults who had suffered strokes at a Boston aphasia research unit. He was struck by how selectively the brain fails. A stroke could destroy a man's language and leave his musical or spatial abilities intact; a child could be a prodigy at music and ordinary at everything else. In Frames of Mind (1983) he proposed that there is no single intelligence but several relatively independent ones. This is the theory of multiple intelligences.

Gardner did not use factor analysis. Instead he set eight criteria that a candidate intelligence should meet: it can be isolated by brain damage; it is shown in exceptional form by savants, prodigies or other exceptional individuals; it has an identifiable core operation; it has a distinctive developmental history ending in expert performances; it has a plausible evolutionary history; it is supported by experimental tasks; it is supported by psychometric findings; and it can be encoded in a symbol system (language, number, musical notation). Gardner considered and rejected a "spiritual" intelligence; existential intelligence is the nearest he came to admitting one, and he has never fully accepted it. The theory was enormously popular with teachers, because it promised that every child is intelligent in some way, and schools around the world, including many in India, adopted "multiple intelligence" lesson plans. Researchers were cooler. The criticisms are worth knowing. First, Gardner offered little direct empirical test of the theory; the intelligences were argued for, not measured. Second, when researchers did build tests for them (for example Beth Visser, Michael Ashton and Philip A. Vernon in 2006; this is a different Vernon from the hierarchical theorist of Section 9.4), most of the "cognitive" intelligences correlated substantially with one another and with g, which is the opposite of independence; only the bodily-kinaesthetic and, partly, musical abilities stood apart. Third, critics argued that musical and bodily abilities are better called talents; calling them intelligences changes the word, not the facts.

Exam trap

Gardner's book is Frames of Mind (1983): seven intelligences; naturalist added in 1999 to make eight; existential considered, not fully accepted; spiritual rejected. "Linguistic, logical-mathematical and spatial" is a Gardner list, never a Sternberg or Cattell one. Gardner used no factor analysis, so a statement that he "derived the intelligences by factor analysis" is false.

Sternberg's Triarchic Theory and Successful Intelligence

Robert Jeffrey Sternberg (born 1949) has a personal reason to distrust IQ tests: as a primary-school pupil he suffered from such severe test anxiety that he scored poorly on group IQ tests, until a teacher who expected more of him changed how he performed. His triarchic theory of intelligence, set out in Beyond IQ (1985), has three parts, or subtheories (Table 9.10).

The componential subtheory describes the mental mechanisms of intelligent thought, the components that analytical intelligence uses. There are three kinds. Metacomponents are executive processes that plan what to do, monitor it while it is being done and evaluate it afterwards. Performance components carry out the plan: encoding the problem, inferring relations, applying them. Knowledge-acquisition components learn new information: selective encoding (picking out the relevant facts), selective combination (putting them together) and selective comparison (relating them to what you already know). This is the kind of intelligence school tests measure best, and Sternberg calls the resulting ability analytical.

The experiential subtheory concerns how intelligence relates to experience. Intelligence is shown most clearly at two points: when a task is new, where it shows as the ability to cope with novelty and produce insight, and when a task is becoming familiar, where it shows as the ability to automatise performance so that attention is freed for other things. A person strong here has creative intelligence.

The contextual subtheory relates intelligence to the external world. Intelligent behaviour is purposive adaptation to one's environment, shaping the environment to suit oneself and, when neither works, selection of a better environment. A clerk who learns the unwritten rules of her office adapts; one who persuades her manager to change a pointless procedure shapes; one who leaves for a better job selects. The resulting ability is practical intelligence, "street smarts".

In the late 1990s Sternberg extended the triarchic theory into the theory of successful intelligence: the ability to achieve success in life, by one's own standards and within one's culture, by capitalising on strengths and correcting or compensating for weaknesses through a balance of analytical, creative and practical abilities. Central to practical intelligence is tacit knowledge, the practical, usually unspoken know-how that is rarely taught directly but is needed to succeed in a particular setting; Richard Wagner and Sternberg (1985) measured it in business managers and found it predicted their success independently of IQ. A striking illustration came from Brazil, where Terezinha Carraher, David Carraher and Analúcia Schliemann (1985) studied children who sold coconuts and snacks in street markets. The children did complex sums in their heads almost flawlessly while selling, but often failed the same sums presented as school arithmetic. Critics, notably Linda Gottfredson (2003), argue that tests of practical intelligence correlate with g more than Sternberg admits and that the evidence for a separate practical ability is thin.

Exam trap

Componential = analytical; experiential (not "experimental") = creative; contextual = practical. The ability to adapt to the present environment, select a better one or modify (shape) it is contextual. Sternberg's three are analytical, creative and practical, never "verbal, performance and full scale" (Wechsler), "fluid, crystallised and general" (Cattell and Spearman) or "linguistic, logical-mathematical and spatial" (Gardner).

The PASS Model

A third alternative started from neuropsychology. The Soviet neuropsychologist Alexander Romanovich Luria (1902 to 1977), studying soldiers with brain wounds, described the brain as three "functional units". The first, based in the brainstem and its reticular activating system, regulates arousal and attention. The second, in the occipital, parietal and temporal lobes, receives, processes and stores information. The third, in the frontal lobes, programmes, regulates and verifies behaviour.

Building on Luria, Jagannath Prasad Das (born 1931), an Odisha-born psychologist who spent most of his career at the University of Alberta in Canada, together with Jack A. Naglieri and John R. Kirby, proposed the PASS model of intelligence (set out in their 1994 book). PASS stands for four cognitive processes:

  • Planning: deciding on, carrying out and checking strategies for solving problems (Luria's third unit, the frontal lobes).
  • Attention: focused, selective and sustained attention while resisting distraction, with the necessary arousal (Luria's first unit).
  • Simultaneous processing: integrating separate pieces of information into a whole that can be seen at once, as in understanding a map, a matrix or a sentence like "the boy is taller than the girl" (Luria's second unit, chiefly occipital-parietal).
  • Successive processing: dealing with information in a strict serial order, as in repeating a phone number, following a recipe or sounding out a word (also Luria's second unit, chiefly fronto-temporal).

Naglieri and Das turned the model into a test, the Cognitive Assessment System (CAS) (1997; second edition 2014), for school-age children. Because it measures processes rather than acquired knowledge, it shows smaller differences between ethnic and social groups than traditional IQ tests and is designed to link assessment to remediation; Das's PASS Reading Enhancement Programme (PREP) trains successive and simultaneous processing in children with reading difficulties. The Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (1983) was also built on Luria's distinction between simultaneous and sequential processing.

Indian context

The PASS model is one of the few internationally influential theories of intelligence with an Indian-born principal author. J. P. Das's work in the 1970s on simultaneous and successive syntheses led directly to the model, and he also wrote on Indian conceptions of intelligence such as buddhi (Section 9.1). Questions sometimes ask you to expand PASS; it is Planning, Attention, Simultaneous, Successive.

Ceci's Bioecological View

The American developmental psychologist Stephen J. Ceci argued in 1990 that intelligence is not one general ability but a set of multiple cognitive potentials, each of which develops only when the context (the knowledge domain, the setting, the culture) and the person's motivation call it out. His bioecological model takes its name from Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory (the Developmental book). His favourite evidence came from a 1986 study with Jeffrey Liker of expert racetrack handicappers, men who predicted the odds on horse races. The best of them reasoned using a complex model that weighed many interacting variables at once, a feat of cognitive complexity, yet their IQ scores bore no relation to their expertise. Complex thinking, Ceci concluded, depends on knowledge and context, not on IQ alone.

Emotional Intelligence

Thorndike's social intelligence of 1920 and Gardner's two personal intelligences were the ancestors of one of the most popular ideas in modern psychology. In 1990 Peter Salovey and John D. Mayer introduced the term emotional intelligence (EI) in its modern sense, defining it as the ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide one's thinking and actions. In 1997 they refined it into the four-branch model, which treats EI as a genuine ability, like other intelligences:

  1. Perceiving emotions accurately in oneself, in others' faces and voices, and in art.
  2. Using emotions to facilitate thought, for example harnessing a good mood for creative work.
  3. Understanding emotions: how they blend, change and follow from events.
  4. Managing emotions in oneself and others.

Their test, the Mayer-Salovey-Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT, 2002), scores answers against expert or consensus judgements, just as an ability test should.

The idea reached the public through the science journalist Daniel Goleman (born 1946), whose bestseller Emotional Intelligence: Why It Can Matter More Than IQ (1995) described five domains: knowing one's own emotions, managing one's emotions, motivating oneself, recognising emotions in others, and handling relationships. In Working with Emotional Intelligence (1998) he relabelled them self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills. Goleman's version is a mixed model, because it mixes emotional abilities with personality traits and motivation such as optimism and persistence. Another mixed model came from Reuven Bar-On, whose self-report Emotional Quotient Inventory (EQ-i, 1997) measures intrapersonal, interpersonal, adaptability, stress-management and general-mood scales. Finally, Konstantinos Petrides and Adrian Furnham (around 2000) argued that self-report questionnaires cannot measure an ability at all, only how people see their own emotional dispositions; they renamed this trait emotional intelligence, a personality trait measured by questionnaires such as the TEIQue. In India, the Emotional Intelligence Scale of Anukool Hyde, Sanjyot Pethe and Upinder Dhar (2002) is widely used in research. Table 9.11 compares the four approaches.

Salovey and Mayer coined the modern concept (1990); Goleman popularised it (1995). Goleman's five parts in the 1995 wording are knowing one's emotions, managing emotions, motivating oneself, recognising emotions in others and handling relationships; the 1998 labels (self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills) describe the same five, so if both appear as options, match the wording the question uses. "IQ, EQ, SQ, CQ" is not anyone's model of EI.

Social, Practical, Spiritual and Other Intelligences

Once intelligence was allowed to be plural, the list grew. Social intelligence, Thorndike's ability to understand and manage people, was measured as early as 1927 by the George Washington Social Intelligence Test, and appears as Guilford's behavioural content and Gardner's interpersonal intelligence. Practical intelligence and tacit knowledge belong to Sternberg. Spiritual intelligence was proposed by the physicist-philosopher Danah Zohar and the psychiatrist Ian Marshall in SQ: Spiritual Intelligence, the Ultimate Intelligence (2000), as the capacity to address problems of meaning and value; the psychologist Robert Emmons made a similar case the same year. Cultural intelligence (CQ), the ability to function effectively across cultures, was proposed by P. Christopher Earley and Soon Ang (2003). These ideas have practical uses, but none has the measurement base of g.

Exam trap

Beware the "quotients". IQ is the intelligence quotient; DQ the developmental quotient of infant scales; EQ the emotional quotient (Bar-On's term); CQ cultural intelligence. "SQ" is ambiguous: in the Vineland Social Maturity Scale it is the social quotient (Section 9.8), but in Zohar and Marshall it is spiritual intelligence. Read the context.

9.6 Intelligence, Speed and the BrainLower yield · not asked yet

Factor theories describe the structure of abilities; they say nothing about what goes on in the head when someone solves a problem. From the 1970s, psychologists began asking a different question: which basic information-processing operations distinguish people who score high on IQ tests from those who score low? Galton, you may recall, had bet on simple speed and lost. A century later his bet was partly vindicated.

Speed of Processing: Reaction Time and Inspection Time

In the cognitive-correlates approach, researchers measure performance on simple laboratory tasks and correlate it with IQ. Earl Hunt and his colleagues (1975) showed, for example, that high-verbal university students retrieved the names of letters from memory faster than low-verbal students. Arthur Jensen pursued reaction time (RT). Using a panel of lights and buttons, he measured how quickly people lifted a finger from a home button and pressed the button beside whichever light came on, with one to eight possible lights. Choice reaction time rises as the number of alternatives grows (Hick's law), and Jensen found that higher-IQ people were somewhat faster and, even more clearly, less variable from trial to trial. The correlations are modest, typically about −.2 to −.4 (negative because faster times go with higher IQ), but they are remarkable given that the task involves no knowledge at all.

An even purer measure is inspection time, introduced by Ted Nettelbeck and M. Lally in 1976. Two vertical lines of clearly different length are flashed for a few hundredths of a second and then covered by a mask; the person simply says which line was longer. Inspection time is the briefest exposure at which the person can still judge accurately. Shorter inspection times go with higher IQ: across many studies the correlation is about −.3 before correction and about −.5 after statistical correction for unreliability and restricted range. Speed of intake of very simple information, in other words, is part of what IQ tests capture.

Working Memory

The strongest cognitive correlate of intelligence is working memory capacity, the ability to hold information in an active state while processing it (Chapter 7). In an influential 1990 paper, Patrick Kyllonen and Raymond Christal asked whether reasoning ability is "little more than working-memory capacity" after finding very high correlations between the two at the level of latent factors. Later studies put the relationship lower, but working memory remains closely tied to fluid intelligence and much less to crystallised intelligence, which depends on stored knowledge. One reason is that solving a novel problem, such as a Raven's matrix, requires you to hold several partial rules in mind at once while testing them; lose one and the solution collapses. This is why working memory now has its own index on the Wechsler scales and its own factor on the SB5.

Brain Size, Neural Efficiency and Genes

Are bigger brains smarter? Nineteenth-century craniologists thought so and were famously biased in how they measured skulls. Modern magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies of living people find a real but modest positive correlation between total brain volume and IQ, about .2 to .4 depending on the meta-analysis. Brain size explains only a small part of the variation, and it says nothing about differences between groups, whose average head sizes differ for many reasons including body size and nutrition.

How a brain works may matter more than how big it is. In 1988 Richard Haier and his colleagues used positron emission tomography (PET) to measure how much glucose people's brains consumed while they solved Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices. Surprisingly, the people who scored highest used the least glucose: their brains seemed to work less hard to solve the same problems. In a follow-up, people who practised the computer game Tetris for several weeks became better at it while their brains' glucose use fell. This is the neural efficiency hypothesis: more intelligent brains accomplish a task with less neural effort. The picture is not simple (on very difficult tasks, high scorers sometimes show more activation, because they keep working when others give up), and in 2007 Rex Jung and Haier summarised imaging studies in the parieto-frontal integration theory, which locates the networks most consistently linked to intelligence in the frontal and parietal lobes and the white-matter tracts between them.

Finally, the genes. Modern genome-wide studies of hundreds of thousands of people show that intelligence is massively polygenic: it is influenced by thousands of genetic variants, each with a tiny effect. There is no "gene for intelligence". Earlier searches used linkage studies, which track whether a trait travels through families together with known stretches of DNA; that method works well for single-gene disorders such as Huntington's disease but found nothing reliable for intelligence, precisely because no single gene has a large effect. How such genes combine with environments is the subject of the next section.

Remember

Correlates are not causes. Faster reaction time, shorter inspection time, larger working memory, a slightly larger brain and more efficient glucose use all go with higher IQ, but each could be a cause, an effect (years of schooling and reading change the brain) or a shared result of something else, such as better health and nutrition.

9.7 Heredity and EnvironmentLower yield · 2 Qs

No question in psychology has generated more heat than whether differences in intelligence are inherited. The heat comes from politics; the light comes from a small number of research designs that separate the effects of genes from the effects of environment. Learn the designs first, then the one statistic, heritability, that is most often misunderstood.

Family and Twin Studies

The simplest evidence comes from family studies: the more closely two people are related, the more similar their IQs. But relatives share homes as well as genes, so family resemblance alone cannot separate the two. Twin studies, first suggested by Galton in 1875, do better. Monozygotic (MZ) twins develop from a single fertilised egg and share virtually all their genes; dizygotic (DZ) twins develop from two eggs and share on average half their genes, like ordinary siblings. If both kinds of twins grow up together, their environments are about equally similar, so if MZ twins are more alike than DZ twins, the extra similarity is attributed to their extra genetic similarity. The most powerful version studies twins who were separated early in life and reared apart, because any similarity between them cannot come from a shared home.

In 1981 Thomas Bouchard and Matt McGue pooled 111 studies of family resemblance in IQ. Their weighted averages, shown in Figure 9.6, form a clear gradient: the more genes two people share, the more similar their IQs, and for the same degree of relationship, people reared together are more alike than people reared apart. Key study: The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart (from 1979)

Question. Would identical twins raised in different families turn out alike or different? Method. Thomas J. Bouchard Jr. and his colleagues at the University of Minnesota located twins who had been separated in infancy and brought each pair to Minneapolis for about 50 hours of testing on intelligence, personality, interests and health. Pinel and Barnes report 59 pairs of MZ twins and 47 pairs of DZ twins reared apart, aged 19 to 68, plus many twins reared together. Result. The MZ twins reared apart correlated about .70 on IQ, nearly as high as MZ twins reared together, and in their 1990 report the team concluded that about 70 per cent of the variance in IQ in their sample was associated with genetic variation. The project became famous for anecdotes such as the "Jim twins", separated at a few weeks of age and reunited at 39, who had both married women named Linda and then women named Betty and had both named a son James Alan or James Allan. Criticism. Anecdotes are not data; coincidences are guaranteed when you compare two lives in enough detail. More seriously, many "separated" twins were placed with relatives or similar families, some had met before testing, and all were reared in industrialised countries by families screened for adoption, so the range of environments was narrow. As you will see, a narrow range of environments inflates heritability.

Twin research also carries a warning from its history. The British psychologist Cyril Burt, whose hierarchical model you met in Section 9.4, reported very high IQ correlations for MZ twins reared apart from the 1940s to the 1960s. After his death in 1971, Leon Kamin (1974) noticed that Burt's correlations stayed identical to three decimal places (.771) as his sample supposedly grew, a statistical near-impossibility, and that his collaborators could not be traced. Most historians concluded that the later data were fraudulent, although a few writers have defended him. Modern estimates do not depend on Burt: the independent studies agree with one another.

Adoption Studies

Adoption studies compare adopted children with their biological parents, who gave them their genes but not their upbringing, and with their adoptive parents, who gave them their upbringing but not their genes. Large projects such as the Texas Adoption Project and the Colorado Adoption Project (begun in 1975 by Robert Plomin and John DeFries) produced a consistent and at first surprising result. As adopted children grow older, their IQs come to resemble those of their biological parents more and more, while the resemblance to their adoptive parents fades. Sandra Scarr and Richard Weinberg (1978) found that the IQ correlation between unrelated adolescents reared in the same adoptive home was close to zero.

But adoption studies also show something just as important: adoption changes the level of IQ. In a French cross-fostering study, Christiane Capron and Michel Duyme (1989) found that both the social class of the biological parents and that of the adoptive parents made a substantial difference to children's IQ. In another French study, Duyme and his colleagues (1999) followed children with low IQs who were adopted between the ages of 4 and 6; by adolescence, those adopted into high-status homes had gained about 20 points and those adopted into low-status homes about 8. The two findings are not in conflict. Correlations describe who ranks above whom; means describe how high everyone is. Genes can strongly influence the rank order of children within a home while the home raises or lowers the level of all of them.

Heritability: What It Is and What It Is Not

Heritability (written h2) is the proportion of the variation in a trait, within a particular population at a particular time, that is associated with genetic variation among the members of that population. In symbols, h2 = genetic variance ÷ total phenotypic variance. A heritability of .6 for IQ means that 60 per cent of the differences among people in that population are associated with their genetic differences. It does not mean that 60 per cent of your intelligence comes from your genes and 40 per cent from your upbringing; for an individual that sentence has no meaning, because every bit of your intelligence comes from genes and environment acting together, as the music of a sitar comes from both the instrument and the player.

You can estimate heritability from twin correlations with a simple formula proposed by Douglas Falconer: h2 = 2 × (rMZ − rDZ). Push Bouchard and McGue's figures through it: 2 × (.86 − .60) = 2 × .26 = .52. Because MZ twins reared together share all their genes and their home, the rest of their resemblance (.86 − .52 = .34) is attributed to the shared environment, the aspects of the home that make siblings alike, and whatever makes even identical twins differ (1 − .86 = .14) to the non-shared environment and measurement error. Three findings about heritability are essential:

  • It changes with age. The heritability of IQ rises from about .2 in infancy to about .4 to .5 in childhood and .6 or more in adulthood, while the effect of the shared family environment shrinks almost to zero by adulthood. Bouchard named this the Wilson effect, after Ronald Wilson's Louisville twin study. One explanation is that, as people grow up, they increasingly choose environments that suit their genetic tendencies.
  • It depends on the environment. In 2003 Eric Turkheimer and his colleagues studied 7-year-old American twins. In middle- and high-income families the heritability of IQ was about .70; in the poorest families it was close to .10, and the shared environment dominated. Where environments are adequate, genetic differences show; where environments are poor, poverty swamps them. Later meta-analyses found this pattern in the United States but weaker or absent in countries with stronger welfare systems, which is itself evidence that environments matter.
  • Within-group heritability says nothing about between-group differences. The geneticist Richard Lewontin (1929 to 2021) made the point in 1970 with seeds. Take a sack of genetically varied seed corn and plant one handful in rich soil and another in poor soil. Within each tray, differences in height are entirely genetic (heritability 100 per cent), because every plant in the tray received the same soil. Yet the difference between the two trays is entirely environmental. High heritability within groups is therefore no evidence at all that a difference between groups is genetic.

Finally, heritable does not mean unchangeable. Phenylketonuria (PKU), an inherited inability to break down the amino acid phenylalanine, has a heritability close to 100 per cent and once caused severe intellectual disability; a low-phenylalanine diet begun in infancy prevents it. Height is highly heritable too, yet average heights have risen by several centimetres in a few generations as nutrition improved.

Exam trap

Heritability is a population statistic, not an individual one; it describes differences, not amounts; it is specific to a population and its range of environments; it rises with age; it does not explain differences between groups; and a highly heritable trait can still be changed by the environment. "Individual differences in intelligence are due to genes only" and "environment only" are both wrong: the answer is many factors, genetic and environmental, acting together.

Reaction Range and the Interplay of Genes and Environment

Behaviour geneticists now speak less of genes versus environment and more of how they work together. In 1963 Irving Gottesman (1930 to 2016) proposed the concept of reaction range: a genotype sets upper and lower limits on how a trait can develop, and the environment determines where within that range a person ends up. A child with a favourable genotype in a deprived home may score lower than a child with a less favourable genotype in a stimulating one. (A related idea is canalisation, the tendency of some traits to develop along the same path across almost any environment.)

Genes and environments are also correlated. Sandra Scarr and Kathleen McCartney (1983) described three kinds of gene-environment correlation. It is passive when parents provide both genes and a matching environment (bookish parents pass on genes and fill the house with books). It is evocative when a child's genetically influenced traits draw responses from others (a curious child gets more answers). It is active when people seek out environments that suit them, which Scarr called niche-picking (the curious child grows up and joins the library). Active correlation becomes stronger with age, which helps explain why heritability rises. Turkheimer's finding is an example of gene-environment interaction: the effect of genes depends on the environment.

How the Environment Matters

Which environmental factors make a difference? The list begins before birth: maternal malnutrition, alcohol (the fetal alcohol syndrome of Section 9.8), infections and toxins can all lower IQ. After birth, nutrition matters. Iodine deficiency is the world's most preventable cause of intellectual impairment; a 1994 meta-analysis found that children in iodine-deficient areas scored about 13 points lower on average, which is why India adopted universal salt iodisation. Exposure to lead, from old paints, fuel and some cookware, lowers IQ even at low levels.

Schooling raises IQ. Children's scores drop slightly over long summer holidays, children who start school later fall behind age-mates, and a 2018 meta-analysis of natural experiments by Stuart Ritchie and Elliot Tucker-Drob estimated that each additional year of education raises IQ by about 1 to 5 points. Family environment matters too: the responsiveness of parents, the availability of books and play materials and the amount of conversation a child hears all correlate with later IQ. Robert Zajonc and Gregory Markus's confluence model (1975) even tried to explain small birth-order effects by the "intellectual climate" of the family, which is diluted as more young children are added. Teachers' expectations can matter as well (the Pygmalion effect, covered in the Social book).

The most dramatic evidence comes from deprivation and its reversal. In the 1930s Harold Skeels moved a group of apparently retarded toddlers from a bleak Iowa orphanage to an institution where women with intellectual disability doted on them. The transferred children gained, on average, close to 30 IQ points; the children left behind lost ground. Skeels followed them up as adults in 1966: the transferred group were self-supporting, while many of the others were still in institutions. More recently, Michael Rutter's study of children adopted into British homes from grossly depriving Romanian orphanages found that those adopted before about 6 months of age largely caught up, while those adopted later often showed lasting cognitive deficits. Early, severe deprivation does lasting harm; early rescue can undo much of it.

The Flynn Effect

The most startling evidence for environmental influence came from an unexpected source. In 1984 the political philosopher James Robert Flynn (1934 to 2020), working in New Zealand, showed that Americans had been gaining about 3 IQ points per decade throughout the twentieth century; in 1987 he reported massive gains in 14 nations. Every time a test was renormed, the new sample had to do better than the old one to earn the same IQ of 100. The gains were largest on tests of fluid reasoning such as Raven's matrices (Dutch military conscripts gained about 20 points between 1952 and 1982) and smallest on vocabulary and general information. The rise in average scores across generations is called the Flynn effect, a name coined by Herrnstein and Murray in The Bell Curve (1994); Richard Lynn had noticed the same trend in Japan a little earlier.

Genes cannot change that fast, so the cause must be environmental. Candidate explanations include better nutrition and health, more and better schooling, smaller families, a more visually and technologically complex environment and greater familiarity with tests. Flynn's own explanation was that modern life has taught people to put on "scientific spectacles": to classify things abstractly and to take hypothetical problems seriously, exactly what fluid-reasoning tests demand. Since the mid-1990s the effect has stalled or reversed in some countries such as Norway and Denmark; because the reversal appears even within families (younger brothers scoring lower than older ones), it too must be environmental.

Clinical link

The Flynn effect means test norms go stale. A test normed 15 years ago gives scores about 4 or 5 points too high today. An adult who scores 72 on outdated norms might score 67 or 68 on current norms, and that difference can decide whether a person receives a diagnosis of intellectual disability, a disability certificate or special educational support. In the United States, where the Supreme Court barred the execution of people with intellectual disability in 2002, courts have had to rule on such corrections. Always use the most recent norms available, and say which norms you used in your report.

Group Differences and Stereotype Threat

In The Bell Curve (1994) the psychologist Richard J. Herrnstein (1930 to 1994) and the political scientist Charles Murray (born 1943) argued that IQ strongly shapes social outcomes such as income, poverty and crime, that American society was stratifying into a "cognitive elite" and a struggling underclass, and that the average difference of about 15 points between Black and White Americans on IQ tests was probably partly genetic. The book sold hundreds of thousands of copies and provoked fierce criticism.

In response, the American Psychological Association appointed a task force chaired by Ulric Neisser, whose report "Intelligence: Knowns and Unknowns" (1996) remains the most balanced summary. Its main conclusions: IQ tests measure something real and predict school and job performance; the tests predict outcomes about equally well for different groups, so they are not "biased" in that narrow statistical sense; the heritability of IQ within groups is substantial; but there was no direct evidence that the average difference between Black and White Americans is genetic, and Lewontin's argument shows that within-group heritability cannot settle the question. Groups differ in history, income, schooling, health and discrimination, and the Flynn effect shows how large purely environmental changes in IQ can be. Since then the gap has narrowed: William Dickens and Flynn (2006) estimated that Black Americans gained 4 to 7 points on White Americans between 1972 and 2002.

One environmental mechanism can act during the test itself. Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995) gave Black and White Stanford University students a set of difficult verbal items from the Graduate Record Examination, with differences in their entrance-test (SAT) scores statistically controlled. When the test was described as a diagnostic measure of intellectual ability, Black students did markedly worse than White students; when the same test was described as a laboratory problem-solving task unrelated to ability, the difference disappeared. Steele called this stereotype threat: the fear of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group, which creates anxiety and uses up the working memory needed for the task. Similar effects were reported for women taking difficult mathematics tests. Later, larger studies have found the effects smaller and less consistent than the early experiments suggested, so stereotype threat is best seen as one contributing factor rather than the whole explanation (the Social book covers it further).

What about men and women? On overall measures of general intelligence the average difference is negligible; the tests were deliberately balanced to avoid one, and independent measures agree. There are small to moderate differences in specific abilities, most reliably an advantage for males in mental rotation and for females in verbal fluency and processing speed, and male scores tend to be more variable, with more men at both extremes.

Exam trap

The Flynn effect is a rise in average scores across generations, not a rise within one person's lifetime, and it is largest on fluid (Raven's-type) tests, not vocabulary. Herrnstein and Murray named it; Flynn documented it. Stereotype threat is Steele and Aronson (1995), not Rosenthal (teacher expectations) or Jensen (Level I and II). The Bell Curve (1994) is Herrnstein and Murray; "Intelligence: Knowns and Unknowns" (1996) is Neisser's APA task force.

Binet built his scale to find the children at one end of the distribution. The two tails of the curve in Figure 9.1, the 2 per cent below 70 and the 2 per cent above 130, remain the places where intelligence testing matters most to individual lives.

Defining Intellectual Disability

The name has changed repeatedly, each time because the old name had become an insult. "Idiot", "imbecile" and "moron" gave way to "mental deficiency", then to mental retardation, the term of DSM-IV-TR and of India's Persons with Disabilities Act of 1995, and finally to intellectual disability. DSM-5 (2013) uses intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder), DSM-5-TR (2022) puts the second name first, and ICD-11 speaks of disorders of intellectual development. Across all modern systems, the definition has three parts:

  1. Deficits in intellectual functions, such as reasoning, problem solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgement and learning, confirmed by clinical assessment and individually administered standardised intelligence testing. This means an IQ about two standard deviations or more below the mean, that is, about 70 or below; allowing for measurement error, DSM-5 speaks of scores of about 65 to 75.
  2. Deficits in adaptive functioning. Adaptive functioning means the conceptual, social and practical skills that people need to meet the standards of personal independence and social responsibility expected for their age and culture, such as handling money, communicating, keeping safe and looking after oneself.
  3. Onset during the developmental period. DSM-IV-TR required onset before 18; the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (AAIDD) extended its own definition to before age 22 in 2021.

The biggest change in DSM-5 concerns severity. Under DSM-IV-TR and ICD-10, the four levels (mild, moderate, severe and profound) were defined by IQ ranges (Table 9.13). DSM-5 keeps the four levels but defines them by adaptive functioning in the conceptual, social and practical domains, because it is adaptive functioning that determines how much support a person needs, and because IQ scores become unreliable at the very low end. About 1 per cent of the population has intellectual disability, and about 85 per cent of them are in the mild range.

Measuring Adaptive Behaviour

Because the diagnosis depends on everyday functioning, clinicians need a way to measure it. The classic tool was built at the same Vineland Training School where Goddard had worked. Edgar Arnold Doll (1889 to 1968) published the Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS) in 1935. It is completed by interviewing someone who knows the person well, and it covers self-help (general, eating and dressing), self-direction, occupation, communication, locomotion and socialisation. The scale yields a social age (SA), the age level of the person's social competence, and a social quotient (SQ) = (SA ÷ CA) × 100, a ratio exactly like Stern's IQ. A. J. Malin's Indian adaptation of the VSMS is the version most used in Indian clinics and disability certification. Its modern American successor, the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (Sara Sparrow, David Balla and Domenic Cicchetti, 1984; third edition 2016), assesses communication, daily living skills, socialisation and motor skills.

Keep the quotients apart: intelligence tests give mental age and IQ; the VSMS gives social age and social quotient; infant scales such as Gesell's and Bayley's give developmental age and developmental quotient.

Causes, in Brief

Causes are identifiable in perhaps half of all cases, more often in severe than in mild disability. The most common chromosomal cause is Down syndrome, described by John Langdon Down in 1866 and shown by Jérôme Lejeune in 1959 to result from an extra copy of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21). The most common inherited cause is fragile X syndrome, caused by an expanded repeat in the FMR1 gene on the X chromosome. Metabolic causes include PKU (first described by Asbjørn Følling in 1934, now prevented by newborn screening and diet) and congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism), linked to iodine deficiency. Environmental causes include fetal alcohol syndrome, prenatal infections such as rubella, birth asphyxia, head injury, severe malnutrition and lead poisoning. Edward Zigler (1967) distinguished an "organic" group with an identifiable cause, usually more severe, from a "familial" or "cultural-familial" group, usually mild, representing the lower end of the normal distribution combined with poverty and deprivation. The disorders themselves are covered in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

Indian context

The National Trust for the Welfare of Persons with Autism, Cerebral Palsy, Mental Retardation and Multiple Disabilities Act, 1999 covers exactly the four groups in its title, so autism and intellectual disability are both included. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 recognises 21 disabilities and lists specific learning disabilities and autism spectrum disorder under its intellectual disability heading. Disability certification for intellectual disability rests on a standardised IQ test together with an adaptive-behaviour measure such as the VSMS. The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI Act, 1992) regulates the training of the professionals, including clinical psychologists, who carry out such assessments, and the National Institute for the Empowerment of Persons with Intellectual Disabilities in Secunderabad (founded in 1984) leads national work in the field.

Giftedness

At the other tail, an IQ of 130 or above (the top 2 per cent) is the most common working cut-off for giftedness, but definitions have broadened. An influential 1972 United States government report listed six areas of giftedness: general intellectual ability, specific academic aptitude, creative or productive thinking, leadership, the visual and performing arts, and psychomotor ability.

Key study: Terman's Genetic Studies of Genius (from 1921)

Question. What becomes of exceptionally bright children? Popular opinion held that gifted children were frail, odd and doomed to "early ripe, early rot". Method. In 1921 Lewis Terman began screening California schoolchildren and selected about 1,500 (1,528) with Stanford-Binet IQs of about 135 or more, most above 140; their average IQ was about 151. The children, nicknamed the "Termites", were then followed for the rest of their lives, the study being carried on after Terman's death by Melita Oden, Robert Sears and others. Result. As a group the gifted children were healthier, taller, better adjusted and far more successful in education and careers than average; the stereotype of the sickly genius was wrong. Within the group, Terman and Oden found that the most successful men differed from the least successful not in IQ but in persistence, self-confidence, goal-directedness and family background. Textbook summaries add that the social and behavioural problems that did occur were concentrated among those with extremely high childhood IQs, of about 180 or more. Criticism. The sample was selected partly through teacher nomination and was disproportionately white, urban and middle class; many of the women, constrained by their era, never had careers to match their ability. And the screening missed two boys, William Shockley and Luis Alvarez, who were tested but not selected and later won Nobel Prizes in physics, a reminder that an IQ cut-off is not a measure of creative achievement.

Terman's contemporary Leta Stetter Hollingworth (1886 to 1939) studied the very top of the range. Her book Children Above 180 IQ, published posthumously in 1942, found that such children often struggled socially, because they had few true peers, and she argued that an IQ of about 125 to 155 was "socially optimal". This is consistent with the Terman finding that adjustment problems clustered above 180.

Many researchers now reject the idea that giftedness is simply a high IQ. Joseph Renzulli (born 1936) proposed the three-ring conception of giftedness (1978): gifted behaviour arises from the interaction of above-average (not necessarily exceptional) ability, task commitment and creativity. Françoys Gagné later distinguished gifts (natural abilities) from talents (systematically developed skills), with motivation, environment and chance turning one into the other. The relationship between creativity and intelligence, including the threshold hypothesis that IQ matters for creativity only up to about 120, is taken up in Chapter 8.

Savant Syndrome and Prodigies

Savant syndrome is the coexistence of a serious developmental disability, often autism or intellectual disability, with an "island" of exceptional ability. Langdon Down coined the term "idiot savant" in 1887; the psychiatrist Darold Treffert, who studied savants for decades, distinguished splinter skills, talented savants and the very rare prodigious savants. Roughly half of savants are autistic, and perhaps one autistic person in ten shows some savant skill. Typical domains are calendar calculation (naming the weekday of any date), rapid arithmetic, music, art and memory. Kim Peek (1951 to 2009), the inspiration for the film Rain Man (1988), memorised thousands of books; the British artist Stephen Wiltshire (born 1974), who is autistic, has drawn detailed panoramas of cities from memory after a single helicopter ride. Gardner used savants as evidence that abilities can be isolated, one of his criteria for separate intelligences.

A savant must be distinguished from a prodigy, a child of otherwise typical or high ability who performs at an adult expert level in some domain. India's Shakuntala Devi (1929 to 2013), the "human computer" who in 1980 multiplied two 13-digit numbers in her head at Imperial College London, was a calculating prodigy, not a savant; Arthur Jensen tested her in 1988 and found extraordinary speed on numerical tasks.

Clinical link

For a clinical psychologist, the lesson of both tails is the same: the score starts the assessment, it does not end it. A child at IQ 68 with good adaptive skills may need classroom support rather than a diagnosis; a child at IQ 145 may need help with anxiety or social isolation more than an accelerated syllabus; and an autistic adult with a savant skill may need adaptive-skills training to live independently. Always interpret IQ alongside adaptive functioning, history, culture and the person's own goals.

The case of Binet's scale, revisited

At the start of this chapter Binet made two claims: that intelligence is complex and a test can only sample it, and that it can be raised. A century of research has been kind to both. The factor theories found that intelligence is neither one thing nor a loose bundle of unrelated things, but a hierarchy: a general factor, broad abilities such as fluid and crystallised intelligence, and many narrow skills, with emotional, practical and creative abilities that standard tests barely touch. And the evidence that it can be raised is overwhelming: the Flynn effect, the gains of adopted children, the effects of schooling, iodine and early intervention, and the recovery of Skeels's toddlers.

The hereditarians who borrowed Binet's scale were not wholly wrong either; within a given population, a large share of the differences in adult IQ is associated with genes. What they missed is that heritability is not destiny: it describes differences within one set of environments, it says nothing about differences between groups, and it coexists with large environmental effects on everyone's level. Binet's original purpose, to find the children who need help and then help them, survives in the DSM-5's insistence on adaptive functioning and in India's disability laws, which tie an IQ score to a right to support rather than to a label.

Chapter summary

9.1 What is intelligence? Definitions range from Binet's judgement and adaptation, through Terman's abstract thinking, Thorndike's good responses and Boring's operational "what the tests test", to Wechsler's aggregate or global capacity to act purposefully, think rationally and deal effectively with the environment (1944), Sternberg's adapting, shaping and selecting, and Gottfredson's 1994 consensus. Achievement tests measure past learning, aptitude tests predict specific future learning, and intelligence tests predict learning in general. Implicit theories matter: Dweck's entity versus incremental theories (fixed versus growth mindset) shape persistence.

9.2 From Galton to Binet. Galton (Hereditary Genius, 1869; anthropometric laboratory, 1884) measured sensory acuity and reaction time; James McKeen Cattell coined "mental test" (1890), but Wissler (1901) showed such tests did not predict grades. Binet and Simon's 1905 scale of 30 items, revised in 1908 (mental age) and 1911, measured judgement directly. Goddard translated it, Stern coined the intelligence quotient (1912), and Terman's Stanford-Binet (1916) made IQ = MA ÷ CA × 100 famous. Yerkes's Army Alpha (verbal) and Beta (non-verbal) of 1917 began group testing, and their misuse is a lasting warning.

9.3 The IQ and the modern tests. The ratio IQ fails for adults, so Wechsler (1939) introduced the deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15; IQ = 100 + 15z), which converts directly to percentiles. The WAIS-IV has four indices (VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI) with 10 core subtests; the WISC-V (6 to 16 years 11 months) has five; the SB5 (2003) has five CHC factors. Raven's matrices (1938) and Cattell's Culture Fair test measure fluid reasoning with minimal language; Indian tests include the Binet-Kamat, MISIC and Bhatia's performance battery. IQ tests are highly reliable and predict school and job performance with correlations around .5.

9.4 Factor theories. Spearman (1904) found a positive manifold and proposed g plus specific s. Thorndike's multifactor theory named abstract, mechanical and social intelligence; Thomson explained correlations by overlapping samples of bonds; Thurstone (1938) found seven primary mental abilities; Guilford's cube had 120, then 150 and 180 abilities. Burt and Vernon arranged g, v:ed, k:m and specific factors in a hierarchy. Cattell and Horn split g into fluid and crystallised intelligence; Carroll's three strata (1993) and the CHC model now organise most tests. Jensen distinguished associative Level I from conceptual Level II abilities.

9.5 Broader views. Gardner (1983) proposed seven, later eight, relatively independent intelligences, attractive to teachers but weakly supported by data. Sternberg's triarchic theory (1985) has componential (analytical), experiential (creative) and contextual (practical) parts and grew into successful intelligence and tacit knowledge. The PASS model of Das, Naglieri and Kirby, based on Luria, measures planning, attention, simultaneous and successive processing with the CAS. Ceci stressed context and knowledge. Emotional intelligence comes in ability (Salovey and Mayer), mixed (Goleman, Bar-On) and trait (Petrides) versions; social, practical and spiritual intelligences extend the list.

9.6 Intelligence, speed and the brain. Higher IQ goes with faster and less variable reaction times, shorter inspection times, larger working memory capacity (especially for fluid intelligence), slightly larger brains and, in Haier's studies, more efficient brain metabolism. Intelligence is highly polygenic. These are correlates, not established causes.

9.7 Heredity and environment. Family, twin and adoption studies (Bouchard and McGue's review; the Minnesota study of twins reared apart) show substantial genetic influence. Heritability is a population statistic that rises with age, falls in poverty (Turkheimer) and cannot explain group differences (Lewontin). Gottesman's reaction range and gene-environment correlation and interaction describe how genes and environments combine. Nutrition, schooling, adoption and early intervention (Abecedarian, Perry, Head Start, ICDS) raise IQ or life outcomes, and the Flynn effect shows large environmental gains across generations. The Bell Curve's genetic claims for group differences are not supported; stereotype threat is one environmental factor in test performance.

9.8 The extremes. Intellectual disability requires deficits in intellectual and adaptive functioning beginning in the developmental period; DSM-IV-TR graded it by IQ bands, DSM-5 by adaptive functioning. Adaptive behaviour is measured by the VSMS (social age and social quotient) and the Vineland scales; global developmental delay applies under age 5. Causes include Down syndrome, fragile X and PKU. Giftedness is studied through Terman's longitudinal study, Hollingworth's children above 180 and Renzulli's three rings; savant syndrome pairs disability with islands of exceptional skill.

From the past papers

  1. What type of psychological test is the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS)? (RML 2025)

    (a) Aptitude test (b) Intelligence test (c) Attitude test (d) Achievement test

    Answer: (b) Intelligence test. The WAIS samples broad reasoning, knowledge, working memory and speed and combines them into a Full Scale IQ, which is the job of an intelligence test (Section 9.1). An aptitude test predicts how well someone will learn one specific skill with training (the Differential Aptitude Tests), an achievement test measures what a known course of teaching has already produced (a board examination), and an attitude test measures an evaluative stance towards an object, which is a Social book topic.

  2. Which of the following technique/process involved in guidance is called "the heart of the guidance programme"? (RML 2022)

    (a) Case study (b) Interview (c) Cumulative record (d) Aptitude test

    Answer: (d) Aptitude test (as keyed). Aptitude tests forecast how well a person will learn a course or an occupation, so the guidance decisions about which stream or career to choose rest on them. The case study, the interview and the cumulative record are also guidance tools, but they describe the person's past and present (an intensive life history, a face-to-face conversation, a running school file) rather than predict future learning. Be aware that the standard textbook phrase is "counselling is the heart of the guidance programme"; counselling is not offered here, but if it appears as an option, choose it.

  3. Which of the following psychologists is associated with the development of the first intelligence test? (RML 2025)

    (a) Freud (b) Piaget (c) Binet (d) Skinner

    Answer: (c) Binet. Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon published the first practical intelligence scale, 30 items of increasing difficulty, in 1905 (Section 9.2). Piaget is the bait: he worked in Simon's Paris laboratory around 1920 standardising reasoning tests, but his interest in children's errors led to a theory of cognitive development, not to the first test. Freud (psychoanalysis) and Skinner (operant conditioning) had nothing to do with intelligence testing.

  4. The term IQ was invented by (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Lewis Termon (sic) (b) Gordon Allport (c) William Stern (d) John Watson

    Answer: (c) William Stern. Stern proposed the Intelligenzquotient, mental age divided by chronological age, in 1912. Terman is the tempting wrong answer: he multiplied Stern's quotient by 100 and made the IQ famous through the 1916 Stanford-Binet, but he did not coin it. Allport is a trait theorist of personality and Watson the founder of behaviourism; neither worked on the IQ.

  5. IQ if any body has a mental age of 8 and a chronological age of 10 is: (RML 2020)

    (a) 108 (b) 125 (c) 80 (d) 92

    Answer: (c) 80. Ratio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100 = (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80. The figure 125 comes from turning the ratio upside down (10 ÷ 8 × 100), the commonest slip; 108 and 92 are not produced by any calculation with these numbers. A quick check: whenever mental age is below chronological age, the IQ must be below 100.

  6. To determine whether a newly developed intelligence test truly accurately measures intelligence or not, it is necessary to administer the test with one that has been in the field for some time. Which of the following needs to be looked for? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Construct validity (b) Concurrent validity (c) Content validity (d) Predictive validity

    Answer: (b) Concurrent validity. Giving the new test together with an established test and correlating the two sets of scores is criterion-related validity with the criterion measured at the same time, that is, concurrent validity (Section 9.3). Predictive validity would need a criterion gathered later, such as next year's grades; content validity is judged by examining whether the items sample the domain, not by correlating with another test; and construct validity is the broader question of whether the test behaves as the theory of intelligence predicts across many kinds of evidence.

  7. In the WAIS-IV (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fourth Edition), how many core subtests are there in the Verbal Comprehension Index and the Perceptual Reasoning Index respectively? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) 3 verbal, 4 performance (b) 3 verbal, 3 performance (c) 4 verbal, 3 performance (d) 2 verbal, 4 performance

    Answer: (b) 3 verbal, 3 performance. The VCI core is Similarities, Vocabulary and Information; the PRI core is Block Design, Matrix Reasoning and Visual Puzzles (Table 9.5). The options with a 4 are tempting because each index also has supplemental subtests (Comprehension for VCI; Picture Completion and Figure Weights for PRI), but supplemental subtests only substitute for core ones and do not count towards the core total of 10.

  8. Which of the following subtests is part of the core battery for the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-IV) but NOT typically found in the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC-V)? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Digit Span (b) Symbol Search (c) Matrix Reasoning (d) Letter-Number Sequencing

    Answer: (d) Letter-Number Sequencing (as keyed). Digit Span, Symbol Search and Matrix Reasoning are primary subtests in both the WAIS-IV and the WISC-V, so none of them can be the answer. Letter-Number Sequencing is the only option that is not a primary WISC-V subtest (it is a secondary one there). Strictly, it is a supplemental, not core, subtest of the WAIS-IV (it was core in the WAIS-III), so the stem is loose, but (d) is clearly the best of the four.

  9. What is the age range valid for Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, 5th edition (WISC-V)? (RML 2022)

    (a) 5 years to 12 years (b) 6 years to 16 years 11 months (c) 7 years to 16 years 11 months (d) 6 years to 15 years 11 months

    Answer: (b) 6 years to 16 years 11 months. The WISC-V fills the gap between the WPPSI-IV, which ends at 7 years 7 months, and the WAIS-IV, which begins at 16 years (Table 9.4). Options (c) and (d) each move one end of the range by a year, and (a) matches no Wechsler scale.

  10. Raven's Progressive Matrices (RPM) is primarily designed to measure which type of intelligence? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Crystallized intelligence (b) Fluid intelligence (c) Verbal intelligence (d) Social intelligence

    Answer: (b) Fluid intelligence. Each item requires working out the rule behind a novel pattern of figures, with almost no language or learned knowledge, which is fluid reasoning (Sections 9.3 and 9.4). Crystallised intelligence is acquired knowledge and is measured by vocabulary and information subtests; verbal intelligence is exactly what a non-verbal test avoids; and social intelligence, Thorndike's ability to understand and manage people, is not sampled at all.

  11. Raven's Standard Progressive Matrices (SPM) measure which kind of intelligence? (RML 2022)

    (a) Crystallised intelligence (b) Fluid intelligence (c) Both a and b (d) General Intelligence

    Answer: (b) Fluid intelligence. The SPM (1938) is the classic measure of Gf. Option (d) is the tempting one, because Raven's is also the best single marker of Spearman's g and Gf and g are closely related; but when fluid intelligence is offered, it is the more precise and expected answer. "Both" is wrong because crystallised knowledge is precisely what the matrices were designed to keep out.

  12. A 10-year-old child from a rural area, with no formal schooling, is brought for an intellectual assessment due to academic struggles. The child is illiterate and has had minimal exposure to formal testing materials. Which of the following tests would be the MOST appropriate to use to minimize cultural and educational bias? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence (b) Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC-IV) (c) Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale (5th ed.) (d) Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC)

    Answer: (a) Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence. Its five performance subtests (Kohs blocks, pass-along, pattern drawing, immediate memory, picture construction) need no reading, and it has Indian rural norms (Section 9.3). The WISC-IV and SB5 lean heavily on school-built verbal knowledge and were normed on other populations. MISIC is the tempting Indian option, but half of it is verbal, adapted from the WISC's information, comprehension, arithmetic and vocabulary items.

  13. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale can be used to assess eligibility for: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Housing benefit (b) Disability allowance (c) Suicide risk (d) Special educational needs

    Answer: (b) Disability allowance. A WAIS Full Scale IQ, together with a measure of adaptive behaviour, documents intellectual disability or acquired cognitive impairment for a disability certificate or allowance (Clinical link, Section 9.3). Special educational needs is the tempting option, but it concerns children, who are assessed with the WISC or an Indian equivalent rather than the adult scale. Housing benefit depends on income and need, not on cognitive ability, and suicide risk is assessed by clinical interview and specific risk scales, not by an intelligence test.

  14. Cattell considered crystallised intelligence to be a (an) (RML 2021)

    (a) Development Process (b) Ability to deal with new problems (c) Repertoire of information, cognitive skills and strategies (d) Function of brain damage

    Answer: (c) Repertoire of information, cognitive skills and strategies. Gc is what fluid ability has "invested" in and crystallised through education and experience: vocabulary, general knowledge and learned methods (Section 9.4). Option (b), the ability to deal with new problems, is the definition of fluid intelligence, the classic swap. Gc does develop, but "a development process" describes how it grows, not what it is; and far from being a function of brain damage, crystallised abilities such as vocabulary are the ones that hold up best with ageing.

  15. According to the Guilford model of intelligence, how many dimensions of intelligence are there? (RML 2021)

    (a) Three dimensions (b) 120 dimensions (c) 10 dimensions (d) A variable number depending on I.Q.

    Answer: (b) 120 dimensions (as keyed). The key uses "dimensions" loosely to mean the separate abilities, the cells of Guilford's cube: 5 operations × 4 contents × 6 products = 120 (1967), later 150 and then 180 (Section 9.4). Strictly, the cube itself has three dimensions (operations, contents and products), so on a literal reading (a) is the correct fact; the paper's key nevertheless went to 120, so for this exact question mark (b), and whenever a question asks how many abilities or factors, choose 120. Ten and "a variable number depending on IQ" have no basis in the model.

  16. Which type of intelligence according to Sternberg's Triarchic theory of intelligence includes the ability to easily adapt to the present environment or select a more favourable environment than the existing one or modify the environment to fit one's need? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Componential (b) Contextual (c) Incremental (d) Experimental

    Answer: (b) Contextual. Adapting to, selecting and shaping (modifying) the environment are the three functions of the contextual subtheory, whose ability Sternberg later called practical intelligence (Section 9.5). Componential intelligence is the analytical machinery of metacomponents, performance and knowledge-acquisition components. "Incremental" belongs to Dweck's incremental (growth) theory of intelligence, and "experimental" is a corruption of experiential, the subtheory of coping with novelty and automatising, that is, creative intelligence.

  17. Individual differences in intelligence are due to: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Only genetic factors (b) Only environmental factors (c) Genetic and economic factors (d) Many factors including genetics and environment

    Answer: (d) Many factors including genetics and environment. Twin and adoption studies show substantial heritability, but it is well below 1 and varies with age and circumstances; nutrition, schooling, adoption, early intervention and the Flynn effect show large environmental effects, and the two interact (Section 9.7). Options (a) and (b) each ignore half the evidence, and (c) names only one environmental factor, socio-economic status, leaving out schooling, nutrition, family environment and the rest.

  18. The cardinal DSM-IV-TR diagnostic criterion for Mental Retardation is based primarily on an IQ score: (RML 2021)

    (a) Seven standard deviations below the mean (b) One standard deviation below the mean (c) Two standard deviations below the mean (d) Four standard deviations below the mean

    Answer: (c) Two standard deviations below the mean. With a mean of 100 and an SD of 15, two SDs below is an IQ of about 70, the DSM-IV-TR cut-off for significantly subaverage intellectual functioning (Section 9.8). One SD below (85) is merely low average; four SDs below (40) already lies at the moderate to severe boundary; and seven SDs below would be an IQ of about −5, which does not exist on the scale.

  19. In which of the following situations would a diagnosis of "global developmental delay" be inappropriate? (RML 2022)

    (a) The patient is a child who is too young to fully manifest specific symptoms or to complete requisite assessments. (b) The patient, a 7-year-old boy, has a full-scale IQ of 65 and severe impairment in adaptive functioning. (c) The patient's scores on psychometric tests suggest intellectual disability, but there is insufficient information about the patient's adaptive functional skills. (d) The patient's impaired adaptive functioning suggests intellectual developmental disorder, but there is insufficient information about the level of cognitive impairment measured by standardized instruments.

    Answer: (b). Global developmental delay is a provisional DSM-5 diagnosis for children under 5 whose severity cannot yet be assessed reliably (Section 9.8). The 7-year-old is over 5 and has been fully assessed, with both intellectual and adaptive deficits documented, so he receives a diagnosis of intellectual disability itself. Options (a), (c) and (d) all describe an assessment that is incomplete or not yet possible, which is exactly the situation the provisional label exists for.

  20. Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS) is a measure of: (RML 2022)

    (a) Mental age and Intelligence quotient (b) Social age and social quotient (c) Developmental age and developmental quotient (d) Emotional age and emotional quotient

    Answer: (b) Social age and social quotient. Doll's VSMS (1935) converts social competence into a social age, and SQ = (SA ÷ CA) × 100 (Section 9.8). Mental age and IQ come from intelligence tests; developmental age and DQ come from infant scales such as Gesell's and Bayley's; and "emotional age" is not a standard measure, while EQ is Bar-On's emotional quotient, which has nothing to do with the VSMS.

  21. In Terman's study of gifted children, social and behavioural problems were found only in those with: (RML 2022)

    (a) IQs of 150 or higher (b) IQs of 180 or higher (c) IQs of 180 or higher in adulthood (d) IQs of 180 or higher in childhood

    Answer: (d) IQs of 180 or higher in childhood. As a group, Terman's "Termites" were healthier and better adjusted than average; the problems that did occur were concentrated among those whose childhood IQs were about 180 or more, in line with Hollingworth's Children Above 180 IQ (Section 9.8). Option (a) cannot be right because 150 is about the average of the whole gifted group; (b) omits the key point that the IQs were childhood scores, and (c) wrongly places the testing in adulthood.

  22. Which of the following disabilities is explicitly covered under the scope of The National Trust Act (1999) in India for the welfare of persons with the disability? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Autism Spectrum Disorder (b) Specific Learning Disability (c) Intellectual Disability (Mental Retardation) (d) Both (a) and (c)

    Answer: (d) Both (a) and (c). The Act's full title names its four groups: autism, cerebral palsy, mental retardation and multiple disabilities (Indian context, Section 9.8). Choosing (a) or (c) alone misses the other named group. Specific learning disability is not among the four; it is recognised under the broader Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016.
