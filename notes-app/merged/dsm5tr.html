DSM-5-TR, the Exam Cut

Every disorder the Indian clinical-psychology entrances actually test, written the way you need it in the hall: the criteria in plain language rather than manual English, the numbers that carry the mark (duration, age of onset, symptom counts, exclusion clauses), and then, under every single disorder, a Comorbidity line and a Differential line. The differential is where papers hide the mark, because the stem almost never asks "what is the definition of X", it describes a person and asks which of four neighbouring diagnoses fits. So read a card as a whole: the criteria tell you what it is, the differential tells you what it is not, and the comorbidity tells you what else to expect sitting on top of it. DSM-5-TR (2022) is used throughout, with the ICD-11 line noted wherever the two systems disagree in a way an examiner can exploit.

1. How DSM-5-TR is built, and how to use it in an exam

Before any disorder, understand the machine. A surprising number of entrance questions are about the classification system itself rather than about any single disorder, and those are free marks because there is nothing to remember except structure.

What DSM is, and what it deliberately is not

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders is published by the American Psychiatric Association. It is a categorical, descriptive, atheoretical nosology. Descriptive means it defines disorders by observable signs and reported symptoms, not by presumed cause. Atheoretical means it deliberately refuses to commit to psychoanalytic, behavioural, or biological explanation, so a clinician of any orientation can use the same code. That neutrality was the single biggest change at DSM-III (1980), which is the edition that introduced explicit operational criteria, the multiaxial system, and the abandonment of the psychoanalytic word "neurosis". If a paper asks which DSM edition marked the turn to operational criteria and reliability, the answer is always DSM-III.

Edition timeline, memorise the years

DSM-I 1952 (106 categories, "reactions", Meyerian). DSM-II 1968 (182 categories, still psychodynamic language). DSM-III 1980 (265 categories, operational criteria, multiaxial, atheoretical, homosexuality already removed as a diagnosis in 1973 from DSM-II's printing). DSM-III-R 1987. DSM-IV 1994, DSM-IV-TR 2000. DSM-5 2013 (Arabic numeral so it can be revised 5.1, 5.2; multiaxial system abolished; dimensional and spectrum thinking added). DSM-5-TR March 2022.

What changed in the 2022 Text Revision, specifically

DSM-5-TR is a text revision, so most of the change is in the explanatory text rather than the criteria. The examinable changes are:

  • One entirely new disorder: Prolonged Grief Disorder, placed in the Trauma and Stressor-Related chapter. This is the highest-yield single fact in the whole TR.
  • Unspecified Mood Disorder was brought back, for presentations where you cannot yet tell depressive from bipolar.
  • Suicidal behaviour and non-suicidal self-injury became separate coded conditions (ICD-10-CM codes) that can be recorded alongside any diagnosis, rather than only being criteria inside borderline personality disorder and depression.
  • Terminology. "Intellectual Disability" now carries the parenthetical "(Intellectual Developmental Disorder)"; "neuroleptic" was largely replaced by "antipsychotic"; the term "sexual identity" was dropped; "desired gender" became "experienced gender", "cross-sex medical procedure" became "gender-affirming medical procedure", and "natal male/female" became "individual assigned male/female at birth" in Gender Dysphoria.
  • Culture and racism. Text throughout was rewritten by an Ethnoracial Equity and Inclusion work group; "race" is discussed as a social construct, "racialised" and "ethnoracial" replace older usage, and the impact of racism and discrimination on prevalence and presentation is stated in the text.
  • Prevalence, risk and culture sections were updated across almost every disorder, and ICD-10-CM codes were refreshed.

The multiaxial system, and why it is gone

DSM-IV-TR used five axes: Axis I clinical disorders, Axis II personality disorders and mental retardation, Axis III general medical conditions, Axis IV psychosocial and environmental problems, Axis V the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF), a 0 to 100 scale. DSM-5 abolished all of it. Axes I, II and III were merged into a single list of diagnoses; Axis IV was replaced by the ICD Z-codes for psychosocial and contextual factors; Axis V was dropped because the GAF was psychometrically poor (it confounded symptom severity with functioning) and replaced by the WHODAS 2.0, the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule, a 36-item measure across six domains (cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities, participation).

Trap

Questions still ask "on which axis was mental retardation coded?" The answer is Axis II, in DSM-IV-TR, and the correct full answer adds that the multiaxial system no longer exists in DSM-5 or DSM-5-TR and that GAF was replaced by WHODAS 2.0. Answer both halves.

The definition of a mental disorder

DSM-5-TR defines a mental disorder as a syndrome characterised by clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation, or behaviour that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning. Mental disorders are usually associated with significant distress or disability in social, occupational, or other important activities. Three exclusions are written into that definition and are examined constantly:

  • An expectable or culturally approved response to a common stressor or loss (for example ordinary bereavement) is not a mental disorder.
  • Socially deviant behaviour (political, religious, sexual) and conflicts primarily between the individual and society are not mental disorders, unless the deviance results from a dysfunction in the individual.
  • The disturbance must not be better explained by another condition, including a substance or another medical condition.
The clause under almost every disorder

Two clauses recur so often that you should assume them unless told otherwise. Clinical significance: "the symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning." Exclusion: "not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance or another medical condition, and not better explained by another mental disorder." When a question gives you a person with symptoms but explicitly says work and relationships are unaffected and they are not bothered, the answer is usually "no diagnosis", not the disorder that matches the symptom list.

Categorical versus dimensional, and where DSM-5-TR hedges

DSM remains categorical: you either meet criteria or you do not. The known problems with that are heterogeneity (two people with the same diagnosis can share almost no symptoms), arbitrary thresholds (five of nine symptoms is a diagnosis, four is not), high comorbidity (a sign the categories are not carving nature at its joints), and heavy use of "other specified" and "unspecified" residual categories. DSM-5 added dimensional elements without abandoning categories: severity specifiers on almost every disorder, cross-cutting symptom measures, the collapse of autism's subtypes into one spectrum, the collapse of substance abuse and dependence into a single graded substance use disorder, and the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders in Section III. The fully dimensional alternatives you should be able to name are HiTOP (Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology), RDoC (Research Domain Criteria, NIMH, organised by domains such as negative valence and positive valence systems crossed with units of analysis from genes to self-report), and the p-factor (a general psychopathology factor).

DSM-5-TR versus ICD-11, the comparisons that get asked

  • Publisher and purpose. DSM is by the American Psychiatric Association, primarily a diagnostic manual for clinicians and researchers. ICD-11 (in effect from 1 January 2022) is by the WHO, is a global public-health statistical classification covering all diseases, is free, and is the system India officially uses for reporting.
  • Structure. ICD-11 uses alphanumeric codes; mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders sit in Chapter 06. ICD-11 gives prototypical descriptions with flexible boundaries rather than DSM's counted criteria, on the reasoning that counted thresholds create false precision in general medical settings.
  • Disorders that exist in one and not the other. ICD-11 has Complex PTSD, Gaming Disorder, Prolonged Grief Disorder (DSM added it in the TR), Body Integrity Dysphoria, Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder and Olfactory Reference Disorder; DSM-5-TR does not have Complex PTSD or Gaming Disorder as full diagnoses (internet gaming disorder is in Section III, conditions for further study). DSM has Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder; ICD-11 folds that presentation into oppositional defiant disorder "with chronic irritability-anger".
  • Personality. ICD-11 abolished the ten categorical personality disorders entirely and replaced them with a single Personality Disorder diagnosis rated mild, moderate or severe, plus five trait domain qualifiers (negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, anankastia) and a borderline pattern qualifier. DSM-5-TR keeps the ten categories in Section II and offers its own trait model only in Section III.
  • Schizophrenia. Both dropped the classical subtypes (paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic, undifferentiated, residual, simple). ICD-11 requires one month of symptoms; DSM requires six months of total disturbance with at least one month of active-phase symptoms. That six versus one month contrast is one of the most reused questions in the whole syllabus.
How to read a vignette fast

Four questions, in this order. (1) Duration. Almost every differential in DSM is settled by time: 1 day / 1 month / 6 months / 2 years. Find the duration in the stem first. (2) Onset age. Before 12, before 18, after 60. (3) Is there a substance or medical cause? If the stem mentions steroids, thyroid, stroke, or a drug, that is not decoration. (4) Impairment. If it says functioning is intact, suspect a subthreshold or "no diagnosis" option.

2. Neurodevelopmental disorders

Onset in the developmental period, meaning the deficits are present early and are not a decline from a previously normal level. That last phrase is the whole differential against the neurocognitive chapter: neurodevelopmental means never acquired, neurocognitive means acquired and then lost.

Intellectual Developmental Disorder (Intellectual Disability) F70-F73

In plain language. The person's reasoning and their ability to run their own life are both below what is expected for their age, and this has been true since childhood. DSM-5 made the important move of deciding severity by adaptive functioning, not by the IQ number, because how well someone copes is what determines the support they need.
  • Deficits in intellectual functions (reasoning, problem solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgment, academic learning, learning from experience), confirmed by clinical assessment and individualised standardised testing.
  • Deficits in adaptive functioning so that the person fails to meet developmental and sociocultural standards for personal independence and social responsibility, across at least one of three domains: conceptual (language, reading, money, time), social (empathy, friendships, gullibility), practical (self-care, job, money management, transport).
  • Onset during the developmental period.
Numbers IQ roughly two standard deviations below the mean, about 65 to 75 allowing for the measurement error band, but severity is graded Mild / Moderate / Severe / Profound on adaptive functioning, not IQ. Prevalence about 1 percent overall, about 6 per 1000 for severe. Male to female roughly 1.6:1 for mild.
Related Global Developmental Delay is the diagnosis under age 5 when severity cannot yet be reliably assessed; it requires reassessment. Unspecified Intellectual Disability is for over-5s who cannot be assessed because of sensory or physical impairment or severe behaviour.
Comorbidity Three to four times the general-population rate of other mental disorders. Commonest: ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, stereotypic movement disorder (with or without self-injury), depressive and bipolar disorders, anxiety disorders, impulse-control disorders. Medically: epilepsy, cerebral palsy, sensory impairment.
Differential Major neurocognitive disorder (there the ability was present and was lost; here it was never acquired, though the two can coexist). Specific learning disorder (deficit is confined to one academic domain, general intelligence is normal). Communication disorders (language impaired, non-verbal reasoning intact). Autism (social deficits out of proportion to overall developmental level). Also rule out sensory impairment, deprivation, and poor test-taking due to unfamiliarity with the testing language, which is a real issue in Indian samples.

Communication Disorders F80.x

In plain language. Five conditions, each about a different part of using language.
  • Language Disorder. Persistent difficulty acquiring and using language across modalities (spoken, written, sign) because of deficits in comprehension or production: reduced vocabulary, limited sentence structure, impaired discourse. Abilities are substantially below age expectation. Onset in the early developmental period.
  • Speech Sound Disorder. Persistent difficulty with speech sound production that interferes with intelligibility. The child is understood poorly, though language content is fine.
  • Childhood-Onset Fluency Disorder (Stuttering). Disturbance in the normal fluency and time patterning of speech: sound and syllable repetitions, prolongations, broken words, audible or silent blocking, circumlocutions, physical tension. Causes anxiety about speaking.
  • Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder. Persistent difficulty with the social use of verbal and non-verbal communication: greeting and sharing information appropriately, changing communication to match the listener or context, following rules of conversation and storytelling (turn taking, rephrasing when misunderstood), and understanding what is not explicitly stated (inference, idiom, humour, metaphor). New in DSM-5.
  • Unspecified Communication Disorder.
Numbers Stuttering onset usually between ages 2 and 7, with 80 to 90 percent onset by age 6; recovery in about 65 to 85 percent. Social pragmatic communication disorder is rarely diagnosed under age 4 or 5 because the skills are not yet expected.
Comorbidity Specific learning disorder, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, developmental coordination disorder, social anxiety disorder (especially with stuttering).
Differential The examined one is Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder versus Autism Spectrum Disorder. Both have the social communication deficit. Autism additionally requires restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, and if those are present now or were ever present in the past, the diagnosis is autism, not SPCD. So SPCD is autism's Criterion A without autism's Criterion B, and you must ask about childhood history before settling on it. Also rule out intellectual disability, hearing loss, selective mutism (the child can speak, but does not in specific settings), and normal variation.

Autism Spectrum Disorder F84.0

In plain language. Two things together, both from early childhood: the person does not do the ordinary back-and-forth of relating to other people, and the person's behaviour and interests are narrow, repetitive, and sensitive to change. DSM-5 collapsed autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder and PDD-NOS into one spectrum, and cut the old triad to a dyad by merging social and communication deficits into one criterion.
  • A. Persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction across contexts, and all three must be present: (1) deficits in social-emotional reciprocity, from abnormal back-and-forth conversation and failure to share interests or affect through to failure to initiate or respond to social interaction; (2) deficits in non-verbal communicative behaviours, from poorly integrated verbal and non-verbal communication through abnormal eye contact and body language to a total lack of facial expression and gesture; (3) deficits in developing, maintaining and understanding relationships, from difficulty adjusting behaviour to context through difficulty making friends to an absence of interest in peers.
  • B. Restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, at least two of four: (1) stereotyped or repetitive motor movements, use of objects or speech (lining up toys, echolalia, idiosyncratic phrases); (2) insistence on sameness, inflexible routines, ritualised patterns, extreme distress at small change; (3) highly restricted, fixated interests abnormal in intensity or focus; (4) hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment (indifference to pain or temperature, adverse response to sounds or textures, excessive smelling or touching, visual fascination with lights or movement). Criterion B4, the sensory criterion, is new in DSM-5.
  • C. Symptoms present in the early developmental period, though they may not fully show until social demands exceed capacity, and may be masked by learned strategies later.
  • D. Clinically significant impairment. E. Not better explained by intellectual disability or global developmental delay; to diagnose both, social communication must be below that expected for general developmental level.
Specifiers Severity is coded separately for social communication and for restricted repetitive behaviours, as Level 1 requiring support, Level 2 requiring substantial support, Level 3 requiring very substantial support. Also specify: with or without accompanying intellectual impairment; with or without accompanying language impairment; associated with a known medical, genetic or environmental factor; associated with another neurodevelopmental, mental or behavioural disorder; with catatonia.
Numbers Prevalence now reported around 1 in 100 worldwide and higher in US surveillance data (about 1 in 36 in recent CDC estimates). Male to female about 4:1, and girls are under-identified because of camouflaging. Concordance in monozygotic twins is high (roughly 60 to 90 percent for the broader phenotype); heritability is among the highest in psychiatry.
Comorbidity Around 70 percent have at least one comorbid mental disorder and about 40 percent have two or more. Commonest: intellectual disability, ADHD (DSM-5 for the first time allowed the two to be diagnosed together, DSM-IV forbade it), anxiety disorders (especially specific phobia and social anxiety), depression, OCD, tic disorders, specific learning disorder, developmental coordination disorder, avoidant restrictive food intake disorder, sleep disorders, epilepsy (about 20 to 30 percent), gastrointestinal problems, and known genetic syndromes such as fragile X, tuberous sclerosis and Rett syndrome.
Differential Social (pragmatic) communication disorder: no Criterion B, ever. Intellectual disability without autism: social skills are at the level of the mental age, not below it. Rett syndrome: in DSM-5-TR this is no longer a separate diagnosis but is coded as an associated medical condition; the pattern of regression with hand-wringing between 5 and 48 months is the clue. Selective mutism: normal social reciprocity outside the feared setting. ADHD: inattention and social intrusiveness without the qualitative social-reciprocity deficit or the repetitive behaviours. Schizophrenia with childhood onset: delusions and hallucinations appear after a period of normal development. Stereotypic movement disorder: stereotypies without the social criterion. Reactive attachment disorder: severe social neglect history, and the social deficit improves once care improves.
ICD-11 Also uses a single "Autism spectrum disorder" with qualifiers for intellectual and language impairment, so the two systems agree here. ICD-11 kept Rett syndrome out of the mental disorders chapter as well.

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder F90.x

In plain language. Either the child cannot hold attention, or cannot stay still and cannot wait, or both, to a degree that is out of step with their age and is spoiling their life in more than one place.
  • A. A persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, with at least six of nine symptoms in the relevant list, present for at least six months, at a level inconsistent with developmental level. From age 17 and older, five of nine is enough.
  • Inattention list (9): careless mistakes and inattention to detail; difficulty sustaining attention; does not seem to listen when spoken to directly; does not follow through on instructions and fails to finish; difficulty organising tasks; avoids tasks requiring sustained mental effort; loses things; easily distracted by extraneous stimuli; forgetful in daily activities.
  • Hyperactivity-impulsivity list (9): fidgets or squirms; leaves seat when remaining seated is expected; runs or climbs inappropriately (in adults, restlessness); unable to play quietly; "on the go" as if driven by a motor; talks excessively; blurts out answers; difficulty waiting turn; interrupts or intrudes.
  • B. Several symptoms present before age 12. C. Symptoms present in two or more settings (home, school, work, with friends). D. Clear interference with functioning. E. Not occurring exclusively during schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder, and not better explained by another mental disorder.
Specifiers Predominantly inattentive presentation, predominantly hyperactive/impulsive presentation, combined presentation. Note the word: DSM-5 says presentation, not subtype, because people move between them over time. Also in partial remission, and severity mild / moderate / severe.
Numbers The age-of-onset criterion was raised from 7 in DSM-IV to 12 in DSM-5, which is the single most examined ADHD fact. Symptom count drops from 6 to 5 at age 17. Prevalence about 5 percent of children and about 2.5 percent of adults. Male to female about 2:1 in children, closer to 1.6:1 in adults; girls present more often with the inattentive picture.
Comorbidity Oppositional defiant disorder (about half of combined presentation), conduct disorder (about a quarter), specific learning disorder, anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, autism spectrum disorder, tic disorders including Tourette's, substance use disorders in adolescence and adulthood, and antisocial personality disorder in adults.
Differential Oppositional defiant disorder: the child refuses because they resist demands, not because they cannot sustain attention. Specific learning disorder: inattention appears only in the academic domain that is hard. Intellectual disability: symptoms must exceed what the mental age predicts. Bipolar disorder: ADHD is chronic and continuous, mania is episodic with a clear change from baseline, and mania brings elevated mood and reduced need for sleep, which ADHD does not. Anxiety: inattention there is driven by worry and rumination. Reactive attachment disorder and medication effects (bronchodilators, thyroid). Also normal high activity in a young child, and an unstimulating classroom.

Specific Learning Disorder F81.x

In plain language. A child of ordinary intelligence, taught properly, still cannot read, write or do arithmetic at anything like age level.
  • A. Difficulties learning and using academic skills, with at least one of six symptoms persisting for at least six months despite intervention: inaccurate or slow effortful word reading; difficulty understanding what is read; difficulties with spelling; difficulties with written expression; difficulties mastering number sense, number facts or calculation; difficulties with mathematical reasoning.
  • B. The affected academic skills are substantially and quantifiably below those expected for chronological age and cause significant interference, confirmed by individually administered standardised achievement measures (or, over age 17, a documented history).
  • C. Begin during school-age years, though they may not fully appear until demands exceed capacity.
  • D. Not better accounted for by intellectual disability, uncorrected visual or auditory acuity, other mental or neurological disorders, psychosocial adversity, lack of proficiency in the language of instruction, or inadequate educational instruction.
Specifiers With impairment in reading (word reading accuracy, reading rate or fluency, reading comprehension; the alternative term dyslexia is retained in the text), in written expression (spelling accuracy, grammar and punctuation, clarity or organisation; dysgraphia), or in mathematics (number sense, memorisation of arithmetic facts, accurate or fluent calculation, accurate mathematical reasoning; dyscalculia). Severity mild / moderate / severe.
Numbers Prevalence 5 to 15 percent of school-age children across languages and cultures; about 4 percent in adults. Reading is the commonest domain. DSM-5 abolished the old IQ-achievement discrepancy requirement, which is a favourite question: you no longer need a gap between IQ and achievement, you need low achievement plus failure to respond to intervention.
Comorbidity ADHD (very high overlap), communication disorders, developmental coordination disorder, autism, anxiety and depressive disorders (secondary to school failure), and conduct problems in adolescence.
Differential Normal variation in academic attainment; lack of instruction or schooling disruption, which is common in Indian samples and is an explicit exclusion; intellectual disability (all domains low, not one); ADHD (performance varies with attention and improves with structure); neurocognitive disorder (skills were acquired and lost); psychosocial adversity; sensory impairment.

Motor Disorders and Tic Disorders F82, F98.4, F95.x

In plain language. Clumsiness that is out of proportion to age, repetitive purposeless movement, and tics.
  • Developmental Coordination Disorder. Acquisition and execution of coordinated motor skills is substantially below age expectation (clumsiness, slowness and inaccuracy in catching, using scissors, handwriting, cycling, sports). It interferes with daily living and academics, begins in the early developmental period, and is not explained by intellectual disability, visual impairment, or a neurological condition affecting movement (cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy).
  • Stereotypic Movement Disorder. Repetitive, seemingly driven and purposeless motor behaviour (hand shaking or waving, body rocking, head banging, self-biting, hitting own body). Onset in the early developmental period, interferes with activities or results in self-injury. Specify with or without self-injurious behaviour, and whether it is associated with a known medical or genetic condition or environmental factor (for example Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, intellectual disability, intrauterine alcohol exposure).
  • Tic disorders. A tic is a sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic motor movement or vocalisation. Tourette's Disorder: multiple motor tics and one or more vocal tics, both present at some time during the illness though not necessarily concurrently, persisting for more than 1 year since first tic onset, with onset before age 18. Persistent (Chronic) Motor or Vocal Tic Disorder: motor or vocal tics but not both, more than 1 year, onset before 18. Provisional Tic Disorder: single or multiple motor and/or vocal tics present for less than 1 year since first onset.
Numbers Tics typically begin between ages 4 and 6, peak in severity between 10 and 12, and decline through adolescence. Tourette's prevalence 3 to 8 per 1000 school-age children; male to female 2:1 to 4:1. Coprolalia occurs in only about 10 percent, which is the standard trap because popular culture treats it as the defining feature.
Comorbidity Tourette's with ADHD (about half) and OCD (roughly a third to a half) is the classic triad. Also anxiety, depression, disruptive behaviour, and learning problems. Developmental coordination disorder co-occurs with ADHD (the old "DAMP" concept), specific learning disorder and autism. Stereotypic movement disorder co-occurs with intellectual disability and autism.
Differential Tics versus stereotypies: tics are faster, non-rhythmic, preceded by a premonitory urge and can be briefly suppressed; stereotypies are rhythmic, prolonged, fixed, start earlier (before 3) and are not preceded by an urge. Tics versus compulsions: compulsions are performed in response to an obsession or a rule and aim to prevent something; complex tics can look identical, and the two coexist. Chorea (random, flowing, not suppressible), dystonia (sustained), myoclonus, seizures. Also substance-induced (stimulants) and other medical conditions (Huntington's, post-viral).

3. Schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders

This chapter is arranged along a gradient of psychopathology, from least to most: schizotypal personality disorder, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder. Everything in it is defined by five domains: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking (speech), grossly disorganised or abnormal motor behaviour including catatonia, and negative symptoms. Learn the five, then learn the durations, because duration is what separates almost every member of this chapter from its neighbour.

The duration ladder, the single most examined thing in the chapter

Brief psychotic disorder: at least 1 day, less than 1 month, with full return to premorbid functioning. Schizophreniform disorder: at least 1 month, less than 6 months. Schizophrenia: at least 6 months of total disturbance, including at least 1 month of active-phase symptoms. Delusional disorder: at least 1 month of delusions. So: one day, one month, six months. If a stem gives you three months of psychosis, the answer is schizophreniform, and the word "provisional" belongs in the answer if the person has not yet recovered.

Delusional Disorder F22

In plain language. One or more fixed false beliefs, for a month or more, and apart from what follows directly from the belief, the person is otherwise entirely normal. The person is not obviously odd, their speech is coherent, they hold down a job.
  • A. One or more delusions of 1 month or longer.
  • B. Criterion A for schizophrenia has never been met. If hallucinations occur they are not prominent and are related to the delusional theme (for example the sensation of insects in delusional parasitosis).
  • C. Apart from the impact of the delusion, functioning is not markedly impaired and behaviour is not obviously bizarre or odd.
  • D. If manic or major depressive episodes have occurred, they have been brief relative to the duration of the delusional periods.
  • E. Not attributable to a substance, another medical condition, or better explained by another disorder such as body dysmorphic disorder or OCD.
Subtypes Erotomanic (another person, usually of higher status, is in love with the person), grandiose, jealous (the "Othello syndrome"), persecutory (the commonest), somatic, mixed, unspecified. Also specify with bizarre content. Course specifiers after 1 year: first episode currently in acute episode / in partial remission / in full remission; multiple episodes; continuous.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence about 0.2 percent, so it is rare. Onset later than schizophrenia, typically middle to late adult life. The DSM-5 change worth naming: the requirement that delusions be non-bizarre was removed, and "with bizarre content" became a specifier instead. Shared delusional disorder (folie a deux) was also removed as a separate diagnosis; it is now coded as delusional disorder or under other specified schizophrenia spectrum disorder.
Comorbidity Obsessive-compulsive disorder and body dysmorphic disorder (especially where insight is absent), depressive disorders (often secondary to the delusion), and paranoid, schizoid and avoidant personality disorders, which often precede the onset.
Differential Schizophrenia (Criterion A met, functioning declines, negative symptoms). OCD with absent insight (the belief grew out of an obsession, and the person has compulsions). Body dysmorphic disorder with absent insight. Paranoid personality disorder (suspiciousness that never reaches delusional conviction, and is lifelong and trait-like). Substance or medication induced, and psychotic disorder due to another medical condition. Major depressive or bipolar disorder with mood-congruent psychotic features (the delusion only exists inside the mood episode).

Brief Psychotic Disorder F23

In plain language. Psychosis that arrives suddenly, lasts days or a few weeks, and then goes completely, leaving the person as they were.
  • A. Presence of one or more of: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour. At least one must be from the first three (delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech), so catatonia alone does not qualify.
  • B. Duration at least 1 day but less than 1 month, with eventual full return to premorbid level of functioning.
  • C. Not better explained by a depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features or another psychotic disorder, and not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
Specifiers With marked stressor(s) (this is the old brief reactive psychosis), without marked stressor(s), with peripartum onset (during pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum), and with catatonia. Severity rated on the 5 psychotic dimensions, 0 to 4.
Numbers Accounts for roughly 9 percent of first-onset psychosis in some series. Twice as common in females. Average age of onset in the mid thirties. Suicide risk is elevated, particularly in the acute phase and in younger patients.
Comorbidity Personality disorders, especially borderline, schizotypal, paranoid and histrionic, are frequent precursors.
Differential Schizophreniform disorder (a month or more). Substance-induced psychotic disorder, which is the leading practical mimic in a young patient, so ask about cannabis and stimulants and take a urine screen. Psychotic disorder due to another medical condition (delirium, epilepsy, thyrotoxicosis, autoimmune encephalitis). Malingering and factitious disorder. Culturally sanctioned response, such as trance or a possession state that is a normal part of a religious practice, which is explicitly excluded. Bipolar or depressive disorder with psychotic features. Personality disorder with transient stress-related paranoia, especially borderline, where the psychotic-like symptoms are brief, stress-triggered, and recur across a long history.

Schizophreniform Disorder F20.81

In plain language. Schizophrenia's symptom picture, but the clock has not run long enough yet. It is a provisional label you use while waiting.
  • A. Same as schizophrenia Criterion A: two or more of delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms, each present for a significant portion of a 1-month period, with at least one being delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech.
  • B. An episode lasting at least 1 month but less than 6 months. If you are diagnosing while the person is still ill, qualify as "provisional".
  • C. Schizoaffective and depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features ruled out. D. Not substance or medical.
  • Note that unlike schizophrenia, impaired functioning is not required.
Specifiers With good prognostic features requires at least two of: onset of prominent psychotic symptoms within 4 weeks of the first noticeable change in usual behaviour; confusion or perplexity; good premorbid social and occupational functioning; absence of blunted or flat affect. Otherwise, without good prognostic features. Also with catatonia.
Numbers Roughly a third recover within the 6 months and keep the diagnosis; about two-thirds progress to schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. Incidence in developed countries is about a fifth that of schizophrenia.
Comorbidity As for schizophrenia: substance use, depression, anxiety.
Differential Brief psychotic disorder (under 1 month). Schizophrenia (6 months or more, including prodrome and residual). Schizoaffective (a full mood episode runs for the majority of the illness). Substance-induced and medical causes.

Schizophrenia F20.9

In plain language. For at least six months the person has been drifting away from ordinary reality and ordinary functioning, and for at least one of those months they have actively had psychotic symptoms.
  • A. Two or more of the following, each present for a significant portion of time during a 1-month period (or less if successfully treated), and at least one must be (1), (2) or (3): (1) delusions; (2) hallucinations; (3) disorganised speech (frequent derailment or incoherence); (4) grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour; (5) negative symptoms (diminished emotional expression or avolition).
  • B. Level of functioning in work, interpersonal relations or self-care is markedly below the level achieved before onset (or, with childhood or adolescent onset, failure to achieve the expected level).
  • C. Continuous signs of the disturbance persist for at least 6 months, which must include at least 1 month of Criterion A (active-phase) symptoms, and may include periods of prodromal or residual symptoms, during which the disturbance may show only negative symptoms or two or more Criterion A symptoms in attenuated form.
  • D. Schizoaffective disorder and depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features have been ruled out because either no major mood episodes have occurred concurrently with the active phase, or, if they have, they have been present for a minority of the total duration of the active and residual periods.
  • E. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
  • F. If there is a history of autism spectrum disorder or a childhood-onset communication disorder, schizophrenia is diagnosed only if prominent delusions or hallucinations, plus the other required symptoms, are also present for at least 1 month.
Specifiers Course, only after 1 year of symptoms: first episode currently in acute episode / in partial remission / in full remission; multiple episodes (same three); continuous; unspecified. Also with catatonia. Severity is rated dimensionally 0 to 4 on each of the five psychotic domains. The classical subtypes (paranoid, disorganised or hebephrenic, catatonic, undifferentiated, residual) were deleted in DSM-5 because they had poor reliability, low diagnostic stability and no validity for treatment response. Naming that deletion, and why, is a stock question.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence about 0.3 to 0.7 percent. Onset typically early to mid twenties in men, late twenties in women, with a second smaller peak in women after 40. About 5 to 6 percent die by suicide, about 20 percent attempt it. Heritability roughly 80 percent; monozygotic concordance about 45 to 50 percent, dizygotic about 15 percent, one affected parent about 13 percent, both parents about 45 percent. Life expectancy is reduced by roughly 15 to 20 years, mostly cardiovascular. The DSM-5 change to name: DSM-IV allowed a single symptom if the delusion was bizarre or the hallucination was a running commentary or two voices conversing (the Schneiderian first-rank concession); DSM-5 removed that special treatment, so two symptoms are always required.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders are the big one, over half, and tobacco use is very high. Also obsessive-compulsive disorder and panic disorder at rates above the general population, schizotypal and paranoid personality disorders (often premorbid), depression, and medical comorbidity: metabolic syndrome, diabetes and cardiovascular disease, driven partly by antipsychotics.
Differential Schizoaffective disorder and mood disorder with psychotic features, both settled by the mood-episode arithmetic in Criterion D. Schizophreniform and brief psychotic, settled by duration. Delusional disorder (Criterion A never met, no functional decline). Schizotypal personality disorder (attenuated symptoms, no frank psychosis, lifelong pattern). Obsessive-compulsive disorder with absent insight and body dysmorphic disorder. PTSD (flashbacks and hypervigilance can be mistaken for hallucinations and paranoia, but they are trauma-cued). Autism (Criterion F). Substance-induced psychotic disorder and psychotic disorder due to another medical condition, including temporal lobe epilepsy, delirium, Wilson's disease, SLE, and NMDA-receptor encephalitis.
ICD-11 Requires 1 month of symptoms, not six, and does not require functional decline. So the same patient at week five can be schizophrenia under ICD-11 and schizophreniform under DSM-5-TR. That is a favourite comparison question.

Schizoaffective Disorder F25.x

In plain language. Psychosis and a full mood episode run alongside each other, but the psychosis has also stood alone, without the mood episode, for at least two weeks. That standalone stretch is the whole diagnosis.
  • A. An uninterrupted period of illness during which there is a major mood episode (major depressive or manic) concurrent with Criterion A of schizophrenia. If it is a major depressive episode, depressed mood must be one of the symptoms.
  • B. Delusions or hallucinations for 2 or more weeks in the absence of a major mood episode during the lifetime duration of the illness. This is the criterion that defines the disorder.
  • C. Symptoms that meet criteria for a major mood episode are present for the majority of the total duration of the active and residual portions of the illness.
  • D. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
Specifiers Bipolar type (a manic episode is part of the presentation; major depressive episodes may also occur) or depressive type (only major depressive episodes). Also with catatonia, course specifiers after 1 year, and dimensional severity ratings.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence about 0.3 percent, roughly a third that of schizophrenia. More common in women, driven by the depressive type. Prognosis sits between schizophrenia and mood disorder, better than schizophrenia and worse than bipolar disorder. Criterion C, the "majority of the total duration" rule, was changed in DSM-5 from a cross-sectional judgment to a lifetime one, so the diagnosis became longitudinal rather than a snapshot.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders, anxiety disorders, and general medical conditions at rates similar to schizophrenia.
Differential Two-sided, and this is a guaranteed question. Against schizophrenia: in schizophrenia mood episodes are present for a minority of the illness, in schizoaffective they are present for the majority. Against major depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features: there, the psychosis occurs only during mood episodes, so if you can find two weeks of delusions or hallucinations with no mood episode, it is schizoaffective. Also substance-induced psychotic disorder and psychotic disorder due to another medical condition.

Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder, and Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

  • Substance-induced: delusions and/or hallucinations that developed during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal or exposure to a medication capable of producing them, and are not better explained by an independent psychotic disorder. Evidence for independence would be: symptoms preceded the substance use, or persisted for a substantial period, about 1 month, after acute withdrawal ended. Does not occur exclusively during delirium. Specify onset during intoxication or during withdrawal. Common agents: alcohol, cannabis, amphetamine and other stimulants, cocaine, hallucinogens, inhalants, sedatives; medications: corticosteroids, anticholinergics, L-dopa, isoniazid, interferon.
  • Due to another medical condition: prominent hallucinations or delusions, with evidence from history, examination or labs that the disturbance is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition, not better explained by another mental disorder, not exclusively during delirium, and causing significant impairment. Specify with delusions or with hallucinations. Classic causes: temporal lobe epilepsy, stroke, brain tumour, traumatic brain injury, Huntington's, Parkinson's, multiple sclerosis, thyroid and parathyroid disease, Cushing's, SLE, B12 deficiency, autoimmune encephalitis, HIV, neurosyphilis.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders by definition, and very often a primary psychotic disorder as well, since cannabis and stimulant use are markedly elevated in people with schizophrenia. For the medical form, the comorbidity is the underlying disease itself plus the depression and anxiety that follow it.
Differential Delirium is the first thing to exclude, because if consciousness and attention fluctuate, the diagnosis is delirium and not a psychotic disorder. Then a primary psychotic disorder in someone who also happens to use substances, which is settled by timing and by the one-month post-withdrawal rule.

Catatonia F06.1, F20.81 etc.

In plain language. A syndrome of marked psychomotor disturbance, which in DSM-5 stopped being a type of schizophrenia and became something you can attach to many disorders.
  • Requires 3 or more of 12: stupor (no psychomotor activity, not actively relating to environment), catalepsy (passive induction of a posture held against gravity), waxy flexibility (slight even resistance to positioning by the examiner), mutism, negativism (opposition or no response to instructions or external stimuli), posturing (spontaneous and active maintenance of a posture against gravity), mannerism (odd caricature of normal actions), stereotypy (repetitive, abnormally frequent, non-goal-directed movements), agitation not influenced by external stimuli, grimacing, echolalia, echopraxia.
  • DSM-5 gives three ways to record it: catatonia associated with another mental disorder (used as a specifier for schizophrenia, schizoaffective, brief psychotic, schizophreniform, bipolar and depressive disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders), catatonic disorder due to another medical condition, and unspecified catatonia.
Numbers Historically framed as a schizophrenia subtype, but the majority of catatonia in practice occurs in mood disorders, particularly bipolar. Responds to benzodiazepines (lorazepam challenge) and to ECT.
Comorbidity Most commonly a mood disorder, particularly bipolar disorder, then schizophrenia spectrum disorders, autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability. Medically: encephalitis, autoimmune disease, metabolic derangement and drug toxicity.
Differential Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (fever, rigidity, autonomic instability, raised CK, following antipsychotic exposure) and malignant catatonia, which overlap dangerously. Non-convulsive status epilepticus, akinetic mutism, locked-in syndrome, severe Parkinsonism, and stupor from a medical or toxic cause.

4. Bipolar and related disorders

DSM-5 pulled the bipolar disorders out of the old "Mood Disorders" chapter and placed them between the psychotic disorders and the depressive disorders, to signal that they bridge the two in symptoms, family history and genetics. Everything here is built from three blocks: the manic episode, the hypomanic episode, and the major depressive episode.

The three episode definitions, learn these once

Manic Episode

  • A. A distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood AND abnormally and persistently increased goal-directed activity or energy, lasting at least 1 week and present most of the day, nearly every day (or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary). The addition of "increased activity or energy" to Criterion A is a DSM-5 change from DSM-IV, which required mood alone.
  • B. During the mood disturbance, 3 or more of the following (4 if the mood is only irritable), to a significant degree and representing a noticeable change from usual behaviour: inflated self-esteem or grandiosity; decreased need for sleep (feels rested after 3 hours, which is not the same as insomnia); more talkative than usual or pressure to keep talking; flight of ideas or subjective racing thoughts; distractibility; increase in goal-directed activity (social, occupational, sexual) or psychomotor agitation; excessive involvement in activities with a high potential for painful consequences (buying sprees, sexual indiscretion, foolish business investments). The mnemonic is DIG FAST: Distractibility, Indiscretion, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep deficit, Talkativeness.
  • C. The disturbance is severe enough to cause marked impairment in social or occupational functioning, or to necessitate hospitalisation, or there are psychotic features. Any one of these three makes it mania rather than hypomania.
  • D. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition. Note the DSM-5 rule: a full manic episode that emerges during antidepressant treatment or ECT and persists at a fully syndromal level beyond the physiological effect of that treatment counts as a manic episode, and therefore as bipolar I. DSM-IV excluded it.

Hypomanic Episode

  • Same Criterion A mood plus energy change, but lasting at least 4 consecutive days.
  • Same list of 7 symptoms, same 3 or 4 threshold.
  • The episode is associated with an unequivocal change in functioning that is uncharacteristic of the person when not symptomatic, and the change is observable by others.
  • The episode is not severe enough to cause marked impairment, does not require hospitalisation, and there are no psychotic features. If any of those three appear, it is by definition mania.

Major Depressive Episode

  • 5 or more of 9 symptoms during the same 2-week period, representing a change from previous functioning, and at least one must be (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest or pleasure.
  • The nine: (1) depressed mood most of the day nearly every day (in children and adolescents, can be irritable mood); (2) markedly diminished interest or pleasure in all or almost all activities; (3) significant weight loss or gain (more than 5 percent of body weight in a month) or change in appetite (in children, failure to make expected weight gain); (4) insomnia or hypersomnia; (5) psychomotor agitation or retardation, observable by others, not merely subjective; (6) fatigue or loss of energy; (7) feelings of worthlessness or excessive or inappropriate guilt, which may be delusional; (8) diminished ability to think or concentrate, or indecisiveness; (9) recurrent thoughts of death, recurrent suicidal ideation without a specific plan, a suicide attempt, or a specific plan. Mnemonic SIG E CAPS plus mood: Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicide.

Bipolar I Disorder F31.x

In plain language. One manic episode, ever, in a lifetime. That is the entire requirement. Depression is common but is not needed for the diagnosis.
  • Criteria have been met for at least one manic episode. The manic episode may have been preceded by and may be followed by hypomanic or major depressive episodes.
  • The occurrence of the manic and major depressive episode(s) is not better explained by schizoaffective disorder, schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, delusional disorder, or other specified or unspecified schizophrenia spectrum disorder.
Specifiers (these apply across bipolar and depressive disorders): with anxious distress (mild to severe, based on 2 to 5 symptoms); with mixed features (DSM-5 replaced the DSM-IV "mixed episode", which needed full criteria for both mania and depression simultaneously, with a much more usable specifier requiring 3 symptoms of the opposite pole); with rapid cycling (4 or more mood episodes in 12 months); with melancholic features; with atypical features (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, interpersonal rejection sensitivity); with mood-congruent or mood-incongruent psychotic features; with catatonia; with peripartum onset (during pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum, which is a much narrower window than the popular usage of "postpartum depression"); with seasonal pattern. Also specify current or most recent episode (manic, hypomanic, depressed, unspecified), severity, psychotic features, and remission status.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 0.6 percent; roughly equal in men and women, though rapid cycling is commoner in women. Mean age of onset about 18. Lifetime suicide risk is roughly 15 times the general population, and bipolar disorder may account for a quarter of all completed suicides. Mania lasting 1 week, hypomania 4 days: those two numbers are asked constantly.
Comorbidity Extremely high. Around three-quarters have a comorbid anxiety disorder (panic disorder, social anxiety, specific phobia); more than half have an alcohol use disorder; ADHD, oppositional defiant and conduct disorder in younger patients; borderline and antisocial personality disorders; migraine; metabolic syndrome.
Differential Bipolar II (never a full manic episode). Major depressive disorder (the trap: a person presenting depressed whose past hypomania has never been asked about; always screen). Generalised anxiety, panic, substance use. ADHD (chronic, not episodic, and no decreased need for sleep, and onset before 12). Borderline personality disorder, where mood shifts last hours and are reactive to interpersonal events, versus bipolar where episodes last days to weeks and are autonomous. Schizoaffective and schizophrenia. Substance-induced bipolar and related disorder (stimulants, steroids). Bipolar due to another medical condition (hyperthyroidism, Cushing's, multiple sclerosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury).

Bipolar II Disorder F31.81

In plain language. At least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, and never a full manic episode. It is not a milder illness, and DSM-5-TR says so explicitly, because the depressive burden is heavier and lasts longer.
  • A. Criteria met for at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode.
  • B. There has never been a manic episode.
  • C. Not better explained by a schizophrenia spectrum disorder.
  • D. The symptoms of depression, or the unpredictability caused by frequent alternation between periods of depression and hypomania, cause clinically significant distress or impairment. Note that the impairment must come from the depression or the alternation, because if the hypomania itself caused marked impairment it would be mania.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 0.8 percent. Mean age at onset in the mid twenties, slightly later than bipolar I. People with bipolar II spend roughly three times as long depressed as people with bipolar I. About 5 to 15 percent go on to have a manic episode, at which point the diagnosis changes to bipolar I permanently.
Comorbidity About 60 percent have three or more co-occurring disorders. Anxiety disorders (about 38 percent), substance use disorders (about 37 percent), eating disorders, especially binge-eating disorder, and personality disorders.
Differential Major depressive disorder, the commonest misdiagnosis, because patients present in the depressed phase and do not report hypomania as a problem. Cyclothymic disorder (never a full major depressive episode and never a full hypomanic episode meeting all criteria). Borderline personality disorder. ADHD. Substance-induced states.

Cyclothymic Disorder F34.0

In plain language. Two years of bobbing up and down, never quite reaching a full hypomanic or a full depressive episode, and never getting a clear two-month stretch of feeling normal.
  • A. For at least 2 years (1 year in children and adolescents), numerous periods with hypomanic symptoms that do not meet criteria for a hypomanic episode and numerous periods with depressive symptoms that do not meet criteria for a major depressive episode.
  • B. During that 2-year period, the hypomanic and depressive periods have been present for at least half the time, and the person has not been without the symptoms for more than 2 months at a time.
  • C. Criteria for a major depressive, manic or hypomanic episode have never been met.
  • D. Not better explained by a schizophrenia spectrum disorder. E. Not substance or medical. F. Clinically significant distress or impairment. Specify with anxious distress.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence 0.4 to 1 percent, equal in men and women, though women present for treatment more. Usual onset in adolescence or early adult life. Risk of progressing to bipolar I or II is roughly 15 to 50 percent.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders and sleep disorders. In children, ADHD is a frequent comorbidity.
Differential Bipolar II (full episodes have been met). Persistent depressive disorder (only depressive symptoms, no hypomanic periods). Borderline personality disorder, again, where the instability is interpersonally triggered. Rapid cycling bipolar. Substance-induced mood lability.

Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar and Related Disorder, and Bipolar Due to Another Medical Condition

  • A prominent and persistent disturbance in mood, elevated or irritable with or without depressed mood, that developed during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal or exposure to a medication capable of producing it, or that is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition.
  • Common substances: amphetamines and other stimulants, cocaine, phencyclidine, steroids. Common medical causes: Cushing's syndrome, hyperthyroidism, multiple sclerosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury.
  • Remember the DSM-5 rule again: if a fully syndromal manic episode persists beyond the physiological effect of the antidepressant or ECT that triggered it, it is counted as a manic episode and the person has bipolar I, not a substance-induced disorder.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders themselves, plus a primary bipolar disorder in a substantial proportion, since stimulant use is elevated in bipolar disorder. For the medical form, the endocrine or neurological disease and the depression that commonly follows it.
Differential Primary bipolar I or II disorder in someone who also uses substances, settled by whether mood symptoms predate use and whether they persist about a month beyond acute intoxication or withdrawal. Delirium. Substance-induced psychotic disorder where psychosis rather than mood predominates. Personality disorders with affective instability.

5. Depressive disorders

DSM-5 added two disorders to this chapter that carry a disproportionate share of exam questions because they are new: Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder and Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder. It also merged dysthymia and chronic major depression into Persistent Depressive Disorder, and removed the bereavement exclusion. Know those four changes and you have most of the chapter's marks.

Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder F34.81

In plain language. A child who is angry or irritable almost all of the time, in between frequent explosive outbursts that are far out of proportion to whatever set them off. It was created deliberately to stop children with chronic irritability being diagnosed as paediatric bipolar disorder, which was over-diagnosed in the 2000s in the United States.
  • A. Severe recurrent temper outbursts, verbal or behavioural, grossly out of proportion in intensity or duration to the situation or provocation.
  • B. The outbursts are inconsistent with developmental level.
  • C. Outbursts occur on average 3 or more times per week.
  • D. The mood between outbursts is persistently irritable or angry most of the day, nearly every day, and is observable by others. This inter-episode criterion is what separates it from an ordinary tantrum problem.
  • E. Criteria A to D have been present for 12 or more months, without a period of 3 or more consecutive months free of all of them.
  • F. Present in at least 2 of 3 settings (home, school, with peers) and severe in at least one.
  • G. Diagnosis should not be made for the first time before age 6 or after age 18.
  • H. By history or observation, the age at onset of criteria A to E is before 10 years.
  • I. There has never been a distinct period lasting more than 1 day during which the full symptom criteria, except duration, for a manic or hypomanic episode have been met.
  • J. The behaviours do not occur exclusively during a major depressive episode and are not better explained by another mental disorder such as autism, PTSD, separation anxiety, or persistent depressive disorder.
  • K. Not attributable to a substance or another medical or neurological condition.
Numbers The four numbers to hold: outbursts 3 or more times a week, duration 12 months, diagnose only between ages 6 and 18, onset before 10. 6-month to 1-year prevalence roughly 2 to 5 percent, commoner in males and in school-age children.
Comorbidity Very high. ADHD, oppositional defiant disorder, anxiety disorders and major depressive disorder. Longitudinally, these children tend to develop unipolar depressive and anxiety disorders in adulthood, not bipolar disorder, which is the evidence base the diagnosis was built on.
Differential Bipolar disorder is the point of the whole diagnosis: bipolar is episodic with a distinct period of elevated mood and decreased need for sleep, DMDD is persistent and non-episodic. Oppositional defiant disorder shares the irritability, but DMDD requires the severe outbursts and the persistent between-outburst mood; DSM-5 rules that if a child meets criteria for both, only DMDD is diagnosed. Intermittent explosive disorder requires only 3 months and has no persistent inter-episode irritability. ADHD, autism, PTSD, separation anxiety, where the irritability is explained by the other condition.
ICD-11 Has no DMDD. The same child is coded as oppositional defiant disorder with chronic irritability-anger.

Major Depressive Disorder F32.x single, F33.x recurrent

In plain language. At least two weeks in which the person is down or has lost all interest, plus enough of the other classic symptoms, and this is a change from how they were.
  • A. Five or more of the nine symptoms of a major depressive episode over the same 2-week period, one of which is depressed mood or anhedonia (list given in the bipolar chapter above).
  • B. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • C. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
  • D. Not better explained by a schizophrenia spectrum or other psychotic disorder.
  • E. There has never been a manic or hypomanic episode.
Specifiers Single or recurrent episode; mild, moderate, severe; with psychotic features; in partial or full remission. Plus the cross-cutting set: with anxious distress, with mixed features, with melancholic features (loss of pleasure in all activities, lack of reactivity, distinct quality of mood, early morning awakening, marked psychomotor change, excessive guilt, worse in the morning), with atypical features, with mood-congruent or mood-incongruent psychotic features, with catatonia, with peripartum onset, with seasonal pattern.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 7 percent in the United States; lifetime around 15 to 20 percent. Female to male about 2:1, a ratio that appears at puberty. Peak onset in the twenties. About two-thirds of episodes remit with treatment; recurrence risk after one episode about 50 percent, after two about 70 percent, after three about 90 percent.
Comorbidity Anxiety disorders (panic and generalised anxiety are commonest), substance use disorders, borderline personality disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, eating disorders, and medical illness (post-stroke, post-MI, diabetes, hypothyroidism, cancer, Parkinson's).
Differential Bipolar disorders, always screen for past hypomania. Persistent depressive disorder (2 years, milder). Adjustment disorder with depressed mood (identifiable stressor, does not meet full MDE criteria). Bereavement and prolonged grief disorder. Substance-induced depressive disorder (alcohol, sedatives, withdrawal from stimulants; medications: interferon, isotretinoin, some antihypertensives, corticosteroids). Depressive disorder due to another medical condition (hypothyroidism, anaemia, Cushing's, Parkinson's, stroke). Major neurocognitive disorder versus pseudodementia in an older adult. ADHD (distractibility without mood change). Demoralisation and normal sadness.
DSM-5 change The bereavement exclusion was removed. In DSM-IV you could not diagnose MDD within 2 months of a death unless there were specific features. DSM-5 dropped that, on the reasoning that grief and depression can coexist and that withholding a diagnosis withheld treatment; a long footnote in the criteria set explains how to distinguish grief (waves of emptiness and yearning, self-esteem preserved, thoughts of joining the deceased) from a major depressive episode (persistent depressed mood, worthlessness, self-critical rumination, suicidal ideation about ending one's own life because of worthlessness). That footnote, and the removal itself, are heavily examined.

Persistent Depressive Disorder F34.1

In plain language. Depression that has been there for at least two years, most days, at whatever intensity. DSM-5 merged DSM-IV's dysthymic disorder and chronic major depressive disorder into this one label; DSM-5-TR restored the name "dysthymia" as an alternative in the title text.
  • A. Depressed mood for most of the day, for more days than not, for at least 2 years (1 year in children and adolescents, where mood may be irritable).
  • B. While depressed, 2 or more of six: poor appetite or overeating; insomnia or hypersomnia; low energy or fatigue; low self-esteem; poor concentration or difficulty making decisions; feelings of hopelessness.
  • C. During the 2 years, the person has never been without the symptoms in Criteria A and B for more than 2 months at a time.
  • D. Criteria for a major depressive disorder may be continuously present for 2 years.
  • E. There has never been a manic or hypomanic episode, and criteria for cyclothymic disorder have never been met.
  • F. Not better explained by a persistent schizophrenia spectrum disorder. G. Not substance or medical. H. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
Specifiers The distinctive ones: with pure dysthymic syndrome (full MDE criteria not met in the preceding 2 years), with persistent major depressive episode (full criteria met throughout the preceding 2 years), with intermittent major depressive episodes, with current episode, and with intermittent major depressive episodes, without current episode. Also early onset (before 21) or late onset (at 21 or after), plus anxious distress, atypical features, remission and severity.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 0.5 percent for persistent depressive disorder and about 1.5 percent for chronic major depressive disorder. Early onset before 21 predicts a worse course and more personality-disorder comorbidity.
Comorbidity Compared with MDD, higher rates of anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, and personality disorders, especially cluster B and cluster C. "Double depression", a major depressive episode superimposed on dysthymia, is the classic pattern and is now captured by the intermittent-episodes specifier.
Differential Major depressive disorder (episodic rather than 2 years continuous). Cyclothymia (hypomanic periods present). Bipolar II. Psychotic disorders with residual depression. Personality disorders, which may be diagnosed alongside. Depressive disorder due to another medical condition.

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder F32.81

In plain language. In the week before the period, mood collapses in a specific patterned way, it lifts within a few days of bleeding starting, and it is gone in the week after. Moved from the DSM-IV appendix into the main text in DSM-5, which is exactly what gets asked.
  • A. In the majority of menstrual cycles, at least 5 symptoms present in the final week before the onset of menses, starting to improve within a few days after the onset of menses, and becoming minimal or absent in the week post-menses.
  • B. One or more of these four must be present: marked affective lability; marked irritability or anger or increased interpersonal conflicts; marked depressed mood, hopelessness or self-deprecating thoughts; marked anxiety, tension, or feelings of being keyed up or on edge.
  • C. One or more of these seven must additionally be present, to reach a total of five: decreased interest in usual activities; subjective difficulty concentrating; lethargy, easy fatigability, marked lack of energy; marked change in appetite, overeating, or specific food cravings; hypersomnia or insomnia; a sense of being overwhelmed or out of control; physical symptoms such as breast tenderness or swelling, joint or muscle pain, bloating or weight gain.
  • D. Significant distress or interference with work, school, usual social activities or relationships. E. Not merely an exacerbation of another disorder. F. Criterion A should be confirmed by prospective daily ratings during at least 2 symptomatic cycles (a provisional diagnosis may be made before that). G. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
Numbers 12-month prevalence between 1.8 and 5.8 percent of menstruating women when strict prospective ratings are used, which is far below the rate of self-reported premenstrual complaints. Symptoms cease after menopause.
Comorbidity Major depressive disorder is the commonest, plus anxiety disorders and bipolar disorder.
Differential Premenstrual syndrome, which is far more common, needs no affective symptom, and is not as impairing. Premenstrual exacerbation of an existing depressive, bipolar or anxiety disorder, which is Criterion E, and is distinguished by symptoms being present throughout the cycle and simply worsening premenstrually. Dysmenorrhoea (pain starting with menses, not before). Hormonal treatment effects, and thyroid disease.

Substance/Medication-Induced Depressive Disorder, and Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

  • A prominent and persistent disturbance in mood, predominantly depressed mood or markedly diminished interest or pleasure, that developed during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal or medication exposure, or that is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition.
  • Common substances: alcohol, sedative-hypnotics and anxiolytics (during use), stimulants and cocaine (during withdrawal), opioids. Medications: corticosteroids, interferon-alpha, isotretinoin, reserpine and older antihypertensives, some hormonal contraceptives, varenicline.
  • Common medical causes: hypothyroidism, Cushing's disease, Parkinson's disease, stroke (particularly left frontal), traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, Huntington's, pancreatic carcinoma, vitamin B12 deficiency, obstructive sleep apnoea.
Comorbidity The substance use disorder itself, and, for the medical form, the physical illness plus the anxiety, insomnia and pain conditions that travel with it. A primary depressive disorder frequently coexists, and having one raises the risk of the other.
Differential Primary major depressive disorder in someone who also has a medical illness or uses substances. The tests are temporal (did it start with the exposure), dose-related, and whether it resolves when the exposure ends, plus whether the symptom picture is atypical for primary depression.
The two other-specified categories worth naming

Other Specified Depressive Disorder covers, among others: recurrent brief depression (depressed mood plus 4 other symptoms for 2 to 13 days, at least once a month for 12 months, never meeting criteria for another disorder), short-duration depressive episode (4 to 13 days), and depressive episode with insufficient symptoms. Examiners like recurrent brief depression because the duration is so distinctive.

6. Anxiety disorders

DSM-5 broke the old anxiety chapter into three: anxiety disorders proper, obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, and trauma and stressor-related disorders. Inside the anxiety chapter, the big structural changes were that panic disorder and agoraphobia were unlinked into two separate diagnoses (DSM-IV had "panic disorder with agoraphobia" and "agoraphobia without history of panic"), that separation anxiety disorder and selective mutism moved in from the childhood chapter and are no longer restricted to children, and that panic attack became a specifier usable across the whole manual. The chapter is ordered developmentally, by typical age of onset.

The 6-month rule

Specific phobia, social anxiety disorder, agoraphobia and generalised anxiety disorder all require 6 months or more. In DSM-IV that 6-month requirement applied only to people under 18; DSM-5 extended it to all ages, to stop transient fears being diagnosed. Panic disorder is the exception: it needs 1 month of worry or behaviour change after an attack. Separation anxiety needs 4 weeks in children and adolescents, 6 months or more in adults. Selective mutism needs 1 month, not counting the first month of school.

Separation Anxiety Disorder F93.0

In plain language. Fear of being away from the people you are attached to, that is far beyond what is normal for your age, and that shapes what you will and will not do.
  • A. Developmentally inappropriate and excessive fear or anxiety concerning separation from attachment figures, with at least 3 of 8: recurrent excessive distress when separation is anticipated or occurs; persistent excessive worry about losing attachment figures or about harm befalling them; persistent excessive worry about an untoward event causing separation (getting lost, being kidnapped, having an accident, becoming ill); persistent reluctance or refusal to go out, away from home, to school, to work, because of fear of separation; persistent and excessive fear of or reluctance about being alone or without attachment figures; persistent reluctance or refusal to sleep away from home or to sleep without being near a major attachment figure; repeated nightmares involving the theme of separation; repeated complaints of physical symptoms (headaches, stomach aches, nausea, vomiting) when separation from attachment figures occurs or is anticipated.
  • B. Duration: at least 4 weeks in children and adolescents, typically 6 months or more in adults.
  • C. Clinically significant distress or impairment. D. Not better explained by another disorder such as autism (refusal to leave home because of resistance to change), psychotic disorders (delusions about separation), agoraphobia (refusal to go out without a trusted companion), generalised anxiety disorder (worry about ill health befalling others), or illness anxiety disorder.
Numbers The most prevalent anxiety disorder in children under 12, about 4 percent in children, about 1.6 percent in adolescents, and 0.9 to 1.9 percent in adults. DSM-5's change: onset before age 18 is no longer required, so adults can be diagnosed for the first time.
Comorbidity In children: generalised anxiety disorder and specific phobia. In adults: specific phobia, PTSD, panic disorder, generalised anxiety disorder, social anxiety, agoraphobia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and personality disorders.
Differential Agoraphobia (fear of being unable to escape or get help, not fear of losing the attachment figure). Generalised anxiety disorder (worry is broad, not focused on separation). Autism. Conduct disorder (truancy without fear of separation, so the child leaves home rather than refusing to). School refusal is a behaviour, not a diagnosis, and may arise from separation anxiety, social anxiety, depression, or bullying, which examiners like to test.

Selective Mutism F94.0

In plain language. A child who talks normally at home says nothing at school. It is not that they cannot speak, it is that in specific social settings they do not.
  • A. Consistent failure to speak in specific social situations where there is an expectation for speaking (for example school), despite speaking in other situations.
  • B. Interferes with educational or occupational achievement or with social communication.
  • C. Duration at least 1 month, not limited to the first month of school.
  • D. The failure is not attributable to a lack of knowledge of, or comfort with, the spoken language required (which matters in multilingual Indian classrooms).
  • E. Not better explained by a communication disorder, and does not occur exclusively during autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder.
Numbers Rare, prevalence 0.03 to 1 percent, and does not vary much by sex or ethnicity. Onset usually before age 5, though it often comes to attention only on entering school.
Comorbidity Social anxiety disorder is by far the commonest and many researchers regard selective mutism as a variant of it. Also separation anxiety disorder, specific phobia, oppositional behaviour confined to the mute settings, and communication delays.
Differential Communication disorders (the difficulty is not situation-specific). Autism. Intellectual disability. Social anxiety disorder (the child may speak, with distress). Normal shyness and the silent period of a child newly immersed in a second language, which is explicitly excluded by Criterion D.

Specific Phobia F40.2xx

In plain language. One thing, reliably terrifying, avoided or endured with dread, for at least six months, out of proportion to the real danger.
  • A. Marked fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation. In children this may be expressed by crying, tantrums, freezing or clinging.
  • B. The phobic object or situation almost always provokes immediate fear or anxiety.
  • C. It is actively avoided or endured with intense fear or anxiety.
  • D. The fear is out of proportion to the actual danger posed, and to the sociocultural context.
  • E. Persistent, typically lasting 6 months or more. F. Clinically significant distress or impairment. G. Not better explained by another disorder.
  • DSM-5 removed the requirement that the person recognise the fear as excessive or unreasonable; the clinician now judges proportionality. That change also appears in social anxiety disorder and agoraphobia.
Specifiers Code by stimulus: animal; natural environment (heights, storms, water); blood-injection-injury (needles, other medical procedures); situational (aeroplanes, lifts, enclosed places); other (choking, vomiting, in children loud sounds or costumed characters).
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 7 to 9 percent, the commonest anxiety disorder. Female to male about 2:1. Median onset between ages 7 and 11. The blood-injection-injury type is the exception you must be able to name: it produces a diphasic vasovagal response, with an initial rise then a sharp fall in heart rate and blood pressure and frequent fainting, whereas every other phobia produces sympathetic arousal only. It also has the strongest family loading, roughly two-thirds of sufferers have an affected relative, and it is treated with applied tension rather than pure relaxation.
Comorbidity Most people with one specific phobia have several (the average is three). Comorbid with other anxiety disorders, depression, substance use, and somatic symptom disorder. Specific phobia commonly comes first and predicts later depression.
Differential Agoraphobia (fear of two or more of the five agoraphobic situations, and the fear is of not escaping or not getting help). Social anxiety disorder (fear of scrutiny). Separation anxiety. Panic disorder (attacks are unexpected, not cued). OCD (avoidance is driven by an obsession, for example contamination). PTSD (avoidance follows a traumatic event). Illness anxiety disorder. Normal fear, which is proportionate and non-impairing.

Social Anxiety Disorder (Social Phobia) F40.10

In plain language. Fear of being watched and judged, and of doing something humiliating in front of others, that lasts six months and shrinks the person's life.
  • A. Marked fear or anxiety about one or more social situations in which the person is exposed to possible scrutiny by others: social interactions (conversation, meeting unfamiliar people), being observed (eating, drinking), and performing in front of others. In children, the anxiety must occur in peer settings and not just during interactions with adults.
  • B. The person fears they will act in a way, or show anxiety symptoms, that will be negatively evaluated, that is, will be humiliating or embarrassing, will lead to rejection, or will offend others.
  • C. The social situations almost always provoke fear or anxiety. In children this may appear as crying, tantrums, freezing, clinging, shrinking or failing to speak.
  • D. Avoided or endured with intense fear. E. Out of proportion to the actual threat and to the sociocultural context. F. Persistent, typically 6 months or more. G. Significant distress or impairment. H, I, J. Not due to a substance or medical condition, not better explained by another mental disorder, and if another medical condition is present (Parkinson's, obesity, disfigurement from burns or injury) the fear is clearly unrelated or excessive.
Specifier Performance only, where the fear is restricted to speaking or performing in public. DSM-5 replaced the DSM-IV "generalized" specifier with this narrower "performance only" one, because the generalised subtype could not be reliably drawn.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 7 percent in the United States, lower in much of Asia (0.5 to 2 percent). Slightly more common in females in community samples, roughly equal in clinical samples. Median onset age 13; about 75 percent onset between 8 and 15.
Comorbidity Other anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder (usually later, and social anxiety typically precedes it), substance use disorder, especially alcohol, used as self-medication, body dysmorphic disorder, and avoidant personality disorder, which overlaps so heavily that many argue they are the same construct at different severities.
Differential Normal shyness, which is a common trait and only becomes a disorder with impairment. Agoraphobia (the person is comforted by a companion, in social anxiety a companion often makes it worse). Panic disorder (unexpected attacks). Body dysmorphic disorder (fear centres on a perceived physical defect). Autism (social communication skills are deficient, not merely inhibited by anxiety). Major depressive disorder (social withdrawal from anhedonia). Avoidant personality disorder (broader, earlier, more pervasive; often diagnosed together). Taijin kyofusho, the Japanese variant where the fear is of offending or embarrassing others with one's gaze, body odour or blushing, is the standard culture question.

Panic Disorder F41.0

In plain language. Repeated attacks of terror that come out of nowhere and peak within minutes, followed by at least a month of dreading the next one or rearranging life to avoid it.
  • A. Recurrent unexpected panic attacks. A panic attack is an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort that reaches a peak within minutes, during which 4 or more of 13 symptoms occur: palpitations or accelerated heart rate; sweating; trembling or shaking; sensations of shortness of breath or smothering; feelings of choking; chest pain or discomfort; nausea or abdominal distress; feeling dizzy, unsteady, light-headed or faint; chills or heat sensations; paraesthesias; derealisation or depersonalisation; fear of losing control or going crazy; fear of dying. Culture-specific symptoms such as tinnitus, neck soreness, headache or uncontrollable screaming or crying may be seen but do not count toward the four.
  • B. At least one attack has been followed by 1 month or more of one or both: persistent concern or worry about additional attacks or their consequences (losing control, having a heart attack, going crazy); and/or a significant maladaptive change in behaviour related to the attacks (avoiding exercise or unfamiliar situations).
  • C. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition (hyperthyroidism, cardiopulmonary disorders).
  • D. Not better explained by another mental disorder, where the attacks would be expected and cued: social anxiety (social situations), specific phobia (the phobic object), OCD (obsessions), PTSD (trauma reminders), separation anxiety (separation).
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 2 to 3 percent in adults and adolescents. Female to male about 2:1. Median onset about age 20 to 24, uncommon before 14 and after 45. A limited-symptom attack has fewer than 4 symptoms. Nocturnal panic attacks, waking from sleep in a panic, occur in a substantial minority and are a favourite MCQ. Panic attack is not a disorder: it is a specifier that can be attached to any DSM-5 diagnosis, which was the key DSM-5 change alongside the unlinking from agoraphobia.
Comorbidity Agoraphobia (very high, and now coded as two diagnoses), other anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, alcohol use disorder, and medical conditions: mitral valve prolapse, thyroid disease, asthma, COPD, irritable bowel syndrome. Suicidal ideation and attempts are elevated.
Differential Anxiety disorder due to another medical condition: hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, phaeochromocytoma, vestibular dysfunction, seizure disorders, cardiopulmonary conditions. Substance-induced: caffeine, cannabis, stimulants, alcohol withdrawal. Other anxiety disorders with cued attacks, which is Criterion D. Illness anxiety disorder. PTSD. The clinical rule to state in a written answer: unexpectedness is the defining feature, so ask "was there a trigger?".

Agoraphobia F40.00

In plain language. Fear of situations you might not be able to get out of, or get help in, if something goes wrong. Not just fear of open spaces, which is the common misreading.
  • A. Marked fear or anxiety about 2 or more of 5 situations: using public transportation; being in open spaces (car parks, marketplaces, bridges); being in enclosed places (shops, theatres, cinemas); standing in line or being in a crowd; being outside of the home alone. The requirement of two or more is a DSM-5 change and is heavily examined.
  • B. The person fears or avoids these situations because of thoughts that escape might be difficult or help might not be available in the event of developing panic-like symptoms or other incapacitating or embarrassing symptoms (falling in the elderly, incontinence).
  • C. The situations almost always provoke fear or anxiety. D. Actively avoided, require the presence of a companion, or are endured with intense fear. E. Out of proportion to the actual danger and the sociocultural context. F. Persistent, typically 6 months or more. G. Significant distress or impairment. H. If another medical condition is present, the fear is clearly excessive. I. Not better explained by another mental disorder.
  • DSM-5 makes it explicit: agoraphobia is diagnosed irrespective of the presence of panic disorder, and if the person meets criteria for both, both diagnoses are given.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 1.7 percent in adolescents and adults. Female to male about 2:1. Mean onset about 17 years, with a second peak after 40. About 30 to 50 percent have panic disorder first.
Comorbidity Other anxiety disorders (specific phobia, panic disorder, social anxiety, generalised anxiety), depressive disorders, PTSD, and alcohol use disorder.
Differential Specific phobia, situational type: if only one of the five situations is feared, and the fear is not about escape or help, it is specific phobia. Social anxiety disorder (fear of scrutiny, and a companion does not help). Separation anxiety (fear of being away from the attachment figure, and the person is fine outside the home if the figure is present). Major depressive disorder with apathy and withdrawal. Psychotic disorders with persecutory avoidance. Realistic avoidance due to a medical condition such as inflammatory bowel disease or Parkinson's, where the concern is proportionate.

Generalised Anxiety Disorder F41.1

In plain language. Six months of worrying about many everyday things, that the person cannot switch off, plus the physical toll of that worrying.
  • A. Excessive anxiety and worry (apprehensive expectation), occurring more days than not for at least 6 months, about a number of events or activities such as work or school performance.
  • B. The person finds it difficult to control the worry.
  • C. The anxiety and worry are associated with 3 or more of 6 symptoms, with at least some present more days than not for the past 6 months (only 1 item is required in children): restlessness or feeling keyed up or on edge; being easily fatigued; difficulty concentrating or mind going blank; irritability; muscle tension; sleep disturbance. The mnemonic is WATCHERS or, more simply, remember that muscle tension is the item that best discriminates GAD from other anxiety disorders.
  • D. Significant distress or impairment. E. Not substance or medical (hyperthyroidism). F. Not better explained by another mental disorder.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 2.9 percent in adults, 0.9 percent in adolescents. Female to male about 2:1. Median onset age 30, later than the other anxiety disorders, and onset in childhood or adolescence is unusual. Course is chronic and fluctuating with low full-remission rates.
Comorbidity Major depressive disorder is the commonest and the overlap is so large that some argue for a combined "distress" construct. Also other anxiety disorders, persistent depressive disorder, substance use, somatic symptom disorder, and the physical conditions worry drives, including irritable bowel syndrome, headaches and insomnia.
Differential Non-pathological anxiety, distinguished by controllability, breadth, impairment and physical symptoms. Anxiety disorder due to another medical condition, above all hyperthyroidism. Substance-induced, especially caffeine and stimulant use, and withdrawal states. Social anxiety, OCD, separation anxiety, illness anxiety, anorexia nervosa, PTSD, adjustment disorder, in each of which the worry is confined to a specific theme: scrutiny, obsessions, separation, illness, weight, the trauma, the stressor. Depressive disorders, where rumination is about the past rather than apprehension about the future.

Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder, and Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

  • Panic attacks or anxiety predominate, developed during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal or medication exposure, or are the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition, and are not better explained by an independent anxiety disorder, and do not occur exclusively during delirium.
  • Substances: caffeine (the classic), cannabis, cocaine and amphetamines, hallucinogens, and withdrawal from alcohol, sedatives and opioids. Medications: bronchodilators, thyroid hormone, corticosteroids, decongestants, some antidepressants early in treatment.
  • Medical conditions: hyperthyroidism, phaeochromocytoma, hypoglycaemia, hyperparathyroidism, cardiac arrhythmias, congestive heart failure, pulmonary embolism, COPD and asthma, vestibular dysfunction, seizure disorders.
Comorbidity The substance use disorder itself, plus primary anxiety and depressive disorders, which are common and often predate the exposure. For the medical form, the underlying disease and the depression that follows it.
Differential Primary anxiety disorders in someone who also uses substances or has a medical illness, settled by whether the anxiety predates the exposure and whether it persists about a month past acute withdrawal. Delirium, which excludes the diagnosis. Panic disorder, where attacks are unexpected and not tied to an exposure. Illness anxiety disorder, where the worry is about having a disease rather than a direct physiological effect.

7. Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders

A new chapter in DSM-5, created because these conditions share repetitive thoughts and behaviours, family loading, neurocircuitry (the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop) and treatment response. Hoarding disorder and excoriation disorder are brand new diagnoses in DSM-5, body dysmorphic disorder moved here from somatoform disorders, and trichotillomania moved here from impulse-control disorders. Every disorder in the chapter carries an insight specifier: with good or fair insight, with poor insight, with absent insight or delusional beliefs. That specifier is the answer to "how does DSM-5 handle a patient whose obsession has become a delusion?": you do not add a psychotic diagnosis, you use the absent-insight specifier.

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder F42.2

In plain language. Thoughts that force their way in and will not leave, and acts the person feels driven to perform to make the thoughts tolerable, eating at least an hour a day.
  • A. Presence of obsessions, compulsions, or both. Obsessions are recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges or images that are experienced, at some time during the disturbance, as intrusive and unwanted, and that in most people cause marked anxiety or distress; the person attempts to ignore or suppress them or to neutralise them with some other thought or action. Compulsions are repetitive behaviours (hand washing, ordering, checking) or mental acts (praying, counting, repeating words silently) that the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rules that must be applied rigidly; they are aimed at preventing or reducing anxiety or some dreaded event, but they are not connected in a realistic way to what they are meant to neutralise, or are clearly excessive.
  • B. The obsessions or compulsions are time-consuming (more than 1 hour per day) or cause clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • C. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition. D. Not better explained by another mental disorder (see the differential, which DSM lists at unusual length here).
Specifiers Insight: good or fair / poor / absent or delusional. Plus tic-related, that is, the individual has a current or past history of a tic disorder, which marks out a distinct subtype with earlier onset, male predominance, more symmetry and ordering symptoms, and a better response to antipsychotic augmentation.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 1.2 percent. Slightly more common in adult women, but males predominate in childhood onset. Mean age at onset 19.5, with about 25 percent by age 14; onset after 35 is unusual. Untreated the course is chronic. Common symptom dimensions: contamination and cleaning; harm obsessions and checking; symmetry, ordering and counting; forbidden or taboo thoughts (aggressive, sexual, religious) with mental rituals.
Comorbidity Up to 76 percent have an anxiety disorder, and up to 63 percent a depressive or bipolar disorder, with major depressive disorder commonest. About 30 percent have a lifetime tic disorder. Also obsessive-compulsive personality disorder in roughly a quarter to a third, body dysmorphic disorder, trichotillomania, hoarding, and eating disorders. In children, the sudden-onset presentation after streptococcal infection (PANDAS, now often subsumed under PANS) is a stock question.
Differential Generalised anxiety disorder: worries there are about real-life concerns and are ego-syntonic, obsessions are intrusive, often senseless, and ego-dystonic. Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder: a lifelong trait pattern of perfectionism and control, no true obsessions or compulsions, and it is ego-syntonic, so the person sees nothing wrong. This pair is the most reliably examined distinction in the chapter. Body dysmorphic disorder (thoughts confined to appearance). Hoarding disorder (no true obsession; distress arises from discarding). Trichotillomania and excoriation (no obsession precedes the act). Eating disorders (thoughts confined to food and weight). Tics and stereotyped movements (no preceding obsession, and there is a premonitory sensory urge instead). Psychotic disorders (use the absent-insight specifier rather than adding schizophrenia, unless there are other psychotic features). Major depressive disorder (ruminations are mood-congruent and not linked to compulsions).

Body Dysmorphic Disorder F45.22

In plain language. The person is convinced some part of how they look is ugly or wrong, other people cannot see it, and they spend hours checking, camouflaging or seeking reassurance.
  • A. Preoccupation with one or more perceived defects or flaws in physical appearance that are not observable or appear slight to others.
  • B. At some point during the course, the person has performed repetitive behaviours (mirror checking, excessive grooming, skin picking, reassurance seeking) or mental acts (comparing their appearance with that of others) in response to the appearance concerns. This criterion was added in DSM-5.
  • C. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • D. The appearance preoccupation is not better explained by concerns with body fat or weight in a person whose symptoms meet criteria for an eating disorder.
Specifiers With muscle dysmorphia (the belief that one's build is too small or insufficiently muscular; occurs mostly in males and is the specifier most often asked about), plus the insight specifier. Around a third have absent insight or delusional beliefs.
Numbers Point prevalence about 2.4 percent in the United States, 9 to 15 percent in dermatology patients and 7 to 8 percent in cosmetic-surgery patients. Mean age at onset 16 to 17, median 15, with two-thirds onset before 18. Roughly equal in men and women. Suicidal ideation is reported in about 80 percent and attempts in about a quarter, far higher than in OCD, which is a discriminating fact.
Comorbidity Major depressive disorder is commonest and usually starts after BDD. Also social anxiety disorder, OCD, substance use disorders (particularly in muscle dysmorphia, with anabolic steroid use), and avoidant personality disorder.
Differential Normal appearance concerns and clearly visible defects (which do not qualify). Eating disorders, which is Criterion D. Obsessive-compulsive disorder (the preoccupation is not restricted to appearance). Social anxiety disorder (fear of scrutiny in general, not because of a specific perceived defect). Major depressive disorder (poor self-image without the specific preoccupation and repetitive behaviours). Excoriation disorder (picking without the appearance-defect belief; if picking is to fix a perceived flaw, it is BDD). Gender dysphoria, olfactory reference syndrome (in DSM-5-TR handled under other specified obsessive-compulsive and related disorder; ICD-11 has it as a full diagnosis).

Hoarding Disorder F42.3

In plain language. Getting rid of things feels intolerable, so possessions build up until the rooms cannot be used for what rooms are for. New in DSM-5.
  • A. Persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of their actual value.
  • B. The difficulty is due to a perceived need to save the items and to distress associated with discarding them.
  • C. The difficulty results in the accumulation of possessions that congest and clutter active living areas and substantially compromise their intended use. If living areas are uncluttered, it is only because of the interventions of third parties such as family members, cleaners or authorities.
  • D. Clinically significant distress or impairment, including maintaining a safe environment for self and others.
  • E. Not attributable to another medical condition (brain injury, cerebrovascular disease, Prader-Willi syndrome). F. Not better explained by another mental disorder (obsessions in OCD, decreased energy in major depressive disorder, delusions in schizophrenia, cognitive deficits in major neurocognitive disorder, restricted interests in autism).
Specifiers With excessive acquisition (present in about 80 to 90 percent, either buying or acquiring free items), plus the insight specifier. Insight is characteristically poor, and many present only under pressure from family or housing authorities.
Numbers Prevalence roughly 2 to 6 percent. Symptoms typically begin between ages 11 and 15, become interfering by the mid twenties and clinically impairing by the mid thirties, and severity increases with each decade of life. Prevalence is roughly three times as high in adults aged 55 to 94 as in those aged 34 to 44. Roughly equal by sex in epidemiological samples, though clinical samples are mostly female.
Comorbidity About 75 percent have a comorbid mood or anxiety disorder, with major depressive disorder in around half. OCD in about 20 percent. ADHD, inattentive presentation, in about 30 percent.
Differential OCD, the key one: hoarding in OCD is driven by a classic obsession (contamination fear, fear of harm from discarding, incompleteness), is usually unwanted and distressing rather than pleasurable, and there is no excessive acquisition driven by desire. Major depressive disorder (clutter accumulates from psychomotor retardation and low energy, with no attachment to the objects). Major neurocognitive disorder (accumulation begins after cognitive decline, and is self-neglect rather than saving). Autism. Schizophrenia (delusional reasons). Normal collecting, which is organised, selective and non-impairing.

Trichotillomania (Hair-Pulling Disorder) F63.3

  • A. Recurrent pulling out of one's hair, resulting in hair loss.
  • B. Repeated attempts to decrease or stop hair pulling.
  • C. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • D. Not attributable to another medical condition (a dermatological condition).
  • E. Not better explained by the symptoms of another mental disorder (for example attempts to improve a perceived defect in body dysmorphic disorder).
  • Note what DSM-5 removed from the DSM-IV criteria: the requirements for mounting tension before pulling and pleasure, gratification or relief when pulling, because many patients, particularly those pulling automatically while reading or watching television, do not report them.
Numbers 12-month prevalence 1 to 2 percent. Female to male about 10:1 in adults, roughly equal in children. Onset usually coincides with, or follows, puberty. Sites: scalp commonest, then eyebrows and eyelashes; some pull from pubic, axillary and facial regions. Trichophagia, eating the hair, occurs in a minority and can cause a trichobezoar requiring surgery, which is a favourite MCQ.
Comorbidity Major depressive disorder and excoriation disorder, which co-occurs in roughly a fifth to a third. Also OCD, anxiety disorders, and other body-focused repetitive behaviours (nail biting, lip chewing) coded under other specified obsessive-compulsive and related disorder.
Differential Normal hair removal or grooming for cosmetic reasons. Alopecia areata and other dermatological causes, which are distinguished by the pattern; in trichotillomania hairs of varying lengths remain in the affected patch. OCD (pulling in response to an obsession, for example to achieve symmetry). Body dysmorphic disorder (pulling to remove a perceived defect). Stereotypic movement disorder. Psychotic disorder (pulling in response to a delusion or hallucination).

Excoriation (Skin-Picking) Disorder F42.4

  • A. Recurrent skin picking resulting in skin lesions. B. Repeated attempts to decrease or stop. C. Clinically significant distress or impairment. D. Not attributable to a substance (cocaine) or another medical condition (scabies). E. Not better explained by another mental disorder (delusions or tactile hallucinations in a psychotic disorder, appearance concerns in body dysmorphic disorder, stereotypies, or intent to self-harm in non-suicidal self-injury).
  • New in DSM-5.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence about 1.4 percent or slightly higher; three-quarters or more are female. Most commonly picked sites are the face, arms and hands. Onset most often in adolescence, frequently coinciding with the onset of puberty or with acne. Course is chronic with waxing and waning.
Comorbidity OCD, trichotillomania, major depressive disorder, and other body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders.
Differential Non-suicidal self-injury, where the intent is to relieve emotional pain through harm; in excoriation the intent is to remove an irregularity. Body dysmorphic disorder. Psychotic disorder with delusional parasitosis or formication. Substance-induced, above all methamphetamine and cocaine. Scabies, eczema and other dermatological conditions.

Substance-Induced and Medical Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders, and Other Specified

  • Substance/medication-induced, for example the obsessions and compulsions that appear with cocaine or amphetamine use. Due to another medical condition, for example after streptococcal infection, or with basal ganglia lesions, Sydenham's chorea, Huntington's or traumatic brain injury.
  • Other specified obsessive-compulsive and related disorder is where DSM-5-TR parks several named syndromes examiners like: body-focused repetitive behaviour disorder (nail biting, lip chewing, cheek chewing), obsessional jealousy, olfactory reference syndrome (jikoshu-kyofu, the belief that one emits a foul body odour), koro (an episode of sudden intense anxiety that the penis, or in women the vulva and nipples, will recede into the body and cause death), and shubo-kyofu (excessive fear of having a bodily deformity).
Comorbidity Primary obsessive-compulsive disorder, tic disorders and, for the post-streptococcal and basal-ganglia presentations, the neurological condition itself. The named cultural syndromes co-occur with anxiety and depressive disorders.
Differential Primary OCD in someone who also uses stimulants, settled by timing and by persistence past withdrawal. Psychotic disorders, where the belief is delusional and unaccompanied by rituals. Body dysmorphic disorder for shubo-kyofu, and delusional disorder, somatic type for olfactory reference syndrome with absent insight. Panic disorder for a koro episode, where the acute anxiety is the presenting feature.

8. Trauma and stressor-related disorders

The one chapter in DSM where an identifiable external event is part of the criteria. It contains the two attachment disorders, acute stress disorder, PTSD, the adjustment disorders, and, new in DSM-5-TR, prolonged grief disorder.

Reactive Attachment Disorder F94.1

In plain language. A severely neglected young child who has given up on adults: they do not seek comfort when distressed and do not accept it when it is offered. The internalising half of what DSM-IV called reactive attachment disorder.
  • A. A consistent pattern of inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behaviour toward adult caregivers, shown by both: the child rarely or minimally seeks comfort when distressed, and rarely or minimally responds to comfort when distressed.
  • B. A persistent social and emotional disturbance with at least 2 of 3: minimal social and emotional responsiveness to others; limited positive affect; episodes of unexplained irritability, sadness or fearfulness that are evident even during non-threatening interactions with adult caregivers.
  • C. The child has experienced a pattern of extremes of insufficient care, shown by at least one of: social neglect or deprivation (persistent lack of having basic emotional needs for comfort, stimulation and affection met); repeated changes of primary caregivers that limit opportunities to form stable attachments (frequent foster placements); rearing in unusual settings that severely limit opportunities to form selective attachments (institutions with high child-to-caregiver ratios).
  • D. The care in Criterion C is presumed to be responsible for the disturbed behaviour in Criterion A.
  • E. Criteria are not met for autism spectrum disorder.
  • F. The disturbance is evident before age 5. G. The child has a developmental age of at least 9 months.
Specifiers Persistent (present for more than 12 months), and severe (all symptoms present at relatively high levels).
Numbers Rare, under 10 percent even among severely neglected children, which is an important point: neglect is common, this disorder is not. Developmental age floor of 9 months matters because selective attachments have not formed before then.
Comorbidity Cognitive and language delays, stereotypies, medical conditions of malnutrition, and depressive symptoms.
Differential Autism spectrum disorder, which is Criterion E and is the examined pair: both show social deficits, but autism shows restricted repetitive behaviours, unusual sensory reactivity and language delay in its own pattern and does not require a history of pathogenic care; also, children with RAD improve markedly once caregiving improves, while autism does not remit. Intellectual disability (social behaviour matches mental age, and attachment behaviours are present). Depressive disorders. Disinhibited social engagement disorder, its opposite number.

Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder F94.2

In plain language. The same neglect history, the opposite behaviour: the child is over-friendly with total strangers and will wander off with anyone. DSM-5 split this out from reactive attachment disorder, because unlike RAD it does not require an absence of attachment and does not resolve simply by improving caregiving.
  • A. A pattern of behaviour in which a child actively approaches and interacts with unfamiliar adults, with at least 2 of 4: reduced or absent reticence in approaching and interacting with unfamiliar adults; overly familiar verbal or physical behaviour that is inconsistent with culturally sanctioned and age-appropriate social boundaries; diminished or absent checking back with the adult caregiver after venturing away, even in unfamiliar settings; willingness to go off with an unfamiliar adult with minimal or no hesitation.
  • B. The behaviours are not limited to impulsivity, as in ADHD, but include socially disinhibited behaviour.
  • C. Same pattern of extremes of insufficient care as in RAD. D. That care is presumed responsible. E. Developmental age of at least 9 months. Specify persistent and severe as above.
Numbers Around 20 percent of children in high-risk institutional populations. Unlike RAD, it can persist even after the caregiving environment is corrected and after a secure attachment has formed, which is the fact that justifies the split.
Comorbidity Cognitive and language delay, stereotypies, ADHD, and other signs of severe neglect.
Differential ADHD, which is Criterion B: ADHD children are impulsive and intrusive but do not typically show the indiscriminate willingness to go off with a stranger and do not require a neglect history. Williams syndrome, which produces indiscriminate friendliness on a genetic basis. Autism. Normal extraversion, distinguished by the absence of a neglect history and by the intact checking-back behaviour.

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder F43.10

In plain language. After a real, severe trauma, the event keeps coming back uninvited, the person avoids everything connected to it, their view of the world and themselves has turned dark, and their nervous system is stuck in alarm, for more than a month.
  • A. Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, in one or more of four ways: directly experiencing the event; witnessing it in person as it occurred to others; learning that it occurred to a close family member or close friend (in cases of actual or threatened death, the event must have been violent or accidental); or experiencing repeated or extreme exposure to aversive details of traumatic events, as with first responders collecting human remains or police repeatedly exposed to details of child abuse. That fourth route does not apply to exposure through electronic media, television, movies or pictures unless the exposure is work-related, which is the single most examined line in the criteria set. DSM-5 deleted the DSM-IV Criterion A2, which had required the person's response to involve intense fear, helplessness or horror.
  • B. Intrusion, 1 or more of 5: recurrent, involuntary and intrusive distressing memories (in children over 6, repetitive play expressing themes of the trauma); recurrent distressing dreams (in children, frightening dreams without recognisable content); dissociative reactions such as flashbacks in which the person feels or acts as if the event were recurring (in children, trauma-specific re-enactment in play); intense or prolonged psychological distress at exposure to internal or external cues that symbolise or resemble an aspect of the event; marked physiological reactions to such cues.
  • C. Avoidance, 1 or both: avoidance of, or efforts to avoid, distressing memories, thoughts or feelings about the event; avoidance of, or efforts to avoid, external reminders (people, places, conversations, activities, objects, situations).
  • D. Negative alterations in cognitions and mood, 2 or more of 7: inability to remember an important aspect of the event, typically due to dissociative amnesia rather than head injury or substances; persistent and exaggerated negative beliefs or expectations about oneself, others or the world ("I am bad", "no one can be trusted", "the world is completely dangerous"); persistent distorted cognitions about the cause or consequences of the event leading the person to blame themselves or others; persistent negative emotional state (fear, horror, anger, guilt, shame); markedly diminished interest or participation in significant activities; feelings of detachment or estrangement from others; persistent inability to experience positive emotions. This whole cluster is new in DSM-5; DSM-IV had three clusters, DSM-5 has four.
  • E. Alterations in arousal and reactivity, 2 or more of 6: irritable behaviour and angry outbursts with little or no provocation, typically expressed as verbal or physical aggression toward people or objects; reckless or self-destructive behaviour (new in DSM-5); hypervigilance; exaggerated startle response; problems with concentration; sleep disturbance.
  • F. Duration of Criteria B, C, D and E is more than 1 month. G. Clinically significant distress or impairment. H. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
Specifiers With dissociative symptoms, which is new in DSM-5 and requires persistent or recurrent depersonalisation (feeling detached from, and as if an outside observer of, one's own mental processes or body) or derealisation (experiencing the surroundings as unreal, dreamlike, distant or distorted). With delayed expression, where full criteria are not met until at least 6 months after the event, though some symptoms may begin immediately. There is also a separate PTSD for children 6 years and younger criteria set, the "preschool subtype", the first developmental subtype in DSM, which needs only one symptom from a combined avoidance-and-negative-cognitions cluster and drops the more verbally demanding items.
Numbers Lifetime risk in the United States about 8.7 percent by age 75; 12-month prevalence about 3.5 percent. Rates are much lower in most of Asia, Africa and Latin America, roughly 0.5 to 1 percent. Females are at roughly twice the risk and experience longer duration, partly because of higher exposure to sexual violence. Highest conditional rates follow rape, torture and captivity. Duration threshold 1 month: under a month, consider acute stress disorder.
Comorbidity About 80 percent have at least one comorbid disorder. Commonest: major depressive disorder, substance use disorders, other anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, and in military and veteran samples traumatic brain injury with overlapping symptoms. In children, oppositional defiant and separation anxiety disorders.
Differential Acute stress disorder (3 days to 1 month). Adjustment disorder, where either the stressor does not meet Criterion A (divorce, job loss) or it does but the symptom pattern does not meet PTSD criteria. Other specified trauma and stressor-related disorder. Anxiety and obsessive-compulsive disorders (intrusions in OCD are not related to a traumatic event). Major depressive disorder (no Criterion B intrusions or Criterion E arousal). Personality disorders, especially borderline, which shares affective instability and interpersonal difficulty. Dissociative disorders. Conversion disorder. Psychotic disorders (flashbacks and reliving versus hallucinations, and this misdiagnosis is common). Traumatic brain injury. Malingering, which must be considered explicitly where compensation or legal proceedings are involved.
ICD-11 Takes a completely different approach: PTSD is narrowed to three core clusters (re-experiencing in the here and now, avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat), and a separate diagnosis of Complex PTSD adds three disturbances of self-organisation: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbances in relationships. DSM-5-TR has no Complex PTSD; the nearest DSM approximation is PTSD with the dissociative subtype, sometimes alongside a personality-disorder diagnosis. Naming ICD-11 CPTSD and its three extra clusters is one of the highest-yield contrasts in the whole syllabus.

Acute Stress Disorder F43.0

In plain language. The same kind of aftermath as PTSD, in the first month, and diagnosed by counting symptoms across all categories rather than requiring a set from each.
  • A. The same exposure criterion as PTSD, with the same four routes and the same media exclusion.
  • B. 9 or more of 14 symptoms from any of five categories, beginning or worsening after the event: intrusion (intrusive memories, distressing dreams, dissociative reactions or flashbacks, intense or prolonged distress or marked physiological reactions to reminders); negative mood (persistent inability to experience positive emotions); dissociative (altered sense of the reality of one's surroundings or oneself, inability to remember an important aspect of the event); avoidance (of internal reminders, of external reminders); arousal (sleep disturbance, irritable behaviour and angry outbursts, hypervigilance, problems with concentration, exaggerated startle response). The key DSM-5 change: DSM-IV required at least three dissociative symptoms; DSM-5 dropped that requirement, because many people develop PTSD without ever dissociating.
  • C. Duration is 3 days to 1 month after trauma exposure. Symptoms typically begin immediately but must persist at least 3 days.
  • D. Clinically significant distress or impairment. E. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition (mild traumatic brain injury) and not better explained by brief psychotic disorder.
Numbers The two numbers are 9 of 14 and 3 days to 1 month. Prevalence after trauma varies widely, under 20 percent after events not involving interpersonal assault, higher after assault, rape and mass shootings. Roughly half of those who go on to develop PTSD initially present with acute stress disorder, so it is a poor screening test taken alone.
Comorbidity Prior mental disorders raise risk; depression and pain conditions commonly co-occur; head injury frequently coexists in road traffic and combat contexts.
Differential PTSD after a month. Adjustment disorder (stressor does not meet Criterion A, or symptom threshold not met). Panic disorder (unexpected attacks). Dissociative disorders. Traumatic brain injury and postconcussive syndrome. Normal, adaptive acute stress reactions, which is the most important one clinically: transient distress after trauma is expected, and it becomes a disorder only at this symptom count with impairment.

Adjustment Disorders F43.2x

In plain language. A response to an ordinary life stressor that is bigger than you would expect or is clearly wrecking function, but does not amount to any other disorder.
  • A. Development of emotional or behavioural symptoms in response to an identifiable stressor(s) occurring within 3 months of the onset of the stressor.
  • B. These symptoms are clinically significant, shown by one or both of: marked distress that is out of proportion to the severity or intensity of the stressor, taking into account external context and cultural factors; and/or significant impairment in social, occupational or other important areas of functioning.
  • C. The disturbance does not meet criteria for another mental disorder and is not merely an exacerbation of a pre-existing one.
  • D. The symptoms do not represent normal bereavement and are not better explained by prolonged grief disorder.
  • E. Once the stressor or its consequences have terminated, the symptoms do not persist for more than an additional 6 months.
Subtypes With depressed mood; with anxiety; with mixed anxiety and depressed mood; with disturbance of conduct; with mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct; unspecified. Also specify acute (under 6 months) or persistent/chronic (6 months or longer, which happens when the stressor itself is enduring).
Numbers Very common: 5 to 20 percent of outpatient mental-health caseloads, and up to about 50 percent in hospital psychiatric consultation samples, which makes it the commonest diagnosis in consultation-liaison work. The two numbers to hold: symptoms begin within 3 months of the stressor and resolve within 6 months of the stressor ending.
Comorbidity Can accompany most medical illnesses, and is associated with increased suicide risk, notably in adolescents and in the medically ill.
Differential Major depressive disorder (full criteria met, in which case MDD is diagnosed, not adjustment disorder). PTSD and acute stress disorder (Criterion A trauma and the specific symptom pattern). Personality disorders (long-standing pattern, though a personality disorder can be exacerbated by stress). Normal stress reactions, which is the judgment call: proportionality and impairment. Prolonged grief disorder and uncomplicated bereavement, which is now Criterion D.

Prolonged Grief Disorder F43.8 NEW IN DSM-5-TR

In plain language. A year or more after a death, the person is still gripped by yearning and preoccupation with the person who died, and their life has not restarted. This is the only new disorder in the 2022 Text Revision, so treat it as a guaranteed question.
  • A. The death, at least 12 months ago, of a person who was close to the bereaved (at least 6 months ago for children and adolescents).
  • B. Since the death, a persistent grief response characterised by one or both of: intense yearning or longing for the deceased; and/or preoccupation with thoughts or memories of the deceased (in children and adolescents, preoccupation may focus on the circumstances of the death). This has been present to a clinically significant degree nearly every day for at least the last month.
  • C. Since the death, 3 or more of 8 symptoms present to a clinically significant degree nearly every day for at least the last month: identity disruption (feeling as though part of oneself has died); a marked sense of disbelief about the death; avoidance of reminders that the person is dead; intense emotional pain (anger, bitterness, sorrow) related to the death; difficulty reintegrating into relationships and activities (problems engaging with friends, pursuing interests, planning for the future); emotional numbness, that is, an absence of or marked reduction in emotional experience; feeling that life is meaningless; intense loneliness, feeling alone or detached from others.
  • D. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • E. The duration and severity of the bereavement reaction clearly exceed expected social, cultural or religious norms for the individual's culture and context. This cultural clause is deliberate and is examinable, because mourning periods differ widely across Indian communities.
  • F. Not better explained by another mental disorder such as major depressive disorder or PTSD, and not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
Numbers The three that matter: 12 months in adults, 6 months in children and adolescents, and symptoms present nearly every day for at least the last month. Prevalence in bereaved adults is roughly 4 to 10 percent, higher after the death of a child, after violent or unexpected death, and after suicide.
Comorbidity Major depressive disorder, PTSD (especially after violent death), separation anxiety disorder, and substance use disorders. Suicide risk is elevated.
Differential Normal grief, distinguished by time course and by whether the person's life has resumed. Major depressive disorder: in PGD the emotional pain is focused on the deceased and yearning is the core, while in depression the low mood is generalised and self-worth is attacked; note that yearning and preoccupation are not criteria for MDD. PTSD: intrusions there are about the traumatic event and carry a sense of current threat, whereas PGD intrusions are about the person and carry longing. Separation anxiety disorder: fear of future separation from living attachment figures, not longing for a dead one. Adjustment disorder, now excluded by Criterion D of that disorder.
ICD-11 Also has Prolonged Grief Disorder, with a threshold of at least 6 months rather than 12. If a question gives a bereavement of 8 months and asks which system allows the diagnosis, the answer is ICD-11 only.

9. Dissociative disorders

Disruption of, or discontinuity in, the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, motor control and behaviour. DSM-5 placed this chapter immediately after the trauma chapter, without folding it in, to mark the close relationship without asserting that dissociation is always post-traumatic.

Dissociative Identity Disorder F44.81

In plain language. Two or more distinct ways of being a self take turns running the person, and there are memory gaps that ordinary forgetting does not explain.
  • A. Disruption of identity characterised by two or more distinct personality states, which in some cultures may be described as an experience of possession. The disruption involves marked discontinuity in sense of self and sense of agency, accompanied by related alterations in affect, behaviour, consciousness, memory, perception, cognition and sensory-motor functioning. These signs and symptoms may be observed by others or reported by the individual, which is a DSM-5 change: DSM-IV required that a state actually take control, DSM-5 permits self-report.
  • B. Recurrent gaps in the recall of everyday events, important personal information, and/or traumatic events that are inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. DSM-5 added everyday events, since DSM-IV limited the amnesia to important personal information.
  • C. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • D. The disturbance is not a normal part of a broadly accepted cultural or religious practice. In children, the symptoms are not better explained by imaginary playmates or other fantasy play.
  • E. Not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance (blackouts during alcohol intoxication) or another medical condition (complex partial seizures).
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 1.5 percent in one US community sample, with a near-equal sex ratio in that sample though clinical samples are heavily female. Reported rates of childhood abuse and neglect in clinical samples in North America, Europe and Turkey run around 90 percent. Suicide attempts occur in over 70 percent of outpatients with the diagnosis.
Comorbidity PTSD is nearly universal. Also depressive disorders, substance use disorders, somatic symptom disorder, conversion disorder, eating disorders, sleep disorders, and personality disorders, especially borderline and avoidant.
Differential Other specified dissociative disorder, where identity disturbance is less than fully distinct. Major depressive disorder. Bipolar disorder (the rapid shifts here are within-day and identity-linked, not episodic mood). PTSD. Psychotic disorders: voices in DID are typically experienced as coming from inside the head and as belonging to identities, and reality testing is otherwise intact. Borderline personality disorder, which shares identity disturbance and dissociation under stress but lacks the distinct amnesia between states. Seizure disorders, particularly complex partial seizures. Factitious disorder and malingering, which DSM-5-TR discusses at length because of the forensic history around this diagnosis.

Dissociative Amnesia F44.0, with fugue F44.1

  • A. An inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, that is inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. Note that dissociative amnesia most often consists of localised or selective amnesia for a specific event or events, or generalised amnesia for identity and life history.
  • B. Clinically significant distress or impairment. C. Not attributable to a substance or a neurological or other medical condition. D. Not better explained by DID, PTSD, acute stress disorder, somatic symptom disorder, or major or mild neurocognitive disorder.
  • Specify: with dissociative fugue, that is, apparently purposeful travel or bewildered wandering associated with amnesia for identity or other important autobiographical information. Dissociative fugue is no longer a separate disorder in DSM-5, it is a specifier here. That change is asked frequently.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 1.8 percent (1.0 percent in men, 2.6 percent in women) in one US community study. Types to be able to list: localised (no recall of events during a circumscribed period, the commonest), selective (some but not all of the events in a period), generalised (whole life and identity, rare and abrupt), systematised (a specific category of information, for example all memories of one person), and continuous (each new event as it occurs).
Comorbidity Depressive disorders, PTSD, conversion disorder, somatic symptom disorder, and personality disorders. Suicide risk is elevated, particularly when memory returns suddenly.
Differential Neurocognitive disorders (anterograde deficits predominate, and personal identity is retained until very late). Transient global amnesia (abrupt, anterograde, resolves within 24 hours, older patients, retained identity). Post-ictal and post-traumatic amnesia. Substance-related blackouts. Malingering and factitious disorder, where secondary gain is evident. Normal and age-related forgetting.

Depersonalisation/Derealisation Disorder F48.1

  • A. Persistent or recurrent experiences of depersonalisation, derealisation, or both. Depersonalisation: experiences of unreality, detachment, or being an outside observer with respect to one's thoughts, feelings, sensations, body or actions (perceptual alterations, distorted sense of time, unreal or absent self, emotional and/or physical numbing). Derealisation: experiences of unreality or detachment with respect to surroundings (individuals or objects experienced as unreal, dreamlike, foggy, lifeless or visually distorted).
  • B. During these experiences, reality testing remains intact. This is the criterion that separates the disorder from psychosis and is the one examiners test.
  • C. Clinically significant distress or impairment. D. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition (seizures). E. Not better explained by another mental disorder such as schizophrenia, panic disorder, major depressive disorder, acute stress disorder, PTSD, or another dissociative disorder.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence about 2 percent; sex ratio roughly 1:1. Mean age at onset 16, with about 20 percent onset after 20 and only 5 percent after 25; onset after 40 is unusual and should prompt a search for a medical cause. Transient depersonalisation experiences are extremely common in the general population (roughly half of adults report at least one), so the disorder requires persistence and impairment.
Comorbidity Unipolar depressive disorder and anxiety disorders are the commonest; also avoidant, borderline and obsessive-compulsive personality disorders.
Differential Panic disorder (depersonalisation only during attacks). Psychotic disorders (reality testing lost). Substance-induced, especially cannabis, ketamine, hallucinogens. Medical causes: temporal lobe epilepsy, migraine, vestibular disease, head injury. PTSD with the dissociative subtype. Major depressive disorder with numbing.

10. Somatic symptom and related disorders

The chapter DSM-5 rewrote most radically. DSM-IV's somatisation disorder, hypochondriasis, pain disorder and undifferentiated somatoform disorder were all deleted and replaced. The organising change: DSM-IV defined these disorders by the absence of a medical explanation, which was unreliable and, worse, reinforced mind-body dualism and made patients feel disbelieved. DSM-5 defines them instead by the presence of positive psychological features, namely disproportionate thoughts, feelings and behaviours about the symptoms. Say that sentence in any long-answer question on this chapter.

Somatic Symptom Disorder F45.1

  • A. One or more somatic symptoms that are distressing or result in significant disruption of daily life. Note it is one, not the eight-symptom count DSM-IV demanded across four body systems, and the symptom may or may not be medically explained.
  • B. Excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours related to the somatic symptoms or associated health concerns, shown by at least 1 of 3: disproportionate and persistent thoughts about the seriousness of one's symptoms; persistently high level of anxiety about health or symptoms; excessive time and energy devoted to these symptoms or health concerns.
  • C. Although any one somatic symptom may not be continuously present, the state of being symptomatic is persistent, typically more than 6 months.
Specifiers With predominant pain, which is the replacement for DSM-IV's pain disorder. Persistent (severe symptoms, marked impairment, duration more than 6 months). Severity mild (one Criterion B symptom), moderate (two or more), severe (two or more plus multiple somatic complaints or one very severe symptom).
Numbers Prevalence estimated 5 to 7 percent in the general adult population, higher in women. Compare DSM-IV somatisation disorder at under 1 percent: the criteria were loosened deliberately, and the standard criticism to reproduce in an essay is that this risks pathologising normal illness worry in the medically ill.
Comorbidity Depressive and anxiety disorders, PTSD, and genuine medical illness, which frequently coexists. That coexistence is the point: DSM-5 explicitly permits the diagnosis alongside a diagnosed medical condition.
Differential Illness anxiety disorder (few or no somatic symptoms, the fear is of having or acquiring an illness). Panic disorder (symptoms are episodic and attack-bound). Generalised anxiety disorder (worry is broader than health). Major depressive disorder. Conversion disorder (loss of function, not distress about a symptom). Delusional disorder, somatic type (fixed, unshakeable belief). Body dysmorphic disorder. OCD with health obsessions.

Illness Anxiety Disorder F45.21

  • A. Preoccupation with having or acquiring a serious illness.
  • B. Somatic symptoms are not present or, if present, are only mild in intensity. If another medical condition is present or there is a high risk of developing one, the preoccupation is clearly excessive or disproportionate.
  • C. There is a high level of anxiety about health, and the person is easily alarmed about personal health status.
  • D. The person performs excessive health-related behaviours (repeatedly checking the body for signs of illness) or exhibits maladaptive avoidance (avoids doctor appointments and hospitals).
  • E. Illness preoccupation has been present for at least 6 months, although the specific illness feared may change.
  • F. Not better explained by another mental disorder.
  • Specify: care-seeking type or care-avoidant type.
Numbers Prevalence 1.3 to 10 percent, roughly equal in men and women. About 75 percent of those who would have been diagnosed with DSM-IV hypochondriasis now fall under somatic symptom disorder because they do have somatic symptoms; the remaining quarter, who have few or no symptoms, become illness anxiety disorder. That reallocation is a stock question.
Comorbidity Anxiety disorders, especially generalised anxiety and panic, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and depressive disorders. Personality disorders in about a fifth.
Differential Somatic symptom disorder (prominent somatic symptoms). Generalised anxiety disorder. OCD (obsessions about contracting disease in the future, with compulsions; illness anxiety is about currently having it). Specific phobia of illness. Delusional disorder, somatic type. Major depressive disorder. Adjustment disorder after a genuine diagnosis.

Conversion Disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder) F44.x

  • A. One or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function.
  • B. Clinical findings provide evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognised neurological or medical conditions. This is positive evidence, for example Hoover's sign (hip extension weakness resolves on contralateral hip flexion against resistance), a tremor that entrains to a tapped rhythm, or tubular visual fields. DSM-5's key change was requiring this positive incompatibility rather than requiring a preceding psychological stressor.
  • C. Not better explained by another medical or mental disorder. D. Causes clinically significant distress or impairment or warrants medical evaluation.
  • Specify symptom type: with weakness or paralysis; with abnormal movement (tremor, dystonic movement, myoclonus, gait disorder); with swallowing symptoms; with speech symptom (dysphonia, slurred speech); with attacks or seizures (psychogenic non-epileptic seizures); with anaesthesia or sensory loss; with special sensory symptom (visual, olfactory or hearing disturbance); with mixed symptoms. Also acute (under 6 months) or persistent (6 months or more), and with or without psychological stressor.
Numbers Incidence of persistent conversion symptoms about 2 to 5 per 100,000 per year; symptoms are seen in roughly 5 percent of referrals to neurology clinics. Two to three times more common in women. La belle indifference, a bland lack of concern about the deficit, is described classically but is not a criterion and has poor diagnostic value, which is exactly why it is asked. A history of a preceding stressor is also no longer required.
Comorbidity Depressive and anxiety disorders, PTSD, dissociative disorders (which co-occur frequently), personality disorders, and genuine neurological disease, which co-occurs often, so the presence of epilepsy does not exclude psychogenic non-epileptic seizures.
Differential Neurological disease, which requires proper examination, and the historically high misdiagnosis rate has fallen to around 4 percent in modern series. Factitious disorder (deliberate falsification, motive is the sick role). Malingering (deliberate, motive is external gain: compensation, avoiding duty). The distinction from both is that conversion symptoms are not intentionally produced. Somatic symptom disorder (distress about a symptom versus loss of function). Body dysmorphic disorder. Depersonalisation/derealisation. Dissociative disorders.

Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions F54

  • A. A medical symptom or condition (other than a mental disorder) is present.
  • B. Psychological or behavioural factors adversely affect the medical condition in one of four ways: they have influenced the course of the condition, as shown by a close temporal association; they interfere with treatment (poor adherence); they constitute additional well-established health risks; or they influence the underlying pathophysiology, precipitating or exacerbating symptoms or necessitating medical attention.
  • C. Not better explained by another mental disorder. Severity mild to extreme.
  • This diagnosis replaced the old psychosomatic and DSM-IV Axis I "psychological factors affecting medical condition" framing, and it is the correct label for, say, denial of chest pain delaying treatment, or anxiety worsening asthma.
Comorbidity Depressive and anxiety disorders, adjustment disorder, and the personality traits (hostility, denial, avoidance) that drive the non-adherence. The medical condition itself is the other half of the diagnosis, so cardiovascular disease, asthma, diabetes and cancer are the usual settings.
Differential Somatic symptom disorder (there the psychological features attach to symptoms, here they attach to a diagnosed medical condition and worsen it). Adjustment disorder (the medical illness is the stressor causing a psychiatric response, rather than psychological factors worsening the illness). Illness anxiety disorder.

Factitious Disorder F68.1x

  • Factitious disorder imposed on self. (A) Falsification of physical or psychological signs or symptoms, or induction of injury or disease, associated with identified deception. (B) The person presents themselves to others as ill, impaired or injured. (C) The deceptive behaviour is evident even in the absence of obvious external rewards. (D) Not better explained by another mental disorder such as delusional disorder or another psychotic disorder. Specify single episode or recurrent episodes.
  • Factitious disorder imposed on another (previously factitious disorder by proxy, and the older lay term Munchausen syndrome by proxy). Falsification or induction in another person, associated with identified deception. Note the critical coding rule: the perpetrator, not the victim, receives the diagnosis. The victim is given an abuse diagnosis code.
Numbers Prevalence unknown, estimated about 1 percent of hospital inpatients. Commonest in women in the imposed-on-self form and in mothers in the imposed-on-another form; healthcare workers are over-represented.
Comorbidity Depressive disorders, substance use disorders, and personality disorders, especially borderline, which is the commonest comorbid personality diagnosis in the imposed-on-self form. In the imposed-on-another form, personality pathology in the perpetrator and genuine iatrogenic harm in the victim.
Differential Malingering, which is not a mental disorder in DSM-5 but a V code, "malingering", listed among other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention. The line is motive: factitious disorder is driven by the internal need to occupy the sick role, malingering is driven by an obvious external incentive (money, drugs, avoiding work, military duty or prosecution). Somatic symptom disorder and conversion disorder, where symptoms are not intentionally produced. Borderline personality disorder, where self-injury is present but is not accompanied by deception about its origin.

11. Feeding and eating disorders

DSM-5 merged the childhood feeding disorders with the adult eating disorders into one chapter, added binge-eating disorder as a full diagnosis (it was in the DSM-IV appendix), created avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder to replace "feeding disorder of infancy or early childhood", and loosened anorexia and bulimia in specific ways that are examined constantly.

Pica F98.3 children, F50.89 adults

  • A. Persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances over a period of at least 1 month (paper, soap, cloth, hair, string, wool, soil, chalk, talcum powder, paint, gum, metal, pebbles, charcoal, ash, clay, starch, ice).
  • B. The eating is inappropriate to the developmental level of the individual (so it is not diagnosed under a developmental age of about 2 years, when mouthing is normal).
  • C. The eating behaviour is not part of a culturally supported or socially normative practice, which matters because geophagia is normative in some communities.
  • D. If it occurs in the context of another mental disorder (intellectual disability, autism, schizophrenia) or medical condition (pregnancy), it is sufficiently severe to warrant additional clinical attention. Note that unlike the other eating disorders, pica can be diagnosed alongside anorexia and bulimia.
Comorbidity Intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, trichotillomania (with trichophagia), excoriation disorder. Medically, iron-deficiency anaemia and zinc deficiency, and in pregnancy.
Differential Anorexia nervosa (eating non-food to control appetite). Factitious disorder (swallowing objects for the sick role). Non-suicidal self-injury. Normative cultural practice, which is Criterion C.

Rumination Disorder F98.21

  • A. Repeated regurgitation of food over a period of at least 1 month. Regurgitated food may be re-chewed, re-swallowed or spat out.
  • B. The repeated regurgitation is not attributable to an associated gastrointestinal or other medical condition such as gastro-oesophageal reflux or pyloric stenosis.
  • C. The eating disturbance does not occur exclusively during anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder or ARFID.
  • D. If it occurs in the context of another mental disorder such as intellectual disability, it is severe enough to warrant additional clinical attention.
Comorbidity Intellectual disability, other neurodevelopmental disorders, and, in infants, neglect and understimulation. In older children and adults it co-occurs with anxiety and with the other feeding and eating disorders.
Differential Gastrointestinal conditions (reflux, gastroparesis, pyloric stenosis), which are Criterion B. Bulimia nervosa (regurgitation there is part of purging after a binge, in the service of weight control).

Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) F50.82

In plain language. The person does not eat enough, but not because of any concern about weight or shape. That last clause is the whole diagnosis. New in DSM-5, replacing DSM-IV's feeding disorder of infancy or early childhood, and applicable at any age.
  • A. An eating or feeding disturbance, for example apparent lack of interest in eating or food, avoidance based on the sensory characteristics of food, or concern about aversive consequences of eating (fear of choking or vomiting after a choking episode), that persistently fails to meet appropriate nutritional and/or energy needs, associated with 1 or more of 4: significant weight loss or failure to achieve expected weight gain or faltering growth in children; significant nutritional deficiency; dependence on enteral feeding or oral nutritional supplements; marked interference with psychosocial functioning.
  • B. Not better explained by lack of available food or by an associated culturally sanctioned practice (so famine, poverty and religious fasting are excluded).
  • C. The disturbance does not occur exclusively during anorexia or bulimia, and there is no evidence of a disturbance in the way one's body weight or shape is experienced.
  • D. Not attributable to a concurrent medical condition or better explained by another mental disorder; where it occurs alongside one, it exceeds what is routinely associated and warrants additional clinical attention.
Comorbidity Anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, autism spectrum disorder (the sensory-based subtype in particular), ADHD, and intellectual disability.
Differential Anorexia nervosa, settled by Criterion C: no body-image disturbance and no fear of fatness in ARFID. Specific phobia of choking or vomiting, which can be an alternative label when the food restriction is not the focus. Depression and psychotic disorders with reduced appetite. Normal picky eating, which is common and does not cause nutritional or psychosocial harm. Medical causes: coeliac disease, food allergy, oesophageal disorders, inflammatory bowel disease.

Anorexia Nervosa F50.01 restricting, F50.02 binge-eating/purging

  • A. Restriction of energy intake relative to requirements, leading to a significantly low body weight in the context of age, sex, developmental trajectory and physical health. Significantly low weight is defined as less than minimally normal, or, for children and adolescents, less than that minimally expected.
  • B. Intense fear of gaining weight or of becoming fat, or persistent behaviour that interferes with weight gain, even though at a significantly low weight. That second clause is a DSM-5 addition, added because many patients, particularly in Asian samples, deny the fear but act on it.
  • C. Disturbance in the way one's body weight or shape is experienced, undue influence of body weight or shape on self-evaluation, or persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness of the current low body weight.
  • DSM-5 removed the amenorrhoea criterion that DSM-IV required (three consecutive missed cycles), because it excluded males, premenarchal girls, postmenopausal women and women on hormonal contraceptives, and because those who met all criteria except amenorrhoea did not differ clinically. This deletion is the most examined single fact about anorexia.
Subtypes and severity Restricting type: during the last 3 months, no recurrent episodes of binge eating or purging; weight loss is accomplished by dieting, fasting and/or excessive exercise. Binge-eating/purging type: during the last 3 months, recurrent episodes of binge eating or purging (self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics or enemas). Severity for adults is by BMI: mild BMI at or above 17, moderate 16 to 16.99, severe 15 to 15.99, extreme below 15. Also in partial or full remission.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 0.4 percent among young females; female to male about 10:1. Peak onset in adolescence and young adulthood; onset before puberty or after 40 is unusual. It has one of the highest mortality rates of any mental disorder, with a crude mortality rate around 5 percent per decade, from medical complications and from suicide. Physical signs to be able to list: amenorrhoea, bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, lanugo hair, dry skin, hypercarotenaemia, osteopenia, leukopenia, raised cortisol, low T3, and in purging patients hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, dental enamel erosion, parotid enlargement and Russell's sign (calluses on the dorsum of the hand). Watch for refeeding syndrome, above all hypophosphataemia, on rapid nutritional restoration.
Comorbidity Major depressive disorder (very high), obsessive-compulsive disorder (especially the restricting type), social anxiety disorder, alcohol and substance use disorders (especially binge-purge type), and personality disorders (obsessive-compulsive with restricting, borderline with binge-purge).
Differential Bulimia nervosa, and the rule to state is simple: if criteria for anorexia are met, anorexia takes precedence, so a person who binges and purges but is significantly underweight has anorexia, binge-eating/purging type, not bulimia. ARFID (no body-image disturbance). Avoidant, depressive or psychotic causes of weight loss. Body dysmorphic disorder. Medical causes of weight loss: coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, hyperthyroidism, malignancy, tuberculosis, HIV, superior mesenteric artery syndrome.

Bulimia Nervosa F50.2

  • A. Recurrent episodes of binge eating, defined by both: eating, in a discrete period (for example within any 2-hour period), an amount of food that is definitely larger than what most individuals would eat in a similar period under similar circumstances; and a sense of lack of control over eating during the episode.
  • B. Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviours to prevent weight gain: self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics or other medications, fasting, or excessive exercise.
  • C. Both the binge eating and the compensatory behaviours occur, on average, at least once a week for 3 months. DSM-IV required twice a week for 3 months, so the frequency threshold was halved in DSM-5. That halving is examined.
  • D. Self-evaluation is unduly influenced by body shape and weight.
  • E. The disturbance does not occur exclusively during episodes of anorexia nervosa.
Severity By average number of episodes of inappropriate compensatory behaviour per week: mild 1 to 3, moderate 4 to 7, severe 8 to 13, extreme 14 or more. DSM-5 also deleted the purging and non-purging subtypes.
Numbers 12-month prevalence 1 to 1.5 percent among young females; female to male about 10:1. Peak onset in late adolescence and young adulthood, slightly later than anorexia. Weight is typically normal or overweight, which is why it goes undetected for years. Crude mortality about 2 percent per decade.
Comorbidity Depressive and bipolar disorders, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders in about 30 percent (alcohol and stimulants), and borderline personality disorder in a substantial minority. Self-harm and suicide risk are elevated.
Differential Anorexia nervosa, binge-eating/purging type, distinguished by significantly low body weight, and anorexia takes precedence. Binge-eating disorder (binges without compensatory behaviour). Kleine-Levin syndrome and other neurological causes of hyperphagia. Major depressive disorder with atypical features (overeating without the loss-of-control quality and without compensation). Borderline personality disorder, where binge eating may be one of several impulsive behaviours.

Binge-Eating Disorder F50.81

In plain language. Binges without the compensating. Promoted from the DSM-IV research appendix to a full DSM-5 diagnosis, which is the fact examiners want.
  • A. Recurrent episodes of binge eating, defined exactly as in bulimia (large amount, discrete period, loss of control).
  • B. The binge-eating episodes are associated with 3 or more of 5: eating much more rapidly than normal; eating until feeling uncomfortably full; eating large amounts of food when not feeling physically hungry; eating alone because of feeling embarrassed by how much one is eating; feeling disgusted with oneself, depressed, or very guilty afterward.
  • C. Marked distress regarding binge eating is present.
  • D. The binge eating occurs, on average, at least once a week for 3 months.
  • E. The binge eating is not associated with recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviour and does not occur exclusively during bulimia nervosa or anorexia nervosa.
Severity By binge episodes per week: mild 1 to 3, moderate 4 to 7, severe 8 to 13, extreme 14 or more.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 1.6 percent in adult females and 0.8 percent in adult males, so it is the commonest eating disorder and has the least skewed sex ratio (roughly 2:1 rather than 10:1). It occurs across normal-weight, overweight and obese individuals. Obesity is not a mental disorder in DSM-5 and is not the same as binge-eating disorder, a distinction that gets tested.
Comorbidity Bipolar disorders, depressive disorders, anxiety disorders and, to a lesser degree, substance use disorders. Medically: obesity, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes.
Differential Bulimia nervosa (compensatory behaviour present). Obesity without binge eating, where overeating is not in discrete episodes and lacks the loss-of-control quality and the distress. Major depressive disorder with atypical features. Bipolar disorder in an episode with increased appetite. Prader-Willi syndrome and other medical hyperphagia.

12. Elimination disorders

Enuresis F98.0

  • A. Repeated voiding of urine into bed or clothes, whether involuntary or intentional.
  • B. The behaviour is clinically significant, shown by a frequency of at least twice a week for at least 3 consecutive months, or by clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • C. Chronological age is at least 5 (or the equivalent developmental level).
  • D. Not attributable to a substance (a diuretic, an antipsychotic) or another medical condition (diabetes, spina bifida, a seizure disorder).
  • Specify: nocturnal only (the commonest, "monosymptomatic enuresis"), diurnal only (also called urinary incontinence), or nocturnal and diurnal. Also primary (continence never achieved) or secondary (relapse after at least 6 months of continence).
Numbers Prevalence about 5 to 10 percent at age 5, 3 to 5 percent at age 10, and about 1 percent at 15 and above. Spontaneous remission is roughly 5 to 10 percent per year. Commoner in boys for the nocturnal form. Treatment of choice is the bell-and-pad urine alarm (classical conditioning), with desmopressin as pharmacological backup.
Comorbidity Encopresis, sleepwalking and sleep terrors, ADHD, and developmental delays. Secondary enuresis frequently follows a psychosocial stressor such as the birth of a sibling, school entry, or family disruption.
Differential Urinary tract infection, diabetes mellitus and insipidus, seizure disorder, neurogenic bladder, structural urological abnormalities, and obstructive sleep apnoea, all Criterion D.

Encopresis F98.1

  • A. Repeated passage of faeces into inappropriate places (clothing, floor), whether involuntary or intentional.
  • B. At least one such event per month for at least 3 months.
  • C. Chronological age is at least 4 (or the equivalent developmental level).
  • D. Not attributable to a substance (laxatives) or another medical condition except through a mechanism involving constipation.
  • Specify: with constipation and overflow incontinence (the commoner form, with a palpable faecal mass and loose overflow soiling), or without constipation and overflow incontinence (more often associated with oppositional defiant disorder or conduct disorder).
Numbers About 1 percent of 5-year-olds; more common in males. Note the two age floors: enuresis 5, encopresis 4.
Comorbidity Enuresis, which co-occurs frequently, plus oppositional defiant disorder and conduct disorder in the without-constipation form, and ADHD. Chronic constipation with painful defecation is the usual physical driver, and shame and social withdrawal follow.
Differential Hirschsprung's disease, hypothyroidism, anal fissure with retention, spinal cord lesions, and dietary causes of chronic constipation.

13. Sleep-wake disorders

DSM-5 abandoned DSM-IV's split into primary sleep disorders, sleep disorders related to another mental disorder, and those due to a general medical condition, because that split forced a causal judgment clinicians could not reliably make. The DSM-5 approach is to diagnose the sleep disorder in its own right alongside any coexisting condition, on the reasoning that insomnia and depression have a bidirectional relationship and that the sleep problem needs treating either way. Say that in an essay.

Insomnia Disorder F51.01

  • A. A predominant complaint of dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality, associated with 1 or more of 3: difficulty initiating sleep; difficulty maintaining sleep, with frequent awakenings or problems returning to sleep; early-morning awakening with inability to return to sleep.
  • B. Clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • C. The sleep difficulty occurs at least 3 nights per week. D. Present for at least 3 months. E. Occurs despite adequate opportunity for sleep.
  • F. Not better explained by, and does not occur exclusively during, another sleep-wake disorder. G. Not attributable to a substance. H. Coexisting mental disorders and medical conditions do not adequately explain the predominant complaint.
  • Specify: with non-sleep disorder mental comorbidity, with other medical comorbidity, with other sleep disorder; and episodic (1 to 3 months), persistent (3 months or more), recurrent (two or more episodes within a year).
Numbers The exam numbers are 3 nights a week for 3 months. About one third of adults report insomnia symptoms, 10 to 15 percent have daytime impairment, and 6 to 10 percent meet full criteria. Commoner in women (about 1.44:1) and increases with age. First-line treatment is CBT-I (stimulus control, sleep restriction, sleep hygiene, cognitive restructuring, relaxation), not hypnotics.
Comorbidity Depressive, bipolar and anxiety disorders (insomnia is both a risk factor for and a residual symptom of depression), substance use disorders, and medical conditions with pain.
Differential Normal sleep variation and short sleepers. Situational or acute insomnia (under 3 months). Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, particularly delayed sleep phase type, where the person sleeps well if allowed their own schedule. Restless legs syndrome and breathing-related sleep disorders. Substance-induced sleep disorder (caffeine, alcohol rebound, stimulants). Narcolepsy.

Hypersomnolence Disorder and Narcolepsy F51.11, G47.4xx

  • Hypersomnolence disorder. Self-reported excessive sleepiness despite a main sleep period of at least 7 hours, with at least one of: recurrent periods of sleep or lapses into sleep within the same day; a prolonged main sleep episode of more than 9 hours per day that is non-restorative; difficulty being fully awake after abrupt awakening (sleep inertia or sleep drunkenness). Occurs at least 3 times per week for at least 3 months, with distress or impairment.
  • Narcolepsy. Recurrent periods of an irrepressible need to sleep, lapsing into sleep, or napping occurring within the same day, at least 3 times per week over the past 3 months, plus at least 1 of 3: (1) episodes of cataplexy, meaning, in long-standing disease, brief episodes of sudden bilateral loss of muscle tone with retained consciousness precipitated by laughter or joking, or, in children or within 6 months of onset, spontaneous grimaces or jaw-opening episodes with tongue thrusting or global hypotonia without an emotional trigger; (2) hypocretin-1 (orexin) deficiency in CSF, at or below one third of control values; (3) nocturnal polysomnography showing REM sleep latency of 15 minutes or less, or a multiple sleep latency test with mean sleep latency of 8 minutes or less and two or more sleep-onset REM periods.
Numbers Narcolepsy prevalence about 0.02 to 0.04 percent. Typical onset in adolescence or the early twenties. Strong association with HLA-DQB1*06:02 and with loss of hypothalamic hypocretin-producing neurons. The classic tetrad to name: excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic (or hypnopompic) hallucinations. Note that only sleepiness plus one of the three objective or cataplexy criteria is required; sleep paralysis and hallucinations are not criteria in DSM-5.
Comorbidity Depressive disorders (which frequently precede or follow hypersomnolence), bipolar disorder, substance use disorders, and, in narcolepsy specifically, obesity, obstructive sleep apnoea, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression and anxiety, plus the social impairment that comes from cataplexy in public.
Differential Sleep deprivation and insufficient sleep syndrome, the commonest cause of daytime sleepiness. Obstructive sleep apnoea hypopnoea. Major depressive disorder with hypersomnia. Substance effects. Kleine-Levin syndrome (recurrent hypersomnia with hyperphagia and hypersexuality in young males). Cataplexy specifically must be distinguished from syncope, atonic seizures and conversion.

Breathing-Related Sleep Disorders, Circadian Rhythm Disorders, and Parasomnias

  • Obstructive Sleep Apnoea Hypopnoea. Either polysomnographic evidence of at least 5 obstructive apnoeas or hypopnoeas per hour of sleep plus nocturnal breathing symptoms (snoring, snorting, gasping, breathing pauses) or daytime sleepiness and fatigue; or polysomnographic evidence of 15 or more events per hour regardless of symptoms. Severity by apnoea-hypopnoea index: mild under 15, moderate 15 to 30, severe over 30. Also Central Sleep Apnoea and Sleep-Related Hypoventilation.
  • Circadian Rhythm Sleep-Wake Disorders. A persistent pattern of sleep disruption due to an alteration of the circadian system or a misalignment between the endogenous rhythm and the required schedule. Types: delayed sleep phase (the commonest in adolescents; a familial form exists), advanced sleep phase (older adults), irregular sleep-wake type, non-24-hour sleep-wake type (common in blind individuals), and shift work type. Jet lag type was removed in DSM-5.
  • Non-REM Sleep Arousal Disorders. Recurrent episodes of incomplete awakening from sleep, usually during the first third of the major sleep episode (that is, from slow-wave, N3 sleep), accompanied by either sleepwalking or sleep terrors. The person has no or little dream imagery recalled, is relatively unresponsive during the episode, and has amnesia for it. Sleep terrors involve abrupt terror arousals with a panicky scream, intense fear and autonomic arousal.
  • Nightmare Disorder. Repeated occurrences of extended, extremely dysphoric and well-remembered dreams, usually involving threats to survival, security or physical integrity, generally occurring during the second half of the major sleep episode (that is, from REM sleep). On awakening the person rapidly becomes oriented and alert.
  • REM Sleep Behaviour Disorder. Repeated episodes of arousal during sleep associated with vocalisation and/or complex motor behaviours, arising during REM sleep and therefore usually more than 90 minutes after sleep onset, more frequent in the later portions of the sleep period. Upon awakening the person is completely awake, alert and not confused. Requires either REM sleep without atonia on polysomnography, or a history suggestive of the disorder plus an established synucleinopathy diagnosis. Highly examinable point: RBD is a prodrome of the alpha-synucleinopathies, so a large majority of cases eventually convert to Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy.
  • Restless Legs Syndrome. An urge to move the legs, usually accompanied by or in response to uncomfortable sensations, with all three features: the urge begins or worsens during periods of rest or inactivity; is partially or totally relieved by movement; is worse in the evening or at night. Occurs at least 3 times per week for at least 3 months. Associated with iron deficiency, pregnancy and renal failure.
  • Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder, with insomnia, daytime sleepiness, parasomnia or mixed types.
Comorbidity Obstructive sleep apnoea with obesity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and depression. Circadian disorders with depressive disorders and, in the delayed phase type, adolescence itself. Non-REM arousal disorders with other parasomnias, enuresis and, in adults, obstructive sleep apnoea acting as the arousal trigger. REM sleep behaviour disorder with the alpha-synucleinopathies and with antidepressant use, which can unmask it. Restless legs syndrome with iron deficiency, pregnancy, renal failure, depression and ADHD.
The differential that gets asked Sleep terrors versus nightmares. Sleep terrors: first third of the night, non-REM slow-wave sleep, marked autonomic arousal, the person is inconsolable and unresponsive, there is amnesia for the episode, and dream recall is minimal. Nightmares: second half of the night, REM sleep, the person wakes fully and can describe the dream vividly, and there is no amnesia and no confusion. Sleep terrors versus RBD. RBD occurs in REM, later in the night, the behaviour enacts a recalled dream, the person is instantly alert afterwards, and it is a disorder of older men that predicts Parkinson's. Nocturnal panic attacks also wake the person from non-REM sleep, but they are fully alert and there is no amnesia. Nocturnal seizures are stereotyped and brief.

14. Sexual dysfunctions, gender dysphoria, paraphilic disorders

Sexual Dysfunctions F52.x

In plain language. A problem in the ability to respond sexually or to experience sexual pleasure, that has lasted at least six months and causes distress. DSM-5 made three structural changes: it became gender-specific where the evidence supported that, it added a minimum duration of about 6 months and a frequency requirement of 75 to 100 percent of occasions to almost every dysfunction, and it merged several DSM-IV categories.
  • Delayed Ejaculation. Marked delay in, infrequency of, or absence of ejaculation on almost all or all (75 to 100 percent) partnered sexual occasions, without the person desiring the delay.
  • Erectile Disorder. At least one of three on almost all or all occasions: marked difficulty obtaining an erection, marked difficulty maintaining one until completion, or a marked decrease in erectile rigidity.
  • Female Orgasmic Disorder. Marked delay in, infrequency of, or absence of orgasm, and/or markedly reduced intensity of orgasmic sensations, on almost all or all occasions.
  • Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder. Lack of, or significantly reduced, sexual interest or arousal, shown by at least 3 of 6: absent or reduced interest in sexual activity; absent or reduced sexual or erotic thoughts or fantasies; no or reduced initiation of sexual activity and typically unreceptive to a partner's attempts to initiate; absent or reduced sexual excitement or pleasure during sexual activity in almost all encounters; absent or reduced sexual interest or arousal in response to any internal or external sexual or erotic cues; absent or reduced genital or non-genital sensations during sexual activity in almost all encounters. This diagnosis merged DSM-IV's female hypoactive sexual desire disorder and female sexual arousal disorder, on the evidence that desire and arousal are not separable in women.
  • Male Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder. Persistently or recurrently deficient or absent sexual or erotic thoughts or fantasies and desire for sexual activity, judged by the clinician taking into account age and context.
  • Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder. Persistent or recurrent difficulties with 1 or more of 4: vaginal penetration during intercourse; marked vulvovaginal or pelvic pain during vaginal intercourse or penetration attempts; marked fear or anxiety about vulvovaginal or pelvic pain in anticipation of, during, or as a result of vaginal penetration; marked tensing or tightening of the pelvic floor muscles during attempted vaginal penetration. This merged DSM-IV's vaginismus and dyspareunia, because the two could not be reliably distinguished. That merger is the most-asked fact in this section.
  • Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction, and Other Specified / Unspecified Sexual Dysfunction.
  • All require duration of approximately 6 months, clinically significant distress, and that the dysfunction is not better explained by a non-sexual mental disorder, severe relationship distress (partner violence) or other significant stressors, and is not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
  • Specify: lifelong or acquired; generalised or situational; and severity mild, moderate or severe. DSM-5 deleted the DSM-IV subtypes "due to psychological factors" versus "due to combined factors".
Numbers The two to remember across the whole group: 6 months minimum duration and 75 to 100 percent of occasions. Sexual aversion disorder was deleted in DSM-5 for lack of evidence and rare use.
Comorbidity Depressive and anxiety disorders, relationship distress, and, medically, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, prostate surgery, and pelvic floor pathology. Antidepressants, particularly SSRIs, are a leading iatrogenic cause and should always be asked about.
Differential Substance and medication effects (SSRIs, antipsychotics, antihypertensives, alcohol, opioids). Another medical condition (diabetic neuropathy, vascular disease, endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, hypogonadism, hyperprolactinaemia). Another mental disorder, especially depression. Relationship distress and interpersonal factors, including partner violence, which are explicit exclusions. Inadequate sexual stimulation and lack of knowledge, which is common and is not a disorder.

Gender Dysphoria F64.x

In plain language. Distress arising from a marked incongruence between the gender a person experiences themselves to be and the one they were assigned at birth. DSM-5 renamed DSM-IV's Gender Identity Disorder to Gender Dysphoria to shift the diagnostic focus from identity itself to the distress, precisely so that being transgender is not framed as a disorder. That renaming and its rationale is the whole examinable point.
  • In adolescents and adults. A marked incongruence between one's experienced or expressed gender and assigned gender, of at least 6 months duration, with at least 2 of 6: a marked incongruence between experienced gender and primary and/or secondary sex characteristics; a strong desire to be rid of one's primary and/or secondary sex characteristics; a strong desire for the primary and/or secondary sex characteristics of the other gender; a strong desire to be of the other gender; a strong desire to be treated as the other gender; a strong conviction that one has the typical feelings and reactions of the other gender. Plus clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • In children. Duration at least 6 months, with at least 6 of 8, and Criterion A1 is mandatory: a strong desire to be of the other gender or an insistence that one is the other gender. The remaining seven cover cross-dressing preference, cross-gender roles in make-believe play, preference for the toys and activities stereotypically used by the other gender, preference for playmates of the other gender, rejection of the toys and activities typical of one's assigned gender, dislike of one's sexual anatomy, and a desire for the primary or secondary sex characteristics matching one's experienced gender.
  • Specify: with a disorder of sex development; and, for adolescents and adults, posttransition, meaning the person has transitioned to full-time living in the desired gender and has undergone or is preparing for at least one gender-affirming medical procedure. DSM-5-TR updated the language here: "desired gender" became "experienced gender", "cross-sex medical procedure" became "gender-affirming medical procedure", "natal male/female" became "individual assigned male/female at birth".
Numbers Prevalence estimates in adults range roughly 0.005 to 0.014 percent for individuals assigned male at birth and 0.002 to 0.003 percent for those assigned female, though these come from clinic attendance and undercount. In children referred to clinics, rates of persistence into adolescence and adulthood have historically been reported as low for those assigned male and higher for those assigned female, but the figures are contested and the DSM-5-TR text is careful about them.
Comorbidity Anxiety and depressive disorders, notably high rates of suicidality, driven substantially by stigma, family rejection and discrimination (the minority stress framework, which DSM-5-TR names). Autism spectrum disorder co-occurs at rates above the population base rate.
Differential Non-conformity to gender roles, which is explicitly not a disorder. Transvestic disorder (cross-dressing for sexual arousal, with no incongruence of gender identity). Body dysmorphic disorder. Schizophrenia with delusions of belonging to another gender, which is rare. Other clinical presentations such as the desire for castration without a desire for the other gender's characteristics, which ICD-11 codes as body integrity dysphoria.
ICD-11 Went further: it removed gender incongruence from the mental disorders chapter altogether and placed it in a new chapter on conditions related to sexual health, in order to retain access to care while removing the mental-illness framing. That contrast between DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 is high-yield.

Paraphilic Disorders F65.x

In plain language. A paraphilia is an intense and persistent sexual interest in something other than genital stimulation or preparatory fondling with phenotypically normal, physically mature, consenting human partners. A paraphilic disorder is a paraphilia that is currently causing distress or impairment to the individual, or whose satisfaction has entailed personal harm or risk of harm to others. DSM-5's central move was to separate the two: a paraphilia by itself is not a mental disorder. Say that sentence in any answer on this chapter.
  • The eight named disorders: Voyeuristic Disorder (observing an unsuspecting person who is naked, disrobing or engaging in sexual activity; the individual must be at least 18); Exhibitionistic Disorder (exposure of one's genitals to an unsuspecting person; specify whether sexually aroused by exposing to prepubertal children, physically mature individuals, or both); Frotteuristic Disorder (touching or rubbing against a non-consenting person); Sexual Masochism Disorder (being humiliated, beaten, bound or otherwise made to suffer; specify with asphyxiophilia); Sexual Sadism Disorder (the physical or psychological suffering of another person); Pedophilic Disorder; Fetishistic Disorder (use of non-living objects or a highly specific focus on non-genital body parts; the criteria explicitly exclude articles of clothing used in cross-dressing, which would be transvestic disorder, and devices designed for genital stimulation); Transvestic Disorder (cross-dressing; specify with fetishism or with autogynephilia).
  • All require the arousal pattern over a period of at least 6 months, plus either the person having acted on the urges with a non-consenting person, or the urges causing clinically significant distress or impairment.
  • Pedophilic Disorder specifically: over at least 6 months, recurrent, intense sexually arousing fantasies, sexual urges or behaviours involving sexual activity with a prepubescent child or children (generally age 13 or younger); the person has acted on these urges, or they cause marked distress or interpersonal difficulty; and the person is at least 16 years old and at least 5 years older than the child. The criteria exclude an individual in late adolescence involved in an ongoing sexual relationship with a 12 or 13 year old. Specify exclusive or non-exclusive type; sexually attracted to males, females, or both; and whether limited to incest.
  • Every paraphilic disorder can be specified in a controlled environment (where opportunity is restricted, for example institutional settings) and in full remission (no distress, impairment or acting out for at least 5 years while in an uncontrolled environment).
Numbers The three that get asked: pedophilic disorder requires the person to be at least 16 and at least 5 years older than the child, and the child to be generally 13 or younger; voyeuristic disorder requires the person to be at least 18; all require 6 months. Paraphilic disorders are far more common in males; the male to female ratio for most is very high.
Comorbidity Multiple paraphilias frequently coexist in the same individual. Also substance use disorders, depressive and anxiety disorders, ADHD, and antisocial personality disorder, particularly with the coercive paraphilias.
Differential A paraphilia without distress, impairment or harm, which is not a disorder. Conduct disorder and antisocial personality disorder, where the offending is part of a broader antisocial pattern rather than paraphilic arousal. Substance intoxication and major neurocognitive disorder, particularly frontotemporal, where disinhibited sexual behaviour appears late in life without a lifelong pattern. Manic episode. Intellectual disability. Fetishistic versus transvestic disorder is the classic pair: if the clothing is used in cross-dressing, it is transvestic.

15. Disruptive, impulse-control and conduct disorders

Conditions involving problems in the self-control of emotions and behaviour that violate the rights of others or bring the individual into significant conflict with societal norms or authority figures. DSM-5 placed antisocial personality disorder in this chapter as well as in the personality disorders chapter, dual-listing it, because it belongs conceptually to both.

Oppositional Defiant Disorder F91.3

  • A. A pattern of angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behaviour, or vindictiveness lasting at least 6 months, with at least 4 symptoms from any of these three categories, exhibited during interaction with at least one individual who is not a sibling. DSM-5 grouped the eight symptoms into these three types, which is a change from DSM-IV's flat list, and it added the sibling exclusion.
    Angry/irritable mood: often loses temper; is often touchy or easily annoyed; is often angry and resentful.
    Argumentative/defiant behaviour: often argues with authority figures or, for children and adolescents, with adults; often actively defies or refuses to comply with requests from authority figures or with rules; often deliberately annoys others; often blames others for their mistakes or misbehaviour.
    Vindictiveness: has been spiteful or vindictive at least twice within the past 6 months.
  • Frequency rule, which is a DSM-5 addition and is examinable: for children under 5, the behaviour should occur on most days for at least 6 months; for those 5 and older, at least once per week for at least 6 months (except the vindictiveness item).
  • B. Associated with distress in the individual or others in their immediate social context, or it impacts negatively on social, educational, occupational or other areas of functioning.
  • C. Not occurring exclusively during the course of a psychotic, substance use, depressive or bipolar disorder, and criteria for disruptive mood dysregulation disorder are not met. DSM-5 removed the DSM-IV exclusion that prohibited diagnosing ODD alongside conduct disorder, so the two can now be diagnosed together.
  • Severity: mild (symptoms confined to one setting), moderate (two settings), severe (three or more settings).
Numbers Prevalence about 1 to 11 percent, averaging around 3.3 percent. Commoner in males before adolescence (about 1.4:1); the sex difference is not consistently found in adolescents and adults. First symptoms usually appear in the preschool years, and rarely later than early adolescence.
Comorbidity ADHD is the most frequent, and conduct disorder. Also anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder, and substance use disorders in adolescence. ODD is a well-established risk factor for later anxiety and depression, not only for conduct disorder.
Differential Conduct disorder: ODD's defiance is directed at authority and involves no serious violation of others' basic rights or major societal norms, while conduct disorder involves aggression to people and animals, destruction of property, deceitfulness or theft, and serious rule violations. Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder: if criteria for both are met, only DMDD is diagnosed. ADHD: non-compliance driven by inattention and impulsivity, and children with ADHD alone are not typically spiteful or vindictive. Intermittent explosive disorder (high rates of anger but with serious aggression toward others, and no persistent defiance). Depressive and bipolar disorders with irritability. Normal developmental oppositionality in toddlers and adolescents, which is why the frequency and duration rules exist.

Intermittent Explosive Disorder F63.81

  • A. Recurrent behavioural outbursts representing a failure to control aggressive impulses, shown by either: (1) verbal aggression (temper tantrums, tirades, verbal arguments or fights) or physical aggression toward property, animals or other individuals, occurring twice weekly, on average, for 3 months, where the physical aggression does not result in damage or destruction of property and does not result in physical injury to animals or other individuals; or (2) three behavioural outbursts involving damage or destruction of property and/or physical assault involving physical injury against animals or other individuals occurring within a 12-month period.
  • B. The magnitude of aggressiveness expressed is grossly out of proportion to the provocation or to any precipitating psychosocial stressors.
  • C. The outbursts are not premeditated and are not committed to achieve some tangible objective such as money, power or intimidation. That impulsivity criterion is what separates it from instrumental aggression in conduct disorder or antisocial personality disorder.
  • D. Cause marked distress or impairment, or are associated with financial or legal consequences.
  • E. Chronological age is at least 6 (or the equivalent developmental level).
  • F. Not better explained by another mental disorder, and not attributable to another medical condition or a substance. In children aged 6 to 18, aggressive behaviour occurring as part of an adjustment disorder should not be given this diagnosis.
Numbers The two pathways are the exam point: twice weekly for 3 months of non-destructive aggression, or 3 destructive or injurious outbursts in 12 months. Minimum age 6. 12-month prevalence about 2.7 percent in the United States, commoner in those under 35 to 40 and in males. Onset is most commonly in late childhood or adolescence, rarely after 40.
Comorbidity Depressive, anxiety and substance use disorders. Also ADHD, conduct disorder, oppositional defiant disorder, and antisocial and borderline personality disorders.
Differential Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder: DMDD requires persistent negative mood between outbursts, and IED does not, and DMDD needs 12 months and an onset before 10. Antisocial and borderline personality disorders, where aggression occurs but is part of a wider pattern; DSM-5 permits IED alongside them if the outbursts exceed what those disorders usually produce. Conduct disorder (aggression is often premeditated and instrumental). Delirium, major neurocognitive disorder, personality change due to another medical condition. Substance intoxication or withdrawal. Adjustment disorder in children, an explicit exclusion.

Conduct Disorder F91.x

  • A. A repetitive and persistent pattern of behaviour in which the basic rights of others or major age-appropriate societal norms or rules are violated, with at least 3 of 15 criteria in the past 12 months from any of the four categories below, with at least 1 criterion present in the past 6 months.
    Aggression to people and animals: often bullies, threatens or intimidates; often initiates physical fights; has used a weapon that can cause serious physical harm (a bat, brick, broken bottle, knife, gun); has been physically cruel to people; has been physically cruel to animals; has stolen while confronting a victim (mugging, purse snatching, extortion, armed robbery); has forced someone into sexual activity.
    Destruction of property: has deliberately engaged in fire setting with the intention of causing serious damage; has deliberately destroyed others' property other than by fire setting.
    Deceitfulness or theft: has broken into someone else's house, building or car; often lies to obtain goods or favours or to avoid obligations (cons others); has stolen items of nontrivial value without confronting a victim (shoplifting, forgery).
    Serious violations of rules: often stays out at night despite parental prohibitions, beginning before age 13; has run away from home overnight at least twice while living in the parental or surrogate home, or once without returning for a lengthy period; is often truant from school, beginning before age 13.
  • B. Clinically significant impairment in social, academic or occupational functioning. C. If the individual is 18 or older, criteria are not met for antisocial personality disorder.
Specifiers Childhood-onset type (at least one symptom before age 10), adolescent-onset type (no symptoms before age 10), unspecified onset. Severity mild, moderate, severe. And the DSM-5 addition that is asked most: with limited prosocial emotions, requiring at least 2 of 4 characteristics persistently over at least 12 months in more than one relationship or setting: lack of remorse or guilt; callous, lack of empathy; unconcerned about performance at school or work; shallow or deficient affect. This specifier operationalises the callous-unemotional trait and marks a more severe, more stable and more heritable variant with a worse treatment response.
Numbers The arithmetic to hold: 3 of 15 in 12 months, 1 in the past 6 months, and the childhood-onset cut at age 10. 12-month prevalence 2 to 10 percent, median about 4 percent. Substantially commoner in males. Childhood-onset type is male-predominant, more aggressive, and has a worse prognosis, with a higher likelihood of progressing to antisocial personality disorder; adolescent-onset type is more equally distributed by sex and often desists.
Comorbidity ADHD and oppositional defiant disorder, both very common. Also specific learning disorder, anxiety disorders, depressive and bipolar disorders, and substance use disorders.
Differential Oppositional defiant disorder (defiance without rights violations). ADHD (impulsive disruptive behaviour without rule violations). Intermittent explosive disorder (aggression only, and impulsive, without the deceit, theft and rule-breaking). Depressive and bipolar disorders with irritability and aggression confined to mood episodes. Adjustment disorder with disturbance of conduct. Antisocial personality disorder in those 18 and over, which is Criterion C.

Pyromania and Kleptomania F63.1, F63.2

  • Pyromania. Deliberate and purposeful fire setting on more than one occasion; tension or affective arousal before the act; fascination with, interest in, curiosity about, or attraction to fire and its situational contexts (paraphernalia, uses, consequences); pleasure, gratification or relief when setting fires, witnessing them, or participating in their aftermath. The fire setting is not done for monetary gain, as an expression of sociopolitical ideology, to conceal criminal activity, to express anger or vengeance, to improve one's living circumstances, in response to a delusion or hallucination, or as a result of impaired judgment (as in major neurocognitive disorder, intellectual disability or substance intoxication). Not better explained by conduct disorder, a manic episode, or antisocial personality disorder.
  • Kleptomania. Recurrent failure to resist impulses to steal objects that are not needed for personal use or for their monetary value; increasing sense of tension immediately before the theft; pleasure, gratification or relief at the time of committing the theft; the stealing is not committed to express anger or vengeance and is not in response to a delusion or a hallucination; and it is not better explained by conduct disorder, a manic episode or antisocial personality disorder.
Numbers Both are rare. Kleptomania prevalence roughly 0.3 to 0.6 percent, and about 4 to 24 percent of people arrested for shoplifting; female to male about 3:1. Pyromania is rarer still, and is male-predominant. The critical exam point for both: the great majority of shoplifters and fire setters do not have these disorders, because ordinary theft is for gain and ordinary arson is for revenge, insurance or concealment.
Comorbidity Kleptomania: obsessive-compulsive disorder, bipolar and depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, eating disorders (particularly bulimia nervosa), substance use disorders, and other impulse-control disorders. Pyromania: substance use disorders, gambling disorder, depressive and bipolar disorders, and other impulse-control disorders.
Differential Ordinary theft or arson (motive is gain, revenge or concealment). Malingering, feigning kleptomania to avoid prosecution. Manic episode, psychotic disorders, antisocial personality disorder, conduct disorder, major neurocognitive disorder and intellectual disability, all explicit exclusions.

16. Substance-related and addictive disorders

The two DSM-5 changes to lead with: substance abuse and substance dependence were merged into a single Substance Use Disorder rated mild, moderate or severe, and Gambling Disorder was moved into this chapter, making it the first and only behavioural addiction recognised as a full DSM diagnosis. Alongside those, the criterion of recurrent legal problems was deleted and craving was added as a new criterion.

Substance Use Disorder, the general criteria set F1x.x0

In plain language. The same eleven criteria apply to every substance class. They fall into four groups: impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological criteria.
  • Impaired control (4): (1) the substance is taken in larger amounts or over a longer period than was intended; (2) there is a persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down or control use; (3) a great deal of time is spent in activities necessary to obtain, use, or recover from the substance; (4) craving, or a strong desire or urge to use.
  • Social impairment (3): (5) recurrent use resulting in a failure to fulfil major role obligations at work, school or home; (6) continued use despite persistent or recurrent social or interpersonal problems caused or exacerbated by the effects of the substance; (7) important social, occupational or recreational activities are given up or reduced because of use.
  • Risky use (2): (8) recurrent use in situations in which it is physically hazardous; (9) use is continued despite knowledge of having a persistent or recurrent physical or psychological problem that is likely to have been caused or exacerbated by it.
  • Pharmacological (2): (10) tolerance, defined by either a need for markedly increased amounts to achieve intoxication or the desired effect, or a markedly diminished effect with continued use of the same amount; (11) withdrawal, shown by the characteristic withdrawal syndrome for that substance, or by taking the substance (or a closely related one) to relieve or avoid withdrawal symptoms. Important qualification: tolerance and withdrawal occurring during appropriate medical treatment with prescribed medications such as opioid analgesics, sedatives or stimulants do not count as criteria.
  • Severity: mild 2 to 3 symptoms, moderate 4 to 5, severe 6 or more, within a 12-month period. That grading replaces the DSM-IV abuse/dependence dichotomy.
  • Specify: in early remission (none of the criteria, except craving, met for at least 3 months but less than 12 months), in sustained remission (at least 12 months), on maintenance therapy, in a controlled environment.
Numbers Two symptoms is the diagnostic threshold, and the severity bands 2 to 3, 4 to 5, 6 or more, all within 12 months. Early remission is 3 to 12 months, sustained remission 12 months or more. The 10 substance classes DSM-5 recognises: alcohol; caffeine; cannabis; hallucinogens (phencyclidine and other hallucinogens); inhalants; opioids; sedatives, hypnotics and anxiolytics; stimulants (amphetamine-type substances, cocaine, other); tobacco; and other or unknown substances. Note that caffeine has no use disorder in DSM-5, only intoxication, withdrawal, and caffeine-related conditions, with caffeine use disorder placed in Section III for further study. That asymmetry is examined.
Comorbidity Extremely high with depressive, bipolar, anxiety and psychotic disorders, PTSD, ADHD, conduct disorder and antisocial and borderline personality disorders. Multiple substance use disorders commonly co-occur.
Differential Non-pathological substance use, including heavy use that produces no criteria. Substance-induced mental disorders, which are separate diagnoses (substance-induced psychotic, bipolar, depressive, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, sleep, sexual dysfunction, delirium and neurocognitive disorders). Independent primary mental disorders, distinguished by symptoms preceding substance use or persisting about a month beyond acute withdrawal.

Intoxication and withdrawal syndromes worth knowing cold

  • Alcohol intoxication: slurred speech, incoordination, unsteady gait, nystagmus, impairment in attention or memory, stupor or coma. Alcohol withdrawal: within hours to a few days of cessation, with 2 or more of autonomic hyperactivity, increased hand tremor, insomnia, nausea or vomiting, transient hallucinations or illusions, psychomotor agitation, anxiety, generalised tonic-clonic seizures. Specify with perceptual disturbances. Delirium tremens typically appears 48 to 96 hours after the last drink and is a medical emergency. Related conditions to name: Wernicke's encephalopathy (thiamine deficiency: confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia) and Korsakoff's syndrome (anterograde and retrograde amnesia with confabulation), coded in DSM-5 as alcohol-induced major neurocognitive disorder, amnestic-confabulatory type.
  • Opioid intoxication: pupillary constriction plus drowsiness or coma, slurred speech, impairment in attention or memory; severe overdose produces respiratory depression. Opioid withdrawal: dysphoric mood, nausea or vomiting, muscle aches, lacrimation or rhinorrhoea, pupillary dilation, piloerection, sweating, diarrhoea, yawning, fever, insomnia. It is intensely unpleasant but not usually life-threatening, which is the standard contrast with alcohol and sedative withdrawal, both of which can kill.
  • Stimulant (cocaine, amphetamine) intoxication: tachycardia or bradycardia, pupillary dilation, elevated or lowered blood pressure, perspiration or chills, nausea or vomiting, weight loss, psychomotor agitation or retardation, muscular weakness, respiratory depression, chest pain, cardiac arrhythmias, confusion, seizures, dyskinesias, dystonias or coma. Withdrawal: dysphoric mood plus fatigue, vivid unpleasant dreams, insomnia or hypersomnia, increased appetite, psychomotor retardation or agitation, that is, the "crash".
  • Cannabis intoxication: conjunctival injection, increased appetite, dry mouth, tachycardia. Cannabis withdrawal is new in DSM-5: irritability or anger, nervousness or anxiety, sleep difficulty, decreased appetite or weight loss, restlessness, depressed mood, plus at least one physical symptom such as abdominal pain, tremor, sweating, fever, chills or headache.
  • Caffeine intoxication: recent consumption usually in excess of 250 mg, with 5 or more of restlessness, nervousness, excitement, insomnia, flushed face, diuresis, gastrointestinal disturbance, muscle twitching, rambling flow of thought and speech, tachycardia or cardiac arrhythmia, periods of inexhaustibility, psychomotor agitation. Caffeine withdrawal, new in DSM-5: headache, marked fatigue or drowsiness, dysphoric mood or irritability, difficulty concentrating, flu-like symptoms.
  • Tobacco withdrawal: irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, increased appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, insomnia, following at least several weeks of daily use.
  • Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder: re-experiencing, following cessation of hallucinogen use, of one or more of the perceptual symptoms experienced while intoxicated, for example geometric hallucinations, false perceptions of movement in the peripheral visual fields, flashes of colour, intensified colours, trails of images of moving objects, positive afterimages, halos around objects, macropsia and micropsia.
Comorbidity Every intoxication and withdrawal syndrome sits on top of a substance use disorder, and multiple substance use disorders usually travel together (alcohol with tobacco, opioids with sedatives). Add depressive, anxiety, psychotic and neurocognitive disorders, PTSD, ADHD and antisocial and borderline personality disorders, plus the medical load: liver disease, pancreatitis, cardiomyopathy, hepatitis C and HIV.
Differential The first question in every case is delirium, which changes the management. Then, for each picture: opioid versus stimulant intoxication is settled at the pupil (constricted versus dilated). Alcohol or sedative withdrawal versus opioid withdrawal is settled by danger (seizures and delirium tremens can kill, opioid withdrawal is miserable but rarely lethal). Withdrawal versus a primary anxiety or depressive disorder is settled by timing and by whether it clears within about a month of abstinence. Wernicke-Korsakoff versus dissociative amnesia is settled by the ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and confabulation.

Gambling Disorder F63.0

In plain language. The only behavioural addiction in DSM-5. It was moved out of the impulse-control disorders (where DSM-IV called it "pathological gambling") into the substance-related and addictive disorders chapter, on evidence of shared reward circuitry, tolerance-like escalation, withdrawal-like states and comparable comorbidity.
  • A. Persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behaviour leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, shown by 4 or more of 9 in a 12-month period: needs to gamble with increasing amounts of money to achieve the desired excitement (a tolerance analogue); is restless or irritable when attempting to cut down or stop (a withdrawal analogue); has made repeated unsuccessful efforts to control, cut back or stop; is often preoccupied with gambling; often gambles when feeling distressed (helpless, guilty, anxious, depressed); after losing money, often returns another day to get even (chasing losses); lies to conceal the extent of involvement; has jeopardised or lost a significant relationship, job, or educational or career opportunity; relies on others to provide money to relieve desperate financial situations (a bailout).
  • B. The gambling behaviour is not better explained by a manic episode.
  • Specify: episodic or persistent; in early remission (3 to 12 months with no criteria met) or in sustained remission (12 months or more); severity mild 4 to 5, moderate 6 to 7, severe 8 to 9 criteria.
  • Note the DSM-5 change: the DSM-IV criterion of having committed illegal acts such as forgery, fraud, theft or embezzlement to finance gambling was deleted, and the threshold was lowered from 5 of 10 to 4 of 9.
Numbers Past-year prevalence about 0.2 to 0.3 percent; lifetime about 0.4 to 1.0 percent, and higher in men (about 0.6 percent) than women (about 0.2 percent). Men tend to start earlier and to have problems with strategic forms such as sports betting and card games; women start later, progress faster (the "telescoping" effect) and more often use non-strategic forms such as slot machines and bingo.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders, particularly alcohol and tobacco, depressive and bipolar disorders, anxiety disorders, ADHD, and antisocial personality disorder. Suicide risk is markedly elevated.
Differential Non-disordered social or professional gambling. Manic episode, which is Criterion B: a person who gambles heavily only while manic has bipolar disorder, and if the gambling predates and outlasts the mood episodes, both diagnoses are given. Antisocial personality disorder. Other medical conditions: dopamine agonists used for Parkinson's disease produce impulse-control behaviours including gambling, and that iatrogenic cause is a favourite MCQ.
ICD-11 Recognises Gaming Disorder, online and offline, as a full diagnosis. DSM-5-TR does not: Internet Gaming Disorder sits in Section III as a condition for further study. Do not confuse the two systems here.

17. Neurocognitive disorders

The chapter that used to be "Delirium, Dementia, Amnestic and Other Cognitive Disorders". DSM-5 replaced the word dementia with Major Neurocognitive Disorder, partly because "dementia" carries stigma and partly because it was not used for younger adults with acquired impairment from HIV or head injury. It also created Mild Neurocognitive Disorder as a new diagnosis, to capture the pre-dementia stage that the literature calls mild cognitive impairment, so that early intervention becomes possible. Amnestic disorder was absorbed into these categories. Every NCD is then given an aetiological subtype, so you diagnose twice: the syndrome, then the cause.

The six cognitive domains DSM-5 works from

Complex attention (sustained, divided, selective, processing speed); executive function (planning, decision making, working memory, responding to feedback, inhibition, mental flexibility); learning and memory (immediate memory, recent memory including free recall, cued recall and recognition memory, very-long-term memory, implicit learning); language (expressive, including naming, word finding, fluency, grammar and syntax, and receptive); perceptual-motor (visual perception, visuoconstructional, perceptual-motor, praxis, gnosis); social cognition (recognition of emotions, theory of mind). Social cognition is new in DSM-5 and its inclusion is what allows the behavioural-variant frontotemporal presentation to be captured. Learn all six by name.

Delirium F05

In plain language. Attention and awareness break down over hours or days, they wax and wane through the day, and there is a medical cause behind it.
  • A. A disturbance in attention (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain and shift attention) and awareness (reduced orientation to the environment).
  • B. The disturbance develops over a short period of time, usually hours to a few days, represents an acute change from baseline, and tends to fluctuate in severity during the course of a day.
  • C. An additional disturbance in cognition (memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability or perception).
  • D. The disturbances in A and C are not better explained by another pre-existing, established or evolving neurocognitive disorder, and do not occur in the context of a severely reduced level of arousal such as coma.
  • E. Evidence from history, physical examination or laboratory findings that the disturbance is a direct physiological consequence of another medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, exposure to a toxin, or multiple aetiologies.
  • Specify: substance intoxication delirium, substance withdrawal delirium, medication-induced delirium, delirium due to another medical condition, delirium due to multiple aetiologies. Also acute (hours to days) or persistent (weeks to months), and hyperactive, hypoactive or mixed level of activity.
Numbers Prevalence in the general community about 1 to 2 percent, rising to 14 percent in those over 85. On admission to hospital 14 to 24 percent; incidence during hospitalisation 6 to 56 percent; up to 80 percent of patients in intensive care and up to 60 percent in nursing homes at some point. The hypoactive subtype is the commonest in older adults and is routinely missed, being mistaken for depression, which is a classic exam and clinical trap.
Comorbidity Pre-existing major or mild neurocognitive disorder is the single largest risk factor, and delirium superimposed on dementia is extremely common. Also polypharmacy, sensory impairment, infection, dehydration, hip fracture and post-operative states.
Differential Major neurocognitive disorder, and this is the pair to be able to draw as a table: delirium is acute onset with a fluctuating course, impaired attention and consciousness, disturbed sleep-wake cycle, frequent visual hallucinations, and is usually reversible; dementia is insidious in onset with a progressive course, attention and consciousness are preserved until late, hallucinations are less common early, and it is usually irreversible. Psychotic disorders and bipolar or depressive disorders with psychotic features, in which delusions and hallucinations occur without the acute attentional disturbance and fluctuation. Acute stress disorder. Malingering and factitious disorder.

Major and Mild Neurocognitive Disorder, the syndrome criteria

  • Major NCD, Criterion A: evidence of significant cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more of the six cognitive domains, based on both (1) concern of the individual, a knowledgeable informant, or the clinician that there has been a significant decline, and (2) a substantial impairment in cognitive performance, preferably documented by standardised neuropsychological testing or, in its absence, another quantified clinical assessment. The conventional benchmark is performance 2 or more standard deviations below appropriate norms, that is, at or below the 3rd percentile.
  • Major NCD, Criterion B: the cognitive deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities, that is, at a minimum the person requires assistance with complex instrumental activities of daily living such as paying bills or managing medications.
  • Mild NCD, Criterion A: evidence of modest cognitive decline, conventionally 1 to 2 standard deviations below norms, that is, between the 3rd and 16th percentiles.
  • Mild NCD, Criterion B: the cognitive deficits do not interfere with independence in everyday activities, though greater effort, compensatory strategies or accommodation may be required. That independence line is the entire major-versus-mild distinction, and it is asked constantly.
  • C. The deficits do not occur exclusively in the context of a delirium. D. The deficits are not better explained by another mental disorder such as major depressive disorder or schizophrenia.
  • Specify: the aetiological subtype, and for major NCD, without behavioural disturbance or with behavioural disturbance (psychotic symptoms, mood disturbance, agitation, apathy or other behavioural symptoms), plus severity mild (difficulties with instrumental activities of daily living such as housework and managing money), moderate (difficulties with basic activities such as feeding and dressing), severe (fully dependent).
Comorbidity Depression and anxiety are extremely common at the mild stage, when insight is retained, and apathy, agitation and psychosis appear as the disorder progresses; DSM-5 captures these with the with behavioural disturbance specifier. Delirium is superimposed frequently. Add the vascular risk load (hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation), sensory impairment, falls and malnutrition.
Differential Delirium, which must be excluded first (Criterion C). Major depressive disorder, that is, depressive pseudodementia. Normal ageing, which is why Criterion A2 asks for standardised testing. Intellectual disability, where the ability was never acquired rather than lost. Schizophrenia with cognitive impairment, distinguished by the lifelong history. Malingering and factitious disorder where compensation or litigation is in play.

The aetiological subtypes, with what distinguishes each

  • Alzheimer's disease. Insidious onset and gradual progression, with early and prominent impairment in learning and recent memory. Probable Alzheimer's requires evidence of a causative genetic mutation from family history or genetic testing, or all three of: clear evidence of decline in memory and learning plus at least one other domain; steadily progressive gradual decline without extended plateaus; and no evidence of mixed aetiology. Otherwise possible. It is the commonest cause of major NCD, accounting for roughly 60 to 80 percent of cases. Pathology: extracellular beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau, with early atrophy in the medial temporal lobe and hippocampus and a cholinergic deficit arising from the nucleus basalis of Meynert. Genetics: APOE e4 raises risk for late onset; mutations in APP (chromosome 21), presenilin 1 (chromosome 14) and presenilin 2 (chromosome 1) cause early-onset autosomal dominant disease, and people with Down syndrome almost all develop Alzheimer pathology by their forties because of the extra copy of APP on chromosome 21.
  • Frontotemporal NCD. Insidious onset and gradual progression with either a behavioural variant (3 or more of: behavioural disinhibition, apathy or inertia, loss of sympathy or empathy, perseverative, stereotyped or compulsive/ritualistic behaviour, hyperorality and dietary changes, plus prominent decline in social cognition and/or executive ability) or a language variant (primary progressive aphasia, with prominent decline in speech production, word finding, object naming, grammar or word comprehension). Learning, memory and perceptual-motor function are relatively spared, which is the discriminator against Alzheimer's. Onset is typically earlier, in the fifties and sixties. Pathology: tau or TDP-43 inclusions; Pick bodies in the classic Pick's disease form.
  • NCD with Lewy Bodies. Core diagnostic features: fluctuating cognition with pronounced variations in attention and alertness; recurrent well-formed and detailed visual hallucinations; and spontaneous features of parkinsonism, with onset subsequent to the development of cognitive decline. Suggestive features: meeting criteria for REM sleep behaviour disorder, and severe neuroleptic sensitivity, which is a safety point as well as an exam point. The conventional one-year rule: if cognitive decline begins at least a year before parkinsonism, it is NCD with Lewy bodies; if motor signs clearly precede cognition by more than a year, it is major NCD due to Parkinson's disease.
  • Vascular NCD. The clinical features are consistent with a vascular aetiology, shown by either onset of the deficits temporally related to one or more cerebrovascular events, or evidence of decline that is prominent in complex attention, including processing speed, and in frontal-executive function. There is evidence of cerebrovascular disease from history, physical examination and/or neuroimaging sufficient to account for the deficits. Course is classically stepwise rather than smoothly progressive, with focal neurological signs. The second commonest cause of major NCD, and it frequently coexists with Alzheimer pathology as a mixed dementia.
  • NCD due to Traumatic Brain Injury. There is evidence of a TBI with one or more of: loss of consciousness, post-traumatic amnesia, disorientation and confusion, or neurological signs. The NCD presents immediately after the injury or immediately after recovery of consciousness and persists past the acute post-injury period.
  • Substance/Medication-Induced NCD. The impairments do not occur exclusively during delirium and persist beyond the usual duration of intoxication and acute withdrawal. Alcohol is the leading cause; specify persistent where deficits continue after an extended period of abstinence.
  • NCD due to HIV Infection. Documented HIV infection, with a pattern that is not better explained by secondary brain disease such as progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy or cryptococcal meningitis. Typically subcortical, with slowed processing and executive difficulty.
  • NCD due to Prion Disease. Insidious onset with rapid progression, plus motor features such as myoclonus or ataxia, or biomarker evidence. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is the archetype, and rapid progression over months is the discriminator.
  • NCD due to Parkinson's Disease. Insidious onset and gradual progression in an individual with established Parkinson's disease, where the disturbance is not attributable to another condition. Probable requires no evidence of mixed aetiology and clearly preceding Parkinson's motor disease.
  • NCD due to Huntington's Disease. Insidious onset and gradual progression, with either clinically established Huntington's or risk based on family history or genetic testing. Autosomal dominant, caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion on chromosome 4; presents with chorea, and cognitive change is early and executive.
  • NCD due to Another Medical Condition and due to Multiple Aetiologies, plus Unspecified NCD.
Comorbidity Each subtype carries its own load. Alzheimer's with depression and later psychosis and agitation. Vascular with stroke, hypertension, diabetes and post-stroke depression. Lewy body with REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression, orthostatic hypotension and severe antipsychotic sensitivity. Frontotemporal with motor neurone disease in a subset, and with the highest rate of being misdiagnosed as a primary psychiatric disorder. Traumatic brain injury with PTSD, substance use and chronic pain. Substance-induced with the substance use disorder itself and with Wernicke-Korsakoff.
The differential that decides marks Alzheimer's versus frontotemporal: memory first versus behaviour or language first, later onset versus earlier onset, insight lost early in FTD. Alzheimer's versus Lewy body: steady decline versus fluctuating attention, visual hallucinations early in Lewy body, parkinsonism, neuroleptic sensitivity, REM sleep behaviour disorder. Alzheimer's versus vascular: smooth progression versus stepwise, memory-first versus executive-and-speed-first, focal signs and imaging. Any dementia versus delirium: onset, course, and attention. Any dementia versus depressive pseudodementia: in depression the onset is more abrupt and datable, the person complains loudly of memory loss rather than concealing it, answers "I don't know" rather than confabulating, effort is poor but performance improves with encouragement, and there is a mood history. Any dementia versus normal ageing, which is Criterion A2 and the reason for standardised testing.

18. Personality disorders

An enduring pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from the expectations of the individual's culture, is pervasive and inflexible, has an onset in adolescence or early adulthood, is stable over time, and leads to distress or impairment. The pattern must be manifest in 2 or more of four areas: cognition (ways of perceiving and interpreting self, other people and events), affectivity (the range, intensity, lability and appropriateness of emotional response), interpersonal functioning, and impulse control. Learn that general criteria set, because a question that asks "what are the general diagnostic criteria for a personality disorder" is asking for exactly those four areas plus the pervasiveness, stability, onset and exclusion clauses. DSM-5 kept the ten categorical disorders in three clusters unchanged from DSM-IV; the alternative dimensional model sits in Section III.

The three clusters

Cluster A, the odd or eccentric: paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal. Cluster B, the dramatic, emotional or erratic: antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic. Cluster C, the anxious or fearful: avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive. The clusters have limited validity and high co-occurrence, and DSM-5 says so; that criticism is worth stating when you list them.

Cluster A: Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal F60.0, F60.1, F21

  • Paranoid Personality Disorder. A pervasive distrust and suspiciousness of others such that their motives are interpreted as malevolent, beginning by early adulthood, with 4 or more of 7: suspects without sufficient basis that others are exploiting, harming or deceiving them; is preoccupied with unjustified doubts about the loyalty or trustworthiness of friends or associates; is reluctant to confide in others because of unwarranted fear that the information will be used maliciously against them; reads hidden demeaning or threatening meanings into benign remarks or events; persistently bears grudges; perceives attacks on their character or reputation that are not apparent to others and is quick to react angrily or to counterattack; has recurrent suspicions, without justification, regarding the fidelity of a spouse or sexual partner.
  • Schizoid Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of detachment from social relationships and a restricted range of expression of emotions, with 4 or more of 7: neither desires nor enjoys close relationships, including being part of a family; almost always chooses solitary activities; has little if any interest in having sexual experiences with another person; takes pleasure in few if any activities; lacks close friends or confidants other than first-degree relatives; appears indifferent to the praise or criticism of others; shows emotional coldness, detachment or flattened affectivity.
  • Schizotypal Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of social and interpersonal deficits marked by acute discomfort with, and reduced capacity for, close relationships, plus cognitive or perceptual distortions and eccentricities of behaviour, with 5 or more of 9: ideas of reference (excluding delusions of reference); odd beliefs or magical thinking that influences behaviour and is inconsistent with subcultural norms (superstitiousness, belief in clairvoyance, telepathy or a sixth sense; in children and adolescents, bizarre fantasies or preoccupations); unusual perceptual experiences including bodily illusions; odd thinking and speech (vague, circumstantial, metaphorical, overelaborate or stereotyped); suspiciousness or paranoid ideation; inappropriate or constricted affect; behaviour or appearance that is odd, eccentric or peculiar; lack of close friends or confidants other than first-degree relatives; excessive social anxiety that does not diminish with familiarity and tends to be associated with paranoid fears rather than negative judgments about self. Note that schizotypal is dual-listed, appearing in both the personality disorders chapter and the schizophrenia spectrum chapter, because it is genetically related to schizophrenia.
Comorbidity Cluster A disorders co-occur with each other, with depressive disorders, and with schizophrenia spectrum disorders in relatives. Schizotypal carries the highest risk of transition to a psychotic disorder.
Differential Schizoid versus schizotypal: both are socially isolated, but schizotypal adds the cognitive and perceptual oddness, which schizoid lacks. Schizoid versus avoidant: the avoidant person wants relationships but fears rejection, the schizoid person does not want them. That pair is asked more often than any other in the personality chapter. Paranoid personality versus delusional disorder: suspiciousness that never reaches delusional conviction. Autism spectrum disorder, which can look schizoid but includes restricted repetitive behaviours and has a developmental onset. Schizophrenia in prodrome or residual phase. Personality change due to another medical condition and substance effects.

Cluster B: Antisocial, Borderline, Histrionic, Narcissistic F60.2, F60.3, F60.4, F60.81

  • Antisocial Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of disregard for and violation of the rights of others, occurring since age 15, with 3 or more of 7: failure to conform to social norms with respect to lawful behaviours, as shown by repeatedly performing acts that are grounds for arrest; deceitfulness, as shown by repeated lying, use of aliases or conning others for personal profit or pleasure; impulsivity or failure to plan ahead; irritability and aggressiveness, as shown by repeated physical fights or assaults; reckless disregard for the safety of self or others; consistent irresponsibility, as shown by repeated failure to sustain consistent work behaviour or honour financial obligations; lack of remorse, as shown by being indifferent to or rationalising having hurt, mistreated or stolen from another. B. The individual is at least 18. C. There is evidence of conduct disorder with onset before age 15. D. The antisocial behaviour does not occur exclusively during schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Those three age-related clauses (since 15, at least 18, conduct disorder before 15) are the most examined arithmetic in the chapter.
  • Borderline Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of instability of interpersonal relationships, self-image and affects, and marked impulsivity, with 5 or more of 9: frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment (not including the self-harm in criterion 5); a pattern of unstable and intense interpersonal relationships characterised by alternating between extremes of idealisation and devaluation (splitting); identity disturbance, that is, a markedly and persistently unstable self-image or sense of self; impulsivity in at least two areas that are potentially self-damaging (spending, sex, substance abuse, reckless driving, binge eating), not including the self-harm in criterion 5; recurrent suicidal behaviour, gestures or threats, or self-mutilating behaviour; affective instability due to a marked reactivity of mood, with episodic dysphoria, irritability or anxiety usually lasting a few hours and only rarely more than a few days; chronic feelings of emptiness; inappropriate intense anger or difficulty controlling anger; transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms.
  • Histrionic Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention seeking, with 5 or more of 8: is uncomfortable in situations in which they are not the centre of attention; interaction with others is often characterised by inappropriate sexually seductive or provocative behaviour; displays rapidly shifting and shallow expression of emotions; consistently uses physical appearance to draw attention to self; has a style of speech that is excessively impressionistic and lacking in detail; shows self-dramatisation, theatricality and exaggerated expression of emotion; is suggestible, that is, easily influenced by others or circumstances; considers relationships to be more intimate than they actually are.
  • Narcissistic Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of grandiosity in fantasy or behaviour, need for admiration and lack of empathy, with 5 or more of 9: has a grandiose sense of self-importance; is preoccupied with fantasies of unlimited success, power, brilliance, beauty or ideal love; believes they are special and unique and can only be understood by, or should associate with, other special or high-status people or institutions; requires excessive admiration; has a sense of entitlement; is interpersonally exploitative; lacks empathy; is often envious of others or believes that others are envious of them; shows arrogant, haughty behaviours or attitudes.
Numbers Borderline prevalence about 1.6 percent in the community, rising to about 6 percent in primary care, 10 percent in psychiatric outpatients and 20 percent in inpatients; roughly 75 percent of clinical cases are female, though community samples are closer to even. About 8 to 10 percent die by suicide, which is roughly 50 times the general population rate. Antisocial personality disorder prevalence about 0.2 to 3.3 percent, strongly male-predominant, and much higher in prison populations. Narcissistic 0 to 6.2 percent, with 50 to 75 percent male. Histrionic about 1.84 percent.
Comorbidity Borderline: depressive and bipolar disorders, substance use disorders, eating disorders (especially bulimia), PTSD, ADHD, and other personality disorders. Antisocial: substance use disorders, anxiety and depressive disorders, gambling disorder, somatic symptom disorder, and other Cluster B disorders. Narcissistic: depressive disorders, anorexia nervosa, substance use disorders (particularly cocaine), and other personality disorders.
Differential Borderline versus bipolar II, the one that decides marks: borderline mood shifts last hours and are reactive to interpersonal events, especially perceived abandonment, while bipolar episodes last days to weeks, are relatively autonomous, and bring changes in sleep need and goal-directed activity; borderline also has chronic emptiness, identity disturbance and self-harm as the stable trait background. Borderline versus histrionic: both seek attention, but borderline adds self-destructiveness, angry disruptions of relationships and chronic emptiness. Narcissistic versus antisocial: both are exploitative, but antisocial adds impulsivity, aggression, deceit for material gain and a conduct-disorder history, while narcissistic exploitation serves admiration and status. Antisocial versus adult antisocial behaviour (a V code, where the behaviour is not part of a personality disorder, for example professional criminality without the broader pattern). Personality change due to another medical condition and substance use disorders, in which the behaviour appears only in the context of use.

Cluster C: Avoidant, Dependent, Obsessive-Compulsive F60.6, F60.7, F60.5

  • Avoidant Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation, with 4 or more of 7: avoids occupational activities that involve significant interpersonal contact because of fears of criticism, disapproval or rejection; is unwilling to get involved with people unless certain of being liked; shows restraint within intimate relationships because of the fear of being shamed or ridiculed; is preoccupied with being criticised or rejected in social situations; is inhibited in new interpersonal situations because of feelings of inadequacy; views self as socially inept, personally unappealing or inferior to others; is unusually reluctant to take personal risks or to engage in any new activities because they may prove embarrassing.
  • Dependent Personality Disorder. A pervasive and excessive need to be taken care of that leads to submissive and clinging behaviour and fears of separation, with 5 or more of 8: has difficulty making everyday decisions without an excessive amount of advice and reassurance from others; needs others to assume responsibility for most major areas of their life; has difficulty expressing disagreement with others because of fear of loss of support or approval; has difficulty initiating projects or doing things on their own because of a lack of self-confidence in judgment or abilities rather than a lack of motivation or energy; goes to excessive lengths to obtain nurturance and support from others, to the point of volunteering to do things that are unpleasant; feels uncomfortable or helpless when alone because of exaggerated fears of being unable to care for themselves; urgently seeks another relationship as a source of care and support when a close relationship ends; is unrealistically preoccupied with fears of being left to take care of themselves.
  • Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder. A pervasive pattern of preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism and mental and interpersonal control, at the expense of flexibility, openness and efficiency, with 4 or more of 8: is preoccupied with details, rules, lists, order, organisation or schedules to the extent that the major point of the activity is lost; shows perfectionism that interferes with task completion, for example being unable to complete a project because their own overly strict standards are not met; is excessively devoted to work and productivity to the exclusion of leisure activities and friendships, not accounted for by obvious economic necessity; is overconscientious, scrupulous and inflexible about matters of morality, ethics or values, not accounted for by cultural or religious identification; is unable to discard worn-out or worthless objects even when they have no sentimental value; is reluctant to delegate tasks or to work with others unless they submit to exactly their way of doing things; adopts a miserly spending style toward both self and others, money being viewed as something to be hoarded for future catastrophes; shows rigidity and stubbornness.
Numbers Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder is the most prevalent personality disorder in the general population, roughly 2.1 to 7.9 percent, and is about twice as common in males. Avoidant about 2.4 percent, roughly equal by sex. Dependent about 0.5 to 0.6 percent, and it is diagnosed more often in females, which DSM-5-TR flags as possibly reflecting sex bias in diagnosis rather than true prevalence.
Comorbidity Avoidant with social anxiety disorder (very high), depressive and bipolar disorders, and dependent and borderline personality disorders. Dependent with depressive, anxiety and adjustment disorders. Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder with obsessive-compulsive disorder in a minority, hoarding disorder, eating disorders and depressive disorders.
Differential Avoidant versus social anxiety disorder: overlapping to the point that many argue they are the same dimension, with avoidant being earlier, more pervasive and involving a more global sense of inadequacy; DSM-5 permits both diagnoses together. Avoidant versus schizoid, as above, decided by whether the person desires relationships. Avoidant versus dependent: both are clinging and fear rejection, but the avoidant person avoids relationships until certain of acceptance, while the dependent person rushes into them and clings. OCPD versus OCD: OCPD has no true obsessions or compulsions and is ego-syntonic, OCD has intrusive ego-dystonic obsessions with ritualised compulsions; they can coexist, and only about a quarter to a third of OCD patients have OCPD. OCPD versus hoarding disorder: the miserly and object-retention criteria overlap, and if hoarding is prominent, hoarding disorder should be diagnosed as well.

The other three personality diagnoses

  • Personality Change Due to Another Medical Condition. A persistent personality disturbance that represents a change from the individual's previous characteristic pattern, with direct pathophysiological evidence of a medical cause, not better explained by another mental disorder, and not occurring exclusively during delirium. Specify labile, disinhibited, aggressive, apathetic, paranoid, other, combined or unspecified type. Classic causes: frontal lobe lesions, epilepsy, traumatic brain injury, tumour, Huntington's, multiple sclerosis, endocrine disease. The Phineas Gage case is the standard illustration.
  • Other Specified Personality Disorder and Unspecified Personality Disorder, used for presentations with mixed features or where criteria for no single disorder are fully met. This category is heavily used in practice, which is itself one of the standard criticisms of the categorical system.
Comorbidity Personality change due to another medical condition sits alongside the causative disease and, very often, depression, apathy and the family distress the change produces. The other specified and unspecified categories carry the same comorbid load as the specific disorders they resemble: mood, anxiety and substance use disorders above all.
Two rules that answer many questions Age. Personality disorders may be diagnosed in individuals under 18 only if the features have been present for at least 1 year, with the single exception that antisocial personality disorder cannot be diagnosed before age 18 under any circumstances. Culture. The pattern must deviate from the expectations of the individual's own culture, so a habit of deference or of family interdependence that is normative in an Indian joint family is not evidence of dependent personality disorder. Both rules are examinable and both are commonly missed.

19. Section III: the alternative model, culture, and conditions for further study

DSM-5 is organised in three sections. Section I is the introduction, the definition of a mental disorder, and the use of the manual. Section II is the diagnostic criteria and codes, the operational manual you actually diagnose from. Section III is "Emerging Measures and Models": assessment measures, the cultural formulation, the alternative model for personality disorders, and conditions for further study. Knowing that three-part structure, and knowing which things live in Section III rather than Section II, is worth easy marks.

The Alternative DSM-5 Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD)

A hybrid dimensional-categorical model, proposed for research and placed in Section III because the field could not agree to replace the categories outright. It has two criteria:

  • Criterion A: level of personality functioning, rated on the Level of Personality Functioning Scale across four elements grouped in two domains. Self: identity and self-direction. Interpersonal: empathy and intimacy. Rated 0 (little or no impairment) to 4 (extreme impairment); moderate impairment or greater, that is a rating of 2 or more, is required for a personality disorder.
  • Criterion B: pathological personality traits, in 5 broad domains subdivided into 25 facets: negative affectivity (versus emotional stability), detachment (versus extraversion), antagonism (versus agreeableness), disinhibition (versus conscientiousness), and psychoticism (versus lucidity). Notice that four of the five are maladaptive variants of the Big Five, with openness replaced by psychoticism, and this mapping is exactly what examiners ask for. The trait measure is the PID-5, the Personality Inventory for DSM-5.
  • The model retains only six specific personality disorders: antisocial, avoidant, borderline, narcissistic, obsessive-compulsive and schizotypal, plus personality disorder, trait specified for everything else. The four dropped from the alternative model are paranoid, schizoid, histrionic and dependent.

The Cultural Formulation and the DSM-5-TR treatment of culture

  • The Outline for Cultural Formulation asks for: cultural identity of the individual; cultural conceptualisations of distress; psychosocial stressors and cultural features of vulnerability and resilience; cultural features of the relationship between the individual and the clinician; and overall cultural assessment.
  • The Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI) is a 16-question semi-structured interview, with an informant version and 12 supplementary modules, new in DSM-5.
  • DSM-5 replaced the DSM-IV term culture-bound syndromes with three more precise concepts: cultural syndromes (clusters of symptoms that co-occur in a specific cultural group), cultural idioms of distress (ways of expressing distress that may not refer to specific symptoms, for example "thinking too much"), and cultural explanations or perceived causes (explanatory models, for example nervios, maladi moun). That three-way replacement is a stock question.
  • The named cultural concepts to be able to list: ataque de nervios (Latino, uncontrollable shouting, crying, trembling, heat in the chest rising to the head, often after a family stressor), dhat syndrome (South Asian, anxiety and somatic complaints attributed to semen loss in urine), khyal cap or wind attacks (Cambodian), kufungisisa ("thinking too much", Zimbabwe), maladi moun (Haitian, sent sickness), nervios, shenjing shuairuo (Chinese neurasthenia), susto (fright-induced illness, Latin America), taijin kyofusho (Japanese, fear of offending others through one's appearance or behaviour). Dhat, koro and possession states are the ones most likely to appear in an Indian entrance paper.

Conditions for Further Study, the Section III list

Not official diagnoses. If a question offers one of these as an answer for "which is a DSM-5 diagnosis", it is a distractor. They are: attenuated psychosis syndrome; depressive episodes with short-duration hypomania; persistent complex bereavement disorder (superseded in DSM-5-TR by prolonged grief disorder in Section II, so this one has effectively been promoted and renamed); caffeine use disorder; internet gaming disorder; neurobehavioural disorder associated with prenatal alcohol exposure; suicidal behaviour disorder and non-suicidal self-injury (both of which DSM-5-TR converted into coded conditions that can be recorded alongside any diagnosis, so they too moved forward).

Other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention

These are the Z codes, and they are not mental disorders. They include relational problems, abuse and neglect, educational and occupational problems, housing and economic problems, problems related to the social environment, problems related to crime or interaction with the legal system, and other health service encounters. Three that are examined specifically: malingering, adult antisocial behaviour and child or adolescent antisocial behaviour, borderline intellectual functioning, bereavement (uncomplicated), age-related cognitive decline, and non-adherence to medical treatment. If a question asks "is malingering a mental disorder in DSM-5?", the answer is no, it is a condition that may be a focus of clinical attention.

The DSM-5 and DSM-5-TR change list, in one paragraph

Multiaxial system abolished, GAF replaced by WHODAS 2.0. Schizophrenia subtypes deleted and the Schneiderian single-symptom concession removed. Autism's four disorders collapsed into one spectrum, triad reduced to a dyad, sensory criterion added. ADHD onset age raised from 7 to 12 and the symptom count reduced to 5 at age 17. Specific learning disorder's IQ-achievement discrepancy requirement abolished. Bereavement exclusion removed from major depressive disorder. DMDD and PMDD added; dysthymia and chronic MDD merged into persistent depressive disorder. Panic disorder and agoraphobia unlinked, agoraphobia now needs 2 of 5 situations, panic attack becomes a universal specifier, and the 6-month rule extended to all ages. A new obsessive-compulsive and related disorders chapter with hoarding and excoriation added and insight specifiers throughout. A new trauma and stressor-related chapter, with PTSD gaining a fourth cluster, losing Criterion A2, gaining a dissociative subtype and a preschool subtype. Dissociative fugue demoted to a specifier. Somatoform disorders rebuilt around positive psychological criteria, with somatisation disorder, hypochondriasis and pain disorder deleted and vaginismus and dyspareunia merged. Anorexia's amenorrhoea criterion deleted, bulimia's frequency halved, binge-eating disorder and ARFID added. Substance abuse and dependence merged into a graded substance use disorder, legal problems deleted, craving added, gambling disorder moved in as the first behavioural addiction. Dementia renamed major neurocognitive disorder, mild NCD created, social cognition added as a sixth domain. Personality disorders unchanged in Section II, with the alternative dimensional model in Section III. Then, in DSM-5-TR 2022: prolonged grief disorder added, unspecified mood disorder restored, suicidal behaviour and non-suicidal self-injury given codes, and the whole text revised for cultural and ethnoracial accuracy.
