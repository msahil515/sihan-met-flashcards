In March 1985, Clive Wearing was at the top of his profession. He was a British musician and musicologist, a producer of music programmes for BBC radio and an authority on the Renaissance composer Orlande de Lassus. Then he fell ill with what looked like a bad flu and a headache that would not go away. It was herpes simplex encephalitis: the same virus that causes ordinary cold sores had invaded his brain, and within days it had destroyed the hippocampus on both sides and damaged large parts of his temporal and frontal lobes.

Clive survived, but the man who woke up was trapped inside a moment that lasted only seconds. He could hold a conversation as long as it kept moving, and he could follow a sentence to its end. But a short while after any experience ended, it was gone, and with it any sense that it had ever happened. He felt, continuously, as if he had just regained consciousness for the first time. His diary filled with page after page of entries, each giving the time and announcing that he was now, at last, truly awake; every earlier entry, which he had no memory of writing, was angrily crossed out. When his wife Deborah stepped out of the room for a few minutes and came back, he greeted her with tears of joy, as if they had been apart for years.

The damage reached backwards as well as forwards. Almost every particular episode of his earlier life had become inaccessible to him. He knew certain facts, that he was a musician and that he was married to Deborah, but he could bring back almost no specific event from the decades he had lived.

And yet some things survived untouched. Seat Clive at a piano or in front of a choir and he could still sight-read, play and conduct with his old skill; the music carried him from one bar to the next for as long as it lasted. Nor did he ever lose his love for Deborah or his recognition of her, although he could not remember a single conversation with her from one visit to the next.

Clive Wearing's case raises nearly every question this chapter tries to answer. How can a person hold a thought for a few seconds but not for a minute? How can someone lose his whole personal past and still remember how to play the piano? Is there one memory or many? Where do memories go when we forget them, and why do some of them come back changed? Keep Clive in mind; you will meet him again at the end of the chapter.

7.1 What Memory Is, and How Ebbinghaus Measured ItMedium yield · 5 Qs

You use memory in everything you do. Reading this sentence requires you to hold its first words in mind until you reach its last ones; understanding it requires the meanings of words you learned years ago; and if you can answer a question about it next week, it is because something of it was kept. memory is the capacity to retain information over time and to use it later. Notice that this definition says nothing about consciousness: as Clive Wearing's piano playing shows, much of what we retain is expressed in what we can do rather than in what we can report.

Three processes: encoding, storage and retrieval

Psychologists find it useful to break remembering into three processes, an idea that grew out of the information-processing approach of the 1950s and 1960s, which treated the mind as a system that takes in, transforms, stores and outputs information, much as a computer does. The study of mental processes such as attention, memory and problem solving through this information-processor metaphor is cognitive psychology, whose rise is told in Chapter 2. encoding is the transformation of incoming information into a form, or code, that the memory system can hold. storage is the maintenance of encoded information over time. retrieval is the recovery of stored information when it is needed. A library is a fair analogy: books must be acquired and catalogued (encoding), kept on the shelves (storage), and found again when a reader asks for them (retrieval). A book can be missing because it was never bought, because it was lost from the shelf, or because it was shelved under the wrong number and cannot be found; in the same way, a failure to remember can be a failure at any of the three stages.

The first stage depends heavily on attention. What you do not attend to is usually never encoded at all. In 1979 Raymond Nickerson and Marilyn Adams asked American adults to pick out the correct drawing of a one-cent coin from fifteen versions; fewer than half chose correctly, although every one of them had handled thousands of such coins. You can try the same test on yourself with a ten-rupee coin. The failure is not forgetting in the ordinary sense but encoding failure: the details were never encoded, because nothing in daily life requires you to attend to them, and what was never stored cannot later be retrieved. Attention itself is the subject of Chapter 5.

Ebbinghaus and the first experiments on memory

For most of the nineteenth century, memory was a subject for philosophers. Even Wilhelm Wundt, who founded the first psychology laboratory in 1879, doubted that the higher mental processes could be studied by experiment. The person who proved otherwise was Hermann Ebbinghaus (1850 to 1909), a young German scholar working alone, without a laboratory, who had been inspired by Gustav Fechner's demonstration that sensations could be measured (Chapter 3). If sensations could be measured, Ebbinghaus reasoned, why not memories?

His first problem was material. Real words and poems come loaded with associations: a word you already know is not learned from scratch, and some words are far easier than others. So Ebbinghaus invented the nonsense syllable, a pronounceable but meaningless consonant-vowel-consonant combination such as DAX, BUP or ZOL. He built a pool of about 2,300 of them, drew lists at random, and, acting as his own and only participant, read each list aloud at a steady rate until he could recite it without error. Then, after a delay that ranged from twenty minutes to a month, he learned the same list again to the same standard.

His measure of what remained was ingenious. Even when he could not recall a single syllable, relearning a list was faster than learning it had been the first time. The savings method expresses this as a percentage: savings equals the time (or number of trials) for original learning minus the time for relearning, divided by the time for original learning, multiplied by 100. Here is a worked example. Suppose a list took 20 readings to learn on Monday and 12 readings to relearn on Friday. The savings are (20 minus 12) divided by 20, multiplied by 100, which is 40 per cent. Forty per cent of the original work did not have to be repeated, so something of Monday's learning survived, even if Friday's attempt at recall produced nothing at all.

Key study: Ebbinghaus, Memory (1885)

Ebbinghaus published his results in 1885 as Über das Gedächtnis (On Memory; translated into English in 1913). His central result was the forgetting curve: savings fell steeply at first and then levelled off. After twenty minutes he had already lost about 42 per cent of the original learning; after an hour, more than half; after a day, about two thirds. But after that the loss slowed dramatically, and a month later about a fifth of the learning still survived (Figure 7.1). He also found that a list of about seven syllables could be repeated after a single reading but that the number of readings needed rose steeply for longer lists; that extra repetitions beyond the point of mastery increased later savings; that repetitions spread over several days were more efficient than the same repetitions crowded into one sitting; that syllables at the beginning and end of a list were learned before those in the middle; and that meaningful material (stanzas of poetry) needed far fewer readings than nonsense syllables.

The criticisms are obvious and Ebbinghaus knew most of them: one participant, who was also the experimenter; artificial material stripped of meaning; and syllables that were not, in fact, equally meaningless (later researchers measured the "association value" of each syllable). Frederic Bartlett would later argue that by removing meaning Ebbinghaus had removed the most important thing about human memory (Section 7.9). Even so, his curves have been replicated many times, and his work showed that memory could be studied with the same precision as sensation.

Two features of Figure 7.1 are worth fixing in your mind, because examiners return to them. First, the curve is negatively accelerated: forgetting is fastest immediately after learning and slows as time passes. Second, it never reaches zero; Ebbinghaus's savings method detected traces of learning long after recall had failed. Ebbinghaus also left psychology one of its best-known sentences, from a textbook of 1908: "Psychology has a long past, but only a short history."

Exam trap

A question asks what Hermann Ebbinghaus, Francis Galton and Sigmund Freud have in common. All three worked largely independently and were pioneers, and each devised a new method for studying the mind (Ebbinghaus the nonsense syllable and savings, Galton questionnaires and word association, Freud free association). None of them founded a formal psychology laboratory in the sense that Wundt did (Galton's Anthropometric Laboratory, opened in London in 1884, measured physical and sensory traits of the public rather than running psychological experiments). The RML 2020 key gives "each worked independently and were pioneers" alone, while the Panwar bank keys "(a) and (c)". The difference turns on the word "observational": Ebbinghaus's method was experimental, not observational, which is the likely reason the RML key stops at (a). Read the options carefully and choose the statement that is true of all three without qualification.

Measuring retention: recall, recognition, relearning and reconstruction

Ebbinghaus measured savings, but memory can be measured in several ways, and the same memory can look strong on one test and absent on another. All of the methods below are explicit tests: the person knows that memory is being tested and tries to remember. (Implicit tests, where memory shows itself without any attempt to remember, are described in Section 7.5.)

recall requires the person to reproduce information with few or no hints. It comes in three forms. In free recall the person reports the items in any order, as when you list the films you saw last year. In serial recall the items must be reproduced in the order presented, as when you repeat a phone number. In cued recall a hint is given, such as a category name or the first letter. A special and historically important form of cued recall is paired-associate learning, introduced by Mary Whiton Calkins (1863 to 1930) in 1894: the person learns pairs of items (HOUSE and 47, or a Kannada word and its English meaning) and is later given the first member of each pair as a cue for the second. Learning the vocabulary of a new language is paired-associate learning in everyday life.

recognition requires the person only to identify previously encountered items among new ones, called distractors or lures. It may take a yes or no form ("Was this face in the set?") or a forced-choice form, like the multiple-choice questions in your exam. A police line-up is a recognition test. Recognition is usually easier than recall, because the item itself is supplied and the person need only judge its familiarity.

relearning is Ebbinghaus's savings method: the person learns the material again, and the reduction in time or trials is the measure of what was retained. It is the most sensitive of the measures, because it detects traces too weak to support either recall or recognition. Finally, the reconstruction method presents items in a particular order, then hands them back shuffled and asks the person to put them back into the original order. The score reflects memory for order rather than for the items themselves, which are all in front of the person. Table 7.1 sets the methods side by side.

Exam trap

The "reconstruction method" (restoring the original order of supplied items) is a measurement technique. "Reconstructive memory" (Bartlett's idea that remembering rebuilds the past from schemas, Section 7.9) is a theory. A question describing items presented in order, then scrambled, then put back in order by the participant is asking for the reconstruction method, not recall, recognition or relearning. And when asked which measure is most sensitive, the answer is relearning (savings); recall, with no cues to help, is the least sensitive.

7.2 The Multi-Store ModelHigh yield · 17 Qs

Ebbinghaus studied memory as a single thing that weakens over time. The idea that memory might be made of several different stores is older than experiments on it. William James (1842 to 1910), in his Principles of Psychology (1890), distinguished primary memory, the small amount of information that is still present in consciousness (the sentence you are reading), from secondary memory, the vast store of knowledge that must be brought back into consciousness. In 1965 Nancy Waugh and Donald Norman revived James's terms in an experimental model. Three years later came the version that every textbook teaches.

Atkinson and Shiffrin's modal model (1968)

In 1968 Richard C. Atkinson and Richard M. Shiffrin proposed the multi-store model of memory. Because so many theorists of the period proposed something similar, it is also called the modal model (the most typical model). It has two kinds of parts. The structural features are three stores through which information passes: sensory memory, short-term memory and long-term memory. The control processes are strategies that the person can choose to use, such as attending, rehearsing, coding information in a particular way, and searching memory during retrieval. As Figure 7.2 shows, information from the environment enters the sensory stores; what is attended to passes into short-term memory; what is rehearsed there is transferred to long-term memory; and information can be retrieved from long-term memory back into short-term memory when it is needed.

Sensory memory

Wave a lit sparkler in a circle on Diwali night and you see a circle of light, not a moving dot. The circle exists only in your memory: the image of each position persists for a fraction of a second after the sparkler has moved on. sensory memory (also called the sensory register) is a very brief store that holds a large amount of raw, unprocessed sensory information, one store for each sense, just long enough for attention to select some of it for further processing. Its contents are pre-categorical: they are a record of the stimulus, not yet of its meaning. The visual sensory store is iconic memory and the auditory store is echoic memory; both terms were coined by Ulric Neisser (1928 to 2012) in his book Cognitive Psychology (1967), which gave the new field its name. A tactile store, sometimes called haptic memory, has been studied far less.

Key study: Sperling's partial-report experiments (1960)

When people see an array of twelve letters (three rows of four) flashed for 50 milliseconds, they can report only about four or five of them. Yet they insist that they saw more, and that the rest faded while they were reporting. George Sperling (born 1934) tested this claim with the partial-report procedure. Immediately after the flash, a tone told the participant which row to report: a high tone for the top row, a medium tone for the middle row, a low tone for the bottom row. Because the participant could not know in advance which row would be cued, the proportion of the cued row reported must be true of the whole array. Participants reported about three of the four letters in whichever row was cued, which means that about nine of the twelve letters, roughly 75 per cent, were available at the moment of the tone, about twice as many as the whole-report method suggested.

Then Sperling delayed the tone. With every fraction of a second of delay the partial-report advantage shrank, and by about one second it had vanished: performance fell to the level of whole report. Sperling concluded that a rich visual image persists for a few hundred milliseconds after the stimulus ends and fades rapidly, so that whole report underestimates what was seen simply because the image decays while the first letters are being named. A bright visual stimulus that follows the array (a mask) wipes the icon out even faster.

Iconic memory therefore lasts roughly 250 to 500 milliseconds (a quarter to half a second, a little longer in some conditions). Echoic memory lasts longer. In 1972 Christopher Darwin, Michael Turvey and Robert Crowder ran an auditory version of Sperling's experiment, the "three-eared man" study: participants heard three different lists of letters and digits at once over headphones, one in the left ear, one in the right ear and one in both ears (which is heard as coming from the middle of the head). A visual cue then indicated which "ear" to report. A partial-report advantage appeared again, and it lasted for up to about four seconds. Echoic memory is generally put at about two to four seconds. You use it every time someone speaks to you while your attention is elsewhere: you say "What did you say?" and then realise you can replay the last few words yourself before they repeat them. The longer life of the echo makes sense, because speech unfolds over time and the end of a word can only be understood together with its beginning.

Exam trap

Two past papers ask about "the largest capacity" and get different answers. If a question says "largest capacity but shortest duration" (JEMAS 2025), the answer is sensory memory: it registers almost everything that reaches the senses, far more than short-term memory can hold, but for under a second (icon) or a few seconds (echo). If a question asks only which store has "the largest capacity" in the Atkinson and Shiffrin model, the answer is long-term memory, whose capacity has no known limit. Read the second half of the stem. Also note that "icon and echo" belong to sensory memory, never to short-term or working memory; that iconic memory is shorter than echoic memory; and that when a question says "lasts only a few seconds" and offers sensory memory, sensory memory is the intended answer, not eidetic imagery (Section 7.5), which is a rare, long-lasting visual image.

Short-term memory: capacity and chunking

Information that is attended to passes into short-term memory (STM), a store of very limited capacity that holds information for a matter of seconds unless it is rehearsed. Its capacity is measured by the memory span: the longest sequence of items that a person can repeat back in the correct order immediately after hearing it. Joseph Jacobs measured digit span systematically as early as 1887, and digit span remains part of every major intelligence and memory scale (Psychological Assessment book).

In 1956 George Armitage Miller (1920 to 2012) published a paper with one of the most famous titles in psychology, "The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information." Across many tasks, he noted, people could hold about seven items (between five and nine) in immediate memory. But Miller's deeper point was that the limit applies to meaningful units, not to raw items. He called these units chunks, and the process of grouping items into larger meaningful units chunking. Try to hold this string: I N D I A I I T I S R O. Twelve letters is beyond most people's span. Read it as INDIA, IIT, ISRO and it becomes three chunks, well within span. You chunk your mobile number into groups (98450 12345), and your twelve-digit Aadhaar number into three groups of four, for the same reason.

Chunking depends on knowledge already stored in long-term memory: IIT is a chunk only for someone who knows what it is. The most dramatic demonstration came from a student known as S.F., studied by Anders Ericsson, William Chase and Steve Faloon (1980). S.F. began with an ordinary digit span of about seven. Over more than 200 hours of practice spread across about twenty months he raised it to around 80 digits, by recoding groups of digits as running times (he was a keen club runner, so 3492 became "3 minutes 49.2 seconds, a near world-record mile"). His span for letters, which he did not practise, stayed at about six. His short-term store had not grown; he had learned to chunk. In the same way, chess masters glancing at a board from a real game for five seconds can reconstruct it almost perfectly, but are no better than novices when the pieces are placed at random (Chase and Simon, 1973).

When rehearsal and chunking are prevented, the true capacity of short-term memory appears to be smaller than seven. In 2001 Nelson Cowan reviewed this evidence and argued for "the magical number 4": about four chunks, plus or minus one. Modern textbooks therefore give both figures: Miller's seven plus or minus two for memory span, and Cowan's four for the pure capacity of the store.

Short-term memory: duration, forgetting and coding

How long does information last in short-term memory if you are prevented from rehearsing it? Two studies answered almost simultaneously: John Brown in Britain in 1958 and, in a paper that every student learns, Lloyd and Margaret Peterson in the United States in 1959.

Key study: The Brown-Peterson technique (1958, 1959)

Lloyd Peterson and Margaret Peterson read a consonant trigram (such as CHJ) to each participant, followed at once by a three-digit number. The participant had to count backwards by threes from that number (506, 503, 500 ...) until a signal, then recall the trigram. Counting backwards prevented rehearsal. The retention interval varied from 3 to 18 seconds. Recall fell rapidly: from about 80 per cent after 3 seconds to about 10 per cent after 18 seconds. This method of filling the retention interval with a distracting task became known as the Brown-Peterson technique.

The Petersons interpreted the result as decay: an unrehearsed trace simply fades in under half a minute, which is the origin of the textbook figure of "about 18 to 30 seconds" for the duration of short-term memory. But the decay interpretation did not survive. In 1962 Geoffrey Keppel and Benton Underwood looked at the very first trial each participant received and found almost no forgetting at all, even after 18 seconds. Forgetting grew only over successive trials, as trigrams from earlier trials piled up and competed with the current one. That is proactive interference (Section 7.8), not decay.

Further evidence pointed the same way. Delos Wickens and his colleagues showed in the 1960s and early 1970s that if several Brown-Peterson trials use items from one category (say, fruits), recall declines from trial to trial; but if the category is then switched (to professions), recall jumps back up. This release from proactive interference shows that the earlier items were interfering with the new ones, and, incidentally, that items in short-term memory carry some meaning. Waugh and Norman (1965) presented digits at fast or slow rates and found that forgetting depended on how many items came after the target, not on how much time had passed: new items displace old ones from a store with few slots. The modern view is that short-term forgetting is mostly due to interference and displacement, with decay playing a role, if at all, only over the first few seconds.

What form does information take in short-term memory? In 1964 Reuben Conrad showed people strings of letters visually and recorded their recall errors. The errors were acoustic: people wrote V when the letter had been B, or S when it had been X, letters that sound alike but look different. Even visual material, it seemed, was held as sound. Two years later Alan Baddeley (born 1934) gave people short lists of words to recall immediately. Lists of words that sounded alike (man, mad, cap, can, map) were recalled very poorly; lists of words with similar meanings (big, huge, large, broad, great) caused little difficulty. When recall was delayed by twenty minutes, so that it depended on long-term memory, the pattern reversed: similarity of meaning now caused confusion, and similarity of sound did not. The classic conclusion is that acoustic coding dominates in short-term memory and semantic coding in long-term memory. "Dominates" is the right word: short-term memory can also hold visual codes for a second or two, and Wickens's release effect shows that meaning is present too.

Long-term memory

long-term memory (LTM) is the relatively permanent store of information. Its capacity has no known limit: nobody has ever been turned away from learning a new face because the store was full. Its duration ranges from minutes to a lifetime. Harry Bahrick and his colleagues (1975) tested nearly four hundred American adults on the names and faces of their high-school classmates; even 34 years after graduation, people recognised about 90 per cent of their classmates' faces, although their ability to recall names freely was far poorer and declined steadily. In a later study, Bahrick (1984) found that much of the Spanish people had learned at school was still there fifty years later; he called this stable residue permastore. Information in long-term memory is coded mainly by meaning. Jacqueline Sachs (1967) found that people who had heard a passage could not tell, after a short delay, whether a test sentence matched the exact wording they had heard, but readily detected any change in its meaning: we keep the gist and lose the words. Long-term memory also holds sounds (a song, a voice), images (a face) and movements (how to ride a bicycle), so semantic coding is the typical, not the only, code. Evidence for separate stores: the serial position curve and amnesic patients

If you hear a list of fifteen words and then write down as many as you can in any order, which words will you remember? Not the middle ones. Plot the probability of recall against each word's position in the list and you get a U-shaped curve, the serial position effect, also called the primacy-recency effect (Figure 7.3). The superior recall of the first few items is the primacy effect; the superior recall of the last few is the recency effect. Bennet Murdock (1962) showed how reliable this curve is in free recall across lists of different lengths and presentation rates. The multi-store model explains it neatly. The first items receive more rehearsal than later ones, because each of them is rehearsed alone and then together with the next few, so they are more likely to be transferred to long-term memory: primacy is a long-term memory effect. The last items are still sitting in short-term memory at the moment of recall and are simply read out first: recency is a short-term memory effect. Dewey Rundus (1971) confirmed the first claim by asking people to rehearse aloud; the early items were rehearsed far more often than the rest, and the number of rehearsals predicted recall.

Key study: Glanzer and Cunitz (1966)

If primacy and recency come from different stores, it should be possible to change one without touching the other. Murray Glanzer and Anita Cunitz did exactly that. When recall was delayed by 30 seconds filled with counting (which empties short-term memory), the recency effect disappeared, but the primacy effect was unchanged. When words were presented more slowly (allowing more rehearsal), recall of early and middle items improved, but recency was unaffected. Two variables, two stores: a classic dissociation.

The second line of evidence comes from brain damage. Patients with the classic amnesic syndrome, such as H.M. (Section 7.10) and Clive Wearing, have normal digit spans and normal recency effects, but cannot transfer new information into long-term memory: short-term memory intact, long-term learning destroyed. If the stores are truly separate, the opposite pattern should also exist. It does. In 1970 Tim Shallice and Elizabeth Warrington described K.F., a man who had injured the left side of his brain in a motorcycle accident. His digit span was only about two items, and his recency effect was almost absent, yet his long-term learning was essentially normal. When one patient shows damage to function A with function B intact, and another shows the reverse, the result is a double dissociation, the strongest kind of neuropsychological evidence that two functions depend on different systems.

Exam trap

Who first observed the primacy-recency effect? Ebbinghaus noted in 1885 that the beginning and end of a list were learned first, and many textbooks credit him with the first observation of the serial position effect. The RML 2024 key, however, gives "McCray and Hunter", which refers to J. W. McCrary and W. S. Hunter, who published influential serial position curves for serial learning in 1953. If Ebbinghaus and McCrary and Hunter both appear, remember that the official key for that paper chose McCrary and Hunter, and that the historically earlier observation is Ebbinghaus's. Two more confusables: primacy and recency effects in impression formation and persuasion belong to the Social book, and primacy and recency as order effects in a psychophysics experiment are controlled by counterbalancing (Research Methodology book).

Criticisms of the multi-store model

The modal model gave memory research a common map, and the distinction between a small, fragile short-term store and a vast, durable long-term store has survived. But nearly every detail has been challenged. First, the stores are not unitary. Short-term memory turned out to have separate verbal and visual parts and an attentional controller, which is why it was replaced by the working memory model (Section 7.3); long-term memory turned out to contain several systems (Section 7.5). Second, rehearsal is not the royal road to long-term memory that the model assumed: simply repeating an item can keep it in short-term memory for as long as you like without making it much more memorable later (Section 7.4). Third, K.F. is a problem as well as a support: if information could reach long-term memory only by passing through short-term memory, his damaged short-term store should have crippled his long-term learning, and it did not. Fourth, the order of the stores is wrong in an important way: we can chunk IIT in short-term memory only because long-term memory has already been consulted, so long-term knowledge acts on information before and during its stay in short-term memory, not merely after it. Finally, the model treats short-term memory as a passive waiting room, when in daily life it is where we calculate, reason and understand.

Exam trap

"Which of the following is NOT a store in the stage model of memory?" The stage (multi-store) model has three stores: sensory, short-term and long-term. Working memory is not one of them; it is the separate model of Baddeley and Hitch (1974). A related question asks which store information must pass through before reaching long-term memory in the Atkinson and Shiffrin model: short-term memory, which some practice papers write as "short-term (working) memory" because Atkinson and Shiffrin themselves described the short-term store as a working memory.

7.3 Working MemoryHigh yield · 15 Qs

Try this: multiply 24 by 7 in your head. You must hold the numbers, carry out the steps, store the partial result (140) while you calculate the next one (28), and then combine them. Short-term memory in the Atkinson and Shiffrin sense, a passive box where items wait to be rehearsed or lost, does not capture what your mind just did. In 1974 Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch proposed that short-term memory is better thought of as a working memory: a limited-capacity system for temporarily holding and manipulating information while we carry out complex tasks such as reasoning, comprehension, mental arithmetic and learning.

The dual-task method and the case against a single store

Baddeley and Hitch's main tool was the dual-task method: give people two tasks at once and see whether they interfere. The logic is simple. If two tasks need the same limited resource, doing them together will impair one or both; if they draw on different resources, they can be combined with little cost. In their key experiments, participants held a list of up to six digits (a full short-term memory load, by the multi-store account) while solving reasoning problems, such as deciding whether "A follows B" correctly describes the pair BA. If short-term memory were a single store, filling it with six digits should have wrecked the reasoning. It did not. Reasoning slowed somewhat, but accuracy held up. Baddeley and Hitch concluded that short-term memory has several components: a store that was occupied by the digits, and something else, which was still available to do the reasoning.

The model that resulted originally had three components, a controlling central executive and two "slave" systems, the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad. In 2000 Baddeley added a fourth, the episodic buffer. Figure 7.4 shows the model in its current form.

The phonological loop

The phonological loop holds and rehearses speech-based, auditory-verbal information. It has two parts. The phonological store, sometimes called the inner ear, holds speech sounds for about two seconds before they fade. The articulatory control process, the inner voice, refreshes the contents of the store by silent rehearsal (the way you repeat a phone number under your breath) and converts written material into a speech code so that it can enter the store. Four findings support this account.

  • The phonological similarity effect: lists of similar-sounding items (B, G, V, P, T) are recalled worse than dissimilar ones (F, W, K, R, Y), because similar sounds are confused in the store. This is Conrad's and Baddeley's finding from Section 7.2, now given a home.
  • The word-length effect: memory span is greater for short words than long ones. Baddeley, Neil Thomson and Mary Buchanan (1975) found that people could recall about as many words as they could say in roughly two seconds, so span for words like "wit, sum, harm" is larger than for "university, opportunity, constitutional". Even words with the same number of syllables but longer spoken durations (Friday, harpoon) are recalled worse than quick ones (bishop, wicket). Digit span depends on language for the same reason: Nick Ellis and Richard Hennelly (1980) showed that Welsh-English bilingual children had smaller digit spans in Welsh, whose digit names take longer to say, than in English.
  • articulatory suppression: if people repeat an irrelevant word aloud ("the, the, the") while studying a list, the articulatory process is occupied. Suppression abolishes the word-length effect, and with visually presented items it also abolishes the phonological similarity effect, because written items can no longer be recoded into sound and so never reach the store. With spoken items, the similarity effect survives, because heard speech enters the store directly.
  • The irrelevant speech effect: background speech, even in a language the listener does not know, impairs serial recall of visually presented digits, whereas non-speech noise of the same loudness does not (Pierre Salamé and Baddeley, 1982). Speech gains automatic access to the phonological store.

What is the loop for? Not, mainly, for remembering phone numbers. Baddeley and his colleagues argue that it evolved as a language-learning device: to learn a new word, you must hold its unfamiliar sound sequence long enough to build a long-term representation. A patient known as P.V., whose phonological store was damaged, could learn pairs of words in her own language normally but was almost unable to learn words in Russian, which offered nothing familiar to lean on.

The visuospatial sketchpad

The visuospatial sketchpad holds and manipulates visual and spatial information: the route from your hostel to the exam hall, the layout of your room in the dark, an image you rotate in your mind. Robert Logie (1995) divided it into a visual cache, which stores form and colour, and an inner scribe, which handles spatial and movement information and rehearses the contents of the cache. When Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler (1971) showed that the time needed to decide whether two drawings show the same object increases steadily with the angle through which one must be mentally rotated, they were measuring the sketchpad at work; any question about the component of working memory that "mentally rotates an object" is asking for the visuospatial sketchpad.

The dual-task evidence for a separate sketchpad is elegant. Lee Brooks (1968) asked people to imagine a block capital F and to move around its corners in their mind, classifying each corner as "yes" if it was on the extreme top or bottom and "no" otherwise. They could respond either by saying "yes" or "no" or by pointing to Y and N letters scattered over a sheet of paper. Pointing, which needs spatial control, made the imagery task much slower than speaking. With a verbal task (classifying the words of a remembered sentence), the reverse was true: speaking was slower than pointing. Spatial responses interfere with spatial memory and verbal responses with verbal memory, which is what separate visuospatial and phonological systems predict. Clinically, visuospatial span is measured with the Corsi block-tapping test, in which the examiner taps a sequence of blocks and the patient copies it.

The central executive

The central executive is the most important and least understood component. It is an attentional control system with no storage of its own. Baddeley (1996) listed its functions as focusing attention, dividing attention between tasks, switching attention, and linking working memory with long-term memory. He modelled it on Donald Norman and Tim Shallice's supervisory attentional system, which takes over from habit whenever a situation is new, difficult or dangerous. Later work by Akira Miyake and colleagues (2000) separated three related executive functions: inhibition of unwanted responses, shifting between tasks, and updating the contents of working memory. The best-known measure of inhibition is the Stroop task (John Ridley Stroop, 1935): naming the ink colour of a colour word printed in a conflicting colour (RED in blue ink) is slow because automatic reading competes with the controlled task of colour naming. The Stroop effect is therefore interference in selective attention, not the interference between memories that causes forgetting (Section 7.8); it is taught fully in Chapter 5.

The classic way to load the central executive is random generation. Try saying a random sequence of digits for a minute: you must constantly monitor what you have said, suppress the habitual sequences (1, 2, 3 or your phone number) and decide what to say next. Random number or letter generation disrupts reasoning, planning and the selection of chess moves, and the more demanding the main task, the more random generation suffers. Damage to the frontal lobes, especially the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, produces what Baddeley and Barbara Wilson (1988) called the dysexecutive syndrome: poor planning, distractibility and perseveration, with memory stores themselves intact (Physiological book). The episodic buffer

By the late 1990s the three-part model had a problem. People can repeat back a meaningful sentence of fifteen or sixteen words, far more than the five or six unrelated words the phonological loop can hold. Some densely amnesic patients can recall a short prose passage immediately, at normal levels, although they will have forgotten it entirely an hour later. Where is all this information held, and where are words, images and meaning combined into a single experience? In 2000 Baddeley proposed the episodic buffer: a limited-capacity store, holding perhaps four chunks, that uses a multidimensional code to bind information from the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad and long-term memory into integrated episodes. It is "episodic" because it holds integrated scenes or episodes, and a "buffer" because it serves as an interface between systems that use different codes. It is controlled by the central executive and is thought to be the place where we are consciously aware of the bound result.

Exam trap

The four components of working memory are the central executive, the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad and the episodic buffer. Distractors invent plausible-sounding parts: "visual executive loop", "verbal sketchpad", "semantic buffer". The phonological loop handles auditory-verbal information; the sketchpad handles visual and spatial information (including mental rotation); the episodic buffer integrates information into coherent episodes and was the one added in 2000. The original 1974 model had three components.

Working memory versus short-term memory

Are working memory and short-term memory just two names for the same thing? Not quite. Short-term memory refers to the temporary storage of information, measured by simple span tasks: hear a list, repeat it back. Working memory refers to storage combined with processing, measured by complex span tasks. In the reading span task of Meredyth Daneman and Patricia Carpenter (1980), for example, people read a series of sentences aloud and then try to recall the last word of each one; they must store words while processing sentences. Simple digit span repeated forwards is the classic short-term memory test; digit span repeated backwards requires the person to hold the sequence and reverse it, so it is treated as a working memory test. Working memory capacity, measured by complex span, predicts reading comprehension, reasoning and fluid intelligence far better than simple span does (Chapter 9). In short, short-term memory is a component of the larger working memory system; the multi-store model's single box became Baddeley's set of interacting parts.

Clinical link

Working memory tasks are central to cognitive assessment. The Wechsler intelligence scales combine forward, backward and sequenced digit span into a working memory index, and the Wechsler Memory Scale, Fourth Edition (WMS-IV), reports five index scores: Auditory Memory, Visual Memory, Visual Working Memory, Immediate Memory and Delayed Memory. Working memory deficits are prominent in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, schizophrenia and frontal lobe injury, and tests of executive function such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test tap the central executive. (Test details are in the Psychological Assessment book.)

7.4 Levels of Processing and the Quality of EncodingMedium yield · 4 Qs

The multi-store model said that what gets into long-term memory depends on how long an item is rehearsed in short-term memory. Think about your own studying for a moment. Have you ever read a page five times and remembered nothing, and heard a single striking story once and remembered it for years? If so, you already suspect that the amount of rehearsal is not the whole story. In 1972 Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart turned that suspicion into a theory.

Craik and Lockhart's framework (1972)

Craik and Lockhart argued that memory is not a series of boxes but a by-product of the way information is processed. Incoming information can be analysed at different depths. At the shallowest level, structural (or orthographic) processing, we attend to physical appearance: is the word printed in capital letters? At an intermediate level, phonemic processing, we attend to sound: does the word rhyme with "weight"? At the deepest level, semantic processing, we attend to meaning: is it a kind of fish? Would it fit the sentence "He met a ___ in the street"? According to the levels of processing framework, deeper processing produces more elaborate, longer-lasting and stronger memory traces. Memory is what is left behind by the analyses that perception and comprehension perform, so the crucial question is not "How long did you rehearse it?" but "What did you do with it?"

An important consequence is that the intention to learn matters very little. Thomas Hyde and James Jenkins (1973) gave people word lists with different orienting tasks: some rated each word for pleasantness (a semantic task), others checked whether the word contained the letters E or G (a structural task). Some participants in each condition were warned of a later memory test; others were not. Recall depended strongly on the orienting task and hardly at all on the warning: people who rated pleasantness without trying to learn remembered about as well as people who were deliberately trying to learn. What you do with information determines what you remember, whether or not you mean to remember it.

Key study: Craik and Tulving (1975)

Fergus Craik and Endel Tulving presented words, each preceded by a question that required one of three levels of processing: a structural question ("Is the word in capital letters?"), a phonemic question ("Does the word rhyme with WEIGHT?") or a semantic question ("Would the word fit the sentence 'He met a ___ in the street'?"). Participants did not know that a memory test would follow. On a later surprise recognition test, memory rose steadily from structural to phonemic to semantic questions, with semantic processing producing performance more than three times as good as structural processing. Words that received a "yes" answer were remembered better than "no" words, especially at the semantic level, because a "yes" word fits into a meaningful structure.

Was depth just a disguise for time? Semantic questions take a little longer to answer. Craik and Tulving checked by devising a slow but shallow task (judging the pattern of consonants and vowels in a word). It took longer than the semantic task and still produced poorer memory. In a further experiment, words judged against complex sentence frames ("The great bird swooped down and carried off the struggling ___") were recalled about twice as well as words judged against simple frames ("She cooked the ___"): within the same level, more elaborate processing helped too.

Maintenance versus elaborative rehearsal

Craik and Lockhart drew a distinction that every student should use. maintenance rehearsal (which they called Type I processing) is the simple repetition of information at the same level, like repeating a phone number until you dial it. It keeps information available in short-term memory but adds little to long-term memory. elaborative rehearsal (Type II processing) links the information to other knowledge: thinking about what it means, how it relates to what you already know, what example illustrates it. It is elaborative rehearsal that builds durable memories. Craik and Michael Watkins (1973) demonstrated the difference neatly. Participants heard lists of words and had to keep in mind the most recent word beginning with a target letter, say G, so that some G-words were held in mind for a long time and others only briefly. On a surprise final recall test, the time a word had spent being maintained made almost no difference to whether it was remembered. Repetition kept words in mind; it did not make them memorable.

Two further ideas extend the levels framework. Elaboration refers to the richness of processing within a level, as the complex sentences in Craik and Tulving's study showed. Distinctiveness refers to how different a trace is from other traces; a distinctive memory has fewer competitors at retrieval. Michael Eysenck and Christine Eysenck (1980) asked people to pronounce words with silent letters as they are spelled (the "b" in comb). This was shallow, phonemic processing, yet because it was distinctive it produced recognition as good as semantic processing. (The classic demonstration of distinctiveness, the von Restorff effect, is described in Section 7.8.)

The self-reference, generation and dual-coding effects

If semantic processing beats phonemic processing, is there anything deeper than meaning? In 1977 T. B. Rogers, Nicholas Kuiper and W. S. Kirker added a fourth orienting question to the classic three: "Does this word describe you?" Adjectives judged in relation to the self were recalled about twice as well as adjectives judged for meaning alone. This self-reference effect, the finding that information is remembered better when it is related to oneself, has been confirmed many times. The usual explanation is that the self is the richest, most organised and most often used body of knowledge each of us has, so linking a word to it provides both elaboration and organisation. Interestingly, in some collectivist cultures, relating words to one's mother produces almost as large an advantage as relating them to oneself, which suggests that what matters is an elaborate, well-organised knowledge structure rather than the self as such.

The generation effect is the finding that information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you merely read. Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf (1978) had people either read word pairs (HOT and COLD) or generate the second word from a rule and a cue (an opposite of HOT beginning with C). Generated words were remembered better. The lesson for study is direct: an answer you work out for yourself is worth more than an answer you highlight.

A third principle concerns images. Allan Paivio (1925 to 2016) proposed his dual coding theory in 1971: information can be represented in a verbal code and in an imagery code (in his later terms, by logogens and imagens). Concrete words such as "elephant" are easily coded both ways, so they have two routes to retrieval; abstract words such as "truth" are coded mainly verbally. Dual coding explains why concrete words are remembered better than abstract ones and why pictures are remembered better than words (the picture superiority effect), and it underlies the imagery mnemonics of Section 7.11.

Exam trap

The self-reference effect was demonstrated by Rogers, Kuiper and Kirker (1977); exam keys often write "Rogers and Kuiper". It is not Craik and Lockhart's, although it extends their framework. The other names offered in the RML 2024 question have their own claims: Allan Paivio is dual coding theory; Michael Posner is known for attention research and the letter-matching task (Chapter 5). And the "self-reference effect" is a memory phenomenon; the "sum of a person's impressions of himself", "the beliefs we hold about what we are like" and "the knowledge that we exist as separate individuals" describe self-concept, self-schema and self-awareness (Social and Personality books).

Criticisms and transfer-appropriate processing

The levels framework was enormously productive, but three criticisms stuck. The first is circularity. How do we know that a task involves deep processing? Because it produces good memory. Why does it produce good memory? Because it involves deep processing. Without an independent measure of depth, the theory cannot be tested properly; Alan Baddeley made this point forcefully in a 1978 critique. The second is that depth, elaboration and distinctiveness were never clearly separated or measured. The third, and most important, is that the framework looked only at encoding and ignored the conditions of retrieval.

The third criticism came with an experiment by Donald Morris, John Bransford and Jeffery Franks (1977). Participants answered semantic or rhyme questions about words, as in the levels experiments. Then half of them took a standard recognition test, and half took a rhyming recognition test, in which they had to pick out new words that rhymed with words they had studied. On the standard test, semantic processing won, as usual. On the rhyming test, rhyme processing won. Deep processing is not better in any absolute sense; what matters is the match between the processing done at study and the processing demanded by the test. Morris and colleagues called this transfer-appropriate processing. It is a close cousin of Tulving's encoding specificity principle (Section 7.7), and it explains why practising with past papers in exam format is so valuable: the processing you rehearse is the processing the exam will require.

7.5 The Systems of Long-Term MemoryHigh yield · 15 Qs

Clive Wearing could not tell you a single thing he had done the day before, yet he knew that he was a musician, knew what a piano was, and could play one. If long-term memory were a single store, this pattern would be impossible. The modern view, built from just such cases, is that long-term memory is a family of systems that store different kinds of information, operate by different rules and depend on different parts of the brain.

Episodic, semantic and procedural memory

In 1972 the Estonian-born Canadian psychologist Endel Tulving (1927 to 2023) proposed a distinction that is now part of everyday psychology. episodic memory is memory for personally experienced events, each located in a particular time and place: what happened, where it happened and when. Remembering your first day of college, or what you ate for dinner last night, is episodic memory. semantic memory is general knowledge about the world: facts, concepts, word meanings and rules, held without any memory of when or where they were learned. Knowing that Mysuru is in Karnataka, that a sparrow is a bird and that "anterograde" means forward-acting is semantic memory. Semantic memory is often described as a vast network of associated facts and concepts that make up our knowledge of the world (Section 7.6). Because its contents are generalised across many experiences rather than tied to one, some textbooks call it generic memory.

Tulving later (1985) tied each kind of memory to a kind of consciousness. Episodic remembering involves autonoetic consciousness ("self-knowing"), the feeling of mentally travelling back in time and re-experiencing an event as one's own. Semantic memory involves noetic consciousness ("knowing"), an awareness of facts without reliving anything. Procedural memory involves anoetic ("non-knowing") consciousness, with no awareness of the memory at all. Tulving turned this into a simple test, the remember-know procedure: when you recognise a word from a list, do you "remember" seeing it (you can recall the moment, what you thought) or do you just "know" it was there? Remember responses reflect episodic recollection; know responses reflect familiarity.

The third major kind of long-term memory is procedural memory, memory for how to do things: riding a bicycle, tying shoelaces, typing, playing the piano. The philosopher Gilbert Ryle (1949) had already distinguished "knowing that" from "knowing how", and in 1980 Neal Cohen and Larry Squire used the same contrast to separate declarative from procedural memory. Procedural memories are acquired gradually through practice, are expressed in performance rather than words, and are remarkably resistant both to forgetting and to brain damage that devastates episodic memory.

The strongest evidence that episodic and semantic memory are separable is again neuropsychological. Tulving's patient K.C. (Kent Cochrane, 1951 to 2014) suffered a motorcycle accident in 1981 that damaged his medial temporal lobes and other regions. Afterwards his general knowledge, language and intelligence were normal; he knew facts about his own life, such as the address of the family's summer cottage. Yet he could not recall a single event that he had personally experienced, at any point in his life. K.C. was the man who could not travel back in time. The opposite pattern appears in semantic dementia, a degenerative disease of the anterior temporal lobes in which patients progressively lose the meanings of words and concepts while their memory for recent personal events is relatively spared. Explicit and implicit memory

A second way of dividing long-term memory cuts across Tulving's. explicit memory involves conscious recollection of information or of the situation in which it was learned; it is tested by recall and recognition. implicit memory is an influence of past experience on behaviour, feelings or thoughts without any conscious recollection of that experience. The terms were introduced by Peter Graf and Daniel Schacter in 1985. Larry Squire's preferred terms, declarative and nondeclarative memory, mean almost the same thing: declarative memory can be "declared" (stated in words); nondeclarative memory is expressed through performance.

Implicit memory was discovered through amnesia. In 1911 the Swiss psychologist Édouard Claparède hid a pin in his hand and pricked an amnesic patient as they shook hands. Next time, she refused to shake his hand, although she had no memory of the pinprick and could not say why; when pressed, she offered only that "sometimes pins are hidden in people's hands". In the 1960s and 1970s Elizabeth Warrington and Lawrence Weiskrantz found that amnesic patients who could not recall or recognise a list of words nevertheless identified fragmented versions of those words more easily, and completed word stems with them, just as normal people did. Graf, Squire and George Mandler (1984) sharpened the contrast: given the stem MOT___, amnesic patients told to recall a studied word performed poorly, but told simply to write the first word that came to mind, they produced the studied word (MOTEL) as often as healthy participants. The same memory trace, tested two ways, was absent explicitly and intact implicitly.

H.M., the most studied amnesic patient (Section 7.10), showed the same dissociation for skills. Asked to trace a star while watching his hand only in a mirror, he improved steadily over three days of practice, just like anyone else, while denying each day that he had ever done the task before.

Squire gathered these findings into the taxonomy shown in Figure 7.5. Declarative memory (episodic and semantic) depends on the hippocampus and the surrounding medial temporal lobe. Nondeclarative memory is a collection of quite different abilities: procedural skills and habits (dependent on the striatum, part of the basal ganglia), priming (dependent on the neocortex), simple classical conditioning (the amygdala for emotional responses, the cerebellum for skeletal responses such as the conditioned eyeblink), and non-associative learning such as habituation and sensitisation (dependent on reflex pathways; Chapter 6).

Exam trap

Implicit memory does NOT involve conscious recollection; that is the definition of explicit memory. A "which statement does not accurately reflect these terms" question will usually hide this swap ("implicit memory involves a conscious recollection of the original events"). Also note the matched pairs: explicit = declarative = conscious recollection = recall and recognition; implicit = nondeclarative = performance without awareness = priming, skills, conditioning. Procedural memory is one kind of implicit memory, not a synonym for it.

Priming

priming is a change in the speed, accuracy or likelihood of processing a stimulus as a result of earlier exposure to the same or a related stimulus, without any intention to remember. In repetition priming (also called direct or perceptual priming), the earlier stimulus is the same item: having read "assassin" on a list, you are more likely to complete the fragment _ss_ss_n correctly later, even if you cannot recognise "assassin" as a word from the list (Tulving, Schacter and Stark, 1982). In semantic priming, the earlier stimulus is related in meaning. David Meyer and Roger Schvaneveldt (1971) asked people to decide as quickly as possible whether strings of letters were words (a lexical decision task). People recognised BUTTER as a word faster when it followed BREAD than when it followed NURSE. The prime activated a region of semantic memory, and activation spread to related concepts (Section 7.6).

Priming can occur even when the prime is not consciously seen. In masked priming, a word is flashed for a few milliseconds and immediately covered by a pattern mask, so that people cannot report it; yet it still speeds responses to related words. Anthony Marcel (1983) used this method to argue for semantic processing without awareness, and masked semantic priming became one of the main techniques for studying subliminal perception (Chapter 5).

Exam trap

An RML 2022 question asks what "semantic priming" is used to study and keys "subliminal perception". Semantic priming is, first of all, a tool for studying the organisation of semantic memory and spreading activation. Its masked (subliminal) form is the standard way of showing that meaning can be processed without awareness, which is why the key chose subliminal perception from options that otherwise belong to other subjects (signal detection is psychophysics, field dependence is a cognitive style, locus of control is personality). If "semantic memory organisation" were offered, it would be the better answer.

Autobiographical memory and the reminiscence bump

autobiographical memory is memory for the events and facts of one's own life. It contains specific episodes (the day you received your Class 12 results) and personal semantic facts (the name of your school, your father's occupation), organised around the self and its goals. It is the memory that makes you a person with a history.

Francis Galton (1879) pioneered its study with the cue-word technique, revived by Herbert Crovitz and Harold Schiffman (1974): give a person a word such as "river" or "fire" and ask for the first specific personal memory it brings to mind, then ask how old they were at the time. When adults over about forty are tested this way and their memories are plotted against age at the time of the event, a characteristic curve appears. Hardly any memories come from the first three or four years (infantile amnesia, Section 7.10). Many come from the last few years (ordinary recency). And there is a surprising hump of extra memories from the ages of about 10 to 30: the reminiscence bump, described by David Rubin, Scott Wetzler and Robert Nebes (1986). Explanations include the many "firsts" of adolescence and early adulthood (first love, first job, leaving home), which are novel and distinctive; the formation of a stable identity in those years, so that events become anchors of the self; the peak of cognitive efficiency; and a cultural life script, which tells us that the major events of a life (marriage, career, children) happen in that period, so that we search our memories accordingly.

A handful of people have astonishingly complete autobiographical memories. The first described, A.J. (Elizabeth Parker, Larry Cahill and James McGaugh, 2006), could name the day of the week and the events of almost any date from her adolescence onwards. This highly superior autobiographical memory, or hyperthymesia, is not a general super-memory: such people are ordinary at memorising lists.

Prospective versus retrospective memory

Almost everything so far concerns retrospective memory, remembering information or events from the past. prospective memory is remembering to carry out an intended action in the future: to take a tablet at 8 p.m., to buy milk on the way home, to pass on a message when you see a friend. Gilles Einstein and Mark McDaniel (1990) distinguished event-based prospective memory, triggered by an external cue (seeing your friend reminds you of the message), from time-based prospective memory, which must be self-initiated at the right time (remembering at 8 p.m. that it is time for the tablet). Time-based tasks are generally harder because nothing in the environment prompts them. Prospective memory failures are the commonest memory complaints of everyday life, and in clinical work they matter enormously: a patient who forgets to take medication is a patient whose treatment fails.

Flashbulb memories

Many people in India can tell you exactly where they were when they heard about the attacks on Mumbai in November 2008, or when they watched the final of the 2011 Cricket World Cup. In 1977 Roger Brown (1925 to 1997) and James Kulik named such memories flashbulb memory: vivid, detailed and long-lasting memories of the circumstances in which a person learned of a surprising, emotionally significant event. Their main example was the assassination of President John F. Kennedy in 1963. People typically recalled where they were, what they were doing, who told them, how they felt, how others felt and what happened next. Brown and Kulik proposed that a special neural mechanism, which they called "Now Print!" (borrowing the phrase from the neuroscientist Robert Livingston), was triggered by events that were surprising and consequential and fixed the whole scene like a photograph.

Key study: Neisser and Harsch and the Challenger disaster (1992)

On 28 January 1986 the space shuttle Challenger exploded shortly after launch. The next morning, Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch asked students at Emory University to write down how they had heard the news. About two and a half years later they found 44 of these students and asked them the same questions. The memories had changed dramatically. A typical student who had first written that she heard the news in a religion class, when people started talking, later "remembered" that she had been in her dormitory with her roommate when the news came on television. About a quarter of the students were wrong about every major detail, and few were right about all of them. Yet confidence ratings were high. Some students, shown their own handwritten accounts from the morning after, insisted that their current memory must be the correct one.

The same pattern appeared after the attacks of 11 September 2001. On 12 September, Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin (2003) asked 54 students at Duke University to record how they had heard the news, and also to record an ordinary memory from the preceding days, such as a party or a study session. They retested each student 1, 6 or 32 weeks later. Both kinds of memory lost consistency at the same rate: details changed, dropped out or were added. What differed was the experience of remembering. Flashbulb memories stayed far more vivid, and students' belief in their accuracy stayed high, while their accuracy fell. Kathy Pezdek (2003) found that 73 per cent of American students said they had seen the television footage of the first plane hitting the World Trade Center on 11 September itself; no such footage was broadcast that day.

So flashbulb memories are special in their vividness and in the confidence they inspire, not in their accuracy. No special "Now Print!" mechanism is needed to explain them. Two ordinary processes do the work: emotional arousal at encoding (which, through the amygdala and stress hormones, strengthens the central features of an experience) and repeated rehearsal and retelling afterwards, through conversation and news coverage, which keeps the story alive while slowly reshaping it.

Exam trap

Flashbulb memories: Brown and Kulik (1977). Vividness and confidence are very high and stay high; accuracy and consistency decline like those of ordinary memories (Neisser and Harsch, 1992; Talarico and Rubin, 2003, whose title was "Confidence, not consistency, characterizes flashbulb memories"). The current explanation is emotional arousal plus rehearsal, not a special Now Print! mechanism, not freedom from interference and not eidetic imagery. Do not confuse "flashbulb memory" (an emotional autobiographical memory) with "eidetic imagery" (a lingering visual image) or with an "engram" (the physical memory trace, Section 7.10).

Eidetic imagery and exceptional memory

Is there such a thing as photographic memory? The nearest thing that has been reliably demonstrated is eidetic imagery: a visual image that persists for half a minute or more after a picture is removed, vivid enough that the person can scan it with their eyes and describe details they did not notice while the picture was present. Ralph Haber and Ruth Haber (1964) found it in a small minority of primary school children (estimates range from about 2 to 10 per cent); it is very rare in adults. Even eidetic images are not photographs: they fade, they contain errors, and the children who have them do not have better memories in general.

The most famous mnemonist in psychology, the Russian journalist Solomon Shereshevsky, studied for thirty years by Alexander Romanovich Luria (1902 to 1977) and described in The Mind of a Mnemonist (1968), could reproduce long tables of numbers and lists of words, forwards or backwards, even years later. He did it not with an eidetic image but with intense synaesthesia (words evoked colours, tastes and textures) and by placing images along an imagined street, the method of loci (Section 7.11). His problem was the opposite of ours: he found it hard to forget, and hard to grasp the general meaning of a passage through the crowd of images it evoked.

Metamemory: feeling of knowing and the tip of the tongue

metamemory is knowledge about one's own memory and the ability to monitor and control it: knowing that you remember lists better if you organise them, judging whether you have learned a chapter well enough, sensing that you know an answer you cannot yet retrieve. The term was introduced by John Flavell (1928 to 2025) in 1971, and metamemory is part of his broader concept of metacognition, thinking about one's own thinking. A person who is very good at planning, monitoring and controlling every phase of the memory system, from what to attend to, to how to rehearse, to how to search memory, is showing good metacognition.

Metamemory has two sides: monitoring (judging the state of one's memory) and control (acting on that judgement, for example by restudying what feels weak). Monitoring is often surprisingly accurate. J. T. Hart (1965) asked people general-knowledge questions; when they could not recall an answer, he asked them to rate their feeling of knowing, that is, how likely they were to recognise it among alternatives. Their ratings predicted later recognition well above chance. But monitoring can also mislead: rereading a chapter makes it feel familiar and fluent, which students mistake for having learned it, an illusion of competence that explains why rereading is such a poor study method (Section 7.11).

Key study: Brown and McNeill, the tip of the tongue (1966)

William James described it in 1890: a gap that is "intensely active", in which the sought name beckons and then retreats. Roger Brown and David McNeill set out to catch the tip-of-the-tongue state in the laboratory. They read out dictionary definitions of rare words, such as "a navigational instrument used in measuring angular distances, especially the altitude of the sun, moon and stars at sea" (sextant). Sometimes participants knew the word at once and sometimes they had no idea, but quite often they were in agony: sure they knew it, unable to produce it. In those states they could guess the first letter and the number of syllables well above chance, and they produced words with similar sounds (secant, sextet) and similar meanings (astrolabe, compass). The memory was clearly stored, and partly accessible; the failure was one of retrieval.

Tip-of-the-tongue states become more frequent with age, are more common for proper names than for other words, and are more frequent in bilinguals. They are the best everyday evidence that much forgetting is a failure of retrieval rather than of storage, a theme taken up in Sections 7.7 and 7.8. They are not aphasia: in Wernicke's aphasia (fluent but meaningless speech with poor comprehension) and Broca's aphasia (laboured, non-fluent speech), language itself is damaged (Physiological book), whereas the tip-of-the-tongue state is a temporary failure to retrieve one well-known word.

7.6 How Knowledge Is Organised: Networks, Schemas and ScriptsMedium yield · 4 Qs

Semantic memory holds an enormous amount of knowledge, yet you can answer "Is a canary a bird?" in about a second. That speed is only possible if knowledge is organised. How? Psychologists have proposed several kinds of organisation, from tidy hierarchies to sprawling webs to patterns of connection strength spread across a whole network.

The hierarchical network model (Collins and Quillian, 1969)

Allan Collins and Ross Quillian (1969) proposed that semantic memory is a network of nodes, each representing a concept, linked into a hierarchy (Figure 7.6). Properties are attached to the nodes they belong to. The hierarchical network model adds one more assumption, the principle of cognitive economy: each property is stored only once, at the highest level in the hierarchy to which it applies. "Has skin" is stored with ANIMAL, not separately with every animal; "can fly" is stored with BIRD; "can sing" with CANARY. Exceptions are stored at the lower node ("cannot fly" with OSTRICH).

The model makes a precise prediction. To verify a sentence, you must find the relevant property by travelling along the links, and each link takes time. Collins and Quillian measured how long people took to verify sentences in a sentence verification task. "A canary can sing" (property at the same node, zero links) was verified fastest, "A canary can fly" (one link up) more slowly, and "A canary has skin" (two links up) more slowly still. Category sentences behaved the same way: "A canary is a bird" was faster than "A canary is an animal". Each level added a roughly constant increment of time, just as the model predicted.

But the model ran into trouble. First, the typicality effect: Lance Rips, Edward Shoben and Edward Smith (1973) found that "A robin is a bird" is verified faster than "A penguin is a bird", although both are one link apart. Some members of a category are better examples than others (the idea of prototypes, developed by Eleanor Rosch, is taught in Chapter 8). Second, the hierarchy is sometimes violated: a sentence such as "A dog is an animal" is often verified faster than "A dog is a mammal", although "mammal" should be one link closer. Third, Carol Conrad (1972) showed that the apparent effect of hierarchical distance was partly an effect of familiarity: properties such as "can sing" are more often thought about in connection with canaries than "has skin" is. These problems led Smith, Shoben and Rips (1974) to propose a feature comparison model, in which concepts are lists of defining and characteristic features and verification compares feature lists, and led Collins to a more flexible network.

Spreading activation (Collins and Loftus, 1975)

In 1975 Allan Collins and Elizabeth Loftus (born 1944) abandoned the strict hierarchy. In their spreading activation model, semantic memory is a web of concept nodes, and the length of each link represents how closely the two concepts are related: FIRE ENGINE is close to RED and TRUCK, further from CHERRIES. When a concept is processed, activation spreads outwards along its links, weakening with distance and over time, and partially activates related concepts. A concept that is already partly active is easier to retrieve. This explains semantic priming at once: reading BREAD sends activation to BUTTER, which is then recognised faster. It also explains typicality, because typical members (robin) are closely linked to the category node (BIRD) and atypical members (penguin) are distant. The model's flexibility is also its weakness: with link lengths set to fit the data, it is hard to imagine a result it could not explain.

Propositions and connectionist networks

Knowledge is not only a set of concepts; it is a set of relations among them. A proposition is the smallest unit of knowledge that can be judged true or false, such as "Ravi studies memory" or "the exam is in October". Propositional network models, such as John R. Anderson's adaptive control of thought (ACT) theory and its successors, represent knowledge as networks of interlinked propositions. Propositions are what we keep when we keep the gist of a sentence and lose its words, as in Sachs's study. (In a question about the "meaningful units" that define working memory capacity, however, the answer is chunks, not propositions, features or scripts.)

A more radical alternative came in 1986, when David Rumelhart, James McClelland and the PDP Research Group published Parallel Distributed Processing. connectionism, also called parallel distributed processing (PDP), models memory as a network of simple, neuron-like units (nodes) connected by links of varying strength, or weight. Unlike the earlier semantic networks, a concept is not stored in one node. It is a pattern of activation distributed across many units, and the knowledge of the network lies in its connection weights. Processing is parallel: many units activate one another at the same time. Learning consists of adjusting the weights after experience, a modern version of Donald Hebb's (1949) idea that connections between neurons that fire together are strengthened. Connectionist networks have three properties that resemble human memory. They are content-addressable (any part of a memory can retrieve the rest, as a smell can bring back a whole day). They degrade gracefully (damage to some units blurs memories rather than deleting particular ones, rather like the effect of diffuse brain damage, and recalling Karl Lashley's search for the engram, Section 7.10). And they generalise automatically from examples to new cases.

Exam trap

"Which model proposes that memory is organised in a network of interconnected nodes?" (IHBAS 2024) keys the connectionist model, although "network model" is also offered. Both Collins and Quillian's semantic network and connectionist models use nodes and links. The distinguishing features of connectionism (parallel distributed processing, Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) are distributed representation, simultaneous (parallel) activation of many interconnected nodes, and knowledge stored in connection weights. When a question stresses "parallel", "distributed", "simultaneous activation" or "neural network", choose connectionist or PDP; when it stresses a hierarchy, cognitive economy or sentence verification time, choose Collins and Quillian's hierarchical network model; when it stresses activation spreading to related concepts, choose Collins and Loftus.

Schemas and scripts

Read this passage once: "The procedure is actually quite simple. First you arrange things into different groups. Of course, one pile may be sufficient depending on how much there is to do. If you have to go somewhere else due to lack of facilities, that is the next step..." John Bransford and Marcia Johnson (1972) gave passages like this to students. Without a title it is nearly incomprehensible and is poorly recalled. Told beforehand that it is about washing clothes, students found it easy to understand and recalled about twice as much. (A title given after reading did not help.) The title activated a schema.

A schema is an organised body of knowledge, built from experience, about a concept, object, person, event or situation, which guides how new information is perceived, interpreted, encoded and remembered. The term entered memory research through Frederic Bartlett (1932; Section 7.9). Schemas are economical: they let us fill gaps, make predictions and understand quickly. The price is distortion: we remember what fits the schema, add what the schema leads us to expect, and drop or alter what does not fit. William Brewer and James Treyens (1981) asked students to wait for about half a minute in a room they were told was a graduate student's office. Later asked to recall the contents of the room, many "remembered" books, which an office ought to contain but this one did not, while missing unusual objects that did not fit the office schema. The "schema approach" to memory and social understanding is simply the study of how such organised knowledge shapes what we notice and recall.

A script is a schema for a familiar, routine sequence of events. The concept was introduced by Roger Schank and Robert Abelson in 1977, whose best-known example is the restaurant script: enter, wait to be seated, read the menu, order, eat, pay, leave. An Indian example is the script for a wedding or for taking a train journey: book the ticket, check the platform, find your coach and berth, show the ticket to the conductor. Gordon Bower, John Black and Terrence Turner (1979) found that when people read stories about routine activities, they later "remembered" script actions that had not been mentioned. Scripts belong to generic (semantic) memory: they are abstracted from many similar episodes, which is why the developmental psychologist Katherine Nelson found that young children can tell you what happens at a birthday party in general well before they can recount a particular party.

Exam trap

"Which type of memory produces scripts of familiar routines?" Generic memory (RML 2025), the same thing as general, semantic knowledge abstracted from many episodes. Episodic and autobiographical memory hold particular episodes; a script is precisely what is left when the particulars are stripped away. And "how people organise information in memory and use it to understand behaviour" describes the schema approach. Schema theory is Bartlett's (1932), not Wundt's, Titchener's or Watson's.

7.7 Retrieval: Cues, Context and StateMedium yield · 3 Qs

You walk into the kitchen and cannot remember what you came for. You go back to your desk, and the moment you sit down, it comes back: the glass of water. The memory was there all along; what changed was the setting in which you searched for it. This section is about the conditions that make stored information accessible.

Availability, accessibility and retrieval cues

Endel Tulving and Zena Pearlstone (1966) gave people long lists of words drawn from several categories (animals, fruits, professions and so on). At test, one group simply recalled as many words as they could; the other group was given the category names as prompts. The prompted group recalled far more words. The words the first group failed to recall had not been lost: they were available (stored) but not accessible (retrievable without help). A retrieval cue is any stimulus, internal or external, that helps us gain access to a stored memory: a category name, a smell, a place, a question, a mood.

The availability-accessibility distinction also explains why recognition is usually easier than recall. According to the generate-recognise (two-stage) account, recall involves two steps: generating possible candidates from memory, then recognising which of them are correct. A recognition test supplies the candidates, so only the second step is needed. A multiple-choice question is easier than the same question with blanks because the options themselves act as powerful retrieval cues.

The encoding specificity principle

Which cues work best? In 1973 Endel Tulving and Donald Thomson gave the answer that now organises the whole field. The encoding specificity principle states that a retrieval cue is effective to the extent that the information it contains was encoded together with the memory at the time of learning. Memory traces are not stored in isolation; they are stored together with the context, thoughts and associations present at encoding, and retrieval succeeds when the cue reinstates enough of that encoded package.

Tulving and Thomson's evidence was striking. Participants studied target words paired with weak associates (glue and CHAIR). At test, the weak associate (glue) was a better cue for CHAIR than a strong associate such as "table", even though "table" is far more closely linked to "chair" in general knowledge, because "table" had not been part of the encoded event. In a further twist, participants sometimes failed to recognise a target word when it was presented on its own, yet recalled it moments later when given its weak study partner. This recognition failure of recallable words embarrasses the generate-recognise account, which predicts that anything recallable must also be recognisable, and it shows how strongly memory depends on the match between encoding and retrieval. Transfer-appropriate processing (Section 7.4) is the same idea applied to types of processing.

Context-dependent memory

The external environment is part of what gets encoded. context-dependent memory is the finding that recall is better when the physical context at retrieval matches the context at encoding.

Key study: Godden and Baddeley's divers (1975)

Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley worked with members of a university diving club. The divers listened to lists of unrelated words either on the beach or several metres under the sea (accounts of the exact depth differ), and later recalled them either in the same environment or in the other one. Words learned underwater were best recalled underwater; words learned on land were best recalled on land. Changing the context reduced recall by roughly a third. The study has become the classic demonstration of context-dependent memory. Five years later, however, the same researchers found that changing the environment had no effect on recognition (Godden and Baddeley, 1980). Context helps mainly when we must generate the memory ourselves; when the test provides the item, the item itself is a strong enough cue.

Context effects in everyday settings are real but often small, because rich internal cues (the meaning of the question) usually outweigh the room. One study of real university examinations found no difference between students tested in their usual lecture room and those tested elsewhere. A useful finding for exam candidates is that the context does not have to be physically present: Steven Smith (1979) found that simply imagining the room in which material was learned restored much of the benefit. Mentally reinstating the context is also the first technique of the cognitive interview used with eyewitnesses (Section 7.9).

State-dependent memory

The internal environment matters too. state-dependent memory is the finding that recall is better when a person's physiological or drug-induced state at retrieval matches their state at encoding. Donald Goodwin and his colleagues (1969) had volunteers learn material either sober or after drinking alcohol, and test it either sober or intoxicated. As expected, alcohol at learning impaired memory overall; but material learned while intoxicated was recalled better when the person was intoxicated again than when sober. Clinical reports describe heavy drinkers who hide money or bottles while drunk, cannot find them when sober, and remember the hiding place when they next get drunk. Similar effects have been found with marijuana, nicotine and other drugs. As with context, state-dependent effects are strongest in free recall and weak or absent in cued recall and recognition, where better cues are available (Eric Eich, 1980).

A caution about alcohol "blackouts": heavy intoxication can block the formation of new long-term memories altogether (an alcohol-induced anterograde amnesia), and no change of state can bring back what was never stored. Only memories that were encoded but became inaccessible on sobering up show the state-dependent pattern.

Mood-dependent and mood-congruent memory

Mood is an internal state as well, and it produces two different effects that students often confuse. In mood-dependent memory, recall is better when one's mood at retrieval matches one's mood at encoding, whatever the emotional tone of the material. In mood-congruent memory, people tend to learn and recall material whose emotional tone matches their current mood: when sad, sad memories come to mind more easily; when happy, happy ones. Gordon Bower (1932 to 2020) reported both effects in 1981, using hypnosis to induce happy or sad moods; later studies found mood-congruent memory to be the more robust of the two. Clinical link

Mood-congruent memory helps explain why depression feeds itself. A depressed person recalls failures, losses and rejections more readily than successes, which deepens the low mood, which makes negative memories still more accessible. Depressed patients also tend to produce overgeneral autobiographical memories ("I always mess things up") instead of specific episodes when given cue words, a pattern linked to poorer recovery. Cognitive therapy's insistence on examining specific situations and evidence is, among other things, a way of breaking this retrieval cycle (Clinical book).

7.8 ForgettingHigh yield · 10 Qs

Ebbinghaus showed how fast we forget; he did not show why. Psychologists have offered five main explanations: decay, interference, retrieval failure, motivated forgetting and consolidation failure. Before we examine them, one point is worth making. Forgetting is not simply a defect. A memory that kept everything would drown the important in the trivial, as Shereshevsky found (Section 7.5). By letting unused information fade, forgetting clears the way for current information, reduces interference and helps us abstract general knowledge from particular cases. Retention and forgetting are two sides of one coin: the more that is retained, the less that is forgotten, never the reverse.

Decay (trace) theory

The oldest and most intuitive explanation is decay theory, also called trace decay theory: learning leaves a physical trace (an engram) in the nervous system, and if the trace is not used, it fades with the passage of time, like a footpath that grows over when nobody walks on it. In psychology, the idea is traditionally credited to Edward Lee Thorndike (1874 to 1949), whose law of disuse (the companion of his law of use, together forming the law of exercise; Chapter 6) held that connections not exercised grow weaker. Decay fits the shape of Ebbinghaus's curve and the rapid loss from sensory memory.

The trouble is that time never passes empty. While time passes, other things are learned. In 1932 John McGeoch made the classic objection: iron rusts over time, but time does not cause rust; oxidation does. In the same way, forgetting happens over time, but what causes it might be the events that fill the time. The decisive test is to let time pass while reducing those events, and the classic attempt is one of the most quoted studies in memory research.

Key study: Jenkins and Dallenbach, sleep versus waking (1924)

John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach had two students learn lists of ten nonsense syllables, either late at night just before going to sleep or in the morning before their usual activities. Recall was tested after 1, 2, 4 or 8 hours. After a night's sleep, forgetting was modest and levelled off after about two hours; after eight hours, more than half of the syllables were still recalled. After the same time spent awake, forgetting continued steadily, and after eight hours only about one syllable in ten survived. Since the same amount of time had passed in both conditions, decay could not explain the difference. Jenkins and Dallenbach concluded that forgetting depends less on the passage of time than on "the interference, inhibition or obliteration of the old by the new".

Later studies pointed the same way. Cockroaches that were immobilised after learning forgot less than those left free to move about (Minami and Dallenbach, 1946). Baddeley and Hitch (1977) asked rugby players to recall the teams they had played that season; how much they forgot depended on how many games they had played since, not on how much time had passed. One qualification is important: we now know that sleep does more than protect memories from interference; it actively helps to consolidate them (Section 7.10).

Exam trap

"Who developed the decay theory?" The RML 2024 key is Thorndike, through his law of disuse. Ebbinghaus described the time course of forgetting but did not propose decay as its cause; Tulving is cue-dependent forgetting; Melton and Irwin are the two-factor theory of interference. In short-term memory, the Brown-Peterson studies were first read as evidence for decay and then reinterpreted as interference (Section 7.2).

Interference theory

interference theory holds that forgetting occurs because other memories compete with or disrupt the one we are trying to retrieve. John Bergström reported the first experiments on interference in the early 1890s, using a card-sorting task in which learning new sorting locations disrupted the old ones. In 1900 Georg Elias Müller and Alfons Pilzecker showed that an activity interpolated between learning and recall impaired memory, and named the effect retroactive inhibition. In the 1930s McGeoch made interference the leading theory of forgetting. There are two directions of interference, and they are among the most examined ideas in this chapter.

proactive interference (PI) occurs when earlier learning disrupts the learning or recall of newer information: the old interferes forward with the new. retroactive interference (RI) occurs when newer learning disrupts the recall of older information: the new interferes backward with the old. A simple way to keep them straight: PRO means forward, so in proactive interference old memories push forward and disrupt new ones; RETRO means backward, so in retroactive interference new memories reach back and disrupt old ones. In four words: PRO = old disrupts new; RETRO = new disrupts old.

Work through these cases. You change your mobile number and keep giving people your old one: the old number is disrupting the new one, so this is proactive interference. Years later, you cannot remember your old number at all, because the new one has replaced it: retroactive interference. A student studies psychology, then sociology, and finds the psychology getting in the way when she is tested on sociology: the earlier learning disrupts the later, so proactive interference. If instead she is tested on psychology and finds sociology concepts intruding, that is retroactive interference. An English speaker learning Spanish who keeps applying English grammar is suffering from the earlier language disrupting the newer one: proactive interference.

In the laboratory, interference is studied with paired-associate lists in which the same stimulus terms are paired with different responses: list A-B (for example, DOG-table) followed by list A-C (DOG-river). The reliable test: ask which was learned first and which is being disrupted. Old disrupting new is proactive; new disrupting old is retroactive. "Difficulty recalling information due to interference from new information" (IHBAS 2024) is retroactive interference.

Retrieval failure and retrieval-induced forgetting

The tip-of-the-tongue state, Tulving and Pearlstone's category cues, and the encoding specificity principle all point to a third explanation: much forgetting is a failure to find information that is still stored. Tulving (1974) called this cue-dependent forgetting and contrasted it with trace-dependent forgetting, in which the trace itself has been lost. Cue-dependent forgetting happens when the cues available at retrieval do not match those encoded with the memory. On this view, much of what we call forgetting is temporary, which is why a smell, a song or a return to a childhood home can bring back memories we believed long gone.

Retrieval can also cause forgetting. Michael Anderson, Robert Bjork and Elizabeth Bjork (1994) had people study category-word pairs (FRUIT-orange, FRUIT-banana, DRINK-vodka) and then practise retrieving half of the words from half of the categories (FRUIT-or___). On a final test, the practised words were, unsurprisingly, recalled best. The striking result concerned the unpractised words from practised categories (FRUIT-banana): they were recalled worse than words from categories that had not been practised at all. Retrieving some members of a category had suppressed their competitors. This is retrieval-induced forgetting, and it is thought to reflect inhibition of competing memories during retrieval. It has a practical sting: revising only some topics from a chapter may make the unrevised topics from the same chapter harder to recall than if you had revised none of it.

Exam trap

An RML 2020 question defines retrieval-induced forgetting as "people remembered practised information better than unpractised information". That is the first half of the finding. The defining half is that unpractised items related to the practised ones are remembered worse than baseline items from unpractised categories: retrieving some memories makes related memories harder to retrieve. Choose the keyed option when it is the only one about practice, but be ready for a better-worded version. The options about names and faces are irrelevant distractors.

Motivated forgetting and repression

Sigmund Freud proposed that some forgetting is motivated: memories that would provoke anxiety, guilt or shame are actively kept out of consciousness. repression is the unconscious process by which threatening or anxiety-provoking memories and impulses are excluded from awareness; according to Freud, the repressed material is not lost but continues to exert effects, for example in symptoms and slips of the tongue. Repression must be distinguished from suppression, the deliberate, conscious decision not to think about something. (Freud's other defence mechanisms and his model of conscious, preconscious and unconscious mind belong to the Personality book.)

Laboratory evidence for repression in Freud's sense is weak; after reviewing decades of studies, David Holmes (1990) concluded that there was no convincing controlled evidence for it. Evidence for conscious, motivated forgetting is much better. In directed forgetting experiments, people told to forget some items remember them less well than items they were told to remember. In the think/no-think paradigm of Michael Anderson and Collin Green (2001), people learned word pairs and then, when shown the first word of some pairs, were told to prevent the associated word from coming to mind; the more often they did so, the worse they later recalled those words, even when offered money for correct answers. Brain imaging shows the prefrontal cortex reducing hippocampal activity during such suppression. Motivated forgetting is real; whether the unconscious repression Freud described is real remains disputed, and the dispute became fierce in the recovered memory controversy (Section 7.9).

Exam trap

Repression is unconscious; suppression is conscious. "Very threatening material is unable to gain access to conscious awareness" describes repression. "Motivated forgetting" is the broader category that includes both. Do not confuse Freudian repression with the cognitive term "inhibition", which refers to the active weakening of competing memories (as in retrieval-induced forgetting).

Consolidation failure

The final explanation looks at the biology. New memories are fragile; they become stable only gradually through consolidation, the process by which a memory trace is transformed into a durable form. Müller and Pilzecker introduced the term in 1900, noting that their participants' new associations seemed to "perseverate" and set over time. If consolidation is disrupted, the memory is never fully formed. A blow to the head is the everyday case. American football players who had been concussed could often tell a trainer, immediately afterwards, what play they had been running; a few minutes later they could not, because the memory had not consolidated (Lynch and Yarnell, 1973). Electroconvulsive shock given to rats soon after a learning trial disrupts learning, whereas the same shock given hours later does not (Duncan, 1949), and patients receiving electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) for depression often lose memories for events shortly before the treatment course.

This kind of memory loss shows a temporal gradient: recent memories are more vulnerable than remote ones. The principle was stated by the French psychologist Théodule Ribot in 1881 and is known as Ribot's law: in progressive memory loss, the new perishes before the old. It is why an elderly person with dementia may forget breakfast but vividly recall their wedding. Consolidation and its brain basis are taken up again in Section 7.10.

Classic effects on retention

Several named effects reveal what makes a memory last, and examiners love them because their names are easy to swap.

In the 1920s, the Gestalt psychologist Kurt Lewin noticed that a Berlin waiter remembered every detail of unpaid orders but forgot them as soon as the bill was settled. His student Bluma Zeigarnik (1900 to 1988) tested the observation in 1927: she gave people a series of simple tasks and interrupted half of them before completion. Later, people recalled the interrupted tasks nearly twice as often as the completed ones. This Zeigarnik effect was explained in Lewinian terms: an unfinished task leaves a state of tension that keeps it active in memory. A year later Maria Ovsiankina showed that people also have a strong tendency to resume an interrupted task when given the chance, even without instructions to do so: the Ovsiankina effect. The Zeigarnik effect is about remembering unfinished tasks; the Ovsiankina effect is about returning to them.

Sometimes memory improves with time. In 1913 Philip Ballard had schoolchildren learn poetry and found that many recalled more lines two or three days later than they had immediately after learning, without any further study. This rise in recall after a delay is reminiscence. (A related effect, hypermnesia, is a net increase in recall over repeated tests; Matthew Erdelyi and Jeffrey Becker described it in 1974.) Do not confuse reminiscence with the reminiscence bump of autobiographical memory (Section 7.5).

In 1933 Hedwig von Restorff showed that an item that stands out from the rest of a list (a number among syllables, a word printed in red among black words) is remembered better than the same item in a homogeneous list. This von Restorff effect, also called the isolation effect, is the classic demonstration of distinctiveness (Section 7.4); highlighters exploit it, which is also why highlighting everything makes nothing stand out.

Two final effects matter more than any others for anyone studying for an exam. The spacing effect is the finding that study sessions spread out over time (distributed practice) produce better long-term retention than the same total study time crowded together (massed practice). Ebbinghaus observed it in 1885, and a large review by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues (2006) confirmed it across hundreds of studies; the optimal gap grows with how long you need to remember. The testing effect (or retrieval practice effect) is the finding that retrieving information from memory strengthens it more than restudying it. Arthur Gates (1917) found that schoolchildren who spent much of their study time reciting from memory learned more than those who only read. In a modern classic, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006) had students study a prose passage either four times, or once followed by three recall tests. Five minutes later, the repeated-study group did slightly better; a week later, the repeated-testing group recalled about 50 per cent more, although the repeated-study group had predicted they would do better. 7.9 Memory as Reconstruction: Distortion, False Memory and EyewitnessesMedium yield · 5 Qs

Most of us think of memory as a recording: an experience is filed away, and remembering plays the file back, perhaps faded but essentially faithful. Four of Schacter's seven sins say otherwise. This section tells the story of the research that replaced the recording metaphor with a different one: remembering is less like playing a video and more like a palaeontologist rebuilding a dinosaur from a few bones and a great deal of knowledge about what dinosaurs ought to look like.

Bartlett and the War of the Ghosts (1932)

At Cambridge in the years around the First World War, Frederic Charles Bartlett (1886 to 1969) set out to study memory in a way that was the opposite of Ebbinghaus's. Instead of meaningless syllables he used meaningful material, and instead of counting items correct he looked at what people did to the material when they remembered it. His most famous material was "The War of the Ghosts", a Native American folk tale from the Pacific north-west recorded by the anthropologist Franz Boas. In it, two young men go down to a river to hunt seals; a canoe of warriors appears out of the fog and invites them to a war party; one goes, is wounded but feels nothing, and realises that his companions are ghosts; he returns home, tells his story, and at sunrise "something black came out of his mouth" and he dies. To English students the story was strange: its logic and its supernatural elements did not fit their expectations.

Bartlett used two methods. In repeated reproduction, the same person retold the story after intervals of minutes, days, months and even years. In serial reproduction, one person's version was given to the next, like a message passed along a line of people. His results, published in Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology (1932), showed consistent changes. The stories became shorter, as details were omitted. They became more coherent and conventional: puzzling elements were explained or dropped, a process Bartlett called rationalisation, so that "something black came out of his mouth" became the man foaming at the mouth, or his soul leaving his body. Unfamiliar details were transformed into familiar ones: canoes became boats, and hunting seals became fishing. Some details became more prominent and acquired a central place. And the supernatural theme, so foreign to Edwardian Cambridge, tended to disappear.

Bartlett's explanation was that we do not store and replay experiences. We store a general impression together with a few outstanding details, and at retrieval we rebuild the rest using our schema knowledge (Section 7.6) of how such events usually go. Remembering, he wrote, is an "effort after meaning". reconstructive memory, the view that remembering actively rebuilds the past from fragments and schemas rather than reproducing it, is Bartlett's lasting legacy. Textbooks often describe the changes in his stories with three terms made famous by Gordon Allport and Leo Postman's study of rumour (1947): levelling (the story becomes shorter and simpler), sharpening (a few details are emphasised) and assimilation (details are distorted to fit the listener's expectations and culture).

Bartlett's methods were informal: he gave no standard instructions, used no statistics and reported selected examples. When Alan Gauld and Geoffrey Stephenson (1967) instructed participants to be as accurate as possible, many of the errors disappeared, suggesting that some of Bartlett's "distortions" were deliberate guesses rather than true memory changes. But Erik Bergman and Henry Roediger (1999) repeated the War of the Ghosts study under careful control and found that the distortions grew over repeated reproductions, much as Bartlett had said.

Exam trap

"Rationalisation" has two meanings in psychology. In Bartlett's memory research, it is the reshaping of a story to make it sensible to the rememberer. In psychoanalysis, it is a defence mechanism: offering a plausible, acceptable reason for behaviour after the fact, to hide the real motive (Personality book). Questions about schema and reconstruction belong to Bartlett, not to Wundt, Titchener or Watson.

Loftus and the misinformation effect

If memories are rebuilt at retrieval, information encountered after an event might be built into the memory. In the 1970s Elizabeth Loftus showed that this happens, and her work changed how courts treat eyewitnesses.

Key study: Loftus and Palmer, "smashed" versus "hit" (1974)

Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed 45 students seven short films of traffic accidents. Afterwards, each student answered questions, one of which was critical: "About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?" The verb varied between groups: smashed, collided, bumped, hit or contacted. The average speed estimates followed the verb: about 41 miles per hour for "smashed", 39 for "collided", 38 for "bumped", 34 for "hit" and 32 for "contacted".

Was this just a response bias, or had the memory itself changed? In a second experiment, 150 students saw a film of a multiple-car accident and were asked either the "smashed" question, the "hit" question or no question about speed. A week later, without seeing the film again, they were asked: "Did you see any broken glass?" There was no broken glass in the film. Of those asked the "smashed" question, 32 per cent said yes, compared with 14 per cent of the "hit" group and 12 per cent of the controls. A single word in a question, asked after the event, had altered what people later reported seeing.

The broader phenomenon is the misinformation effect: the distortion of memory for an event by misleading information received afterwards, through leading questions, other witnesses' accounts or media reports. In a classic follow-up, Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns (1978) showed people slides of a car at a stop sign; those later asked a question that presupposed a yield sign were much more likely to "recognise" a slide with a yield sign. Whether misinformation overwrites the original trace or merely creates a competing memory whose source is confused is still debated; Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) showed that with better test procedures the original memory can often still be found, which supports the source-confusion account.

Creating false memories

Changing a detail is one thing. Could a whole event that never happened be implanted? Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell (1995) sent participants booklets describing four events from their childhood, supposedly supplied by a relative. Three were true. The fourth, being lost in a shopping mall at about the age of five, crying, and being rescued by an elderly person and reunited with the family, was false, although the relative had confirmed that it fitted the family's history. Over two interviews, about a quarter of the participants came to remember the false event, fully or partly, sometimes adding details of their own. Other researchers implanted memories of spilling a bowl of punch on the bride's parents at a wedding (Ira Hyman and colleagues, 1995) and, using a doctored photograph, of a childhood hot-air balloon ride; in that study about half of the participants came to report some memory of the ride (Kimberley Wade and colleagues, 2002). Merely imagining an event repeatedly increases confidence that it happened, an effect called imagination inflation.

A false memory is a recollection of an event, or a detail of an event, that did not occur. The simplest way to produce one in the laboratory is the Deese-Roediger-McDermott (DRM) paradigm, introduced by James Deese in 1959 and revived by Henry Roediger and Kathleen McDermott in 1995. Read this list once, cover it, and then write down as many words as you can: bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, snooze, blanket, doze, slumber, snore, nap, peace, yawn, drowsy. Did you write "sleep"? It was not on the list. In Roediger and McDermott's experiments, the related but unpresented "critical lure" was recalled about as often as words that had actually been studied, and people often claimed to "remember" hearing it, with the full sense of re-experiencing that Tulving called autonoetic. Every word on the list activated "sleep" (spreading activation, Section 7.6), and at test that internally generated activation was mistaken for a memory of hearing the word.

Source monitoring, cryptomnesia and other errors of remembering

Why do we confuse what we heard with what we imagined, or what we saw with what we were told? Marcia Johnson and her colleagues (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, 1993) proposed that memories do not carry labels stating where they came from. Instead, we infer the source of a memory from its characteristics: memories of perceived events tend to be rich in sensory and contextual detail, while memories of imagined events contain more traces of the thinking that produced them. source monitoring is the set of processes by which we attribute a memory to its origin: when, where, how and from whom it was acquired. A deficit in source monitoring means being unable to recall the relevant contextual elements of an experience and so misattributing it. A special case, studied earlier by Johnson and Carol Raye (1981), is reality monitoring: telling memories of external events from memories of internally generated ones (what you did from what you only thought of doing). Source monitoring is the general framework; reality monitoring is one kind of source judgement.

Source errors explain a whole family of memory errors. In source amnesia, a person remembers a fact but not how they learned it; it is common in older adults and in patients with frontal lobe damage. In cryptomnesia, or unconscious plagiarism, a person produces an idea or a tune believing it to be new, when in fact it is a memory of someone else's work. In 1976 a court in the United States ruled that George Harrison's song "My Sweet Lord" had unconsciously copied the melody of an earlier song, "He's So Fine". Alan Brown and Dana Murphy (1989) produced the same effect in the laboratory: after a group generated examples of a category in turn, people later "generated" new examples that had in fact been produced by others. In hindsight bias, described by Baruch Fischhoff (1975), people who know how an event turned out remember their earlier predictions as closer to the outcome than they were: the "I knew it all along" effect.

Several experiences of false familiarity and false recall have names that appear in examinations. déjà vu (French for "already seen") is the illusion of recognition: the strong but false feeling that a new situation has been experienced before. It is common and usually harmless, though frequent déjà vu can be an aura of temporal lobe epilepsy. Jamais vu ("never seen") is the reverse: a familiar situation suddenly feels strange and new, as when a common word looks foreign after you have stared at it for a while. confabulation is the production of false memories, often detailed and plausible, to fill gaps in memory, without any intention to deceive; it is typical of Korsakoff's syndrome (Section 7.10). Pseudologia fantastica (pathological lying) is different again: elaborate, often grandiose stories told repeatedly, which the teller may half believe (Psychiatric Disorders book).

Exam trap

"Being unable to recall the relevant elements of an autobiographical experience" is a deficit in source monitoring (RML 2022); reality monitoring is a narrower case (internal versus external origin), and "personal-monitoring" and "social-monitoring" are invented distractors. The "illusion of recognition" is déjà vu; jamais vu is the illusion of unfamiliarity; confabulation fills memory gaps with false but sincere stories; pseudologia fantastica is pathological lying. Hindsight bias is a memory bias, not a memory store or a physical trace.

Eyewitness testimony

Few things persuade a jury like an eyewitness who points at the defendant and says, "That is the man." Yet research on reconstructive memory implies that eyewitnesses should be treated with caution, and the evidence of DNA exonerations confirms it: in the United States, mistaken eyewitness identification played a part in roughly 70 per cent of the wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA testing. The problem is not usually poor eyesight or a tendency to forget; it is that memory can be confidently wrong.

Many factors affect accuracy. At encoding: extreme stress, brief exposure, poor lighting, and weapon focus, the tendency for a visible weapon to capture attention at the expense of the culprit's face (Loftus, Geoffrey Loftus and Jane Messo, 1987). People are also less accurate at identifying faces of another ethnic group than of their own (the cross-race or own-race effect). After the event: leading questions, conversations with other witnesses and media reports introduce misinformation, and source errors can make a familiar face (seen at the scene, or in a photograph shown by police) feel like the culprit's face. At identification: biased line-ups, suggestive instructions and the investigator's own expectations all increase errors. Confirming feedback after an identification ("Good, you identified our suspect") inflates the witness's confidence, so that by the time of the trial a hesitant witness has become certain (Gary Wells and Amy Bradfield, 1998). Confidence expressed at the first, fair identification is reasonably informative; confidence at trial is not. Young children are especially suggestible when questioned repeatedly with leading questions (Stephen Ceci and Maggie Bruck, 1993).

Psychology has also offered remedies. The cognitive interview developed by Ronald Fisher and Edward Geiselman applies memory principles directly: mentally reinstate the context of the event (context-dependent memory), report everything however trivial, recall the events in different orders, and recall them from different perspectives (each provides different retrieval cues). It reliably increases correct recall without much increase in errors. Line-ups conducted by an officer who does not know who the suspect is, with fair fillers and a record of the witness's initial confidence, reduce false identifications.

Clinical link

Clinical and forensic psychologists are often asked about memory in court. The safest summary of the research is that memory is usually accurate in its gist, often inaccurate in its details, readily altered by suggestion, and that confidence and vividness are poor guides to accuracy. A clinician who interviews a child about possible abuse, or an adult about a traumatic event, must therefore use open, non-leading questions, avoid repeated suggestive questioning, and record exactly what was asked and answered.

The recovered memory controversy

In the late 1980s and 1990s, a growing number of adults in the United States and elsewhere reported recovering, often during therapy, memories of childhood sexual abuse that they had previously had no awareness of. Some therapists believed that such memories had been repressed and that techniques such as hypnosis, guided imagery, dream interpretation and repeated suggestive questioning could bring them back. Some recovered memories led to accusations, family breakdown and criminal trials. Critics, led by Elizabeth Loftus, argued that the very techniques used to "recover" memories were the techniques known to create false ones, and the term "false memory syndrome" was coined by an organisation founded in 1992 by families who said they had been falsely accused. The resulting dispute became known as the memory wars.

The evidence supports a middle position. Genuine abuse can be forgotten for long periods and later remembered: Linda Meyer Williams (1994) interviewed women whose abuse in childhood had been documented in hospital records seventeen years earlier and found that 38 per cent did not report it. (Some of these women may simply have chosen not to disclose it, and ordinary forgetting of early childhood events need not involve repression.) But false memories of traumatic events can also be created, especially through suggestion by an authority figure, imagination and repetition, and hypnosis increases confidence far more than it increases accuracy. A recovered memory is therefore neither automatically true nor automatically false; it needs independent corroboration, and therapists should never try to dig up memories that the client does not report.

7.10 Amnesia and the Biology of MemoryHigh yield · 31 Qs

Much of what we know about the organisation of memory has come from people like Clive Wearing whose memories were damaged. This section describes the main forms of amnesia and the classic cases briefly, and outlines what they reveal about consolidation and the brain; the neural detail belongs to the Physiological book.

Kinds of amnesia

amnesia is a pathological loss of memory, disproportionate to any other cognitive impairment. Take the moment of the brain injury, illness or surgery as a fixed point on a time line. anterograde amnesia (forward-acting) is the inability to form new long-term memories for events after that point: the person cannot learn new names, cannot remember what happened an hour ago. retrograde amnesia (backward-acting) is the loss of memories formed before that point: the person cannot remember events that preceded the injury. Most organic amnesias combine the two, with the anterograde component usually the more disabling. Retrograde amnesia typically follows Ribot's law: the years just before the injury are lost, while remote memories from childhood survive.

Amnesia should be distinguished from the other classic disorders that neuropsychologists name with the prefix "a-": aphasia (disorder of language, often listed with alexia, an acquired loss of the ability to read, and acalculia, an acquired loss of the ability to calculate), apraxia (inability to carry out skilled, learned movements despite intact strength), agnosia (inability to recognise objects despite intact sensation) and dementia (progressive decline across several cognitive functions, including memory, judgement and abstraction).

H.M.: the man who changed the study of memory

Henry Gustav Molaison (1926 to 2008), known for over fifty years only by his initials, had suffered from severe epilepsy since childhood. In 1953, when he was 27, the neurosurgeon William Beecher Scoville removed the medial portions of both of his temporal lobes, including most of the hippocampus, the amygdala and the adjacent cortex, in an attempt to stop his seizures. The seizures were greatly reduced. But H.M. was left with a profound anterograde amnesia. Scoville and the psychologist Brenda Milner (born 1918) described the case in 1957, and H.M. was studied for the rest of his life, especially by Milner and later by Suzanne Corkin.

H.M.'s case established four principles. First, memory can be separated from other cognitive abilities: his intelligence, language and perception were normal, and his personality largely unchanged. Second, short-term memory is separate from long-term memory: his digit span was normal, and he could hold a conversation, but as soon as his attention moved on, the content was lost. He read the same magazines again and again with fresh interest and could not learn the address of the house his family moved to after his operation. Third, the medial temporal lobes are needed to form new declarative memories but are not where old memories are stored: his memories of childhood were largely intact, and his retrograde amnesia covered only the years before the surgery (originally estimated at about two years; later testing suggested it reached further back). Fourth, implicit memory is separate from explicit memory: as described in Section 7.5, H.M. learned to trace a star in a mirror, improving over three days just like a healthy person, while having no conscious memory of ever having done the task. He also showed normal priming. After his death in 2008, his brain was preserved and sliced into thousands of sections for study.

Exam trap

H.M.'s most profound deficit was anterograde amnesia for declarative (explicit) memory; his procedural memory (mirror tracing) was spared, his short-term memory was normal, and his retrograde amnesia was limited. Options saying he lost procedural memory, semantic memory "only", working memory span, or all old memories are wrong. The structures removed were the medial temporal lobes on both sides, including the hippocampus and amygdala, not the frontal, parietal or occipital lobes. The hippocampus is the structure most often linked to anterograde amnesia and to the consolidation of new declarative memories, not the hypothalamus (motivation, temperature, hunger), the pituitary (hormones) or the cerebellum (motor coordination and motor learning).

Korsakoff's syndrome and confabulation

Korsakoff's syndrome (also called Korsakoff's psychosis, or Korsakoff's amnesic syndrome) is a memory disorder most commonly seen in people with a long history of heavy alcohol use. It was described by the Russian psychiatrist Sergei Korsakoff in the late 1880s, and it is caused mainly by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1), which commonly accompanies chronic alcoholism because of poor diet and poor absorption. It often follows an acute phase called Wernicke's encephalopathy, described by Carl Wernicke in 1881, marked by confusion, unsteady gait (ataxia) and abnormal eye movements such as nystagmus and paralysis of eye muscles; the two together are called the Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. The damage centres on the medial diencephalon, especially the mammillary bodies and the mediodorsal (also called dorsomedial) nuclei of the thalamus, with more diffuse damage elsewhere.

The memory disorder has three features. There is a severe anterograde amnesia for explicit memories. There is also an extensive retrograde amnesia, which grows as the disease progresses and can reach back into childhood, with the usual temporal gradient. And there is confabulation: asked what they did yesterday, patients may give a fluent, detailed and entirely false account, often built from fragments of real older memories, with no intent to deceive and no awareness that it is false. Confabulation must be distinguished from malingering (deliberately faking or exaggerating symptoms for gain), from delirium (an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and consciousness) and from simple confusion. The neurologist Oliver Sacks described a Korsakoff patient, Jimmie G., who in 1975 still believed he was a nineteen-year-old sailor at the end of the Second World War, "the lost mariner". Treatment with thiamine in the acute Wernicke stage can prevent much of the damage, which is why it matters clinically to recognise the syndrome early.

Other neurological diseases affect memory in contrasting ways. In Alzheimer's disease, episodic memory for recent events is usually the first to fail and semantic memory declines later. In Huntington's and Parkinson's diseases, which damage the basal ganglia, procedural learning is impaired more than declarative memory, the mirror image of H.M. (Physiological and Psychiatric Disorders books).

Other amnesias

infantile amnesia, also called childhood amnesia, is the near-total absence, in adults, of memories from the first three or four years of life; the average earliest memory dates from about three to three and a half years. Freud, who named it, attributed it to repression. Current explanations are developmental: the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex are immature in infancy; young children lack the language in which later memories are organised and retrieved; a stable sense of self, needed to organise memories as "things that happened to me", emerges only around the second year (Mark Howe and Mary Courage, 1993); and the schemas through which an adult searches memory differ from those of the infant who encoded it.

Transient global amnesia is a sudden, temporary anterograde amnesia, usually in middle-aged or older adults, lasting several hours and never more than a day. The person is alert and knows who they are but asks the same questions over and over; the episode resolves completely, leaving only a gap for the hours it lasted. Post-traumatic amnesia is the period of confusion and failed new learning after a head injury; its length is one of the best predictors of the severity of the injury.

Dissociative amnesia, by contrast, is an inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, too extensive to be ordinary forgetting and without a medical cause. It may be localised (a specific period), selective (some events of a period), generalised (the person's whole life history), systematised (a category of information) or continuous (everything from a certain point onwards). It may come with dissociative fugue, purposeful travel or wandering with amnesia for identity, and it is distinct from dissociative identity disorder, in which two or more personality states alternate with gaps in memory. These conditions are taught in the Psychiatric Disorders book.

Consolidation, the hippocampus and the search for the engram

Where is a memory stored? In 1904 the German biologist Richard Semon coined the word engram for the physical trace that learning leaves in the nervous system. The most determined search for it was made by the American neuropsychologist Karl Spencer Lashley (1890 to 1958). For three decades Lashley trained rats to run mazes and then removed different parts of their cerebral cortex, hoping to find the place where the maze memory was kept. He never found it. Memory loss depended on how much cortex was removed, not on which part; he called this the principle of mass action. And different parts of the cortex seemed able to take over for one another, the principle of equipotentiality (both set out in his 1929 book Brain Mechanisms and Intelligence). In his 1950 paper "In search of the engram" he wrote, only half joking, that reviewing the evidence sometimes made him feel "that the necessary conclusion is that learning just is not possible." His conclusion was that memories are not localised in single spots but are distributed widely across the cortex.

Lashley's student Donald Olding Hebb (1904 to 1985) suggested how a distributed memory could form. In The Organization of Behavior (1949) he proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them is strengthened, so that groups of neurons that are active together become linked into cell assemblies, often summarised as "cells that fire together wire together". A physical mechanism of exactly this kind was found in 1973, when Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo showed that brief, high-frequency stimulation of a pathway into the rabbit hippocampus produced a lasting increase in the strength of the synapses. This long-term potentiation (LTP), which depends on NMDA glutamate receptors, is the leading candidate for the cellular basis of learning and memory (Physiological book). Modern methods have even allowed researchers to tag and reactivate the specific "engram cells" that are active during learning in mice, vindicating Semon's term a century later.

How do these findings fit with H.M.? The standard account, the standard consolidation theory, is that the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe bind together the scattered cortical elements of a new declarative memory and are needed for that memory for some time; through repeated reactivation, much of it during sleep, the memory is gradually reorganised so that it can be retrieved from the neocortex without the hippocampus (systems consolidation). This explains why damage to the hippocampus prevents new learning and erases recent memories while sparing remote ones. Rival accounts hold that vivid episodic memories always depend on the hippocampus (multiple trace theory, Lynn Nadel and Morris Moscovitch, 1997), and research on reconsolidation (Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux, 2000) has shown that a consolidated memory, when it is reactivated, becomes fragile again for a time and must be restabilised, a biological counterpart of Bartlett's reconstructive memory. 7.11 Improving MemoryLower yield · 2 Qs

Everything in this chapter can be turned into advice. Attention determines what is encoded; meaning, elaboration, self-reference and imagery determine how well it is encoded; organisation and distinctiveness make it findable; spacing and retrieval practice make it last; matching study to test conditions makes it accessible when needed; and sleep consolidates it. The techniques below are those principles put to work.

Mnemonic devices

A mnemonic is any technique that improves memory by imposing organisation, meaning or imagery on material that lacks them. The oldest is the method of loci. The Roman orator Cicero told how the Greek poet Simonides of Ceos, having stepped out of a banquet hall moments before its roof collapsed, was able to identify the crushed guests by remembering where each had been sitting. Order, Simonides realised, can be held by place. To use the method, you choose a familiar route (your house, the walk from your hostel to the library) and place a vivid image of each item to be remembered at successive locations along it; to recall, you walk the route in imagination and "see" each item where you left it. Shereshevsky used this method, and modern memory champions still do.

The peg-word system replaces locations with a memorised list of "pegs" that rhyme with the numbers: one is a bun, two is a shoe, three is a tree, four is a door, five is a hive, and so on. Each item to be learned is linked to its peg by an interactive image: to remember that the third item is "milk", picture milk pouring over a tree. The method gives direct access to any item by number. The keyword method, developed for foreign vocabulary by Richard Atkinson (1975; tested with Michael Raugh on Russian vocabulary), links a foreign word to its meaning through an English word that sounds like part of it. The Spanish word "pato" means duck; it sounds like "pot", so you imagine a duck sitting in a pot.

Verbal mnemonics use the first letters of the items. An acronym is a word or pronounceable string formed from the first letters of the items, such as VIBGYOR for the colours of the rainbow or OCEAN for the five broad personality traits. An acrostic is a sentence whose words begin with the first letters of the items, such as "Every Good Boy Deserves Fruit" for the notes on the lines of the treble stave (E, G, B, D, F). Other techniques draw on principles we have met. Chunking groups items into fewer, larger units. Categorical clustering organises a list by meaning; W. A. Bousfield (1953) showed that people spontaneously recall randomly ordered lists in category clusters. Interactive imagery links items in a single scene, and it is the interaction, not bizarreness, that matters (Keith Wollen and colleagues, 1972). Narrative chaining weaves items into a story: Gordon Bower and Michael Clark (1969) had people learn twelve lists of ten words each; those who made up a story for each list later recalled about 93 per cent of the words, while those who simply studied recalled about 13 per cent. Indian context

The Vedas were transmitted orally for many centuries before they were written down, with extraordinary fidelity. Reciters learned each text in several forms: the continuous text (samhita patha), word by word (pada patha), in overlapping pairs (krama patha: word 1 and 2, then 2 and 3, then 3 and 4), and in elaborate forward-and-backward weavings (jata and ghana patha). Each recitation pattern acts as a check on the others, so that an error in one would be exposed by the rest. It is one of the most sophisticated memory systems ever devised, and UNESCO recognised the tradition of Vedic chanting as part of the world's intangible heritage in 2003.

Study systems: SQ3R and PQRST

Two study systems for textbooks build elaboration, self-questioning and retrieval practice into reading. SQ3R, devised by the American educational psychologist Francis P. Robinson in 1946, has five steps: Survey (skim headings and summaries to build a framework), Question (turn each heading into a question), Read (read actively to answer the question), Recite (close the book and state the answer from memory) and Review (go over the whole chapter, testing yourself). PQRST, described by Ellen Thomas and H. Alan Robinson (1972) and widely taught in introductory psychology, is a close variant: Preview, Question, Read, Self-recitation and Test. In both, the most important steps are the ones students skip: the questions, which give reading a purpose and a structure, and the recitation, which is retrieval practice.

Spacing, testing, overlearning and sleep

When John Dunlosky and his colleagues (2013) reviewed the evidence on ten popular study techniques, two were rated as having high utility: practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice. Highlighting, rereading and summarising were rated as having low utility. Rereading feels productive because the material becomes fluent and familiar, but fluency is a poor guide to what can be retrieved later (the illusion of competence, Section 7.5). Retrieval practice works because every successful retrieval strengthens the memory and the route to it, and every failed one shows you exactly what to restudy. Spacing works partly because each return to the material after some forgetting requires effortful retrieval, and partly because material studied in different contexts acquires more varied retrieval cues. Robert Bjork has called such techniques "desirable difficulties": they make learning feel harder and slower, and make it last longer. Mixing different kinds of problems in one practice session (interleaving), rather than practising one kind at a time, is another desirable difficulty: it teaches you to recognise which method a problem needs.

Here is a worked example. Suppose you have two days to master a chapter. Massed study would mean reading it four times on the first day. A spaced-retrieval plan would mean reading it once on the first morning; that evening, writing down everything you can recall about each section heading before checking; the next morning, answering the chapter's self-test questions without looking; and the next evening, attempting the past-paper questions and restudying only what you got wrong. The total time is similar. The second plan uses testing, spacing, generation and feedback, and on the evidence reviewed in this chapter it will produce far better retention a week later.

Two further aids complete the picture. overlearning, continuing to practise after material has been mastered, increases retention: William Krueger (1929) found that 50 per cent overlearning (half as many extra trials as were needed to learn) produced a clear gain, and 100 per cent overlearning a smaller additional gain, a case of diminishing returns. And sleep after learning reduces interference and actively consolidates new memories, as Jenkins and Dallenbach's results first hinted; studying until the small hours at the expense of sleep is a poor bargain. (Massed versus distributed practice and overlearning in skill learning are also discussed in Chapter 6.)

Clinical link

Memory rehabilitation for people with brain injury or dementia applies the same principles in modified form. Because amnesic patients cannot tell their own errors from correct responses, errorless learning (Baddeley and Barbara Wilson, 1994), in which the patient is guided to the correct response from the start rather than encouraged to guess, prevents errors from being learned through intact implicit memory. Spaced retrieval, in which a patient recalls a fact at gradually lengthening intervals, and the method of vanishing cues (Elizabeth Glisky, Daniel Schacter and Endel Tulving, 1986), in which letter cues are withdrawn one at a time, exploit spared implicit learning. External aids such as diaries, alarms and smartphone reminders compensate for failures of prospective memory.

The case of Clive Wearing, revisited

Go back to Clive Wearing, writing in his diary yet again that he is now, at last, truly awake. You can now describe what happened to him in the language of this chapter. Herpes simplex encephalitis destroyed the hippocampus and much of the medial temporal lobe on both sides, the structures that consolidate new declarative memories. His short-term (working) memory still worked, which is why he could follow a sentence or a conversation for as long as it kept moving. But nothing he experienced could be consolidated into durable long-term memory: he had a dense anterograde amnesia like H.M.'s. Alongside it, and probably because the damage spread well beyond the hippocampus, he had a far more extensive retrograde amnesia than H.M. ever had, one that wiped out almost every particular episode of his past. Without episodic memory he had none of the autonoetic consciousness that lets a person travel back in time and feel himself to be the same person who lived through earlier events. Every moment of awareness seemed to him like the first, and so every earlier diary entry looked like a lie.

What survived teaches just as much. Clive kept some semantic knowledge (that he was a musician, that he was married to Deborah), much as Tulving's patient K.C. kept facts about his own life while losing every episode. He kept his procedural memory: sight-reading, playing and conducting, skills that depend on circuits outside the hippocampus, exactly as Squire's taxonomy predicts and as H.M.'s mirror tracing had already shown. And his joy at seeing Deborah, and his love for her, survived without a single remembered conversation to anchor them, a reminder that past experience can shape feeling and behaviour without any conscious recollection. In one life, then, you can see three of this chapter's distinctions: short-term versus long-term memory, episodic versus semantic memory, and explicit versus implicit memory. It also shows what the chapter has argued from Ebbinghaus to Bartlett: memory is not one thing but several, and it is less a recording of the past than the means by which each of us builds a continuing self.

Chapter summary

7.1 What memory is, and how Ebbinghaus measured it. Memory is the capacity to retain information over time and use it later. It involves three processes: encoding (turning input into a code the system can hold), storage and retrieval. Encoding depends on attention, and details that were never attended to are lost through encoding failure, as Nickerson and Adams's coin study showed. Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) invented the nonsense syllable, acted as his own participant and measured retention by the savings method: original learning minus relearning, divided by original learning, multiplied by 100. His forgetting curve is negatively accelerated: steep loss in the first hour, then a slow decline that never reaches zero. He also first noted the benefits of overlearning and spaced repetitions, the advantage of the beginning and end of a list, and the ease of meaningful material. Retention can be measured by recall (free, serial and cued, including the paired-associate learning introduced by Calkins in 1894), by recognition, by the reconstruction method (restoring the order of supplied items) and by relearning, which is the most sensitive measure.

7.2 The multi-store model. Building on William James's primary and secondary memory (1890), Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968) proposed three stores (sensory, short-term and long-term) worked by control processes such as attention and rehearsal. Sensory memory briefly holds a large amount of pre-categorical information. Sperling's partial-report procedure (1960) showed that about nine of twelve letters are available in iconic memory for roughly a quarter to half a second, and Darwin, Turvey and Crowder (1972) put echoic memory at about two to four seconds. Short-term memory holds seven plus or minus two items (Miller, 1956), or about four chunks (Cowan, 2001), and chunking draws on long-term knowledge. Without rehearsal it lasts about 18 to 30 seconds (Brown, 1958; Peterson and Peterson, 1959), but Keppel and Underwood (1962) showed that the loss came from proactive interference rather than decay. Coding is mainly acoustic in short-term memory (Conrad, 1964; Baddeley, 1966) and semantic in long-term memory, which has no known capacity limit and can last a lifetime (Bahrick). The serial position curve (primacy from long-term memory, recency from short-term memory; Glanzer and Cunitz, 1966) and the double dissociation between H.M. and K.F. support separate stores. Critics point out that neither store is a single unit, that rehearsal is not the main route into long-term memory, and that long-term knowledge acts on information while it is still in short-term memory.

7.3 Working memory. Baddeley and Hitch (1974) used the dual-task method to show that short-term memory has several parts. The central executive controls attention but stores nothing: it focuses, divides and switches attention and links working memory with long-term memory. Random number generation loads it, and the Stroop task measures its inhibitory function. The phonological loop (a phonological store and an articulatory control process) holds speech-based information. Four findings support it: the phonological similarity effect, the word-length effect, articulatory suppression and the irrelevant speech effect. It also helps in learning new words. The visuospatial sketchpad (a visual cache and an inner scribe, in Logie's terms) holds visual and spatial information and carries out mental rotation. The episodic buffer, added in 2000, binds information from the other components and from long-term memory into integrated episodes. Short-term memory is simple storage, measured by simple span; working memory is storage plus processing, measured by complex span and backward digit span, and it predicts comprehension and reasoning.

7.4 Levels of processing and the quality of encoding. Craik and Lockhart (1972) argued that memory is a by-product of processing and that deeper processing (structural, then phonemic, then semantic) leaves more durable traces whether or not the person intends to learn (Hyde and Jenkins, 1973). Craik and Tulving (1975) found the best recognition after semantic questions, and more elaborate processing helped even within one level. Maintenance (Type I) rehearsal keeps information available but adds little; elaborative (Type II) rehearsal builds lasting memories. Distinctiveness, the self-reference effect (Rogers, Kuiper and Kirker, 1977), the generation effect (Slamecka and Graf, 1978) and Paivio's dual coding theory (1971) show other ways of strengthening encoding. The framework was criticised as circular. Morris, Bransford and Franks (1977) showed with a rhyming test that what matters is transfer-appropriate processing, the match between processing at study and processing at test.

7.5 The systems of long-term memory. Tulving (1972) distinguished episodic memory (personal events located in time and place, recalled with autonoetic consciousness) from semantic, or generic, memory (general knowledge, held with noetic awareness). Procedural memory holds skills. K.C. lost his episodic memory but kept his semantic knowledge. Explicit (declarative) memory involves conscious recollection, while implicit (nondeclarative) memory shows itself in performance without awareness (Graf and Schacter, 1985), as in Claparède's pinprick, word-stem completion by amnesic patients and H.M.'s mirror tracing. Squire's taxonomy links declarative memory to the medial temporal lobe, and links procedural skills, priming, classical conditioning and non-associative learning to other structures. Priming may be repetition priming or semantic priming (Meyer and Schvaneveldt, 1971); its masked form is used to study subliminal perception. Autobiographical memory shows a reminiscence bump for ages 10 to 30. Prospective memory (remembering to act, cued by an event or by time) contrasts with retrospective memory. Flashbulb memories (Brown and Kulik, 1977) are vivid and confidently held but no more accurate than ordinary memories (Neisser and Harsch, 1992; Talarico and Rubin, 2003); emotional arousal and rehearsal explain them, not a "Now Print!" mechanism. Eidetic imagery is rare and found mainly in children. Metamemory (Flavell, 1971) covers the feeling of knowing (Hart, 1965) and the tip-of-the-tongue state (Brown and McNeill, 1966), which is a failure of retrieval, not of storage.

7.6 How knowledge is organised. Collins and Quillian's hierarchical network model (1969) stores concepts as nodes in a hierarchy, with each property stored only at the highest level it applies to (cognitive economy), and predicts that verification time rises with the number of links travelled. Typicality effects and familiarity confounds undermined it. They led to the feature comparison model and to Collins and Loftus's spreading activation model (1975), in which activation spreads along links of varying length; this explains semantic priming and typicality. A proposition is the smallest unit of knowledge that can be true or false. Connectionist, or parallel distributed processing, models (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) represent knowledge as patterns of simultaneous activation across many interconnected units and store it in connection weights; such networks can retrieve a whole memory from any part of it and degrade gradually when damaged. Schemas are organised bodies of knowledge (Bartlett, 1932) that guide understanding and distort recall (Bransford and Johnson, 1972; Brewer and Treyens, 1981). Scripts are schemas for routine events (Schank and Abelson, 1977) and belong to generic memory.

7.7 Retrieval: cues, context and state. Information may be available but not accessible, and retrieval cues such as category names make it accessible (Tulving and Pearlstone, 1966). Recognition is usually easier than recall because the test supplies the item, as the generate-recognise account says, although the recognition failure of recallable words shows that this is not the whole story. Tulving and Thomson's encoding specificity principle (1973) states that a cue works to the extent that it was encoded with the memory. Its special cases include context-dependent memory (Godden and Baddeley's divers, 1975; merely imagining the original room also helps), state-dependent memory (Goodwin and colleagues, 1969, with alcohol) and mood-dependent memory. Mood-congruent memory, the easier recall of material that matches one's current mood (Bower, 1981), helps to keep depression going. Context and state effects are strongest in free recall and weak or absent in recognition.

7.8 Forgetting. Decay theory, traced to Thorndike's law of disuse, holds that unused traces fade. McGeoch (1932) objected that time itself causes nothing, and Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924) found less forgetting after sleep than after the same time awake. Interference theory distinguishes proactive interference (old learning disrupts new) from retroactive interference (new learning disrupts old), and studies both with A-B, A-C designs. Interference grows with similarity, proactive interference is the larger source of laboratory forgetting (Underwood, 1957), and Melton and Irwin (1940) named response competition and unlearning as its two factors. Tulving's cue-dependent forgetting and retrieval-induced forgetting (Anderson, Bjork and Bjork, 1994) show that much forgetting is a failure of access. Freud's repression is unconscious and lacks controlled evidence; suppression, directed forgetting and the think/no-think paradigm show that conscious motivated forgetting is real. Consolidation failure explains the loss of recent memories after concussion or ECT, with the temporal gradient of Ribot's law (1881). The classic named effects are the Zeigarnik effect (1927), the Ovsiankina effect (1928), Ballard's reminiscence (1913), the von Restorff isolation effect (1933), the spacing effect and the testing effect (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). Schacter's seven sins (2001) sort memory failures into sins of omission and sins of commission. Retention and forgetting are inversely related: the more is retained, the less is forgotten.

7.9 Memory as reconstruction. Bartlett (1932) used "The War of the Ghosts" to show that remembering is an "effort after meaning": retold stories became shorter and more conventional, and were rationalised to fit the rememberer's schemas. The changes are often described as levelling, sharpening and assimilation (Allport and Postman, 1947). Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed that the verb "smashed" raised speed estimates and later false reports of broken glass, which is the misinformation effect. Whole false events can be implanted (lost in the mall; Loftus and Pickrell, 1995), and the Deese-Roediger-McDermott paradigm produces confident recall of an unpresented critical lure. Source monitoring (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, 1993) explains source amnesia, cryptomnesia and misattribution. Déjà vu is an illusion of recognition, and confabulation is the sincere filling of memory gaps. Eyewitness testimony is undermined by weapon focus, the cross-race effect, leading questions, source errors and inflated confidence; the cognitive interview and fair line-ups reduce errors. In the recovered memory controversy the evidence supports a middle position: real abuse can be forgotten and later remembered, and false memories can be created by suggestion.

7.10 Amnesia and the biology of memory. Amnesia is a pathological loss of memory. Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new memories after a brain injury; retrograde amnesia is the loss of memories formed before it, usually following Ribot's law. After the removal of both medial temporal lobes, H.M. (Scoville and Milner, 1957) had a severe anterograde amnesia for declarative memory. His short-term memory, intelligence, priming and procedural learning (mirror tracing) were normal, and his retrograde amnesia was limited. Korsakoff's syndrome is caused by thiamine deficiency in chronic alcoholism and is often preceded by Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia and nystagmus). It damages the mammillary bodies and the mediodorsal thalamus and produces anterograde amnesia, an extensive retrograde amnesia and confabulation. Infantile amnesia reflects the development of the brain, language and the self rather than repression. Transient global amnesia and dissociative amnesia (Psychiatric Disorders book) are other forms. Lashley's search for the engram (Semon's term) produced the principles of mass action and equipotentiality and the conclusion that memories are distributed across the cortex; Hebb's cell assemblies (1949) and long-term potentiation (Bliss and Lømo, 1973) suggest the mechanism. The hippocampus consolidates new declarative memories, which in time come to depend on the neocortex.

7.11 Improving memory. Mnemonics add organisation, meaning and imagery to material. The main ones are the method of loci (Simonides), the peg-word system, the keyword method (Atkinson, 1975), acronyms (VIBGYOR), acrostics ("Every Good Boy Deserves Fruit"), chunking, categorical clustering, interactive imagery and narrative chaining (Bower and Clark, 1969). Study systems such as SQ3R (Robinson, 1946: Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) and PQRST (Preview, Question, Read, Self-recitation, Test) build questioning and retrieval practice into reading. The best-supported techniques are practice testing and distributed practice (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013). Other aids are desirable difficulties such as interleaving, overlearning (Krueger, 1929), whose returns diminish, and sleep, which consolidates new learning. Rereading and highlighting feel effective but are not. Memory rehabilitation uses errorless learning, spaced retrieval and the method of vanishing cues.

From the past papers

  1. In an experiment the stimuli were presented to the subjects in certain order and after the presentation of stimuli their order was disturbed. In the test phase the subjects were needed to set them in the presented order. Which of the following methods was used in this experiment? (RML 2024)

    (a) Reconstruction (b) Relearning (c) Recognition (d) Recall

    Answer: (a) Reconstruction. All the items are handed back, and the participant only has to restore their original order, which is the reconstruction method. Recall would require producing the items without them being supplied. Recognition would require picking old items out from among new ones. Relearning measures the time or trials saved when the same material is learned again. Do not confuse the method with Bartlett's theory of reconstructive memory.

  2. Which type of memory has the largest capacity but shortest duration? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Sensory memory (b) Short-term memory (c) Long-term memory (d) Working memory

    Answer: (a) Sensory memory. The sensory register briefly holds almost everything that reaches the senses, for less than a second (icon) or about two to four seconds (echo). Long-term memory also has a vast capacity, but it has the longest duration, not the shortest. Short-term and working memory are small (about seven items, or four chunks) and last seconds rather than fractions of a second.

  3. The capacity of working memory is best-described in terms of number of meaningful units of information, called (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Chunks (b) Features (c) Scripts (d) Proposition

    Answer: (a) Chunks. Miller (1956) showed that the span limit applies to chunks, meaningful units built with long-term knowledge, and Cowan (2001) puts pure capacity at about four chunks. Features are the attributes of a concept (as in the feature comparison model). Scripts are long-term schemas for routine events. Propositions are units of meaning that can be true or false; they describe how knowledge is represented, not how much the short-term system holds.

  4. Who developed the concept of 'magic number'? (RML 2024)

    (a) Heinz (b) Ebbinghaus (c) Triesman (d) Miller

    Answer: (d) Miller. George A. Miller's 1956 paper was titled "The magical number seven, plus or minus two." Anne Treisman (misspelt in the paper) is known for the attenuation model of attention (1964) and feature integration theory (1980), and Heinz for Johnston and Heinz's multimode theory of attention (1978); both belong to Chapter 5. Ebbinghaus noticed that about seven syllables could be repeated after a single reading, but he did not propose the "magic number".

  5. Primacy-recency effect was first observed by whom? (RML 2024)

    (a) McCray and Hunter (b) Ebbinghaus (c) Craik and Lockhart (d) Baddeley and Hitch

    Answer: (a) McCray and Hunter (the official key). J. W. McCrary and W. S. Hunter published influential serial position curves in 1953, and the key credits them. Historically, though, Ebbinghaus had already noted in 1885 that the beginning and end of a list are learned first, so be ready for a paper that keys (b). Craik and Lockhart proposed levels of processing (1972), and Baddeley and Hitch proposed working memory (1974); neither is connected with the discovery of the serial position effect.

  6. Which of the following secondary tasks is thought to affect the central executive in terms of working memory? (RML 2022)

    (a) Continually repeating a word (b) Remembering a spatial configuration (c) Listening to irrelevant speech (d) Random number generation

    Answer: (d) Random number generation. Producing a random sequence requires constant monitoring and suppression of habitual sequences, which is exactly the central executive's job. Repeating a word is articulatory suppression, which occupies the phonological loop. A spatial configuration loads the visuospatial sketchpad. Irrelevant speech gains access to the phonological store.

  7. In Baddeley's model of working memory, the subsystem responsible for integrating information from the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and long-term memory into a single, coherent episode is the: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Central Executive (b) Episodic Buffer (c) Visuospatial Sketchpad (d) Phonological Loop

    Answer: (b) Episodic Buffer. Baddeley added it in 2000 as a limited-capacity store that binds information from different codes into integrated episodes. The central executive controls attention but stores nothing, so it cannot hold the bound result. The sketchpad and the loop each hold only one kind of information.

  8. The concept of "Self-reference effect" was founded by: (RML 2024)

    (a) Craik and Lockhart (b) Posner (c) Paivio (d) Rogers and Kuiper

    Answer: (d) Rogers and Kuiper. Rogers, Kuiper and Kirker (1977) added the question "Does this word describe you?" to the levels-of-processing tasks and found that recall was about twice as good as after semantic judgements. Craik and Lockhart created the framework the effect extends, but they did not discover it. Posner is known for attention research, and Paivio for dual coding theory.

  9. We know that a common distinction is made between explicit and implicit memory. But which one of the following does NOT accurately reflect one of these terms? (RML 2020)

    (a) Explicit memory involves conscious awareness of the original information or the situation in which the learning occurred (b) Implicit memory refers to an influence on behaviour, feelings or thoughts as a result of prior experience (c) Explicit memory involves recollection of the original information or experience that is subsequently recalled (d) Implicit memory involves a conscious recollection of the original events

    Answer: (d). Implicit memory is, by definition, memory without conscious recollection (Graf and Schacter, 1985): the amnesic patient who completes MOT___ with MOTEL without remembering the list. Options (a), (b) and (c) are accurate. The wrong option takes the definition of explicit memory and gives it to implicit memory.

  10. Which type of memory produces scripts of familiar routines? (RML 2025)

    (a) Generic memory (b) Episodic memory (c) Autobiographical memory (d) Working memory

    Answer: (a) Generic memory. A script (Schank and Abelson, 1977) is general knowledge of how a routine usually goes, abstracted from many episodes, so it belongs to generic (semantic) memory. Episodic and autobiographical memory hold particular events of one's own life, which are exactly the details that a script leaves out. Working memory is a temporary workspace, not a store of routines.

  11. A person who is very adept at planning, monitoring, and controlling all phases of the multistore model must have very good: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Attention (b) Inhibition (c) Meta-cognition (d) Knowledge base

    Answer: (c) Meta-cognition. Metacognition (including Flavell's metamemory) is knowledge about one's own cognition and the ability to monitor and control it, at every stage from encoding to retrieval. Attention selects what enters the system but does not plan or monitor the whole process. Inhibition is one executive function, the suppression of unwanted responses or memories. A knowledge base is the stored long-term knowledge that makes chunking and elaboration possible: it is what is remembered, not control over remembering.

  12. Current research explains the vividness of flashbulb memories mainly by: (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) A special 'Now Print!' mechanism that stores the scene permanently and exactly (b) The absence of any interference from later events (c) Photographic (eidetic) memory, which only some people have (d) Emotional arousal at encoding plus repeated rehearsal and retelling

    Answer: (d) Emotional arousal at encoding plus repeated rehearsal and retelling. "Now Print!" was Brown and Kulik's original 1977 proposal, and it was abandoned when Neisser and Harsch (1992) and Talarico and Rubin (2003) showed that flashbulb memories change as much as ordinary ones. They are not free of interference: later accounts and news coverage reshape them. Eidetic imagery is a rare, lingering visual image found mainly in children, and it has nothing to do with flashbulb memories, which almost everyone forms.

  13. Which model proposes that memory is organized in a network of interconnected nodes? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Associative model (b) Connectionist model (c) Network model (d) Linkage model

    Answer: (b) Connectionist model. Connectionism, or parallel distributed processing (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986), represents memories as patterns of activation spread across many interconnected nodes, with knowledge stored in connection weights. The question is loosely worded, because Collins and Quillian's hierarchical "network model" also uses nodes and links. That is why the stem's stress on interconnection points to connectionism. "Associative" and "linkage" describe what any network does rather than naming the model in question.

  14. Rahul thought that he could do much better in the English exam as it was held in the same classroom where his classes were being held whereas in geography exam he was very nervous as this was conducted in a totally new environment. This perhaps was due to: (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Examination fear (b) The principle of congruity (c) His anxious personality (d) The principle of encoding specificity

    Answer: (d) The principle of encoding specificity. The familiar classroom reinstates the context in which the material was encoded, so it supplies extra retrieval cues (Tulving and Thomson, 1973); context-dependent memory is the specific case. Examination fear and an anxious personality might explain nervousness, but they do not explain why the same student expects to do better in the old room. "The principle of congruity" is not a memory principle; the nearest real idea, mood-congruent memory, concerns the match between mood and material.

  15. Alcohol-induced amnesia may be an example of: (RML 2022)

    (a) Dependent memory (b) State-dependent memory (c) Independent memory (d) State-independent memory

    Answer: (b) State-dependent memory. Material learned while intoxicated is recalled better when the person is intoxicated again (Goodwin and colleagues, 1969), so the forgetting on sobering up depends on the mismatch of internal states. "Dependent memory", "independent memory" and "state-independent memory" are not established terms; they are distractors built from the words of the right answer. Strictly, a complete blackout in which nothing was ever encoded is anterograde amnesia, and no change of state can bring it back.

  16. An English-speaking person may have greater difficulty learning Spanish because of his or her tendency to wish to apply English grammar to the new language. This occurs due to which of the following phenomenon? (RML 2024)

    (a) Proactive reference (b) Retroactive reference (c) Trace decay (d) Retrieval failure

    Answer: (a) Proactive interference; the official key, (b), is wrong. English was learned first and is disrupting the newer learning of Spanish. Old disrupting new is proactive interference by definition ("reference" in the options means interference). Retroactive interference would be Spanish making English grammar harder to recall. Trace decay is fading with disuse, and retrieval failure is a missing cue; neither fits an old habit intruding on new learning.

  17. Who developed the Decay theory? (RML 2024)

    (a) Ebbinghaus (b) Thorndike (c) Tulving (d) Melton and Irwin

    Answer: (b) Thorndike. Thorndike's law of disuse held that connections that are not exercised grow weaker, which is the decay idea in psychology. Ebbinghaus charted the time course of forgetting but did not claim that time causes it. Tulving is associated with cue-dependent forgetting, and Melton and Irwin (1940) with the two-factor theory of interference.

  18. Retrieval-induced forgetting refers to which memory-based phenomenon? (RML 2020)

    (a) People remembered practised information better than unpractised information (b) People remembered unpractised information better than practised information (c) People remember names better than faces (d) People remember faces better than names

    Answer: (a). In the study by Anderson, Bjork and Bjork (1994), practised items were recalled best. The defining finding, though, is that unpractised items related to the practised ones were recalled worse than items from unpractised categories: retrieving some memories inhibits their competitors. Option (b) reverses the result, and the options about names and faces have nothing to do with the phenomenon.

  19. Which statement about forgetting is incorrect? (RML 2025)

    (a) Forgetting helps clear out unnecessary information (b) Forgetting improves memory by prioritizing key concepts (c) Less retention leads to less forgetting (d) High retention correlates with less forgetting

    Answer: (c) Less retention leads to less forgetting. Retention and forgetting are two sides of one coin: whatever is not retained has been forgotten, so less retention means more forgetting, as (d) correctly says. Options (a) and (b) describe the adaptive side of forgetting: letting unused detail fade reduces interference and helps us abstract what matters.

  20. Being unable to recall the relevant elements of an autobiographical experience from memory is known as a deficit in: (RML 2022)

    (a) Reality-monitoring ability (b) Personal-monitoring ability (c) Social-monitoring ability (d) Source-monitoring ability

    Answer: (d) Source-monitoring ability. Source monitoring (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, 1993) is the attribution of a memory to its origin from its contextual details. Losing those details produces source errors and source amnesia. Reality monitoring is a narrower case, distinguishing memories of real events from imagined ones. "Personal-monitoring" and "social-monitoring" are invented distractors.

  21. Defense attorneys often protest the prosecutor's use of eyewitness testimony because: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Eyewitnesses are more prone to forgetting (b) A confident witness is usually right (c) Source errors may cause an eyewitness to make a false identification (d) Poor eyesight of eyewitnesses tends to invalidate their testimony

    Answer: (c) Source errors may cause an eyewitness to make a false identification. A face seen at the scene, in a photograph or in a line-up can feel familiar for the wrong reason, and the witness then confidently picks the wrong person. The problem is not that eyewitnesses forget more than anyone else, and it is rarely poor eyesight. Option (b) is the opposite of the research: confidence, especially when inflated by feedback, is a poor guide to accuracy.

  22. According to Karl Lashley's research on memory storage, where did he conclude that memories are primarily stored in the brain? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) In specific localized brain regions dedicated to particular memories (b) Distributed throughout the entire brain cortex (c) Exclusively in the hippocampus (d) Only in the frontal and temporal lobes

    Answer: (b) Distributed throughout the entire brain cortex. Lashley's lesion studies in rats found that maze memory depended on how much cortex was removed (mass action), not on which part, and that cortical areas could substitute for one another (equipotentiality). He therefore rejected localisation (a) and (d). The hippocampus (c) was linked to memory later by the case of H.M., and even then as the structure for consolidation, not the exclusive store.

  23. The famous patient H.M. (Henry Molaison) underwent surgical removal of the medial temporal lobes, including the hippocampus, to treat severe epilepsy. While he retained most of his intellectual abilities and working memory, his primary and most profound deficit was in: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Procedural memory (b) Semantic memory (c) Anterograde amnesia (d) Retrograde amnesia

    Answer: (c) Anterograde amnesia. H.M. could not form new declarative memories after his 1953 operation. His procedural learning was spared: he improved at mirror tracing over three days without remembering the task. His old semantic knowledge was intact, so semantic memory was not selectively lost. His retrograde amnesia covered only the years before the surgery and was far milder than his anterograde loss.

  24. A patient presents with severe memory deficits, confabulation, and nystagmus, with a history of chronic alcohol use. This clinical picture is classic for: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Alzheimer's Disease (b) Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome (c) Vascular Dementia (d) Frontotemporal Dementia

    Answer: (b) Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome. Thiamine deficiency in chronic alcoholism produces Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia and eye signs such as nystagmus), followed by Korsakoff's amnesia with confabulation. Alzheimer's disease is a gradual dementia of older age, beginning with recent episodic memory, without nystagmus or an alcohol link. Vascular dementia typically declines step by step after strokes, and frontotemporal dementia begins with changes in personality, behaviour or language (Psychiatric Disorders book).

  25. In PQRST method, a method of enhancing memory, what does the alphabet 'S' stand for? (RML 2024)

    (a) Storing (b) Self-recitation (c) Sequencing (d) Semantics

    Answer: (b) Self-recitation. PQRST stands for Preview, Question, Read, Self-recitation and Test, a close variant of Robinson's SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review). Self-recitation is retrieval practice, the step that does most for retention. Storing, sequencing and semantics sound like memory terms but are not steps of the method.
