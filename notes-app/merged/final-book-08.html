Heidelberg, 1891. Emil Kraepelin, aged thirty-five, takes up the chair of psychiatry at the university clinic. Years earlier he had worked in Wilhelm Wundt's laboratory in Leipzig, timing how alcohol and other drugs changed simple mental acts, and he brings a laboratory habit with him: record everything, then count. The psychiatry he inherits badly needs it. Diagnostic labels vary from one asylum to the next, and almost all of them describe how a patient looks on the day of admission. None says what will happen to the patient next.

Kraepelin's tool was humble: a card. For every patient admitted, a card recorded the history, the symptoms and a provisional diagnosis. The cards were not filed and forgotten. As months and years passed, the clinic added what became of each patient: recovery, relapse, or a slow slide into apathy and disordered thinking. Sorting the cards again, by outcome rather than by the picture at admission, Kraepelin could see which first impressions had been wrong, and the psychoses fell into two great families. In one, illness began in youth and ran a deteriorating course; he called it dementia praecox, premature dementia. In the other, attacks of mania or melancholia came and went, with recovery between them: manic-depressive insanity. The sixth edition of his textbook (1899) drew the line.

The idea on those cards is the idea of this chapter. A mental disorder, for Kraepelin, was a recognisable cluster of signs and symptoms with its own onset, course and outcome, and a classification was good if it predicted what would happen to the patient. When the American Psychiatric Association rebuilt its manual around explicit criteria in 1980, the psychiatrists behind the change were nicknamed the neo-Kraepelinians. Every disorder in the pages that follow is, in a sense, one of his piles of cards, redrawn by a century of research.

8.1 Abnormality, Causes and the Classification of Mental DisordersMedium yield · 30 Qs

Every disorder in this chapter rests on three prior questions. When does unusual behaviour become a disorder? Why does one person fall ill while another does not? And how do clinicians everywhere agree on what to call what they see? This section answers each in turn, then shows how culture shapes distress itself. (The history of the asylums, and of the models as guides to treatment, is told in Section 7.1.)

What counts as abnormal

No single test separates normal from abnormal, so clinicians weigh the four Ds together. Deviance takes two forms. The statistical criterion calls abnormal whatever deviates from typical patterns; its flaw is that rare talent, a genius IQ, is just as deviant. The social-norm criterion calls abnormal whatever violates a society's explicit and implicit norms; its flaw is cultural relativity, for a woman in possession trance at a temple festival is following custom, not breaking it. Distress is personal unhappiness and suffering, though a person in mania may feel wonderful. Dysfunction is maladaptiveness: behaviour that stops you working, studying or keeping relationships. Danger to self or others is the least common.

Marie Jahoda (1958) described ideal mental health positively, as self-acceptance, growth, integration, autonomy, accurate perception of reality and environmental mastery. Jerome Wakefield (1992) defined disorder as harmful dysfunction: a mental mechanism fails at the job evolution designed it for (a fact), and the failure harms the person by the standards of their culture (a value). In DSM-5-TR, a mental disorder is a syndrome of clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotion regulation or behaviour that reflects a dysfunction in the underlying psychological, biological or developmental processes, usually with significant distress or disability. An expectable or culturally approved response to a common stressor or loss, such as the death of a loved one, is excluded, and so is socially deviant behaviour (political, religious or sexual) unless it results from a dysfunction in the person.

Daniel Offer and Melvin Sabshin (1966) described four views of normality. Normality as health, the medical view, is the absence of manifest psychopathology; judged from the person's own side it becomes the subjective model: no distress, no disability, no help-seeking. Normality as utopia is ideal, optimal functioning; normality as average is the statistical middle of the bell curve; normality as process is the product of systems that change across the lifespan.

Exam trap

Statistical abnormality is deviation from typical patterns, not intense anxiety, loss of contact with reality or withdrawal. Optimal functioning is utopia; no distress, disability or help-seeking is the subjective model; absence of any disorder is the medical view; socially acceptable behaviour is the norm criterion.

The language of psychopathology

Psychopathology is the scientific study of mental disorders; abnormal psychology is the branch of psychology that studies abnormal behaviour and experience, and the terms largely overlap. A symptom is what the patient reports (low mood), a sign what the clinician observes (tearfulness), and a syndrome a cluster of signs and symptoms that occur together (8.2). Aetiology is the study of causes. Onset is acute (sudden) or insidious (gradual). Course may be acute (short), chronic (long-lasting), episodic (recurring with recovery between, as in mood disorders) or time-limited (improving untreated within a short period). Prognosis is the likely course and outcome: a forecast, not the history, the presenting symptoms or the diagnostic criteria.

Prevalence is the proportion of a population that has a disorder, on one day (point), over a stated period such as a year (period) or ever (lifetime); incidence counts only new cases arising in a period. Comorbidity is the co-occurrence of two or more differently defined disorders in one person, such as depression with an anxiety disorder. In psychiatry it is the rule, not the exception.

Exam trap

Comorbidity is not the frequency of mental illness (that is prevalence), not two or more causes of death, not the degree of suffering, and not dying together or fearing death. "Depression always in winter" garbles the seasonal pattern specifier (8.5).

Biological research rests on family, twin and adoption studies. Twin studies compare concordance, the rate at which both twins are affected, in identical and fraternal pairs; adoption studies separate genes from upbringing. Together they estimate heritability, the share of variation in a population due to genetic differences. Disorders are polygenic: many small-effect genes add up to a polygenic risk score. In gene-environment interaction a gene matters only in some environments: Avshalom Caspi (2003) found that a short serotonin transporter variant raised adult depression mainly after childhood maltreatment. In gene-environment correlation, the reciprocal gene-environment model, genes make certain environments likelier, as when an impulsive temperament invites the very stressors that trigger the vulnerability. Neurotransmitter (dopamine) and brain-structure (enlarged ventricles) models complete the strand.

Because there is no single "schizophrenia gene", researchers seek endophenotypes: the genetic mechanisms that ultimately produce the problems behind the symptoms, measurable intermediate traits such as the eye-tracking and working-memory deficits of schizophrenia. Irving Gottesman and James Shields brought the term into psychiatry in the 1970s; Gottesman and Todd Gould revived it in 2003. Contrast the genotype, a person's genetic make-up, the phenotype, the observable disorder, and molecular genetics, a method that studies DNA directly.

The diathesis-stress model joins the strands: a predisposition, the diathesis (genetic, biological or psychological), lies dormant until a stressor sets it off, and neither alone suffices. Paul Meehl (1962) proposed an inherited schizotaxia that under stress could become schizophrenia; Joseph Zubin and Bonnie Spring (1977) recast it as the vulnerability-stress model: the more vulnerable you are, the less stress tips you into illness (Section 7.1 draws the threshold). Broadest is the biopsychosocial model of George L. Engel (1977): illness arises from interacting biological, psychological and social factors.

Exam trap

Any "predisposition plus trigger" or "vulnerability plus stress" wording is diathesis-stress; genes, stress, learning, unconscious conflict or the supernatural alone are wrong. The neurodevelopmental model traces schizophrenia to faulty early brain development (8.4); "clinical-reductionist" and "syndrome-sign" are not models. Biopsychosocial is Engel's, not Kraepelin's, Bleuler's (8.4) or Beck's.

Exam trap

Asked which disorders show strong biological and "little to no" psychosocial evidence, choose schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, the most heritable (bipolar heritability reaches about 90 percent in some twin studies), not bulimia nervosa, in which cultural pressure for thinness weighs heavily (8.8). But the premise is false: identical-twin concordance for bipolar disorder is only 40 to 70 percent and life events trigger relapses, while childhood adversity, migration and urban upbringing raise the risk of schizophrenia. Genes set the diathesis; stress still times the episodes.

Approaches to classification

Hippocrates (about 460 to 377 BC) sorted mental illness into mania, melancholia and phrenitis (feverish delirium), and coined the word hysteria. Modern classification begins with Emil Kraepelin (1856 to 1926), who classified descriptively by symptoms and above all by course, separating deteriorating dementia praecox from episodic manic-depressive insanity (8.4).

The syndromal approach classifies disorders by clinical signs and symptoms that cluster together; the historical approach (aetiological) classifies them by known cause, as medicine does with tuberculosis. Since most causes are unknown, the DSM and ICD are syndromal. A categorical approach says you have a disorder or you do not; a dimensional approach rates everyone on continuous scales; a prototypical approach, the DSM's, lists the features of a typical case and tolerates variation (five of nine symptoms make a major depressive episode, so two patients may share only one).

Reliability is agreement between clinicians who assess the same patient, measured by Cohen's kappa (agreement corrected for chance); validity is whether a category is real and useful, predicting course and treatment response. A diagnosis can be reliable but invalid, never the reverse. A false positive diagnoses a normal person as disordered; a false negative misses a real disorder. Loose criteria breed false positives, strict ones false negatives. Every diagnosis is also a label that can bring stigma (Section 7.2 tells the pseudopatient study).

Exam trap

Syndromal means signs and symptoms; historical means known aetiology; option sets swap them. A high false-positive rate means normal people are diagnosed as disordered (criteria too loose, not too strict); missed cases are false negatives; low reliability is rater disagreement.

The DSM

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) is published by the American Psychiatric Association (APA). Figure 8.1 sets its editions beside the ICD's. DSM-I (1952) and DSM-II (1968) were short glossaries of descriptions. DSM-III (1980), directed by Robert Spitzer (1932 to 2015), brought explicit diagnostic criteria, a descriptive stance neutral about causes, and the multiaxial system; DSM-III-R (1987), DSM-IV (1994) and DSM-IV-TR (2000) followed. DSM-5 (2013) took an Arabic numeral to allow numbered updates. The edition in use, DSM-5-TR (2022), added prolonged grief disorder and codes for suicidal behaviour and non-suicidal self-injury.

From DSM-III to DSM-IV-TR, diagnosis ran on five axes: Axis I, clinical disorders; Axis II, personality disorders and mental retardation; Axis III, general medical conditions; Axis IV, psychosocial and environmental problems; Axis V, the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF). DSM-5 abolished the multiaxial system: Axes I to III are listed together, psychosocial problems take Z codes, and the GAF gave way to the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0).

DSM-5-TR has three sections. Section I explains how to use it. Section II holds the criteria and codes: 20 disorder chapters in rough lifespan order, from neurodevelopmental through paraphilic to other mental disorders, then medication-induced movement disorders and other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention, problems that are not mental disorders, such as relational problems, malingering and borderline intellectual functioning. Subtypes are mutually exclusive ("specify whether"), specifiers can be combined ("specify if"), and severity is rated mild, moderate or severe. A presentation short of full criteria is other specified (reason recorded) or unspecified (reason not given). Section III, "Emerging Measures and Models", holds cross-cutting symptom measures, the WHODAS 2.0, the Cultural Formulation Interview, the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (8.11) and the conditions for further study, proposed criteria sets not approved for clinical use.

Remember: the seven conditions for further study

Attenuated psychosis syndrome (milder psychotic symptoms at least weekly for a month); depressive episodes with short-duration hypomania; caffeine use disorder; internet gaming disorder; neurobehavioural disorder associated with prenatal alcohol exposure; suicidal behaviour disorder; and non-suicidal self-injury disorder (intentional cutting, burning, stabbing or hitting of one's own body on 5 or more days in a year, without suicidal intent). DSM-5's persistent complex bereavement disorder became DSM-5-TR's prolonged grief disorder (8.7).

Exam trap

An option "DSM-V" means DSM-5: choose it for the version in use, knowing the current text is DSM-5-TR (2022). DSM-5 abolished the axes; it added no sixth axis and still gives diagnoses, with ICD-10-CM codes beside them. Axis III is general medical conditions, not personality disorders (II) or psychosocial problems (IV).

8.2 Descriptive Psychopathology: Signs and SymptomsHigh yield · 45 Qs

A psychiatric diagnosis is built from small units: a voice nobody else hears, a belief no argument can shift, a smile at a funeral. This section names and defines those units, one mental function at a time. Section 7.2 shows how the mental status examination draws them out in an interview; the disorders they build begin in Section 8.3.

Jaspers and the descriptive method

Descriptive psychopathology is the precise description and classification of abnormal experiences and behaviour, without claims about their cause. Its founding text is General Psychopathology (1913) by the German psychiatrist and philosopher Karl Jaspers (1883 to 1969), who brought phenomenology into psychiatry: the clinician describes an experience as the patient lives it, setting theory aside. The method is empathic understanding: careful questioning until you grasp what the experience is like. Where empathy fails, where an experience cannot be understood from the person's life and mood, Jaspers suspected a disease process.

Three distinctions run through everything that follows. The form of a symptom is its type (a hallucination); its content is what it is about (a voice calling him a thief). Culture colours the content, but the form carries the diagnosis. A primary symptom arises directly from the illness; a secondary symptom is an understandable reaction to one, as when a man who hears accusing voices concludes he is being watched. A symptom is a subjective complaint the patient reports (low mood); a sign is an objective abnormality the clinician observes (flat affect, slowed movement).

Consciousness, attention and orientation

Clouding of consciousness is the mildest lowering of awareness: drowsiness, poor attention and an incomplete grasp of the surroundings. Confusion adds muddled thinking and disorientation. Delirium, in DSM-5-TR terms, is a disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days, fluctuates during the day (lucid in the morning, worse at night) and brings other cognitive change, often with illusions and visual hallucinations; it signals a medical cause (Section 8.10). In stupor the person is mute and motionless and responds only to strong stimuli (catatonia, severe depression, organic states); in coma even pain brings no response. A twilight state is a narrowed consciousness lasting hours or days, in which complex, seemingly purposeful acts are carried out and later forgotten (after epileptic seizures; alcohol and dissociative states).

Distractibility is attention pulled away by trivial outside stimuli (mania, delirium). Disorientation is loss of awareness of time, place or person: time is lost first, then place, and one's own identity last.

Disorders of perception

In a sensory distortion a real object is perceived but altered in intensity (sounds painfully loud), quality (everything tinged yellow) or size: micropsia (objects seen smaller) and macropsia (larger). In a sensory deception a new perception appears (Figure 8.2).

An illusion is a misperception of a real, existing stimulus. Completion illusions fill in missing parts (a misprint read as correct), affect illusions arise from emotion (a frightened person sees a coat on a hook as an intruder) and pareidolic illusions make vivid images from vague material (faces in clouds). A hallucination is a perception without an external stimulus, located in outer space and as vivid as a real perception. A pseudohallucination is located in inner space ("a voice inside my head") or is recognised as unreal. Hallucinations are most frequent in schizophrenia, and there mostly auditory; voices discussing or arguing about the patient in the third person, a running commentary on his actions and thought echo (hearing one's thoughts aloud) are first-rank symptoms (Section 8.4). A hallucination is a disorder of perception, not of thinking, judgement or intelligence. An illusion distorts a stimulus that exists; a hallucination has none. Hypnagogic hallucinations occur most commonly in normal people (DSM-5-TR counts them as normal experience), not in schizophrenia, delirium or drug dependence; narcolepsy also features them.

Key study: Charles Bonnet's grandfather (1760)

In 1760 the Swiss naturalist Charles Bonnet (1720 to 1793) described his elderly grandfather, almost blind from cataracts, who saw people, birds and buildings that were not there, while knowing they were unreal. Charles Bonnet syndrome now names complex visual hallucinations with significant visual loss, intact cognition and preserved insight. It is not a psychosis.

In depersonalisation the self feels unreal or changed ("as if I am watching myself in a film"); in derealisation the surroundings feel unreal or dreamlike. The person knows it only feels so. The disorder is in Section 8.7.

Disorders of thought

Thought is described by its stream (speed and flow), form (how ideas connect), content (what is thought) and possession (whose the thoughts feel to be). In the stream, pressure of thought is a rapid crowding of ideas (mania); poverty of thought is a scarcity of slow, empty ones (depression, schizophrenia); thought block is a sudden stop in mid-sentence, the mind blank (schizophrenia). Diminished speech output (poverty of speech) is alogia.

Formal thought disorder is a disturbance in the links between ideas, inferred from speech (Table 8.6). In flight of ideas (mania) thought shifts rapidly from one idea to another before any is completed, but the links can be traced: chance, rhymes, puns and clang associations (words chosen for sound). In loosening of associations, or derailment (schizophrenia), the links are lost; the oblique jumps are called knight's move thinking.

Overinclusive thinking was described by Norman Cameron (1896 to 1975). Thought content includes preoccupations, obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts recognised as one's own and resisted; Section 8.6), phobias (excessive fear of a specific object or situation), delusions and overvalued ideas, a term of Carl Wernicke (1848 to 1905): a single, understandable belief that dominates the person's life and is held with strong feeling but without delusional fixity, such as the anorexic patient's conviction that she is too fat.

In thought alienation, thoughts no longer feel one's own: in thought insertion they are put in from outside, in thought withdrawal taken out, and in thought broadcasting they pass out of the mind so that others know them. All three are first-rank symptoms of Kurt Schneider (1887 to 1967); the rest of his list is thought echo, voices arguing, running commentary, delusional perception, somatic passivity and made feelings, made impulses and made actions (Section 8.4). Exam trap

Flight of ideas, tangentiality, derailment, loosening and circumstantiality are disorders of form (process), not content (delusions, obsessions) or possession (insertion, broadcasting). Many Indian notes file flight of ideas, circumstantiality and tangentiality under stream; if form is offered, choose it. Word salad is the most severe formal thought disorder; repeating one idea is perseveration.

Delusions

A delusion is a false, firmly held belief, not shaken by reason or contrary evidence, and out of keeping with the person's cultural and educational background (a community's shared belief in the evil eye is not one). Jaspers named its marks: certainty, incorrigibility and impossibility of content. Delusions, hallucinations and grossly disorganised speech or behaviour mark psychosis, a loss of contact with reality. Table 8.7 separates the delusion from its neighbours.

A primary delusion (autochthonous) cannot be understood from any other experience. It appears as delusional mood (something sinister is going on), delusional perception (a normal perception suddenly carries a private meaning: the traffic light turns red, so he is chosen to save the world), delusional memory or a sudden delusional idea. A secondary delusion is understandable from another experience: guilt from depressed mood, persecution to explain hostile voices. Systematised delusions form a logically connected system around one theme. Mood-congruent delusions fit the mood (grandeur in mania); mood-incongruent ones do not. DSM-5-TR calls a delusion bizarre when it is clearly implausible, not understandable to same-culture peers and not drawn from ordinary life (his organs removed without a scar); a non-bizarre delusion could happen (being followed by the police).

A delusion of persecution, that one is being deliberately interfered with, plotted against, threatened or mistreated, is the most common type, mostly in schizophrenia and delusional disorder; paranoid means persecutory or self-referential, and paranoia was the old name for chronic, systematised delusional states. In a delusion of reference neutral events carry a personal message; an idea of reference is held without full conviction. In delusions of control (passivity) one's acts or feelings are made by an outside force. Grandiose delusions belong to mania; nihilistic delusions (oneself, one's organs or the world do not exist or are dead; DSM-5-TR words it as the conviction that a major catastrophe will occur) and delusions of guilt and poverty to severe depression. Somatic (hypochondriacal) delusions concern disease; jealousy, erotomania, infestation and misidentification have eponyms (Table 8.8).

Disorders of memory

Amnesia is a pathological loss of memory: anterograde (new memories cannot be stored after the onset) or retrograde (memories from before it are lost). Organic amnesia is in Section 8.10, psychogenic (dissociative) amnesia in Section 8.7, and normal memory and false memories in Section 1.7.

Among the paramnesias, confabulation fills memory gaps with false, often detailed and believable memories, without intent to deceive; it is the hallmark of Korsakoff syndrome (alcohol-related thiamine deficiency). Pseudologia fantastica, described by Anton Delbrück in 1891, is fantastic lying that the teller half believes (factitious disorder). Retrospective falsification unconsciously rewrites the past to fit present mood; in cryptomnesia a forgotten memory returns unrecognised, so it feels original; hypermnesia is exaggerated recall (mania, hypnosis).

Déjà vu ("already seen") is the illusion of recognition: a new situation feels already experienced. Déjà entendu is the illusion of having heard something before; déjà vécu, of having lived the whole moment before. Jamais vu is the reverse: the familiar feels unfamiliar. All occur in tired, healthy people and in temporal lobe epilepsy.

Exam trap

Confabulation is not lying: there is no intent to deceive. Malingering is deliberate faking for external gain (Section 8.7); confusion and delirium are disturbances of consciousness, not invented memories.

Disorders of emotion

Mood is a sustained emotional state that the person reports; affect is the immediate, observed expression of emotion. Mood is the climate; affect is the weather. (Emotion is the broader, brief reaction, Chapter 1; feeling is its experienced side.) Affect is described by quality, range, reactivity, intensity, appropriateness and stability; persistence describes mood. Affect may be restricted (reduced range), blunted (severely reduced intensity), flat (almost no expression), labile (shifting rapidly without adequate cause) or inappropriate (incongruous with thought content: giggling about a death).

Anhedonia is the inability to experience pleasure or interest: a core symptom of depression and a negative symptom of schizophrenia. Alexithymia, a term of Peter Sifneos (1920 to 2008) in 1973, is difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings. Apathy is lack of feeling and interest. Ambivalence, a term of Eugen Bleuler (1857 to 1939), is the coexistence of opposite feelings towards the same object. Dysphoria is an unpleasant mood; euthymia is normal mood. Elevated mood is graded by severity (Figure 8.3).

Emotional incontinence is extreme lability that escapes voluntary control (vascular dementia). Pseudobulbar affect is sudden, brief, involuntary laughing or crying, out of proportion to or at odds with the underlying mood, in stroke, multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, brain injury and dementia. La belle indifférence is in Section 8.7. A symptom is ego-syntonic when experienced as consistent with one's self-image, and ego-dystonic when intrusive, unwanted and inconsistent with it (obsessions).

Exam trap

"Our current mood and feelings", the emotion seen at this moment, is affect, not mood. Quality, range and reactivity describe affect; persistence describes mood. Severity: euphoria, elation, exaltation, ecstasy. Pseudobulbar affect is neurological, not the restricted affect of schizophrenia or histrionic emotional shifts.

Motor behaviour, insight and judgement

Psychomotor retardation slows movement, speech and thought (depression); psychomotor agitation is purposeless overactivity with inner tension (agitated depression, mania). Catatonia has twelve DSM-5-TR signs: stupor; catalepsy (a passively induced posture held against gravity); waxy flexibility (slight, even resistance to positioning); mutism; negativism (opposing or ignoring instructions); posturing; mannerisms (odd caricatures of normal actions); stereotypies (repetitive, non-goal-directed movements); agitation; grimacing; echolalia (repeating others' words); and echopraxia (imitating others' movements). Older signs are automatic obedience, Mitgehen (moving with the lightest touch despite being told to resist) and ambitendency (offering, then withdrawing, a hand).

Compulsions are repetitive acts performed in response to an obsession, to reduce distress (Section 8.6); tics are in Section 8.3. Avolition is reduced self-initiated, purposeful activity (a negative symptom: the loss of a normal function; Section 8.4); abulia is lack of will, with difficulty initiating action or deciding, typically after frontal lobe damage.

Insight is awareness and understanding of one's own illness and need for treatment, graded on six levels: (1) complete denial; (2) slight awareness while still denying; (3) awareness, but blamed on others, external or physical causes; (4) awareness of something unknown in oneself; (5) intellectual insight, accepting the illness without changing; (6) true emotional insight, awareness that changes behaviour. Judgement, the ability to assess a situation and act appropriately, is personal (one's own plans), social (conduct with others) or test (a hypothetical problem, such as finding a stamped letter in the street). Section 7.2 shows how both are assessed.

Exam trap

Insight means awareness of one's own illness, not orientation, serial subtraction (attention) or hallucinations (perception). Echolalia and echopraxia point to catatonia, not depression or panic.

8.3 Neurodevelopmental DisordersMedium yield · 29 Qs

The disorders in this section are differences in how the mind is built, not illnesses that strike a mind already formed. A child who cannot read a word he can say, or cannot sit still in class, has not lost a skill: his brain developed differently from the start.

The neurodevelopmental group

DSM-5-TR (2022) defines neurodevelopmental disorders as conditions with onset in the developmental period, often before school entry, in which differences in brain development impair personal, social, academic or occupational functioning. They frequently co-occur: many autistic children have intellectual disability, and many children with ADHD have a specific learning disorder. ADHD belongs here; obsessive-compulsive and generalised anxiety disorder (Section 8.6) and borderline personality disorder (Section 8.11) do not.

DSM-IV-TR (2000) grouped them as "disorders usually first diagnosed in infancy, childhood or adolescence", which included the pervasive developmental disorders: autistic, Rett's, childhood disintegrative and Asperger's disorders and PDD not otherwise specified (PDD-NOS). ICD-10 (1992) spreads them over F70 to F98: mental retardation (F70 to F79), disorders of psychological development (F80 to F89) and childhood behavioural and emotional disorders such as hyperkinetic and tic disorders (F90 to F98).

Children behave differently at home, at school and in the clinic, so diagnosis uses multi-informant assessment: parents, teachers, clinician and child, across settings. ADHD even requires symptoms in two or more settings. The Vineland, CARS, ISAA and ADOS are described in Chapter 2.

Exam trap

Several informants are needed because symptoms vary across contexts, not because parents over-report or teachers add nothing. "Observation alone is not enough" is true but is not the reason.

Intellectual disability: criteria and severity

Intellectual disability (DSM-5-TR: intellectual developmental disorder; DSM-IV-TR: mental retardation) has three criteria. (A) Deficits in intellectual functions such as reasoning, problem solving, abstract thinking and learning, confirmed by clinical assessment and individual standardised intelligence testing. (B) Deficits in adaptive functioning, meeting age and cultural standards of independence and social responsibility, in the conceptual (language, reading, money), social (communication, friendship, judgement) or practical (self-care, work) domain, severe enough to need ongoing support. (C) Onset in the developmental period.

Criterion A means an IQ about two standard deviations (SD) below the mean: 70 on tests with mean 100 and SD 15, a score only about 2.3 percent of people fall below (Figure 8.4). Allowing about 5 points of measurement error, the zone is 65 to 75. Prevalence is about 1 percent, higher in males.

ICD-10 and older DSMs graded severity by IQ (Table 8.9; DSM-IV-TR used overlapping limits such as 50-55 to about 70 for mild), and about 85 percent of cases are mild. DSM-5 grades severity by adaptive functioning, which decides the support needed, because IQ is least valid at the low end. Severe levels show as delayed milestones in the first two years, mild ones often only at school; most people at every level now live in the community, not institutions.

Causes are grouped by timing, beginning with genes (Table 8.10). Communication disorders

Language disorder is persistent difficulty acquiring and using language: small vocabulary, simple sentences, poor discourse. Speech sound disorder is difficulty producing speech sounds, making speech hard to understand, without a physical cause such as cleft palate or deafness. Childhood-onset fluency disorder (stuttering) breaks the flow of speech with repetitions, prolongations, broken words and blocks; 80 to 90 percent of cases begin by age 6. Social (pragmatic) communication disorder is difficulty using language socially (adjusting to the listener, taking turns, grasping hints and idioms) without the repetitive behaviour of autism.

Ask why a child does not communicate. In mutism, as in an inherently mute person, there is no capacity for speech: language itself is the barrier. In selective mutism, an anxiety disorder (Section 8.6), the child speaks at home but not in feared settings, so anxiety is the barrier. In autism the barrier is the social use of language; in specific learning disorder, reading and writing.

Autism spectrum disorder

Eugen Bleuler (1857 to 1939) coined autism (Greek autos, self) in 1911 for the withdrawal seen in schizophrenia. In 1943 Leo Kanner (1894 to 1981) published "Autistic disturbances of affective contact", on eleven children with extreme aloneness and insistence on sameness, and soon called the condition early infantile autism. In 1944 Hans Asperger (1906 to 1980) described boys with fluent speech but odd social behaviour and narrow interests. Bruno Bettelheim's (1903 to 1990) "refrigerator mother" theory (1967) blamed cold parenting; it was discredited when autism proved largely genetic. Lorna Wing (1928 to 2014) and Judith Gould (1979) described a triad of impairments: social interaction, communication and imagination.

DSM-5 (2013) merged autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, PDD-NOS and childhood disintegrative disorder (two or more years of normal development, then loss of skills; Theodor Heller, 1908) into autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Rett syndrome (Andreas Rett, 1966) was left out as a genetic condition (MECP2): girls regress after early normal development, losing speech and hand use, with hand-wringing.

Criterion A: persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts, in all three of social-emotional reciprocity, nonverbal communication (eye contact, gesture) and relationships. Criterion B: restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, at least two of stereotyped movements or speech (hand-flapping, lining up toys, echolalia or echoing others' words), insistence on sameness, intense restricted interests, and sensory hyper- or hyporeactivity. Symptoms start early (though they may show only as social demands rise) and impair functioning. Severity is rated per domain: level 1 "requiring support", level 2 "requiring substantial support", level 3 "requiring very substantial support". Specifiers note accompanying intellectual impairment or language impairment, a known genetic or medical condition, and catatonia; many former Asperger's cases are now ASD without either impairment.

Exam trap

ASD needs social-communication deficits plus repetitive behaviour; delayed speech alone, IQ below 70 and hyperactivity are not required. No eye contact, delayed language and repetitive movements spell ASD, not ADHD, conduct disorder or SLD. Kanner first described infantile autism; Bleuler only coined the word, and Kurt Schneider (Section 8.4) and Cameron, Goldstein and Jaspers (Section 8.2) are distractors. The research deficit is in encoding social cues; trouble deceiving follows from mindblindness, and anger control and memory retrieval are not core deficits.

ASD affects about 1 to 2 percent of people, three to four males per female (DSM-5-TR says 3:1; girls are under-recognised). Outside the communication disorders themselves, language development is compromised more often in ASD than in any other neurodevelopmental disorder. The commonest co-occurring conditions are intellectual disability and ADHD (diagnosable alongside ASD only since DSM-5), then anxiety, depression and epilepsy.

Heritability is about 80 percent (estimates 37 to over 90); some cases carry de novo (new, uninherited) mutations, and older parents, extreme prematurity and prenatal valproate add risk. Autistic people show deficits in encoding social cues such as faces, gaze and tone of voice, and find deliberate deception hard, while rote memory is often a strength. Simon Baron-Cohen (born 1958), Alan Leslie and Uta Frith (born 1941) proposed a deficit in theory of mind (Chapter 6), or "mindblindness". Frith's weak central coherence (1989) is a bias to detail over the whole; executive dysfunction explains rigidity; Baron-Cohen's extreme male brain theory casts autism as strong systemising with weak empathising.

Key study: the Sally-Anne test (1985)

Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith tested 20 autistic, 14 Down syndrome and 27 typical children. Sally puts a marble in her basket and leaves; Anne moves it to her box. Where will Sally look? About 85 percent of typical and Down syndrome children said the basket; 80 percent of autistic children, despite higher verbal mental age than the Down group, said the box, failing to represent Sally's false belief. Criticism: a fifth passed, the deficit is not unique to autism, and it cannot explain repetitive behaviour.

In 1998 Andrew Wakefield linked the MMR vaccine to autism in a 12-child Lancet paper; it was retracted in 2010, he was struck off the medical register, and large studies find no link. Treatment is behavioural and educational: Ivar Lovaas's applied behaviour analysis (1987), TEACCH and PECS (Sections 7.4 and 7.10).

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder

In 1902 George Frederic Still (1868 to 1941) described restless, inattentive children with a "defect of moral control". Later labels were minimal brain dysfunction, hyperkinetic reaction of childhood (DSM-II, 1968), attention deficit disorder (DSM-III, 1980) and ADHD (DSM-III-R, 1987). ICD-10's hyperkinetic disorder (F90) is overactivity, distractibility, short attention span and impulsivity; social withdrawal is not a feature.

DSM-5-TR attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) needs six of nine inattention and/or six of nine hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms (Table 8.11; five from age 17) for at least 6 months; several present before age 12 (DSM-IV: 7) and in two or more settings; and impairment. The presentation is combined, predominantly inattentive or predominantly hyperactive-impulsive.

Specific learning disorder

DSM-5 merged reading, mathematics and written expression disorders into one specific learning disorder (SLD): difficulty in reading, spelling, writing or mathematics persisting at least 6 months despite targeted intervention, achievement well below age expectation on standardised tests, onset in the school years, and no better explanation in intellectual disability, sensory loss or poor teaching. Intelligence is usually average (IQ above about 70 plus or minus 5), hence "unexpected underachievement". It affects 5 to 15 percent of schoolchildren.

The specifiers are impairment in reading (word-reading accuracy, fluency and comprehension), whose core pattern of poor word recognition, decoding and spelling is called dyslexia; in written expression, often called dysgraphia; and in mathematics, dyscalculia.

The Glasgow eye surgeon James Hinshelwood (1859 to 1919) described "congenital word-blindness" (a book of that title in 1917), and Samuel Torrey Orton (1879 to 1948) in the 1920s blamed incomplete cerebral dominance. The modern phonological deficit theory locates dyslexia in linking speech sounds (phonemes) to letters. The old IQ-achievement discrepancy model waited for a child to fail; response to intervention (RTI) identifies children who do not respond to good teaching. Auditory processing disorder, difficulty interpreting heard sounds despite normal hearing, is grouped with the learning difficulties though DSM-5-TR omits it. India's RPwD Act 2016 covers specific learning disabilities (Section 7.1).

Exam trap

SLD means roughly average intelligence with persistent academic difficulty; IQ below 50 points to intellectual disability, "only attention deficit" to ADHD and "motor coordination deficit only" to dyspraxia. Dyslexia is difficulty reading and decoding words, not understanding speech (language disorder) or concentrating in lectures (ADHD).

Motor disorders and tics

Developmental coordination disorder (dyspraxia) is impaired acquisition and execution of coordinated motor skills: clumsiness and slowness in catching, using scissors, handwriting or cycling, without a neurological cause. It differs from syndromes acquired through brain damage (Chapter 5): apraxia, loss of learned purposeful movement despite intact strength; agnosia, failure to recognise despite intact sensation; anomia, inability to name. Stereotypic movement disorder is repetitive, driven, purposeless movement (rocking, head-banging, self-biting), with or without self-injury.

A tic is a sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic motor movement or vocalisation (blinking, shrugging, sniffing, throat-clearing). Seizures are abnormal electrical discharges that cannot be held back, dissociation disrupts consciousness or identity (Section 8.7), and Parkinson's tremor is rhythmic. Tics are often preceded by a premonitory urge and can be briefly suppressed; they begin at 4 to 6 years and peak at 10 to 12. All tic disorders need onset before 18 (Table 8.12). Tourette's disorder was described in 1885 by Georges Gilles de la Tourette (1857 to 1904); coprolalia (involuntary obscenities) occurs in a minority and is not required. Tics often accompany OCD and ADHD; habit reversal training treats them (Section 7.4).

8.4 Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic DisordersHigh yield · 79 Qs

Psychosis means losing contact with shared reality: hearing voices no one else hears, or holding a belief no evidence can shake. Schizophrenia is the central psychotic disorder; its neighbours differ from it mainly in duration. We follow the concept's history, symptoms, criteria, causes and drug treatment.

From dementia praecox to schizophrenia

In the 1850s the French physician Benedict Morel (1809 to 1873) used démence précoce, early loss of mind, for young patients who deteriorated. Ewald Hecker described hebephrenia (silly, disorganised behaviour beginning in youth) in 1871, and Karl Ludwig Kahlbaum described catatonia (stupor, posturing and excitement) in 1874. Emil Kraepelin (1856 to 1926) united these pictures, with paranoid forms, under the Latin dementia praecox, 'premature dementia', in 1893. In the sixth edition of his textbook (1899) he separated it from manic-depressive insanity by course: dementia praecox began early and deteriorated, while manic-depressive insanity came in episodes with recovery between them. These two clinical types still divide Sections 8.4 and 8.5. Despite the name, dementia praecox is the ancestor of schizophrenia, not of Alzheimer's disease or frontotemporal dementia (Section 8.10).

The Swiss psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler (1857 to 1939) saw that not every patient deteriorated or fell ill young. In a 1908 lecture and his 1911 monograph, Dementia Praecox or the Group of Schizophrenias, he coined schizophrenia (Greek schizein, to split, and phren, mind) for a splitting between thought, emotion and behaviour, not a split personality. His fundamental symptoms, the four As, were present in every case:

  • Loosening of Associations: thoughts lose their logical links.
  • Blunted or incongruous Affect, such as smiling at sad news.
  • Ambivalence: opposite feelings or wishes held at once towards the same object, a term Bleuler coined.
  • Autism: withdrawal into an inner world (not the developmental disorder of Section 8.3).

Hallucinations and delusions were only accessory symptoms, because they occur in other illnesses too.

Exam trap

Auditory hallucinations are not one of the four As. Ambivalence was coined by Bleuler, not Kraepelin, Freud or Fromm-Reichmann. Alogia, avolition and anhedonia are later negative-symptom As (8.4.2); apathy, anxiety, aphasia, amnesia, attention and anosognosia are not Bleuler's.

Bleuler's symptoms were hard to rate reliably. The German psychiatrist Kurt Schneider (1887 to 1967) listed instead, in Clinical Psychopathology (English edition 1959), first-rank symptoms (FRS) that, in the absence of brain disease, strongly suggest schizophrenia. Their common theme is a failure of the boundary between self and world:

  • Three kinds of voices: audible thoughts (Gedankenlautwerden, thought echo), voices arguing or discussing the patient in the third person, and voices giving a running commentary on the patient's actions.
  • Thought alienation: thought insertion, thought withdrawal and thought broadcasting.
  • Passivity phenomena (delusions of control): made feelings, made impulses, made volitional acts, and somatic passivity (bodily sensations imposed from outside).
  • Delusional perception: a normal perception suddenly given a private delusional meaning (a traffic light turns red, and he 'knows' he has been chosen).

His second-rank symptoms were other hallucinations, perplexity, sudden delusional ideas, mood changes and emotional blunting. FRS are typical of schizophrenia but not specific: they also occur in mania, so DSM-5 gives them no special weight.

Exam trap

Not first-rank: depersonalisation, loose associations (Bleulerian), delusions of self-reference, catatonic signs such as echolalia, negative symptoms, olfactory hallucinations and memory loss. Delusions of control are first-rank. FRS are Schneider's: Karl Jaspers (General Psychopathology, 1913) described primary delusions (Section 8.2), and Bateson and Wynne are family theorists (8.4.7).

Positive, negative and cognitive symptoms

Positive symptoms are the presence of something abnormal, an excess or distortion of normal function: delusions, hallucinations (commonest in schizophrenia, mostly voices, though mania and psychotic depression can bring them too), disorganised speech and grossly disorganised behaviour. Negative symptoms are the absence or reduction of something normal: affective flattening (less emotional expression in face, voice and gesture), alogia (poverty of speech), avolition (little self-started purposeful activity), anhedonia (reduced pleasure) and asociality. ICD-10 lists its negative symptoms as marked apathy, paucity of speech, and blunting or incongruity of emotional responses. A third, cognitive dimension (poor attention, working memory and executive function) best predicts work and social functioning.

Timothy Crow (1980) proposed Type I schizophrenia (positive symptoms, acute onset, good response to neuroleptics, normal brain structure, dopamine D2 overactivity) and Type II (negative symptoms, chronic course, poor drug response, enlarged ventricles, intellectual decline). Peter Liddle (1987) found three syndromes: reality distortion (delusions, hallucinations), disorganisation (thought disorder, inappropriate affect) and psychomotor poverty (poverty of speech, flat affect, slowed movement). Symptoms are rated with Nancy Andreasen's SANS and SAPS (Scales for the Assessment of Negative and of Positive Symptoms) and the 30-item PANSS (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, 1987); see Chapter 2.

Exam trap

Delusions, hallucinations and disorganised speech or behaviour are positive; flat affect is negative, never positive. Ambivalence is Bleulerian, not a negative symptom. Positive means the presence of something unusual, negative the absence of something normal, never the reverse.

Diagnostic criteria: DSM-5-TR, ICD-10 and ICD-11

DSM-5-TR (2022) diagnoses schizophrenia when these are all met:

  • A. Two or more of five symptoms, each present for a significant part of a 1-month period (less if successfully treated): delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, and negative symptoms. At least one must be one of the first three.
  • B. Functioning in work, relationships or self-care falls markedly below the earlier level.
  • C. Continuous signs last at least 6 months, including at least 1 month of active-phase symptoms; the rest may be prodromal or residual phases, showing only negative or attenuated symptoms (odd beliefs, unusual perceptions).
  • D to F. Schizoaffective and mood disorders, substances and medical conditions are excluded; after childhood autism, prominent delusions or hallucinations must last 1 month.

DSM-5 (2013) dropped the DSM-IV subtypes (paranoid, disorganised, catatonic, undifferentiated, residual) and the rule that one bizarre delusion or one Schneiderian voice was enough. ICD-10 (F20) needs one very clear symptom from groups (a) to (d), or symptoms from two of groups (e) to (h), present most of the time for 1 month or more: (a) thought echo, insertion, withdrawal or broadcasting; (b) delusions of control or passivity, and delusional perception; (c) voices commenting on or discussing the patient; (d) persistent, culturally impossible delusions; (e) persistent hallucinations with delusions; (f) breaks in the train of thought, incoherence or neologisms; (g) catatonic behaviour; (h) negative symptoms. Its subtypes were paranoid (the commonest), hebephrenic, catatonic, undifferentiated, post-schizophrenic depression, residual and simple schizophrenia (insidious negative symptoms without overt psychosis). The spectrum: duration, delusions, mood and catatonia

Symptoms identical to those of schizophrenia can be short-lived, so DSM-5-TR separates the disorders largely by duration (Figure 8.5). Brief psychotic disorder lasts at least 1 day but less than 1 month, with full return to premorbid functioning; one symptom suffices if it is delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech. Specifiers: with marked stressors (brief reactive psychosis), without marked stressors, or with peripartum onset (in pregnancy or within 4 weeks after delivery). Schizophreniform disorder has the full Criterion A picture for at least 1 month but less than 6 months and needs no decline in functioning. Six months or more is schizophrenia.

Exam trap

One keyed answer (IHBAS 2025) calls psychosis lasting 1 to 6 months 'brief psychotic disorder'. It is schizophreniform disorder: brief psychotic disorder ends within a month.

Delusional disorder, Kraepelin's paranoia, is one or more delusions lasting 1 month or more (ICD-10 and ICD-11: about 3 months) in someone who has never met Criterion A for schizophrenia; hallucinations are not prominent, and apart from the delusion functioning is not markedly impaired. Types follow the theme: erotomanic (another person, usually of higher status such as a film star, is secretly in love with the patient), grandiose (an unrecognised great talent or discovery), jealous (the partner is unfaithful), persecutory (being plotted against, spied on or poisoned; the commonest), somatic (bodily functions, such as emitting a foul odour), mixed and unspecified.

Schizoaffective disorder needs an uninterrupted period in which a major mood episode coincides with Criterion A of schizophrenia, plus delusions or hallucinations for 2 weeks or more without a major mood episode, with mood episodes present for most of the illness; it has bipolar and depressive types. Psychotic depression and mania belong to Section 8.5. Schizotypal disorder, eccentricity below the psychotic threshold, is listed in this DSM-5-TR chapter but described in Section 8.11; ICD-10 placed it beside schizophrenia as F21, and ICD-11 keeps it there as 6A22. Psychosis caused directly by a drug, medication or toxin is substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder; psychosis caused by a medical illness is psychotic disorder due to another medical condition.

Catatonia requires three or more of twelve signs: stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, echolalia and echopraxia. DSM-5 makes it not an independent disorder but a specifier across several disorders (catatonia associated with schizophrenia, depression and others), plus catatonic disorder due to another medical condition and unspecified catatonia. Attenuated psychosis syndrome (Section III, for further study) means weaker psychotic symptoms with insight retained, at least weekly for a month. ICD-10 acute and transient psychotic disorders (F23) begin within 2 weeks and usually resolve within 2 to 3 months; ICD-11 6A23 keeps the 2-week onset, with rapidly changing symptoms lasting at most 3 months. They are common in India and recover well.

Folie à deux (Charles Lasègue and Jules Falret, 1877) is a delusion transmitted from a dominant primary case with an established psychotic disorder to a more passive, often socially isolated secondary partner in a close relationship, usually a relative. Separation often lets the secondary partner's belief fade. In folie imposée the delusion is imposed on a healthy partner; in folie simultanée two predisposed people fall ill together; folie à trois involves three. ICD-10 coded it F24 induced delusional disorder, DSM-IV shared psychotic disorder. DSM-5 dropped the category: the partner receives delusional disorder if its criteria are met, otherwise other specified schizophrenia spectrum disorder ('delusional symptoms in the context of relationship with an individual with prominent delusions'). ICD-11 has no separate code either: it describes 'shared or induced delusional disorder' (folie à deux) as a rare form of 6A24 delusional disorder, in its block 'schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorders'.

Exam trap

A belief shared by a whole cultural or religious group is not folie à deux; nor are two independent psychotic episodes, or a stress psychosis in two relatives. ICD-10 F24 is induced delusional disorder; F21 is schizotypal disorder. ICD-11 puts it under neither schizoaffective nor schizotypal disorder: it is a form of delusional disorder among the other primary psychotic disorders.

Epidemiology, course and prognosis

The lifetime risk is usually quoted as about 1 percent; DSM-5-TR gives 0.3 to 0.7 percent. Schizophrenia is above all a disorder of young adults: psychosis emerges between the late teens and the mid-30s, peaking in the early to mid-20s in men and the late 20s in women, with a second peak in women in midlife, around the menopause. Prevalence is about equal in the two sexes, but men have more negative symptoms and worse outcomes. A prodrome of withdrawal and odd ideas precedes the active phase. In the WHO International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (1973, nine countries including India) and the later Determinants of Outcome study, patients in developing countries had a better course, often linked to family support. Schizophrenia is commoner in the lowest social classes: social causation says poverty and stress produce it; social drift says the illness pushes people down the social ladder. Urban birth, migration and heavy adolescent cannabis use raise the risk. Table 8.14 lists the classic prognostic factors.

Biological causes: genes, dopamine and development

Irving Gottesman (1991) pooled about 40 family and twin studies (Figure 8.6): risk climbs with shared genes, to about 48 percent, roughly half, for an identical twin. Because it is not 100 percent, environment matters too. The Genain quadruplets, identical sisters who all developed schizophrenia but with different onset, severity and outcome, were studied by David Rosenthal (1963). Adoption studies separate genes from upbringing: Leonard Heston (1966) found schizophrenia in children of mothers with schizophrenia adopted away at birth but in none of the control adoptees, and the Danish adoption studies of Seymour Kety, Rosenthal and Paul Wender (from 1968) found it concentrated in biological, not adoptive, relatives. Risk is polygenic: many common variants of small effect, partly shared with bipolar disorder and autism.

The dopamine hypothesis holds that positive symptoms reflect excess dopamine activity at D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway. Arvid Carlsson showed that dopamine is a neurotransmitter and that antipsychotics act on its system (Nobel Prize, 2000); amphetamine, which releases dopamine, can cause a paranoid psychosis, and L-dopa for Parkinson's disease can cause hallucinations (it does not relieve negative symptoms). The original support came in 1976, when Philip Seeman and colleagues, and Ian Creese, David Burt and Solomon Snyder, showed that the clinical potency of typical antipsychotics correlates with their affinity for D2 receptors. Dopamine runs in four pathways, so a drug that blocks D2 everywhere both helps and harms (Table 8.15).

The neurodevelopmental hypothesis (Daniel Weinberger, 1987; Robin Murray and Shon Lewis, 1987) proposes that an early brain lesion stays silent until adolescent maturation unmasks it. The evidence: enlarged lateral ventricles, first shown on CT by Eve Johnstone and colleagues (1976); reduced grey matter; hypofrontality, low prefrontal activity during tasks; obstetric complications, prenatal influenza and winter-spring birth; and, later, adolescent cannabis use.

Family and psychological theories

Early family theories blamed parents. Frieda Fromm-Reichmann (1948) described the cold, dominating schizophrenogenic mother; the idea is discredited and caused families needless guilt. Gregory Bateson, with Don Jackson, Jay Haley and John Weakland (1956), proposed the double bind: a person repeatedly receives contradictory messages at two levels from a significant other, and can neither comment on the contradiction nor escape.

Key study: Bateson's double bind (1956)

A young man, fairly well recovered from an acute schizophrenic episode, put his arm round his visiting mother; she stiffened. He withdrew, and she asked, 'Don't you love me any more?' He blushed, and she said, 'Dear, you must not be so easily embarrassed and afraid of your feelings.' After she left he assaulted an aide. Bateson called this the symptomatic double bind: every response was wrong. No controlled study has shown that double binds cause schizophrenia. The group's cybernetic and homeostatic views of the family belong to family therapy (Section 7.7).

Theodore Lidz (1957) described marital schism (parents in open conflict, undermining each other) and marital skew (one dominant, disturbed parent whose distortions the other accepts). Lyman Wynne (1958) described pseudomutuality, a façade of harmony that forbids individuality, and later communication deviance, vague and fragmented parental speech. Ronald David Laing led anti-psychiatry, which saw schizophrenia as an understandable response to an unliveable family situation.

Where these theories sought causes and failed, expressed emotion (EE) predicts relapse. EE is the emotional climate of a key relative towards the patient, rated from the Camberwell Family Interview on three dimensions: critical comments, hostility and emotional over-involvement. Warmth and positive remarks are also rated, but they are not high-EE dimensions.

Key study: Expressed emotion and relapse (1972, 1976)

George Brown, James Birley and John Wing (1972) followed patients discharged to their families: relapse was about four times commoner in high-EE homes. Christine Vaughn and Julian Leff (1976) replicated this and found relapse highest when a patient spent more than about 35 hours a week face to face with a high-EE relative and took no medication. Criticism: the data are correlational; a more disturbed patient may provoke more criticism. In WHO studies in Chandigarh, Indian relatives were far less often high in EE than London relatives, one suggested reason for better outcome in India.

High EE is managed by family psychoeducation and by reducing face-to-face contact hours. Table 8.16 summarises the psychodynamic theories; the vulnerability-stress model that integrates these causes is in Section 8.1.

Antipsychotic drugs and their side effects

Antipsychotic medication (the neuroleptics) is the front-line treatment for schizophrenia; CBT, social skills training, family work and stress management are added to it, not used instead (Sections 7.4, 7.5, 7.7 and 7.9). Neuroleptics are used chiefly for schizophrenia (and in mania), not for depression or anxiety. The era began in 1952, when Jean Delay and Pierre Deniker in Paris gave chlorpromazine to psychotic patients. Typical antipsychotics (first-generation), such as chlorpromazine and haloperidol, act mainly by blocking dopamine D2 receptors (not D1); chlorpromazine also blocks muscarinic (M1) receptors, causing dry mouth and constipation. Atypical antipsychotics (second-generation), such as risperidone, olanzapine and clozapine, block 5-HT2A as well as D2 and cause fewer movement effects; clozapine is reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.

Because a typical drug blocks D2 in the nigrostriatal pathway too, it produces extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), in a fixed order:

  • Acute dystonia, within hours to days: sustained muscle spasms, a twisted neck, eyes forced upwards.
  • Akathisia, within days to weeks: a subjective inner restlessness with a compelling need to pace or move.
  • Drug-induced parkinsonism, within weeks: tremor, rigidity and slowed movement.
  • Tardive dyskinesia, after months to years: involuntary orofacial movements such as lip-smacking, tongue movements and grimacing; the last to appear and the most likely to be irreversible.

Other dangers are neuroleptic malignant syndrome, a rare emergency of high fever, 'lead-pipe' rigidity, unstable pulse and blood pressure and clouded consciousness; hyperprolactinaemia from tuberoinfundibular blockade (breast enlargement, milk secretion, missed periods); metabolic syndrome (weight gain, diabetes, raised lipids) with atypicals, especially clozapine and olanzapine; and agranulocytosis with clozapine, which demands regular blood counts. DSM-5-TR lists these reactions as medication-induced movement disorders and other adverse effects of medication.

Exam trap

Akathisia and tardive dyskinesia are secondary (drug-induced) movement disorders; Huntington's chorea (inherited) and Parkinson's disease (nigral degeneration) are primary. Tardive dyskinesia follows neuroleptics, not antidepressants, anxiolytics or barbiturates. Typicals cause EPS by blocking nigrostriatal D2 receptors, not by blocking serotonin reuptake (SSRIs), stimulating GABA-A receptors (benzodiazepines) or blocking mesolimbic receptors selectively.

Sadness after a loss and elation after a win are normal. A mood disorder (affective disorder) is a low or high mood, with changed sleep, energy, thinking and behaviour, that takes over a life for weeks or years. Such disorders are built from three kinds of episode, so the episodes come first.

Mood episodes: the building blocks

A disorder with depressive episodes only is unipolar; one in which mood swings between the extremes, mania or hypomania as well as depression, is bipolar. DSM-5 (2013) split DSM-IV's "mood disorders" into two chapters, bipolar and related disorders and depressive disorders, placing bipolar disorders between the psychotic and depressive chapters as a bridge. Specific phobias and euphoric mood are not symptoms of a major depressive episode; anhedonia and appetite change are. Depressive disorders

Major depressive disorder (MDD), one or more major depressive episodes with no mania or hypomania, is marked by persistent sadness and loss of interest; it may be a single episode or recurrent. Called the common cold of psychiatry, it affects about 16 percent of people in a lifetime (estimates run 15 to 20 percent), and rates are rising, fastest among the young. Onset grows likelier at puberty, after which women are affected about twice as often as men (in children the ratio is about equal). Episodes are often triggered by a stressful event, and the more severe the depression, the higher the suicide risk.

Exam trap

Asked which statement least accurately describes depression, pick "men are more vulnerable than women": the reverse is true. Widespread and rising incidence, stress triggers and the severity-suicide link are all accurate.

Persistent depressive disorder (PDD), formerly dysthymia, merges dysthymic disorder and chronic major depression. Mood is depressed most of the day, more days than not, for at least 2 years in adults or 1 year in children and adolescents, with two or more of six symptoms: appetite change, sleep change, low energy, low self-esteem, poor concentration and hopelessness. The person is never symptom-free for more than 2 months and has never had mania or hypomania. Dysthymia is persistent mild depression, less severe than MDD but longer. A major depressive episode on top of it is double depression.

Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD), new in DSM-5, means severe temper outbursts grossly out of proportion to the provocation, three or more times a week, with persistently irritable mood between them, for 12 months, in two of three settings (home, school, peers). Onset is before age 10 and diagnosis between 6 and 18. It was added to curb the over-diagnosis of bipolar disorder in children, who in fact grow up to have depression and anxiety.

Exam trap

A 12-year-old whose temper outbursts have just begun cannot have DMDD, because onset must come before age 10. Bipolar disorder, conduct disorder and ADHD (Sections 8.3 and 8.11) all remain possible.

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) needs five or more symptoms, at least one of them mood swings, irritability, depressed mood or anxiety, in the week before menses in most cycles, easing within a few days after menses begin. Depression caused by a drug, or by an illness such as hypothyroidism, is substance-induced or due to another medical condition (Section 8.10).

The "hybrid" disorder is ICD-10's mixed anxiety and depressive disorder (F41.2): anxiety and depression both present, neither enough for its own diagnosis. ICD-11 keeps it among the depressive disorders; DSM-5 does not. (Multiple personality, borderline and schizoid disorders belong to Sections 8.7 and 8.11.) René Spitz (1887 to 1974) described anaclitic depression in 1946: infants separated from their mothers in the second half of the first year grew weepy, then withdrawn, lost weight and stopped developing, recovering if the mother soon returned. His term hospitalism named the graver, sometimes fatal, decline of infants reared in impersonal institutions. (Piaget, Skinner and Eysenck are known for cognitive stages, operant conditioning and personality dimensions.) Masked depression presents as bodily complaints while sadness is denied, common in Indian primary care; depressive pseudodementia is covered in Section 8.10.

Bipolar and related disorders

The two-pole idea came from 1850s France: Jean-Pierre Falret (1794 to 1870) described folie circulaire and Jules Baillarger (1809 to 1890) folie à double forme, mania and melancholia alternating in one patient (Falret's son Jules gave us folie à deux). Emil Kraepelin (1856 to 1926) united recurrent mood illness as manic-depressive insanity in 1899, apart from dementia praecox, and Karl Leonhard split unipolar from bipolar forms in 1957.

Bipolar I disorder needs at least one manic episode; major depressive and hypomanic episodes are common but not required, and subthreshold depressive periods are allowed. Bipolar II disorder needs at least one hypomanic and one major depressive episode and never a full mania, which would make it bipolar I. Bipolar II is not milder, since its depressions are frequent and disabling, and it needs no psychotic features. Cyclothymic disorder is the chronic low-grade form: for at least 2 years (1 in the young), numerous hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet episode criteria, never absent for more than 2 months. Swinging between the poles is what makes a disorder bipolar (Figure 8.7). Bipolar disorder affects about 1 percent of people, men and women equally, usually beginning around age 20.

Exam trap

Bipolar I is defined by mania, so "bipolar I includes hypomania" is the incorrect statement: hypomania defines bipolar II and is optional in bipolar I, which may also include depressive periods, even subthreshold ones. Rapid cycling and psychotic depressions occur in both, and bipolar II has no manic episodes at all, only shorter hypomanias; a full manic episode is what separates I from II.

Causes

Mood disorders run in families: heritability is about 40 percent for MDD and up to about 90 percent for bipolar disorder in twin studies, among the highest in psychiatry, though identical-twin concordance of 40 to 70 percent leaves room for the environment. Joseph Schildkraut's catecholamine hypothesis (1965) linked depression to a deficit of noradrenaline at brain synapses and mania to an excess; the serotonin permissive hypothesis adds that low serotonin lets other transmitters swing towards either pole. Both are now thought too simple. Depression also brings overactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis with raised cortisol: about half of patients fail to suppress cortisol on the dexamethasone suppression test, though the finding is too unspecific for diagnosis. Sleep shows shortened REM latency, dream sleep arriving sooner. Robert Post's kindling hypothesis (1992) holds that each episode sensitises the brain and lowers the threshold for the next, so early episodes follow major stress and later ones need little or none.

The most examined theory is that of Aaron Temple Beck (1921 to 2021), who argued in 1967 that depression is maintained by the negative cognitive triad: negative views of the self ("I am worthless"), the world or present experience ("nothing goes right") and the future ("it will never improve"). Underlying negative schemas, early core beliefs such as "I am unlovable", are activated by stress and generate quick, unbidden automatic thoughts, kept believable by cognitive distortions such as overgeneralisation and personalisation; the conditional rules linking them ("If I am not liked by all, I am worthless") are dysfunctional assumptions. Cognitive therapy targets all of these (Section 7.5).

Exam trap

Beck's triad is self, world and future, not id-ego-superego, past-present-future, mother-father-self, self-others-environment, thoughts-feelings-behaviours, body-mind-spirit or mood-behaviour-somatic state. Automatic thoughts, distortions and dysfunctional assumptions are other parts of the model, not the triad. Repressed sexual conflict, too little reinforcement (Lewinsohn) and insecure attachment belong to other theories.

Key study: Social origins of depression (Brown and Harris, 1978)

George Brown and Tirril Harris interviewed women in Camberwell, south London. Severe losses and disappointments provoked depression mainly in women with vulnerability factors: no confiding relationship, three or more children under 14 at home, loss of the mother before age 11, and no job outside the home. Criticism: one city, women only, and life events recalled after the fact.

First-line drug treatment

For depression, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine are first line, safer in overdose than tricyclics (imipramine) and monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs, which need a low-tyramine diet). For bipolar disorder the gold standard is lithium (John Cade, 1949), used for acute mania and for prophylaxis (preventing further episodes), and it lowers suicide risk. Its therapeutic range is narrow, so blood levels are monitored; toxicity brings coarse tremor, vomiting, unsteadiness and confusion. Valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine are alternatives, and atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, risperidone) are first line for acute mania; haloperidol and lorazepam are adjuncts for agitation only. Electroconvulsive therapy and the psychotherapies are in Chapter 7.

Exam trap

Fluoxetine is not a first-line treatment for acute mania: an antidepressant given alone in bipolar disorder can trigger a switch into mania or rapid cycling. Lithium, valproate and atypical antipsychotics are first line.

Suicide

Suicidal ideation is thinking of killing oneself; a plan fixes method, place and time; intent is how much the person wants to die; an attempt is a self-injurious act with some intent to die that is survived; completed suicide ends in death. Lethality is the medical danger of the method, so the mode of an attempt (hanging or pesticide against a few tablets) shows its lethality, while intent is inferred from planning and precautions against rescue. Norman Kreitman coined parasuicide (1969) for non-fatal deliberate self-injury or overdose whatever its intent, later called deliberate self-harm. Non-suicidal self-injury (cutting or burning without intent to die) is a DSM-5 condition for further study.

The World Health Organization counts over 700,000 suicides a year. India's National Crime Records Bureau recorded about 1.71 lakh in 2022, about seven in ten of them men and most aged 18 to 45, with family problems (about a third) and illness the leading recorded causes. Women attempt suicide more often, but men die by it more often, using more lethal methods: the gender paradox.

Émile Durkheim (1858 to 1917), in Suicide (1897), traced suicide to social ties: egoistic (too little integration, the isolated person), altruistic (too much, dying for the group as in hara-kiri), anomic (too little regulation, sudden upheaval such as losing a fortune) and fatalistic (too much regulation, as for slaves or prisoners). Edwin Shneidman (1918 to 2009), founder of suicidology, saw suicide as escape from psychache, unbearable psychological pain. In Thomas Joiner's interpersonal theory (2005) the wish to die comes from perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness, but lethal attempts also need an acquired capability, fearlessness of pain and death built by exposure such as self-harm. Marie Åsberg (1976) found that depressed patients with low cerebrospinal fluid 5-HIAA, the serotonin metabolite, made more frequent and more violent suicide attempts.

The strongest single predictor of suicide is a previous attempt. Other risk factors are depression and other mental disorders, alcohol, hopelessness (Beck's Hopelessness Scale predicts better than depression severity), male sex, older age, living alone, chronic illness, a family history of suicide and access to means such as pesticides. Social support, children at home, religious objection and restricted means protect. Risk is highest when high lethality, a specific plan, frequent ideation and family history converge. SAD PERSONS (Patterson and colleagues, 1983) is in Section 7.2, and the decriminalisation of attempted suicide by the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 in Section 7.1.

Exam trap

The mode or method of an attempt indicates lethality; intentionality is the wish to die, and conviction and survival are not standard dimensions. A recent attempt of low lethality with no family history carries less risk than high lethality, a specific plan, frequent ideation and family history together.

8.6 Anxiety and Obsessive-Compulsive and Related DisordersMedium yield · 32 Qs

A racing heart before an exam is anxiety doing its job. A man who has avoided buses for three years since he panicked on one, or a woman who washes for three hours a day to cancel a thought of germs, has crossed into disorder. This section sets out the anxiety disorders and why they develop, then obsessive-compulsive disorder and its relatives.

Fear, anxiety and the old neuroses

Fear is the alarm response to danger that is present now: a surge of autonomic arousal and the urge to escape. Anxiety is apprehension about a future threat, felt as muscle tension, vigilance and cautious avoidance. Both are normal, and moderate anxiety even helps performance (the Yerkes-Dodson law, Section 1.10). Anxiety becomes pathological when it is out of proportion to the danger, developmentally inappropriate, persistent (typically six months or more) and impairing.

The Scottish physician William Cullen coined the word neurosis in 1769 for disorders of sense and motion arising from the nervous system without fever or local disease. Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) narrowed neurosis to a non-psychotic disorder of unconscious conflict, with insight preserved, and in 1895 described an "anxiety neurosis". DSM-III (1980) dropped the term because it implied Freud's theory. DSM-5 (2013) split the old anxiety group into three chapters: anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, and trauma- and stressor-related disorders (Section 8.7). ICD-10 kept the umbrella block F40 to F48, "neurotic, stress-related and somatoform disorders", running from phobic anxiety (F40), other anxiety (F41) and OCD (F42) to somatoform (F45) and other neurotic disorders (F48). ICD-11 follows DSM-5, with separate "anxiety or fear-related" and "obsessive-compulsive or related" groupings.

DSM-5-TR orders the anxiety disorders by typical age of onset and separates them by what is feared (Table 8.21); six months is the usual duration. Anxiety caused by a drug or its withdrawal, or by an illness such as hyperthyroidism, is diagnosed instead as substance/medication-induced anxiety disorder or anxiety disorder due to another medical condition.

A specific phobia is a persistent, irrational fear of a specific object or situation, out of proportion to the danger, provoked almost every time and avoided or endured with dread: an anxiety disorder, not a mild stress reaction, a personality disorder or a passing hallucination. Its five types are animal, natural environment (heights, storms, water), blood-injection-injury, situational (aeroplanes, lifts, enclosed places) and other (choking, vomiting). Most phobics show sympathetic arousal, but the blood-injection-injury type shows a diphasic vasovagal response: heart rate and blood pressure rise briefly, then drop, and the person may faint.

Social anxiety disorder is fear of situations of possible scrutiny (conversation, eating in public, a speech) because one may act in a way that is negatively evaluated or embarrassing; in children it must occur with peers. The performance only specifier covers fear confined to speaking or performing in public.

Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is excessive anxiety and worry about a number of events or activities, more days than not for at least six months, that the person finds hard to control, with three or more of six symptoms (only one in children): restlessness, easy fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and sleep disturbance. Hopping from health to money to a child's journey home, it is continued, free-floating apprehension about unspecified things. ICD-10 asks for anxiety on most days for several weeks at a time, usually several months; its research criteria fix six months. ICD-11 asks for at least several months, more days than not, with no fixed number.

Exam trap

GAD is continued apprehension about unspecified things, defined by persistent, excessive worry. "Fear of fear" is panic disorder, flashbacks PTSD and safety checking OCD; mania and mood swings are mood symptoms (Section 8.5), hallucinations psychotic (Section 8.4). GAD needs at least 6 months in adults and specific phobia typically 6 months, not 1 week or 1, 3 or 12 months.

Panic attacks, panic disorder and agoraphobia

A panic attack is an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes (literally a few minutes; not seconds, half an hour or hours), with four or more of thirteen symptoms: palpitations, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, choking, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, chills or heat sensations, paraesthesias (numbness or tingling), derealisation or depersonalisation, fear of losing control or "going crazy", and fear of dying. Expected attacks have an obvious cue (a spider); unexpected ones come out of the blue. A panic attack is not itself a disorder; in other disorders it is a specifier ("PTSD with panic attacks").

Panic disorder requires recurrent unexpected attacks, at least one followed by a month or more of persistent worry about further attacks or their consequences (a heart attack, going mad), a maladaptive change in behaviour (avoiding exercise), or both. One attack is not panic disorder.

Agoraphobia, literally "fear of the marketplace", is marked fear of two or more of five situations: public transport, open spaces, enclosed places (shops, cinemas), queues or crowds, and being outside the home alone, because escape might be difficult or help unavailable if panic-like symptoms strike. The situations are avoided, endured with dread or faced only with a companion, typically for six months. Since DSM-5, agoraphobia is diagnosed independently of panic disorder; a person meeting both receives both.

In 1978 Alan Goldstein and Dianne Chambless argued that after a few attacks people come to fear the bodily sensations of anxiety themselves. This fear of fear marks panic disorder and agoraphobia.

Exam trap

Agoraphobia is the irrational fear of public places. Hearing voices is psychotic, nervousness in a lift is claustrophobia, fear of tall trees or buildings a fear of heights, and re-experiencing trauma PTSD. Panic disorder is recurrent unexpected attacks, not one attack, worry about many things (GAD) or fear of panicking in enclosed spaces.

Why anxiety develops

Biology. Anxiety is moderately heritable, though what is inherited is a general anxious tendency. Benzodiazepines calm anxiety by boosting GABA, the main inhibitory transmitter, so the GABA-benzodiazepine system is the classic anxiety system; noradrenaline from the locus coeruleus drives the alarm, serotonin modulates it, and the amygdala is the hub of the fear circuit. Jerome Kagan (1929 to 2021) found that behavioural inhibition, shyness and withdrawal from novelty visible by four months of age, predicts later anxiety.

Learning. The two-factor theory of O. Hobart Mowrer (1907 to 1982), set out in 1947, explains why fears last. A neutral stimulus paired with a frightening one becomes a conditioned stimulus for fear (classical conditioning, as with Little Albert, Section 1.6); avoiding it then reduces fear, so avoidance is negatively reinforced and the fear is never unlearned. Martin Seligman (born 1942) explained why snakes and heights are feared more than electric sockets with preparedness (1971): evolution has prepared us to learn ancestral fears quickly and keep them. Stanley Rachman (1977) explained phobias with no frightening memory through three pathways: direct conditioning, vicarious learning and information.

Cognition. David M. Clark (1986) traced panic to the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations: a skipped heartbeat is read as a heart attack, and fear and sensations feed each other (Figure 8.8). Catastrophic thinking in general means worrying that the worst will always happen. It differs from other distortions (Section 7.5): dichotomous thinking (everything extremely good or extremely bad), selective abstraction (seeing only the negative) and personalisation (taking things personally). Steven Reiss's anxiety sensitivity, the belief that anxiety symptoms are harmful, predicts panic. For GAD, Thomas Borkovec held that verbal worry avoids the vivid imagery and arousal of fear and is reinforced by that avoidance; Adrian Wells' metacognitive model adds positive and negative beliefs about worry itself; and Michel Dugas stressed intolerance of uncertainty.

The suffocation alarm. Donald F. Klein's suffocation false alarm theory (1993) proposes an evolved suffocation monitor that in panic disorder is oversensitive, firing at harmless rises in carbon dioxide; carbon dioxide inhalation and lactate infusion do trigger attacks far more readily in panic patients. It explains panic disorder, not Down syndrome, autism (Section 8.3) or delirium (Section 8.10). David H. Barlow's triple vulnerability theory then combines a generalised biological vulnerability (an anxious temperament), a generalised psychological vulnerability (a sense that events are uncontrollable) and a specific psychological vulnerability (learning that certain things are dangerous).

Psychodynamic view. Freud later treated anxiety as signal anxiety (1926), a warning to the ego that a forbidden impulse is near, which sets defences going. The defence of phobia is displacement, shifting feeling from a threatening object to a safer one. Each classic neurosis had a signature defence: repression in hysteria, displacement in phobia, isolation, undoing and reaction formation in OCD, introjection in depression.

Key study: Little Hans (Freud, 1909)

Hans, aged five, would not go out for fear that a horse would bite him. Freud, working mainly through the boy's father, read the fear as an Oedipal fear of the father displaced onto horses, which, unlike a father, could be avoided. Critics noted that the evidence came from a father devoted to Freud, and that Hans had seen a horse fall in the street, which suggests simple conditioning.

Exam trap

Displacement is the defence of phobias, not of hysteria, OCD or depression. Catastrophic thinking is expecting the worst, not dichotomous thinking, selective abstraction or personalisation.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder

Obsessions are recurrent, persistent, intrusive and unwanted thoughts, urges or images that cause anxiety and that the person tries to ignore, suppress or neutralise. Compulsions are repetitive behaviours (washing, checking, ordering, tapping) or mental acts (praying, counting) performed in response to an obsession or by rigid rules, to reduce anxiety or prevent a dreaded event, though not realistically connected to it. The cardinal DSM-5 feature is obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming (more than 1 hour a day) or cause significant distress or impairment. Merely having obsessions is not enough, and there is no six-hour or two-week rule.

Priya, 22, after touching a door handle, fears she will carry germs that kill her mother and washes three hours a day: this is OCD. GAD worry concerns realistic matters without rituals, illness anxiety disorder is fear of having a disease (Section 8.7), and a specific phobia simply avoids an external object.

Obsessions are ego-dystonic: intrusive, unwanted and inconsistent with the self-concept, neither part of one's identity nor pleasurable. Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (Section 8.11), a separate disorder, is ego-syntonic: perfectionism and control felt as right, without true obsessions or compulsions. The insight specifier runs from good or fair through poor to absent insight/delusional beliefs (certain the house will burn unless the stove is checked 30 times; 4 percent or fewer). A tic-related specifier marks a current or past tic disorder.

Symptoms cluster in four dimensions: contamination and washing, doubt or harm and checking, symmetry with ordering and counting, and taboo (aggressive, sexual, religious) thoughts. Hoarding is no longer an OCD compulsion, since DSM-5 made hoarding disorder separate. Twelve-month prevalence is about 1.2 percent, and onset is in childhood or early adulthood (mean 19.5 years; boys earlier).

Exam trap

Tapping, washing and checking are compulsions; hoarding is not. OCD is ego-dystonic, OCPD ego-syntonic; obsessions are not always reality-grounded, OCD is not confined to childhood, and total unawareness that obsessions are unreasonable is absent insight. Grandiosity and need for admiration mark narcissistic personality disorder (Section 8.11).

Biology. OCD responds to clomipramine and the SSRIs, which block serotonin reuptake, implicating the central serotonin (5-HT) system; GABA is the anxiety system, and glutamate and dopamine play lesser parts. Imaging implicates the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit: the orbitofrontal cortex and anterior cingulate signal "something is wrong", the caudate nucleus fails to filter the signal, and the thalamus loops it back. Susan Swedo (1998) described PANDAS, sudden childhood OCD or tics after streptococcal infection, thought to be an autoimmune attack on the basal ganglia. First-degree relatives have about twice the rate, ten times after childhood onset.

Learning. Mowrer's two-factor theory applies directly (Figure 8.9): the obsession acquires anxiety by classical conditioning, the compulsion relieves it, and the relief negatively reinforces the compulsion. Compulsive acts are thus escape or avoidance responses (safety behaviours): not conditioned stimuli (the contaminating thought is the stimulus), not unconditioned responses (unlearned reflexes), not positively reinforced (distress is removed, nothing pleasant added) and not involuntary. Conditioning is the general mechanism; escape is the pattern. Exposure and response prevention blocks the ritual (Section 7.4).

Cognition and psychodynamics. Nearly everyone has odd intrusive thoughts; Paul Salkovskis (1985) argued that OCD begins when they are appraised as signs of inflated responsibility for harm, which must be neutralised. Rachman added thought-action fusion, the belief that thinking of an act is as bad as doing it or makes it likelier. Freud's Rat Man (1909), tormented by the thought of rats gnawing into his father, is the classic case of "obsessional neurosis", which Freud traced to regression to anal-stage conflicts over control, dirt and aggression. Its defences are isolation of affect (the thought stays, its feeling goes), undoing (an act that symbolically cancels a forbidden thought, the most characteristic), reaction formation (scrupulous cleanliness against a wish to soil) and intellectualisation. Repression is the defence of hysteria.

Exam trap

Isolation, undoing and reaction formation are all OCD defences. For a single answer choose undoing, the defence of obsessive-compulsive neurosis (not depression, mania or schizophrenia). The defence NOT used in OCD is repression.

The related disorders

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is preoccupation with perceived defects or flaws in appearance that are not observable or appear slight to others, with repetitive behaviours (mirror checking, grooming, skin picking, reassurance seeking) or mental acts (comparing). Specifiers record insight and muscle dysmorphia, the belief that one's body is too small or insufficiently muscular. Enrico Morselli called it dysmorphophobia in 1891. DSM-IV listed BDD among the somatoform disorders; DSM-5 placed it in obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, not with somatic symptom, anxiety or personality disorders. It concerns appearance, unlike somatic symptom disorder (distressing bodily symptoms), illness anxiety disorder (fear of serious illness) and conversion disorder (neurological symptoms without matching disease), all in Section 8.7.

Hoarding disorder is persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value, from a perceived need to save them, so that clutter blocks the use of living spaces. Trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder), named by François Hallopeau in 1889, is recurrent hair pulling causing hair loss, with repeated attempts to stop; excoriation disorder is the same with skin picking and lesions. These body-focused repetitive behaviours are not driven by obsessions. Substance/medication-induced OCRD and OCRD due to another medical condition complete the chapter. ICD-11's obsessive-compulsive or related group also includes olfactory reference disorder (a conviction of emitting a foul body odour) and hypochondriasis (health anxiety), and calls trichotillomania and excoriation body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders. Finally, an obsession is resisted as intrusive and usually recognised as excessive; a GAD worry concerns real-life matters and brings no ritual; a delusion is a false belief held as simply true.

8.7 Trauma, Dissociative and Somatic Symptom DisordersHigh yield · 50 Qs

A soldier who relives a shelling every night, a woman who finds clothes she does not remember buying, a girl whose arm goes limp after a quarrel: all three were once called hysterical. This section follows hysteria as it split into the trauma-related, dissociative and somatic symptom disorders, and ends with illness that is feigned.

Hysteria, from Hippocrates to DSM-III

The word hysteria comes from the Greek hystera, womb: Hippocrates (about 460 to 370 BCE) and his followers blamed its fits and paralyses on a "wandering womb". At the Salpêtrière in Paris, Jean-Martin Charcot (1825 to 1893) showed that hysterical phenomena such as loss of feeling in a limb or fainting could be produced and removed by hypnosis and suggestion, so their cause was psychological, not a lesion.

His pupil Pierre Janet (1859 to 1947) named the mechanism désagrégation (1889), now called dissociation: under strain, a cluster of memories and functions splits off from consciousness and runs on its own. The phrase la belle indifférence, the calm unconcern some patients show about a dramatic symptom, is usually credited to Janet. In Vienna Josef Breuer (1842 to 1925) relieved "Anna O." of her paralyses by letting her talk through their origins under hypnosis, her "talking cure", and with Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) published Studies on Hysteria (1895).

For Freud, hysteria was physical reactions to emotional trauma: a painful memory is pushed out of awareness by repression and its energy converted into a bodily symptom, hence conversion. Most psychodynamic theorists still see repression behind both conversion and dissociative symptoms. The symptom gives primary gain, relief from the anxiety of the conflict, and often secondary gain, attention or escape from a duty.

Key study: Hilgard's hidden observer

Ernest Ropiequet Hilgard (1904 to 2001) gave hypnotised volunteers a suggestion that they would feel no pain and put their hands in iced water. They reported little pain, yet a hidden observer, addressed by a prearranged cue, reported nearly normal pain. His neodissociation theory (Divided Consciousness, 1977) holds that awareness can split into parallel streams. Critics such as Nicholas Spanos argued that the instructions create the observer.

In 1980 DSM-III divided hysteria into somatoform and dissociative disorders (Figure 8.10); ICD-10 kept them together as dissociative (conversion) disorders, F44.

Exam trap

Freud's hysteria is physical reactions to emotional trauma; a phobia of horses is Little Hans, and the Oedipus complex and oral traits belong to Chapter 4. The psychodynamic mechanism of dissociative symptoms is repression: conflict is what gets repressed. Charcot used both hypnosis and suggestion. La belle indifférence is credited to Janet, not Freud, Anna Freud (who catalogued the defence mechanisms, Chapter 4) or Hippocrates.

Posttraumatic and acute stress disorders

First World War soldiers who shook, froze or went mute were said to have shell shock; Abram Kardiner (1891 to 1981) described The Traumatic Neuroses of War (1941), and DSM-III named posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in 1980. DSM-5 moved it from the anxiety disorders into a new trauma- and stressor-related chapter.

The event must involve actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, experienced directly, witnessed in person, learned of as happening to a close family member or friend, or met through repeated exposure to aversive details, as by police officers hearing about child abuse. Symptoms fall into four clusters:

  • Intrusion (1 of 5): distressing memories, nightmares, flashbacks (acting or feeling as if the event were recurring), distress at reminders.
  • Avoidance (1 of 2) of trauma-related thoughts and feelings or of outside reminders.
  • Negative alterations in cognitions and mood (2 of 7): amnesia for part of the event, self-blame, "no one can be trusted", guilt, detachment, no positive emotions.
  • Alterations in arousal and reactivity (2 of 6): irritability, recklessness, hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, poor concentration, poor sleep.

Symptoms must last more than 1 month. Specifiers are the dissociative subtype (persistent depersonalisation or derealisation) and delayed expression (full criteria met only 6 months or more after the event); children 6 and younger have separate criteria. Acute stress disorder covers 3 days to 1 month after the trauma and needs nine of fourteen symptoms from five categories. DSM-IV required dissociative symptoms for it; DSM-5 dropped that rule, so timing now separates it from PTSD, and both sit in the trauma chapter.

ICD-11 places PTSD among disorders specifically associated with stress, which require an extremely threatening or horrific event, and keeps three core elements: re-experiencing in the here and now, avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat. Complex PTSD adds disturbances in self-organisation (affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship difficulties), usually after prolonged, inescapable trauma such as torture or childhood abuse. An acute stress reaction is a normal response, not a disorder.

Who develops PTSD depends first on the dose and type of trauma (most prisoners of war do; interpersonal violence is worse than accidents), then on vulnerability: earlier anxiety, family instability and peritraumatic dissociation (feeling dazed or unreal during the event) raise risk, while social support protects. Conditioning attaches fear to reminders and avoidance blocks extinction. The amygdala is overactive, the hippocampus smaller and the HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) stress axis dysregulated. Prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR are covered in Sections 7.4 and 7.5. Trauma can also bring post-traumatic growth, which Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun (1996) measured in five domains: appreciation of life, relating to others, personal strength, new possibilities and spiritual change.

Exam trap

PTSD has four clusters (DSM-IV had three). Magical thinking and ideas of reference belong to schizotypal personality and psychosis (Sections 8.4 and 8.11), and "denial, anger, bargaining" are Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's stages of dying (Chapter 6). PTSD lasts more than 1 month; acute stress disorder 3 days to 1 month; delayed expression begins at 6 months.

Adjustment, grief and attachment disorders

An adjustment disorder is an emotional or behavioural reaction to an identifiable stressor, such as divorce or job loss, with disproportionate distress or impaired functioning; it begins within 3 months of the stressor and ends within 6 months after the stressor or its consequences end. Its subtypes are with depressed mood, with anxiety, with mixed anxiety and depressed mood, with disturbance of conduct, with mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct, and unspecified. Being brief and tied to a present stressor, it is best treated by brief psychotherapy and crisis intervention (Section 7.9).

Prolonged grief disorder, added in DSM-5-TR (2022), is intense yearning for or preoccupation with the deceased, with symptoms such as identity disruption, disbelief and meaninglessness, at least 12 months after the death (6 months in children and adolescents; ICD-11 needs 6), beyond cultural norms. Ordinary bereavement is not a disorder.

After severe neglect or institutional rearing, a child with reactive attachment disorder is inhibited and emotionally withdrawn, rarely seeking comfort, while one with disinhibited social engagement disorder shows indiscriminate sociability, readily going off with strangers. Both need a developmental age of at least 9 months (Table 8.23).

Dissociative identity disorder

Dissociative disorders disrupt the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception and behaviour, and most follow trauma. Dissociative identity disorder (DID), called multiple personality disorder until DSM-IV (1994), requires two or more distinct personality states, or an experience of possession, and recurrent gaps in the recall of everyday events, personal information or trauma beyond ordinary forgetting, not explained by accepted religious practice.

The identity most often encountered, carrying the person's real name, is the host; the others are alters, which may differ in age, gender and voice, and changing between them is a switch. The amnesia shows in daily life: a woman finds clothes she does not remember buying. About 90 percent of patients report childhood abuse or neglect.

Corbett Thigpen and Hervey Cleckley's The Three Faces of Eve (1957) and Sybil (1973), a case later criticised as shaped by the therapist, frame the debate on causes. The trauma model sees identities as states split off to escape unbearable abuse. The sociocognitive model of Nicholas Spanos (1942 to 1994) sees them as roles shaped in suggestible people by therapists' questions, hypnosis and media; in a 1985 experiment, over 80 percent of students playing an accused murderer produced an "alter" when interviewed.

Exam trap

"Multiple personalities in one body" is DID, not schizophrenia, whose "split" is between thought and emotion (Section 8.4). Bipolar disorder alternates moods, and borderline personality disorder an unstable self-image, not separate identities with amnesia. The identity with the real name is the host; "ghost" and "mixed" identities do not exist.

Dissociative amnesia, fugue and depersonalisation

Dissociative amnesia is an inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually traumatic or stressful, too extensive to be ordinary forgetting and not due to a substance or a neurological or medical condition. It is a potentially reversible, mainly retrograde failure of retrieval, in five forms:

  • Localised: a circumscribed period, such as the hours of an assault (the commonest form).
  • Selective: some, but not all, events of that period.
  • Generalised amnesia: one's entire life history, often with identity.
  • Systematised: one category, such as everything about a violent sibling.
  • Continuous: each new event, as it occurs.

Fugue, a separate diagnosis in DSM-IV, is now the specifier with dissociative fugue: apparently purposeful travel or bewildered wandering with amnesia for identity, as in the man found working in another city with no memory of who he is. Organic amnesias differ. Transient global amnesia is a sudden, hours-long loss of new learning in an older adult who keeps their identity but repeats the same questions; Korsakoff syndrome is thiamine-deficiency amnesia with confabulation in chronic alcohol use; Alzheimer's disease begins with gradually failing new learning (Section 8.10); amnesia from head injury or another medical condition is mainly anterograde. In all of these, personal identity is lost last; in dissociative amnesia it can go first.

Depersonalisation/derealisation disorder means persistent or recurrent experiences of either kind, or both. Depersonalisation is detachment from oneself: being an outside observer of one's own thoughts, feelings, body or actions, or feeling no longer one's normal self. Derealisation is unreality of the surroundings, which seem dreamlike, foggy or distorted. Reality testing stays intact ("it is as if I were dreaming"), which separates it from psychosis.

Exam trap

Self unreal is depersonalisation; world unreal is derealisation. Multiple personality, fugue and amnesia are hysterical dissociative states, but déjà vu (a new situation feels already lived) is not: it is a disorder of recognition memory, seen in healthy people and temporal lobe epilepsy. A hallucination is perception without a stimulus (Section 8.2).

Other specified dissociative disorder covers possession without amnesia, identity change after brainwashing, acute reactions lasting under a month, and dissociative trance, an acute narrowing or loss of awareness of the surroundings with profound unresponsiveness, outside accepted cultural practice. ICD-10 adds dissociative stupor and trance and possession disorders, in which a spirit or deity seems to take over; possession states are among the commonest dissociative presentations in India, though within an accepted ritual they are not disorders. Ganser syndrome, described by Sigbert Ganser (1853 to 1931) in 1898 in prisoners awaiting trial, combines approximate answers (a cow has "five" legs), clouding of consciousness, pseudohallucinations and conversion symptoms.

Somatic symptom and illness anxiety disorders

Somatoform disorders (DSM-III and DSM-IV) were conditions with symptoms suggesting a medical condition without evidence of physical pathology. They included somatisation disorder, earlier Briquet's syndrome after Paul Briquet (1796 to 1881), who in 1859 described patients returning with endless lists of complaints; hypochondriasis; pain disorder; and conversion. Neurasthenia ("lack of nerve strength"), named by George Miller Beard (1839 to 1883) in 1869 for unexplained exhaustion, survives in ICD-10.

DSM-5 renamed the class somatic symptom and related disorders and defined it by positive features. Somatic symptom disorder needs one or more distressing somatic symptoms with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours: disproportionate thoughts about their seriousness, high health anxiety, or excessive time and energy spent on them, typically for more than 6 months. The symptoms may have a medical cause: a woman with real arthritis who spends hours a day researching it qualifies.

Exam trap

"Physical symptoms without a medical cause" is the old somatoform idea, which DSM-5-TR calls "problematic". Against hallucinations, delusions and obsessions it is still the keyed answer for somatic symptom disorder, but the DSM-5-TR feature is excessive response to distressing symptoms.

Illness anxiety disorder (formerly hypochondriasis) is preoccupation with having or acquiring a serious illness when somatic symptoms are absent or mild, with high health anxiety and excessive checking or avoidance, for at least 6 months. It has care-seeking and care-avoidant types. ICD-11 keeps the name hypochondriasis but places it among obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, and replaces somatoform disorders with bodily distress disorder, distressing bodily symptoms with excessive attention to them. Psychological factors affecting other medical conditions covers stress or behaviour that worsens a real disease (Section 7.9).

Exam trap

Chronic fatigue syndrome, or systemic exertion intolerance disease, has no DSM-5-TR criteria. The US Institute of Medicine (2015) requires fatigue for 6 months or more, not relieved by rest; post-exertional malaise; unrefreshing sleep; and cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance. Weight change is not a criterion.

Conversion: functional neurological symptom disorder

Hysteria's direct heir is functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder): one or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function, with clinical findings incompatible with recognised neurological or medical conditions. The symptoms are not intentionally produced. They include paralysis; abnormal movements such as astasia-abasia (cannot stand or walk, yet moves the legs normally in bed); globus, a lump in the throat; aphonia; psychogenic non-epileptic seizures; glove anaesthesia, ending sharply at the wrist where no nerve territory ends; and tunnel vision or functional blindness, explained by psychological factors with no damage to eye, optic nerve or cortex.

Diagnosis rests on positive signs. In Hoover's sign a "paralysed" leg cannot press down on request, yet presses down when the patient lifts the healthy leg against resistance. A woman whose arm becomes paralysed after an argument, with a normal examination and the remark "it's not really bothering me", is the classic picture: sudden onset after a stressor, motor or sensory loss, and la belle indifférence. DSM-5-TR no longer requires a stressor and calls la belle indifférence non-specific: in one review 21 percent of conversion patients showed it, against 29 percent with organic disease. ICD-11 calls the condition dissociative neurological symptom disorder. Conversion differs from hypochondriasis: neurological-like symptoms without organic basis, versus fear of having a serious illness.

Exam trap

La belle indifférence points to conversion disorder, not malingering, factitious disorder, somatic symptom disorder or illness anxiety. Conversion symptoms "affect motor or sensory function, begin suddenly and follow a stressor" (the classic picture, though DSM-5-TR no longer requires the stressor); "suggest a medical disorder" fits any somatic disorder, and normal investigations alone never make the diagnosis.

Factitious disorder and malingering

In factitious disorder the person falsifies signs or symptoms, or induces injury or disease, with deception and presents as ill even without obvious external rewards; the motive is to assume the sick role. The severe form imposed on self, with dramatic stories and many admissions, was named Munchausen syndrome by Richard Asher (1912 to 1969) in 1951, after the tall tales of Baron Munchausen. In factitious disorder imposed on another (Munchausen by proxy, Roy Meadow, 1977) a carer induces illness in a dependent person, like the nursing assistant who secretly injects insulin into elderly patients; the perpetrator receives the diagnosis. DSM-5 lists it in the somatic symptom chapter, but it was never a somatoform disorder: DSM-IV gave it a chapter of its own.

Malingering is intentionally producing false or grossly exaggerated symptoms for external incentives such as compensation, drugs, or escape from work, military duty or prosecution. It is not a mental disorder but a condition that may be a focus of clinical attention (Table 8.24).

8.8 Feeding, Eating, Elimination and Sleep-Wake DisordersLower yield · 20 Qs

Eating, bladder and bowel control, and sleep are among the first things a child learns to regulate, and DSM-5-TR gives each its own family of disorders. The eating disorders, above all anorexia nervosa, threaten life most and come first; the elimination and sleep-wake disorders follow.

Pica, rumination and ARFID

DSM-5-TR's feeding and eating disorders are persistent disturbances of eating that impair health or psychosocial functioning. Three of the six begin mostly in childhood and involve no body-image concern.

Pica is the persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances (chalk, soil, paper, hair, paint) for at least 1 month, inappropriate to the developmental level and not culturally sanctioned, and is diagnosed from about age 2. It is linked with iron or zinc deficiency, pregnancy, intellectual disability and autism. In rumination disorder food is repeatedly regurgitated for at least 1 month, then re-chewed, re-swallowed or spat out, without a medical cause such as reflux; it occurs in infants and in intellectual disability. Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is food avoidance (for texture or smell, lack of interest, or fear of choking) causing weight loss, nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements or tube feeding, or psychosocial interference; mere picky eating does not qualify. ARFID has no disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced, which separates it from anorexia.

Anorexia nervosa: history and criteria

In 1873 two physicians described young women who starved themselves while insisting they were well. In London Sir William Withey Gull (1816 to 1890) named the illness anorexia nervosa (in a paper read in 1873 and printed in 1874); in Paris Ernest-Charles Lasègue (1816 to 1883) called it anorexie hystérique. The name misleads: anorexia means loss of appetite, yet most patients are hungry and driven by fear of fatness. Hilde Bruch (1904 to 1984), in The Golden Cage (1978), described a compliant, high-achieving girl who feels ineffective and finds in control of her body the one thing truly hers.

Anorexia nervosa (AN) has three DSM-5-TR criteria:

  1. Restriction of energy intake leading to a significantly low body weight for age, sex, development and physical health.
  2. Intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, or persistent behaviour that interferes with weight gain, even at a low weight.
  3. Disturbance in how weight or shape is experienced, undue influence of weight or shape on self-evaluation, or persistent failure to recognise the seriousness of the low weight.

Subtypes describe the last 3 months. The restricting type has had no recurrent binge eating or purging; weight is lost by dieting, fasting or excessive exercise. The binge-eating/purging type has had recurrent binges or purging (vomiting, or misuse of laxatives, diuretics or enemas). Adult severity uses body mass index (BMI, weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared): mild 17 or more, moderate 16 to 16.99, severe 15 to 15.99, extreme below 15.

Exam trap

DSM-5 dropped amenorrhoea as a criterion and sets no BMI cut-off: a BMI of 17 marks mild severity, not eligibility. ICD-10 used BMI 17.5 or less; ICD-11 uses below 18.5. Purging does not exclude anorexia; it defines a subtype. The core is fear of weight gain with restriction, not obsession with cleanliness (obsessive-compulsive disorder, Section 8.6).

The starving body and the anorexic personality

Most physical signs are the body's defences against starvation, so think "slow and low". Metabolism slows, giving bradycardia (slow heart rate), hypotension and hypothermia. Fine downy hair called lanugo grows on the body. Falling hormones produce amenorrhoea, the absence of menstrual periods, and in time osteoporosis. Vomiting adds hypokalaemia (low potassium), which can trigger fatal arrhythmias. Hyperthyroidism, tachycardia, hyperkalaemia and polyphagia (overeating) all point the wrong way.

Anorexia nervosa is the most lethal eating disorder and has one of the highest mortality rates of any psychiatric disorder. DSM-5-TR puts its crude mortality at about 5 per cent per decade, from medical complications (cardiac arrhythmia among them) and from suicide. It usually begins in adolescence, far more often in women.

The personality behind it is strikingly consistent: perfectionism, obsessionality, rigidity and anxiety; the patient is over-controlled. Impulsivity belongs to bulimia, while avoidance and dependence define the avoidant and dependent personality disorders (Section 8.11).

Exam trap

Amenorrhoea is no longer required but is still common, so it remains a correct "finding in anorexia". It means absence of menstrual periods, not fear of weight gain or excessive exercise.

Bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder

The London psychiatrist Gerald Russell named bulimia nervosa (BN) in 1979, calling it "an ominous variant of anorexia nervosa". DSM-5-TR requires:

  1. Recurrent binge eating: eating, in a discrete period (about 2 hours), an amount definitely larger than most people would, with a sense of loss of control.
  2. Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviour against weight gain: self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics or other medicines, fasting or excessive exercise.
  3. Both on average at least once a week for 3 months (DSM-IV asked for twice a week).
  4. Self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight.
  5. Not occurring only during episodes of anorexia nervosa.

Secret binges, often set off by low mood, are followed by shame and purging, in repeating cycles. Because purging undoes only part of a binge, people with bulimia are usually of normal weight or overweight. Vomiting leaves marks: eroded dental enamel, swollen parotid glands, hypokalaemia and Russell's sign, calluses on the back of the hand from the teeth. The typical trait is impulsivity.

Binge-eating disorder (BED), a full diagnosis only since DSM-5, is binge eating at least once a week for 3 months, with marked distress and three of five features: eating very rapidly, until uncomfortably full, when not hungry, alone from embarrassment, and with disgust or guilt afterwards. There is no regular compensatory behaviour. Figure 8.11 shows how the three disorders are told apart.

Anorexia and bulimia share a psychology: a distorted body image, an intense fear of being fat, and eating regulated by psychological rather than physiological processes (rules, fear and mood instead of hunger and fullness).

Exam trap

Significantly low body weight separates anorexia from bulimia. Purging, fear of fatness and damage to health occur in both. Binges plus compensation is bulimia; binges with distress and no compensation is binge-eating disorder. Only one of the three is diagnosed within an episode.

Causes and other specified eating disorders

Risks pile up. Sociocultural: they cluster where a thin ideal is prized, as in modelling and ballet. Dieting starts most histories, and restraint provokes bingeing as the body defends its weight (set-point theory, Section 1.10). Family: Salvador Minuchin's enmeshed, overprotective, rigid, conflict-avoiding psychosomatic family (Section 7.7) features in anorexia. Personality: perfectionism in anorexia, impulsivity in bulimia. Biology: twin studies put heritability near one half, and low serotonin activity is linked with impulsivity and binge eating.

Presentations fitting no full pattern are other specified feeding or eating disorder (OSFED), for example atypical anorexia nervosa (every criterion met except low weight: despite significant weight loss, weight is within or above the normal range), bulimia or binge-eating disorder of low frequency or short duration, purging disorder (purging without binge eating) and night eating syndrome (eating after waking at night, or excessively after the evening meal, with recall).

Enuresis and encopresis

The elimination disorders are the repeated passage of urine or faeces into inappropriate places by a child developmentally old enough to control them, voluntary or not. Enuresis is repeated voiding of urine into bed or clothes, at least twice a week for 3 consecutive months or with significant distress or impairment, at an age of at least 5 years, and not caused by a substance (a diuretic) or a medical condition (diabetes, epilepsy). It is nocturnal only (the commonest), diurnal only, or both. In primary enuresis the child has never been dry; secondary enuresis returns after a dry period, usually between 5 and 8 years, often after stress. About 5 to 10 per cent of 5-year-olds wet the bed; most outgrow it. The bell-and-pad alarm is described in Section 7.4.

Encopresis is repeated passage of faeces into inappropriate places (clothing, the floor) at least once a month for 3 months, at age 4 or older. It is specified with constipation and overflow incontinence, the commoner form, in which painful defecation leads to withholding and leakage around impacted stool, or without constipation, often with oppositional or conduct problems. It too may be primary or secondary.

Exam trap

Lack of bowel control is encopresis; lack of bladder control is enuresis. Anorexia is an eating disorder, and "analism" is not a recognised elimination disorder. Ages: urine 5, faeces 4.

The sleep-wake disorders and insomnia

The sleep stages, non-rapid eye movement (NREM) stages N1 to N3 and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, are taught in Section 1.5. DSM-5-TR's sleep-wake disorders, ten disorders or groups (Table 8.25), share a complaint about the quality, timing or amount of sleep with daytime distress. ICD-11 moved them out of the mental disorders into their own chapter, Chapter 7. Four follow a rule of threes, at least three times a week for 3 months: insomnia, hypersomnolence, narcolepsy and restless legs syndrome.

Insomnia disorder is difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early waking, on at least 3 nights a week for 3 months despite adequate opportunity for sleep. Its treatment, cognitive behaviour therapy with stimulus control, sleep restriction and paradoxical intention, is in Section 7.4. In hypersomnolence disorder the person is excessively sleepy despite a main sleep of at least 7 hours, with daytime lapses into sleep, unrefreshing sleep of over 9 hours, or difficulty waking fully.

In the breathing-related sleep disorders breathing falters in sleep: the upper airway collapses (obstructive sleep apnoea hypopnoea), the brain stops driving breathing (central sleep apnoea), or shallow breathing lets carbon dioxide rise (sleep-related hypoventilation). In the circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders the body clock is out of step with the required schedule: sleep comes too late or too early (delayed or advanced phase), is scattered across the 24 hours (irregular type), drifts later each day (non-24-hour type, common in blind people) or clashes with working hours (shift work type).

Narcolepsy

The French physician Jean-Baptiste-Édouard Gélineau (1828 to 1906) coined the term narcolepsy in 1880. DSM-5-TR's narcolepsy is recurrent periods of irrepressible need to sleep or napping within a day, at least three times a week for 3 months, plus one of three markers: cataplexy, brief, sudden, bilateral loss of muscle tone with consciousness preserved, triggered by laughter or joking; hypocretin (orexin) deficiency in the cerebrospinal fluid; or a short REM latency (REM within 15 minutes of sleep onset, or a mean sleep latency of 8 minutes or less with two or more sleep-onset REM periods on the multiple sleep latency test). Type 1 has cataplexy or hypocretin deficiency; type 2 has neither.

Hypocretin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus stabilise waking; when they are lost, fragments of REM sleep break into it. Hence the classic narcoleptic tetrad: sleep attacks, cataplexy (REM atonia while awake), sleep paralysis (awake but unable to move on falling asleep or waking) and vivid hypnagogic hallucinations while falling asleep.

Exam trap

Cataplexy is a sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion, in full consciousness, and it marks narcolepsy. Catalepsy is the reverse, a posture held for long periods with the waxy flexibility of catatonia (Section 8.4).

Parasomnias and restless legs

Parasomnias are abnormal behaviours or experiences arising from sleep, and timing identifies them: deep N3 sleep fills the first third of the night, while REM periods lengthen towards morning. The NREM sleep arousal disorders are incomplete awakenings, usually from N3, in the first third of the night, with little dream recall and amnesia; they are commonest in children. In sleepwalking (somnambulism) the person sits up, walks about or performs complex acts while asleep, blank-faced and hard to wake. In sleep terrors (pavor nocturnus) the sleeper wakes abruptly with a panicky scream, intense fear and autonomic arousal (racing heart, rapid breathing, sweating, dilated pupils), and cannot be comforted.

REM parasomnias come later. Nightmare disorder is repeated, extended, extremely dysphoric dreams, usually in the second half of the night; the dreamer wakes alert and remembers vividly. In REM sleep behaviour disorder the normal muscle atonia of REM fails, so the person acts out dream content, shouting, punching or kicking. Commonest in men over 50, it often precedes Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy, often by more than a decade (about 75 per cent of idiopathic cases within 10 to 15 years).

Restless legs syndrome (Willis-Ekbom disease) is an urge to move the legs, with creeping sensations, that starts at rest, is relieved by movement and worsens in the evening. A substance/medication-induced sleep disorder follows intoxication or withdrawal. Kleine-Levin syndrome, recurrent hypersomnia with hyperphagia and hypersexuality, mostly in adolescent boys, is a separate diagnosis in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3); DSM-5-TR names it only as an example of other specified hypersomnolence disorder.

Exam trap

Sitting, walking or complex behaviour while asleep is somnambulism; screaming terror is sleep terrors; acting out dreams is REM sleep behaviour disorder, from loss of REM atonia, not absence of REM; uncomfortable leg sensations are restless legs. Bedwetting is enuresis; daytime sleepiness alone suggests hypersomnolence.

8.9 Sexual Dysfunctions, Paraphilic Disorders and Gender DysphoriaLower yield · 9 Qs

Few corners of psychiatry have been redrawn as often. A man who ejaculates too soon, a person aroused by shoes, a child who insists she is a boy and a contented gay man in 1970 have all been diagnosed; only some would be today. We start with normal sexual response, take the three DSM-5-TR groups in turn, and end with homosexuality's exit from the manuals.

The sexual response cycle and the language of gender

Disorder is judged against the normal picture drawn by the gynaecologist William Howell Masters (1915 to 2001) and his research partner Virginia Eshelman Johnson (1925 to 2013). In Human Sexual Response (1966), from laboratory recordings of nearly 700 volunteers, they described a four-phase sexual response cycle. In excitement, blood engorges the genitals, producing erection or vaginal lubrication. In plateau, arousal levels off just before climax. Orgasm is a peak of rhythmic muscular contractions, with ejaculation in men. In resolution the body returns to rest, and men pass through a refractory period in which another orgasm is impossible; many women have none and can have multiple orgasms.

Helen Singer Kaplan (1929 to 1995) saw patients whose bodies worked but who did not want sex, and in Disorders of Sexual Desire (1979) put a desire phase first (desire, excitement, orgasm), giving the linear sequence of Figure 8.12; dysfunctions are still sorted by the phase they disturb. Rosemary Basson argued in 2000 that this fits many women in long relationships poorly. In her circular model a woman often starts from sexual neutrality, is receptive for the sake of intimacy, is aroused by sexual stimuli and only then feels responsive desire; satisfaction feeds back into intimacy. DSM-5 merged women's desire and arousal disorders partly for this reason.

Four terms must be kept apart. Sex is biological (chromosomes, gonads, hormones, genitals); gender identity is the inner sense of being a man, a woman or another gender; gender role is the behaviour a culture expects of each gender; sexual orientation is the gender of those one is attracted to. Androgyny, in the 1974 work of Sandra Lipsitz Bem (1944 to 2014) with her Bem Sex Role Inventory, means scoring high on both masculine and feminine traits; it describes a flexible personality, not a disorder.

Sexual dysfunctions

A sexual dysfunction is a clinically significant disturbance in the ability to respond sexually or to experience sexual pleasure. Most of DSM-5-TR's eight share one template: the problem occurs on almost all occasions (about 75 to 100 percent), has lasted at least about 6 months, causes clinically significant distress, and is not better explained by another mental disorder, severe relationship distress, a substance or a medical condition. Specifiers give the course: lifelong (present since sexual activity began) or acquired (after a period of normal function), and generalised or situational (only with certain partners, stimulation or situations). Stop-start and squeeze treat premature ejaculation, not erectile disorder, inhibited male orgasm (delayed ejaculation, the opposite problem) or vaginismus. Premature ejaculation is most commonly associated with an anxiety state: masturbation does not cause it (a myth that also fuels dhat syndrome, Section 8.1), diabetes is the classic link of erectile disorder, and personality disorder has no special tie.

Paraphilias and paraphilic disorders

DSM-5-TR calls any intense and persistent sexual interest other than in genital stimulation or fondling with physically mature, consenting human partners a paraphilia. The key DSM-5 move was to separate interest from disorder. A paraphilic disorder is a paraphilia that currently causes distress or impairment, or whose satisfaction has involved harm, or risk of harm, to others. Criterion A gives the interest, recurrent and intense for at least 6 months in fantasies, urges or behaviours; Criterion B the consequences. Gender dysphoria and gender incongruence

Gender dysphoria in DSM-5-TR is a marked incongruence between one's experienced or expressed gender and one's assigned gender, lasting at least 6 months and causing clinically significant distress or impairment. A child needs six of eight indicators, one of which must be a strong desire to be, or insistence that one is, the other gender; others include cross-gender clothing, play and playmates and dislike of one's anatomy. An adolescent or adult needs two of six, such as a strong desire to be rid of one's sex characteristics or to be treated as the other gender. Specifiers: 'with a disorder/difference of sex development' and, for adults, 'posttransition'.

Homosexuality: pathologised, then depathologised

Pathologisation is classifying a behaviour or identity as a mental disorder; depathologisation is removing that classification (not changing orientation, the aim of discredited conversion therapies, nor coming out). Homosexuality is the clearest case. DSM-I (1952) placed it under sociopathic personality disturbance as a sexual deviation; DSM-II (1968) kept it among the sexual deviations. Alfred Charles Kinsey (1894 to 1956), in Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948), rated behaviour on the Kinsey scale from 0 (exclusively heterosexual) to 6 (exclusively homosexual), showing same-sex experience to be common and graded.

Key study: Hooker's blind judges (1957)

Evelyn Hooker (1907 to 1996) asked whether homosexual men are more maladjusted. She matched 30 homosexual and 30 heterosexual men, none in therapy, for age, IQ and education and gave them projective tests, including the Rorschach. Expert judges blind to group could not tell them apart beyond chance, and rated their adjustment alike. Criticism: a small, non-random sample; but earlier claims had rested on patients in treatment.

With this evidence and activist pressure, the board of the American Psychiatric Association voted in December 1973 to remove homosexuality. The seventh printing of DSM-II (1974) substituted sexual orientation disturbance for people distressed by their orientation; DSM-III (1980) renamed it ego-dystonic homosexuality, and DSM-III-R (1987) dropped it. ICD-10 (endorsed in 1990) no longer listed homosexuality, stating that orientation by itself is not a disorder, but kept F66, including egodystonic sexual orientation; ICD-11 deleted F66.

Indian context

In 2018 the Indian Psychiatric Society stated that homosexuality is not a psychiatric disorder. On 6 September 2018 the Supreme Court, in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, read down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, decriminalising consensual sex between adults of the same sex.

8.10 Substance-Related, Addictive and Neurocognitive DisordersMedium yield · 36 Qs

A man who has drunk heavily for twenty years is admitted for surgery. On his third dry day he is trembling, picking at the sheets and swatting at insects only he can see. Months later, sober, he forgets a nurse within minutes and fills the gap with invented stories. He spans both halves of this section: the disorders drugs produce, and the failures of cognition that drugs, disease and age produce.

The language of substance use

A psychoactive substance is a drug that alters mood, cognition, perception or behaviour by acting on the brain; the word has nothing to do with a drug-induced hallucination or an "active psychopath". Intoxication is the reversible syndrome of recent use. Withdrawal is the syndrome that follows stopping or cutting down after heavy, prolonged use, and it usually mirrors intoxication: a sedative leaves an agitated brain behind, a stimulant a slowed one.

Tolerance is needing markedly more of a drug for the same effect: metabolic tolerance when the liver breaks the drug down faster, pharmacodynamic tolerance when receptors and neurons adapt. Cross-tolerance is tolerance that extends to other drugs, especially of the same class, so a heavy drinker needs large doses of benzodiazepines or barbiturates. In cross-dependence one drug suppresses another's withdrawal. Together these are why alcohol-dependent patients are detoxified with a long-acting benzodiazepine such as chlordiazepoxide, and why methadone can replace heroin. Sensitisation (reverse tolerance) is the opposite: repeated cocaine or amphetamine use brings paranoia or seizures at ever lower doses.

Physical dependence shows itself in a withdrawal syndrome; psychological dependence is a compelling need to use for pleasure or relief. Addiction, compulsive drug seeking despite harm, is different again: a cancer patient on morphine is physically dependent but not addicted. Craving is an intense urge to use, often cued by places and objects linked to the drug, and relapse is a return to use after abstinence (Chapter 7).

ICD-10 codes these disorders F10 (alcohol) to F19 (multiple drugs), and the digit after the point gives the clinical pattern: .0 acute intoxication, .1 harmful use (use that is actually damaging health), .2 dependence syndrome, .3 withdrawal state, .4 withdrawal with delirium, .5 psychotic disorder, .6 amnesic syndrome and .7 residual and late-onset psychotic disorder. The dependence syndrome was described for alcohol in 1976 by Griffith Edwards and Milton Gross: a narrowed drinking repertoire, drink given priority over everything else, tolerance, repeated withdrawal, drinking to relieve it, a felt compulsion, and rapid reinstatement after abstinence.

DSM-IV split this ground into substance abuse and substance dependence. DSM-5 (2013) and DSM-5-TR (2022) merge them into one substance use disorder: at least 2 of 11 criteria within 12 months, in four groups. Impaired control covers using more or longer than intended, failed efforts to cut down, much time spent, and craving; social impairment covers failed roles, interpersonal problems and activities given up; risky use covers hazardous situations and use despite known harm; the pharmacological criteria are tolerance and withdrawal. Severity is mild with 2 or 3, moderate with 4 or 5, and severe with 6 or more. Craving was added and legal problems dropped. "Addiction" is not a DSM-5-TR diagnosis.

DSM-5-TR also includes gambling disorder (four or more of nine criteria in 12 months, such as chasing losses) as its only behavioural addiction; internet gaming disorder is a condition for further study. ICD-11's block "disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours" codes each drug as an episode of harmful use, a harmful pattern of use, or dependence, and adds gambling disorder and gaming disorder: gaming with impaired control, given priority over other interests despite harm.

Exam trap

"Drug abuse" is not an ICD-10 pattern: abuse was a DSM-IV category, and ICD-10's nearest term is harmful use. Gaming disorder sits in ICD-11's substance use and addictive behaviours block, not under stress-associated, mood or impulse control disorders. Cross-tolerance is tolerance carried over to a related drug, not freedom from addiction.

Why drugs capture the brain

Every drug of abuse, by its own route, ends up raising dopamine in the mesolimbic dopamine system, from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens in the ventral striatum (Chapter 5). It is the circuit most consistently altered in substance use disorders; the cerebellar vermis (gait), lateral geniculate nucleus (vision) and vestibular nuclei (balance) are not reward structures. Drugs are positive reinforcers because they give pleasure and negative reinforcers because they remove withdrawal, anxiety or pain (Chapter 1).

In the opponent-process theory of Richard Solomon and John Corbit (1974), every drug high (the a-process) triggers an opposing low (the b-process). With repetition the b-process strengthens: the high shrinks (tolerance), the crash deepens (withdrawal), and the user takes the drug to escape misery rather than to feel good.

Using Swedish adoption records in 1981, C. Robert Cloninger and colleagues separated type I alcoholism (onset after about 25, guilt, binges with loss of control, both sexes, shaped by environment) from type II (early onset, novelty seeking, antisocial behaviour, inability to abstain, mainly men, highly heritable). Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) saw alcohol-dependent people as fixated at the oral stage (Chapter 4). Elvin Morton Jellinek (1890 to 1963) argued in The Disease Concept of Alcoholism (1960) that alcoholism is a disease with five "species": alpha (psychological reliance, no loss of control), beta (physical damage without dependence), gamma (tolerance, withdrawal and loss of control: once started, the drinker cannot stop), delta (inability to abstain) and epsilon (periodic binges).

Key study: Kandel's stages of drug use (1975)

Denise Kandel surveyed New York State high-school students twice and found a regular order: beer or wine, then cigarettes or spirits, then marijuana, then other illicit drugs. Few reached heroin or cocaine without marijuana, so in this gateway hypothesis marijuana is the drug most often cited as the gateway, although alcohol and tobacco come first. Criticism: a sequence is not a cause, and shared risk factors could explain it.

Drugs differ in addictive liability, the chance that a user becomes dependent. Nicotine (tobacco) ranks highest: a larger share of people who try it become dependent than of those who try heroin, cocaine, alcohol or marijuana.

Inhalants intoxicate like alcohol and damage organs. Nicotine acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors; caffeine use disorder is only a condition for further study. Benzodiazepines and barbiturates are cross-tolerant with alcohol, and their withdrawal can also bring seizures and delirium.

Alcohol is a GABA agonist. Heroin is the odd one out among opium alkaloids because it is semi-synthetic. Opioid intoxication gives pinpoint pupils, withdrawal dilated ones. Disulfiram works by aversion through aldehyde dehydrogenase; reducing reward is naltrexone's action and glutamate modulation acamprosate's.

Neurocognitive disorders: terms and domains

ICD-10 groups disorders of failing cognition as organic mental disorders (F00 to F09). DSM-5 renamed them neurocognitive disorders (NCDs), acquired declines from a previous level. It kept delirium and replaced dementia and amnestic disorder with major neurocognitive disorder, a significant decline in one or more of six domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor function, social cognition) that costs independence in daily life, and mild NCD, a modest decline with independence kept. One impaired domain is enough, so pure amnesia now counts as major NCD.

Older terms name the function each disorder chiefly affects: dementia, a global decline that includes abstraction and judgement; amnesia, memory; aphasia, alexia and acalculia, language and symbolic function; apraxia, skilled purposeful movement (motor skills); and agnosia, recognition despite intact senses (Chapter 5).

Delirium

Delirium is a disturbance of attention and awareness (clouded consciousness) that develops over hours to days, fluctuates through the day, and brings disorientation, memory loss or perceptual disturbance, often visual hallucinations. The sleep-wake cycle may reverse, and confusion worsens in the evening (sundowning). DSM-5-TR specifies acute or persistent delirium and hyperactive, hypoactive (quiet and easily missed) or mixed types. Motor signs include asterixis (a flapping tremor of outstretched hands), multifocal myoclonus, tremor, abnormal tone and reflexes, and carphologia or floccillation, picking at the bedclothes: all can occur. The electroencephalogram (EEG) shows diffuse slowing.

Delirium always has a physical cause: infection, metabolic or electrolyte disturbance, hypoxia, drugs, intoxication or withdrawal, or surgery. It affects 11 to 51 percent of older people after operations, and it is the commonest psychiatric syndrome after cardiac surgery, ahead of depression, anxiety and schizophrenia. Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome

Return to the opening drinker. Heavy drinkers eat poorly and absorb thiamine (vitamin B1) badly, and neurons need thiamine to use glucose. Acute deficiency causes Wernicke's encephalopathy, described by Carl Wernicke (1848 to 1905) in 1881: confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia with nystagmus, an emergency treated with thiamine before glucose. Untreated, it leaves Korsakoff's syndrome (Korsakoff's psychosis), described by Sergei Korsakoff (1854 to 1900) in 1887: severe anterograde and retrograde amnesia, apathy, and confabulation, fluent false accounts filling memory gaps without intent to deceive. Immediate memory and intelligence are relatively spared. Together these form Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, with lesions in the mammillary bodies and dorsomedial thalamus.

Exam trap

Memory loss, confabulation and nystagmus in a heavy drinker is Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. The cause is thiamine deficiency, not vitamin B12, vitamin D, folate, hypoxia or lead. Alzheimer's, Pick's, Parkinson's, Huntington's and Creutzfeldt-Jakob diseases are not caused by drinking.

Physical disease can present psychiatrically, so DSM-5-TR has diagnoses such as depressive disorder due to another medical condition (ICD-10 F06 and F07, the "organic" syndromes). Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol) mainly causes depression, anxiety and cognitive impairment, occasionally mania or psychosis; Addison's disease (cortisol deficiency) causes apathy and depression. Hypothyroidism causes depression and, when severe, a psychosis called "myxoedema madness"; hyperthyroidism causes anxiety. Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, neurosyphilis (general paresis, with grandiose delusions), HIV and systemic lupus erythematosus can all cause mood change, psychosis or dementia. Epilepsy can bring an interictal psychosis, and head injury lasting personality change. Frontal damage produces the frontal lobe syndrome, an organic personality disorder of disinhibition, apathy and poor judgement (Chapter 5).

8.11 Personality Disorders, Conduct and Impulse-Control DisordersMedium yield · 32 Qs

Most disorders in this chapter come as episodes. The conditions here describe how a person habitually thinks, feels and relates, year after year. We define personality disorder, sort the ten types into clusters, study borderline and antisocial personality closely, meet the dimensional models, and end with the disorders of conduct and impulse control.

When personality becomes a disorder

Everyone has personality traits, enduring ways of perceiving, relating to and thinking about the world and oneself; they become a personality disorder (PD) only when inflexible and maladaptive. DSM-5-TR defines a PD as an enduring pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from the expectations of the person's culture and shows in two or more of four areas: cognition (how self, others and events are perceived), affectivity (the range, intensity, lability and appropriateness of emotion), interpersonal functioning and impulse control. The pattern is pervasive and inflexible, stable, traceable to adolescence or early adulthood, causes distress or impairment, and is not better explained by another disorder, a substance or a medical condition. A PD can therefore disturb thinking, mood and relationships alike.

The traits are usually ego-syntonic: they feel like "just me", so informants become essential. The median prevalence of any PD is about 10 percent (10.5 percent in DSM-5-TR's review). Diagnosis before 18 needs features present for at least a year, because childhood traits often fade; antisocial PD is never diagnosed before 18. DSM-IV recorded PDs on Axis II (Section 8.1). DSM-5-TR keeps the categorical model, PDs as distinct syndromes, while noting the dimensional model, PDs as extreme variants of normal traits that shade into normality (see the newer models).

It is genetically linked to schizophrenia, so DSM-5-TR also lists it in the schizophrenia spectrum (Section 8.4) and ICD-10 classes it there. Exam trap

There is no Cluster D. Avoidant PD is Cluster C, not A: the schizoid person does not want company; the avoidant person longs for it but fears rejection. Among paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal and borderline, the odd one out is borderline (Cluster B).

Borderline personality disorder

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a pervasive pattern of instability of interpersonal relationships, self-image and affects, with marked impulsivity, beginning by early adulthood. Its signature is emotional instability and impulsivity, not detachment, perfectionism or paranoid ideation. Five or more of nine criteria are needed:

  1. Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment.
  2. Unstable, intense relationships alternating between idealisation and devaluation.
  3. Identity disturbance: a markedly and persistently unstable self-image.
  4. Impulsivity in at least two self-damaging areas (spending, sex, substances, reckless driving, binge eating).
  5. Recurrent suicidal behaviour, gestures or threats, or self-mutilation.
  6. Affective instability from marked reactivity of mood, episodes lasting hours, rarely days.
  7. Chronic feelings of emptiness.
  8. Inappropriate, intense anger or difficulty controlling anger.
  9. Transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociation.

Adolph Stern coined "borderline" in 1938 for patients between neurosis and psychosis. Otto F. Kernberg (born 1928) recast it as borderline personality organisation: identity diffusion, primitive defences and generally intact reality testing. The primary defence is splitting: self and others are seen as all good or all bad, so today's idealised rescuer becomes tomorrow's betrayer. With it come projective identification (disowned feelings are placed in another person, who is pressed into feeling them), primitive idealisation and devaluation. Repression (pushing wishes out of awareness), sublimation (channelling them into valued activity), rationalisation (plausible excuses), regression (retreat to earlier behaviour) and undoing (an act that symbolically cancels another) are not the BPD defence (Chapter 4).

Heinz Kohut (1913 to 1981), founder of self psychology, held that a child needs mirroring (a parent's delighted reflection of its worth) and a calm figure to idealise; when both fail, the self stays fragmented, felt as emptiness and being "no one". He built the theory mainly to explain narcissism. Marsha M. Linehan (born 1943) proposed the biosocial theory: biological emotional vulnerability meets an invalidating environment that dismisses the child's feelings, so regulation is never learned. Treatments (Chapter 7): her dialectical behaviour therapy, mentalisation-based treatment, Kernberg's transference-focused psychotherapy and schema therapy. ICD-10 calls BPD emotionally unstable personality disorder, impulsive or borderline type.

Two differentials. BPD mood shifts last hours and follow interpersonal triggers; bipolar episodes last days to weeks, and both may coexist. Conduct disorder is diagnosed under 18 and centres on rule violation and aggression; BPD is an adult pattern of identity and affective instability and fear of abandonment, and childhood conduct disorder leads to ASPD, not BPD.

Clinical link

BPD is diagnosed more often in women in clinical samples, though not in community samples. Self-harm is common; about 6 percent of hospitalised patients died by suicide over 24 years.

Exam trap

BPD mood is reactive and unpredictable, never "regular and predictable"; eccentric, odd thinking is schizotypal; cruelty to animals and rule-breaking before 15 are conduct disorder. A 2023 paper asking which is NOT a BPD feature offered both "regular and predictable changes in mood" and "eccentric behaviour with odd thinking"; both are strictly correct, but the key is the mood option, the direct inversion of BPD's affective instability. A 2024 paper keys "failure of parental mirroring and idealising, leading to a fragmented self" under object relations theory: strictly it is Kohut's self psychology, a cousin of the object relations school of Melanie Klein (1882 to 1960) and Kernberg. Choose it; oral fixation, Oedipal conflict and a harsh superego are classical Freudian answers.

Antisocial personality disorder and psychopathy

Antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) is a pervasive pattern of disregard for and violation of the rights of others, occurring since age 15, shown by three or more of seven criteria: failure to conform to laws; deceitfulness and conning; impulsivity; irritability and aggressiveness; reckless disregard for safety; consistent irresponsibility; and lack of remorse. The person must be at least 18, with evidence of conduct disorder beginning before 15 (Figure 8.14). ICD-10 calls it dissocial personality disorder. Antisocial behaviour often remits by about age 40.

Philippe Pinel (1745 to 1826) described manie sans délire (mania without delirium) in 1801: impulsive rages with intact reasoning. James Cowles Prichard (1786 to 1848) named moral insanity in 1835. Hervey Cleckley (1903 to 1984), in The Mask of Sanity (1941), listed 16 characteristics of the psychopath, among them superficial charm, absence of nervousness, untruthfulness and lack of remorse: a normal mask over an emotional void. Robert D. Hare (born 1934) developed the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R, 1991), built on Cleckley: 20 items scored 0 to 2 from interview and records (total 40; 30 or more is the usual cut-off for psychopathy), with an interpersonal-affective factor (glibness, grandiosity, callousness) and an antisocial-lifestyle factor (impulsivity, irresponsibility, early behaviour problems).

Psychopathy is defined by traits, ASPD mostly by behaviour: most men in prison meet ASPD criteria, but only a minority are psychopaths. Causes combine genes with adverse rearing (abuse, neglect, erratic discipline). In Deviant Children Grown Up (1966), Lee Nelken Robins (1922 to 2009) found that adult sociopathy almost never arose without childhood antisocial behaviour: the root of the conduct-disorder rule. The underarousal hypothesis adds that psychopaths have low autonomic arousal (low resting heart rate and skin conductance) and seek stimulation.

Key study: Lykken's fearless psychopaths (1957)

David Thoreson Lykken (1928 to 2006) had primary psychopaths, other prisoners and controls learn a "mental maze" in which some wrong choices brought a shock. Psychopaths learned the maze as well but avoided the shocked errors less, reported less anxiety and showed smaller skin conductance responses to a signal paired with shock. Conclusion, the fearlessness hypothesis: punishment teaches psychopaths little. Criticism: small samples, one laboratory task.

Exam trap

The PCL-R is Hare's, built on Cleckley's description; not Cleckley alone, Aaron Temkin Beck (1921 to 2021; cognitive therapy) or Hans Jürgen Eysenck (1916 to 1997; the PEN model). In ASPD the pattern dates from 15, the diagnosis waits until 18, and conduct disorder must begin before 15, not 18. An option reading "conduct disorder before 15 and disregard for others' rights from 18" is loosely worded but still the answer: 18 is the minimum age for the diagnosis.

Histrionic, narcissistic and the rest of Cluster B

Histrionic personality disorder is excessive emotionality and attention seeking: discomfort when not the centre of attention, seductive or provocative behaviour, shallow and rapidly shifting emotion, impressionistic speech lacking detail, theatricality, suggestibility, and seeing relationships as more intimate than they are. Narcissistic personality disorder rests on a core triad of grandiosity, need for admiration and lack of empathy, with fantasies of unlimited success, the belief that one is special and understood only by high-status people, entitlement, exploitation, envy and arrogance. Kohut saw narcissism as arrested development after parents failed to mirror, to be met with empathy; Kernberg saw a pathological grandiose self defending against rage and envy toward cold parents, to be confronted. An admiration seeker who is emotional and seductive rather than grandiose is histrionic. Pervasive instability of relationships and self-image is borderline, not narcissistic.

Cluster C, and OCPD versus OCD

Avoidant personality disorder is social inhibition, inadequacy and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation in someone who wants relationships; it overlaps with social anxiety disorder (Section 8.6). Dependent personality disorder is an excessive need to be taken care of: needing reassurance for everyday decisions, never disagreeing for fear of losing support, urgently seeking a new relationship when one ends.

Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD) is a preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism and mental and interpersonal control at the expense of flexibility, openness and efficiency: lists swallow the task, perfectionism stops it being finished, work crowds out friends, morals are rigid, worthless objects are kept and money is hoarded. The motive is control, not achievement or autonomy; efficiency is what it sacrifices. Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939) described the same trio in the anal character (1908): orderly, parsimonious and obstinate. OCPD is ego-syntonic, with limited insight and no true obsessions or compulsions; OCD is ego-dystonic, its intrusive obsessions and compulsions felt as alien and resisted (Section 8.6). Both can be diagnosed together.

Newer models: ICD-11 and the AMPD

ICD-11 abolished the ten types: one diagnosis of personality disorder is graded mild, moderate or severe, with personality difficulty below that threshold. Five trait domain qualifiers describe its style: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition and anankastia (rigid perfectionism and control), plus a borderline pattern specifier. DSM-5's Section III offers the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD). Criterion A rates the level of personality functioning from 0 to 4 on identity and self-direction (self) and empathy and intimacy (interpersonal); at least moderate impairment is needed. Criterion B requires one or more pathological traits, organised in five domains: negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition and psychoticism. Six types survive: antisocial, avoidant, borderline, narcissistic, obsessive-compulsive and schizotypal, plus personality disorder-trait specified.

Exam trap

Dissociality and anankastia are ICD-11 domains; antagonism and psychoticism are AMPD domains.

Oppositional defiant and conduct disorder

The disruptive, impulse-control and conduct disorders, failures of emotional and behavioural self-control, mostly begin in childhood and are commoner in boys. Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) is angry or irritable mood, argumentative or defiant behaviour, or vindictiveness for at least 6 months, with four or more of eight symptoms shown with someone other than a sibling. Most children with ODD never develop conduct disorder.

Conduct disorder (CD) is a repetitive, persistent pattern violating the basic rights of others or major age-appropriate norms: three or more of 15 criteria in the past 12 months, one in the past 6 months, from four groups: aggression to people and animals, destruction of property, deceitfulness or theft and serious violations of rules (staying out at night or truancy before 13, running away). The childhood-onset type shows a symptom before age 10, the adolescent-onset type none. The specifier with limited prosocial emotions, applied to CD alone, needs two or more of four traits for 12 months: lack of remorse or guilt, callous lack of empathy, unconcern about performance, and shallow or deficient affect. These callous-unemotional traits mark more severe, planned aggression.

Terrie E. Moffitt (1993) explained the two onsets. Life-course-persistent antisocial behaviour is rare, starts in early childhood from neuropsychological deficits meeting adverse homes, and continues into adult crime. Adolescence-limited antisocial behaviour is common, begins at puberty as teenagers copy older peers, and fades in early adulthood.

Explosive, fire-setting and stealing impulses

Intermittent explosive disorder (IED) is recurrent impulsive, verbal or physical, aggressive outbursts grossly out of proportion to the provocation (twice weekly for 3 months, or three destructive or injurious outbursts in a year), unplanned and not aimed at money or power, from age 6. In pyromania the person deliberately and purposefully sets fires on more than one occasion, with tension or arousal before, fascination with fire, and pleasure, gratification or relief after; fires set for money, revenge, ideology or to hide a crime do not count. Pyromania is planned fire setting for gratification; IED is impulsive aggression beyond the trigger. In kleptomania the person repeatedly fails to resist impulses to steal objects not needed for personal use or their value, with rising tension before and pleasure or relief during the theft; women outnumber men three to one.

Charles Chrétien Henri Marc and Jean-Étienne Esquirol (1772 to 1840) named kleptomania in 1838, renaming the klopemanie (stealing madness) of the Swiss physician André Matthey (1816). Not the distractors: Eugen Bleuler (1857 to 1939) named schizophrenia and described ambivalence and autism; Enrico Morselli coined dysmorphophobia (1891); Alistair Munro described monosymptomatic hypochondriacal psychosis. ICD-10's habit and impulse disorders (F63) were pathological gambling, fire-setting and stealing, and trichotillomania. ICD-11's impulse control disorders are pyromania, kleptomania, compulsive sexual behaviour disorder and intermittent explosive disorder; gambling moved to addictive behaviours (Section 8.10), trichotillomania to the obsessive-compulsive group (Section 8.6).

Exam trap

"With limited prosocial emotions" is a specifier of conduct disorder only, not ASPD, ODD or IED. IED aggression may be verbal or physical. "Kleptomania" is Marc's term (with Esquirol), not Bleuler's, Munro's or Morselli's.

Kraepelin's Cards, Revisited

Kraepelin's cards hold this chapter in miniature. Each began with signs and symptoms, the vocabulary of Section 8.2, and ended with onset, course and prognosis, the terms of Section 8.1. The line the cards drew still divides schizophrenia (8.4) from the bipolar and depressive disorders (8.5), and DSM-III turned his method into explicit criteria, which is why every disorder from 8.3 to 8.11 arrives as a criterion set, often with a minimum duration. Yet his deeper hope, that each pile would prove to be a separate brain disease, has not come true. Risk genes for schizophrenia are shared with bipolar disorder and depression, comorbidity is the rule, and the diathesis-stress model explains more than any single cause. So DSM-5 added severity ratings and spectra to his categories, and ICD-11 grades personality disorder by severity.

Chapter summary

8.1 Abnormality, Causes and the Classification of Mental Disorders. No single criterion defines abnormality: clinicians weigh the four Ds, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction and the DSM-5-TR definition, which excludes expectable grief and mere social deviance. Psychopathology has its own vocabulary of aetiology, onset, course, prognosis, prevalence, incidence and comorbidity. Genes, endophenotypes and stress meet in the diathesis-stress model and Engel's biopsychosocial model. Classification is syndromal and prototypical, judged by reliability and validity. The DSM (APA; DSM-5 dropped the axes; DSM-5-TR, 2022, is current) and the ICD (WHO; ICD-10 F codes; ICD-11 Chapter 6) are the two systems, and cultural concepts of distress such as dhat and latah show culture shaping distress.

8.2 Descriptive Psychopathology: Signs and Symptoms. Descriptive psychopathology, founded by Karl Jaspers (General Psychopathology, 1913), describes experiences phenomenologically, separating form from content, primary from secondary symptoms and signs from symptoms. An illusion misreads a real stimulus; a hallucination, a disorder of perception, has no stimulus; hypnagogic hallucinations are normal. Flight of ideas, loosening, tangentiality and circumstantiality are disorders of the form of thought. Persecution is the commonest delusion; Capgras, Fregoli, Cotard, Othello and de Clérambault name delusional themes. Confabulation fills memory gaps without intent to deceive. Affect is observed and momentary, mood sustained; elevated mood runs euphoria, elation, exaltation, ecstasy. Insight has six grades.

8.3 Neurodevelopmental Disorders. Neurodevelopmental disorders begin in the developmental period and often co-occur, so assessment uses several informants. Intellectual disability needs intellectual deficits (IQ about two SD below the mean, 65 to 75), adaptive deficits and early onset; DSM-5 grades severity by adaptive functioning, and global developmental delay is used under age 5. Causes include trisomy 21, fragile X, PKU and fetal alcohol syndrome. Autism spectrum disorder (Kanner, 1943) needs social-communication deficits plus restricted, repetitive behaviour, not low IQ. ADHD needs six of nine symptoms in a domain, several before 12, in two settings. Specific learning disorder occurs with average intelligence; Tourette's needs motor and vocal tics for over a year.

8.4 Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders. Kraepelin's dementia praecox (1893), separated from manic-depressive insanity by its deteriorating course, became Bleuler's schizophrenia (1908, 1911), defined by four As; Schneider's first-rank symptoms stress voices, thought alienation, passivity and delusional perception. Symptoms are positive, negative or cognitive. DSM-5-TR needs 6 months including 1 month of active symptoms; ICD-10 and ICD-11 need 1 month. Brief psychotic disorder lasts 1 day to under 1 month, schizophreniform disorder 1 to under 6 months. Risk is about 48 percent for an identical twin. Positive symptoms reflect mesolimbic dopamine excess; high expressed emotion predicts relapse. Antipsychotics are first-line; nigrostriatal D2 blockade causes dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism and, last, tardive dyskinesia.

8.5 Depressive and Bipolar Disorders, and Suicide. Mood disorders are built from three episodes: a major depressive episode (5 of 9 symptoms for 2 weeks), a manic episode (1 week; marked impairment or psychosis) and a hypomanic episode (4 days, no marked impairment or psychosis). MDD affects women twice as often as men; dysthymia is milder but lasts 2 years; DMDD begins before age 10. Specifiers include mixed features, rapid cycling, melancholic, atypical, peripartum and seasonal. Bipolar I needs mania; bipolar II needs hypomania plus major depression; cyclothymia is subthreshold. Causes range from monoamines and kindling to Beck's cognitive triad. Lithium is the gold-standard mood stabiliser. A previous attempt is the strongest predictor of suicide.

8.6 Anxiety and Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders. Fear answers present danger, anxiety a future threat. DSM-5 split the old neuroses into anxiety, obsessive-compulsive and trauma chapters. Most anxiety disorders need about 6 months; panic disorder and selective mutism need 1 month. A panic attack peaks within minutes with 4 of 13 symptoms; agoraphobia needs 2 of 5 situations and GAD 3 of 6 symptoms. Causes include the GABA system, Mowrer's two-factor theory, Clark's catastrophic misinterpretation, Klein's suffocation alarm and displacement (Little Hans). OCD needs ego-dystonic obsessions or compulsions taking over an hour a day or causing distress; compulsions are negatively reinforced escape responses and undoing its typical defence. BDD, hoarding, trichotillomania and excoriation are its relatives.

8.7 Trauma, Dissociative and Somatic Symptom Disorders. Charcot, Janet, Breuer and Freud explained hysteria by hypnosis, dissociation and repression, and DSM-III (1980) split it into somatoform and dissociative disorders. PTSD needs a qualifying trauma and symptoms in four clusters for more than 1 month; acute stress disorder lasts 3 days to 1 month, and ICD-11 adds complex PTSD. DID combines two or more personality states with amnesic gaps; dissociative amnesia, fugue and depersonalisation/derealisation complete the class. Somatic symptom disorder is an excessive response to symptoms, conversion a neurological-like loss incompatible with disease, and factitious disorder and malingering are deliberate, differing in external reward.

8.8 Feeding, Eating, Elimination and Sleep-Wake Disorders. DSM-5-TR's feeding and eating disorders include pica (non-food eating for at least 1 month), rumination disorder and ARFID, which restricts food with no body-image concern. Anorexia nervosa (Gull and Lasègue, 1873) needs restriction to a significantly low weight, fear of weight gain and disturbed body experience; amenorrhoea was dropped, severity is graded by BMI, and it is the most lethal eating disorder. Bulimia nervosa (Russell, 1979) pairs binges with compensation weekly for 3 months at normal weight; binge-eating disorder lacks compensation. Enuresis starts at age 5, encopresis at 4. Among sleep-wake disorders, narcolepsy brings cataplexy, N3 brings sleepwalking and sleep terrors, and REM brings nightmares and REM sleep behaviour disorder.

8.9 Sexual Dysfunctions, Paraphilic Disorders and Gender Dysphoria. Masters and Johnson's sexual response cycle (1966: excitement, plateau, orgasm, resolution, with a male refractory period) gained Kaplan's desire phase (1979) and Basson's circular model (2000). Most DSM-5-TR sexual dysfunctions need symptoms on 75 to 100 percent of occasions for about 6 months with distress; premature ejaculation is tied to anxiety and treated with stop-start and squeeze. A paraphilic disorder is a paraphilia plus distress, impairment or harm to others; DSM-5-TR names eight. Gender dysphoria replaced gender identity disorder, and ICD-11 moved gender incongruence to Chapter 17 while paraphilic disorders stayed in Chapter 6. The APA removed homosexuality in 1973.

8.10 Substance-Related, Addictive and Neurocognitive Disorders. A psychoactive substance alters mood, cognition or behaviour; tolerance, cross-tolerance, withdrawal and dependence describe how use becomes disorder. ICD-10 codes F10 to F19 patterns (harmful use, Edwards and Gross's dependence syndrome; 'drug abuse' is not one), DSM-5-TR merges abuse and dependence into substance use disorder (2 of 11 criteria), and ICD-11 adds gambling and gaming disorder. Drugs converge on the mesolimbic dopamine system. Alcohol is a GABA-A agonist; heroin is semi-synthetic. Delirium is acute, fluctuating and clouded, unlike dementia; NCD subtypes differ in course and pathology; depression causes pseudodementia; thiamine deficiency causes Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.

8.11 Personality Disorders, Conduct and Impulse-Control Disorders. A personality disorder is an enduring, pervasive, inflexible pattern deviating from cultural expectations in two or more of cognition, affectivity, interpersonal functioning and impulse control, beginning by adolescence or early adulthood; usually ego-syntonic, it affects about 10 percent. DSM-5-TR's ten types form Clusters A (odd), B (dramatic) and C (anxious). Borderline PD (5 of 9 criteria) is instability of relationships, self-image and affect with impulsivity, its defence splitting. Antisocial PD needs age 18 and conduct disorder before 15; Hare's PCL-R measures psychopathy. ICD-11 and the AMPD grade severity and traits. Conduct disorder alone takes the specifier with limited prosocial emotions; pyromania and kleptomania follow tension with relief.

  1. Which of the following culture-bound syndromes is NOT reported in India? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Dhat Syndrome (b) Koro (c) Suchi-bai (d) Latah

    Answer: (d) Latah. Latah, an exaggerated startle with echolalia, echopraxia, automatic obedience and blurted obscenities, belongs to Malaysia and Indonesia. Dhat (anxiety over semen loss), koro (fear that the genitals are retracting, with epidemics in north-east India) and suchi-bai (obsessional cleanliness in Bengal) are all reported in India.

  2. The diathesis-stress model of psychopathology suggests that mental disorders result from: (RML 2023)

    (a) Genetic factors only (b) Environmental factors only (c) A combination of genetic predisposition and environmental stressors (d) Neurobiological abnormalities only

    Answer: (c) A combination of genetic predisposition and environmental stressors. The diathesis, a genetic, biological or psychological vulnerability, produces disorder only when a stressor sets it off; Zubin and Spring (1977) called this the vulnerability-stress model. Every option ending in 'only' names a single cause, the one-dimensional thinking the model replaced.

  3. A high false positive rate in diagnosis means: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Diagnosis is too strict (b) Normal people are diagnosed as disordered (c) Diagnosis has low reliability (d) Disordered people go undetected

    Answer: (b) Normal people are diagnosed as disordered. A false positive wrongly labels a healthy person as ill, which happens when criteria are too loose. Disordered people going undetected are false negatives, the result of over-strict criteria, and low reliability means clinicians disagree with each other, a separate problem.

  4. Hypnagogic hallucinations most commonly occur in: (RML 2024)

    (a) Schizophrenia (b) Delirium (c) Drug dependence (d) Normal

    Answer: (d) Normal. Hallucinations while falling asleep (hypnagogic) or waking (hypnopompic) lie within normal experience and are commonest in healthy people; narcolepsy also features them. The hallucinations of schizophrenia are mainly auditory and occur in clear consciousness, while delirium and drugs bring visual and tactile ones.

  5. Our current mood and feelings are best described by the term: (RML 2024)

    (a) Emotion (b) Mood (c) Affect (d) Feeling

    Answer: (c) Affect. Affect is the immediate, observed, moment-to-moment expression of emotion (the weather); mood is the sustained state the person reports (the climate). Emotion is the broader term for a brief reaction, and feeling is its experienced side.

  6. Choose the correct arrangement of these stages of elevated mood based upon severity: i) Elation, ii) Exaltation, iii) Euphoria, iv) Ecstasy. (Amity 2023)

    (a) i, ii, iii & iv (b) iii, i, ii & iv (c) iv, iii, ii & i (d) None of the above

    Answer: (b) iii, i, ii & iv. Elevated mood rises from euphoria (mild; hypomania) through elation (moderate; mania) and exaltation (intense, with grandiosity; severe mania) to ecstasy (rapture; stuporous or delirious mania). Option (a) puts elation first, and (c) reverses the scale.

  7. Which is NOT a core symptom to diagnose as autism in children as per DSM- 5? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Communication skills (b) Unusual repetitive behavior (c) Social reciprocity (d) I.Q. is below 70

    Answer: (d) I.Q. is below 70. DSM-5 autism rests on two core domains, social communication and reciprocity, and restricted, repetitive behaviour, so (a) to (c) are core. Intellectual impairment is only a specifier (with or without).

  8. In which of the following situations would a diagnosis of "global developmental delay" be inappropriate? (RML 2022)

    (a) The patient is a child who is too young to fully manifest specific symptoms or to complete requisite assessments. (b) The patient, a 7-year-old boy, has a full-scale IQ of 65 and severe impairment in adaptive functioning. (c) The patient's scores on psychometric tests suggest intellectual disability, but there is insufficient information about the patient's adaptive functional skills. (d) The patient's impaired adaptive functioning suggests intellectual developmental disorder, but there is insufficient information about the level of cognitive impairment measured by standardized instruments.

    Answer: (b) The 7-year-old with full-scale IQ 65 and severe adaptive impairment. Global developmental delay is a provisional label for children under 5 whose severity cannot yet be reliably assessed, as in (a), (c) and (d); a testable 7-year-old who meets both criteria has intellectual disability.

  9. Within attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, which of the following is NOT classified as a problem relating to hyperactivity/impulsivity? (RML 2021)

    (a) Experiences difficulties in playing quietly. (b) Rather than sitting still, squirms in seat and fidgets. (c) Talks excessively. (d) Doesn't appear to listen when being told something or spoken to

    Answer: (d) Doesn't appear to listen when spoken to. Not seeming to listen when spoken to directly is one of the nine inattention items; not playing quietly, squirming and fidgeting, and talking excessively are hyperactivity-impulsivity items.

  10. "First rank symptoms" of schizophrenia given by Schneider include all except (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Depersonalization (b) Hallucinatory voice in the form of running commentary (c) Delusional perception (d) Thought broadcasting

    Answer: (a) Depersonalization. Running-commentary voices, delusional perception and thought broadcasting are all on Kurt Schneider's list; depersonalisation, like loose associations and delusions of self-reference, is not a first-rank symptom.

  11. The ICD-10 duration criterion for schizophrenia requires symptoms to persist for at least: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) 6 months (b) 2 weeks (c) 3 months (d) 1 month

    Answer: (d) 1 month. ICD-10 and ICD-11 require 1 month. Six months is the DSM-5-TR total (including 1 month of active-phase symptoms); 2 weeks is the onset window of acute and transient psychotic disorders.

  12. High EE (Expressed Emotion) is known to cause: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Poor treatment outcome (b) Relapse of psychotic illness (c) Negative symptoms of schizophrenia (d) Violent behavior in schizophrenic patients

    Answer: (b) Relapse of psychotic illness. Brown, Birley and Wing (1972) and Vaughn and Leff (1976) showed that critical, hostile or over-involved relatives predict relapse after discharge; EE does not cause the illness, its negative symptoms or violence.

  13. Which of the following statements concerning Bipolar disorder is INCORRECT? (RML 2022)

    (a) Bipolar-I Disorder includes mania (b) Bipolar-II Disorder includes hypomania (c) Bipolar-I Disorder includes hypomania (d) Bipolar-I Disorder includes periods of depression even if these do not reach the threshold for major depressive disorder

    Answer: (c) Bipolar-I Disorder includes hypomania. Bipolar I is defined by at least one manic episode; hypomania is the defining high of bipolar II and is optional in bipolar I. Bipolar I may include depressive periods, even below the major depressive threshold, so (a), (b) and (d) are correct statements.

  14. A 12-year-old boy begins to have new episodes of temper outbursts that are out of proportion to the situation. Which of the following is NOT a diagnostic possibility in this patient? (RML 2022)

    (a) Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (b) Bipolar Disorder (c) Conduct Disorder (d) Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder

    Answer: (a) Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder. DMDD requires onset before age 10 (diagnosis between 6 and 18), so outbursts that begin at 12 rule it out. Bipolar disorder, conduct disorder and ADHD can all begin or present at this age.

  15. Suicidal risk is assessed to be highest when the following criteria are met in comparison to others, the 'time' factor being the same for all (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) High lethality, specific plan, frequent suicidal ideas, family history (b) Disrupted biological functioning, no recent attempt, no suicidal ideation (c) Adequate biological functioning, no previous attempt, moderate intent (d) Frequent suicidal ideation, recent suicide attempt, low lethality, absence of family history

    Answer: (a). Risk is highest when several markers converge: a highly lethal method, a specific plan, frequent ideation and a family history of suicide. Options (b) and (c) lack ideation or attempts, and (d) has low lethality and no family history.

  16. The following defense mechanisms are used in obsessive-compulsive disorder: (RML 2024)

    (a) Isolation (b) Undoing (c) Reaction formation (d) Displacement

    Answer: (b) Undoing. Isolation, undoing and reaction formation are all classic OCD defences, but undoing, an act that symbolically cancels a forbidden thought, is the most characteristic and is the key. Displacement is the defence of phobias (Little Hans).

  17. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) is a pervasive condition in which the sufferer experiences: (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Continued apprehension about unspecified things (b) Fear of fear (c) Continual flashbacks to past events (d) A desire to check that the environment is safe

    Answer: (a) Continued apprehension about unspecified things. GAD is excessive, hard-to-control, free-floating worry about many matters for at least 6 months. Fear of fear marks panic disorder and agoraphobia, flashbacks PTSD and checking OCD.

  18. A client with OCD washes their hands excessively to neutralize the anxiety caused by intrusive thoughts of contamination. This hand-washing behavior is BEST conceptualized as a(n): (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Conditioned stimulus (b) Unconditioned response (c) Avoidance response / Safety behavior (d) Operant behavior maintained by positive reinforcement

    Answer: (c) Avoidance response / Safety behavior. In Mowrer's two-factor account the washing removes anxiety, so it is negatively reinforced. The contamination thought is the conditioned stimulus, an unconditioned response is an unlearned reflex, and nothing pleasant is added, so it is not positive reinforcement.

  19. The term 'la Bella indifference' seen in hysteria was first used by: (RML 2024)

    (a) Sigmund Freud (b) Anna Freud (c) Pierre Janet (d) Hippocrates

    Answer: (c) Pierre Janet. The phrase for the calm unconcern of some conversion patients is usually credited to Janet, who explained hysteria as dissociation. Freud explained hysteria by repression and conversion, Anna Freud catalogued the defences, and Hippocrates blamed a wandering womb.

  20. When an individual has dissociative amnesia it is usually associated with several types of memory disturbances. In contrast, in generalized amnesia: (RML 2022)

    (a) There is a failure of recall of the entire past week (b) There is a failure of recall that encompasses the person's entire life (c) There is a failure of recall that encompasses the person's childhood (d) There is a failure of recall for traumatic events

    Answer: (b) Failure of recall that encompasses the person's entire life. Generalised amnesia covers the whole life history, often with identity. Loss of a set period such as a week is localised amnesia, loss of one category of information is systematised, and loss for traumatic events describes dissociative amnesia in general.

  21. The difference between malingering and factitious disorder lies in: (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) The presence or absence of external rewards (b) Whether the individual believes the symptoms are real (c) The severity of symptoms (d) Age of onset

    Answer: (a) The presence or absence of external rewards. Both deliberately falsify symptoms and know they are false; the malingerer seeks an external incentive such as money or escape from duty, whereas factitious disorder persists without obvious external reward, to occupy the sick role.

  22. Unlike people with anorexia, individuals with bulimia (RML 2022)

    (a) Often purge themselves to stay thin (b) Are obsessed with being too fat (c) Can do damage to their health (d) May appear normal in weight

    Answer: (d) May appear normal in weight. Significantly low body weight is a criterion of anorexia nervosa; people with bulimia are usually of normal weight or overweight. Purging (the binge-eating/purging type of anorexia), fear of fatness and damage to health occur in both disorders, so they do not distinguish them.

  23. In narcolepsy, which of the following is most characteristically present? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Both cataplexy and catalepsy equally (b) Cataplexy, sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotions (c) Neither cataplexy nor catalepsy (d) Catalepsy, maintenance of imposed posture with waxy flexibility

    Answer: (b) Cataplexy. Narcolepsy type 1 shows brief, sudden, bilateral loss of muscle tone with preserved consciousness, triggered by laughter or joking, from loss of hypocretin (orexin) neurons. Catalepsy, a held posture with waxy flexibility, belongs to catatonia.

  24. Lack of bowel control in children is known as: (RML 2024)

    (a) Encopresis (b) Analism (c) Enuresis (d) Anorexia

    Answer: (a) Encopresis. Encopresis is repeated passage of faeces into inappropriate places at least once a month for 3 months from age 4. Enuresis is the urinary counterpart (from age 5), anorexia is an eating disorder, and analism is not a recognised elimination disorder.

  25. Premature ejaculation is most commonly associated with: (RML 2024)

    (a) Diabetes mellitus (b) Masturbation (c) Personality disorder (d) Anxiety state

    Answer: (d) Anxiety state. Premature ejaculation is mostly psychogenic and most tied to performance anxiety; it is commoner in men with anxiety disorders. Masturbation does not cause it (a myth), diabetes is the classic link of erectile disorder, and personality disorder has no special association.

  26. Autogynephilia is defined by: (RML 2022)

    (a) Recurrent, and intense sexual attraction to prepubescent children (b) Recurrent, and intense sexual attraction to thoughts, images, or thoughts about oneself as a woman (c) Recurrent, and intense sexual attraction to oneself (d) Recurrent, and intense sexual attraction to being humiliated

    Answer: (b) Attraction to thoughts or images of oneself as a woman. Ray Blanchard coined the term in 1989, and DSM-5-TR uses it as a specifier of transvestic disorder. Attraction to prepubescent children is pedophilia and to being humiliated is masochism; mere attraction to oneself is not autogynephilia.

  27. When gender incongruence moved out of the mental disorders chapter in ICD-11, which of these stayed in Chapter 6 (Mental, behavioural or neurodevelopmental disorders)? (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    (a) Sexual dysfunctions (b) Paraphilic disorders (c) Sexual pain disorders (d) Gender incongruence of childhood

    Answer: (b) Paraphilic disorders. ICD-11 moved gender incongruence (of childhood as well as of adolescence or adulthood), the sexual dysfunctions and the sexual pain disorders into Chapter 17, Conditions related to sexual health; only the paraphilic disorders, now centred on non-consent, stayed in Chapter 6.

  28. A patient presents with severe memory deficits, confabulation, and nystagmus, with a history of chronic alcohol use. This clinical picture is classic for: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Alzheimer's Disease (b) Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome (c) Vascular Dementia (d) Frontotemporal Dementia

    Answer: (b) Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome. Thiamine deficiency in chronic drinkers damages the mammillary bodies and dorsomedial thalamus: nystagmus belongs to Wernicke's encephalopathy, amnesia with confabulation to Korsakoff's syndrome. Alzheimer's, vascular and frontotemporal dementia are not caused by alcohol and do not produce this triad.

  29. Which of the following substances is NOT a natural alkaloid compound? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Morphine (b) Heroin (c) Codeine (d) Papaverine

    Answer: (b) Heroin. Heroin (diacetylmorphine) is made by chemically modifying morphine, so it is semi-synthetic. Morphine, codeine and papaverine all occur naturally in opium.

  30. A key of distinguishing feature of dementia vs. delirium is : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Delusions in dementia but not delirium (b) Onset gradual in delirium vs. acute in dementia (c) Hallucinations in dementia but not delirium (d) Impaired memory in dementia vs. fluctuating consciousness in delirium

    Answer: (d) Impaired memory in dementia, fluctuating consciousness in delirium. Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness; dementia is an insidious decline in memory and cognition in clear consciousness. Delusions and hallucinations occur in both, and option (b) reverses the two onsets.

  31. Borderline Personality Disorder is most characteristically associated with which primary defense mechanism? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Repression (b) Splitting (c) Sublimation (d) Rationalization

    Answer: (b) Splitting. Splitting, seeing self and others as all good or all bad, is the primitive defence Kernberg placed at the centre of borderline personality organisation. Repression is the classic neurotic defence, sublimation a mature one and rationalisation a plausible excuse; none defines BPD.

  32. Identify the 'odd one out' from the groups: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Paranoid personality disorder (b) Schizoid personality disorder (c) Schizotypal personality disorder (d) Borderline personality disorder

    Answer: (d) Borderline personality disorder. Paranoid, schizoid and schizotypal form Cluster A (odd or eccentric); borderline belongs to Cluster B (dramatic, emotional or erratic).

  33. A client reports a persistent pattern of seeking admiration, but their behavior is characterized by excessive emotionality, attention-seeking, and seductive behavior, rather than grandiosity. This presentation is more characteristic of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Narcissistic Personality Disorder (b) Histrionic Personality Disorder (c) Borderline Personality Disorder (d) Dependent Personality Disorder

    Answer: (b) Histrionic Personality Disorder. Excessive emotionality, attention seeking and seductive behaviour define histrionic PD. Narcissistic PD also seeks admiration but rests on grandiosity and lack of empathy; borderline centres on instability and fear of abandonment; dependent on a need to be taken care of.
