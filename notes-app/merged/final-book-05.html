Graz, Austria, 1921. In the night before Easter Sunday the pharmacologist Otto Loewi woke with an idea, switched on the light and jotted a few notes on a slip of thin paper. At six in the morning he knew he had written down something important, but he could not read his own scrawl. The question behind it was an old one. Physiologists knew that stimulating the vagus nerve slows the heart, but how does a nerve tell a muscle what to do? Most assumed the message simply crossed as electricity. Loewi had long wondered whether the nerve might instead release a chemical, but he had never found a way to test it. The next night, at three o'clock, the idea came back. This time he got up, went straight to his laboratory and ran the experiment.

The design was simple enough to fit on that slip of paper. Loewi kept two frog hearts beating in small chambers of salt solution, the first with its vagus nerve attached. He stimulated the vagus, and the first heart slowed. Then he drew off the fluid that had bathed it and gave it to the second heart, which had been isolated without its nerves. The second heart slowed too. Something had passed from the nerve endings into the fluid, and that something, not an electric current, had carried the message. Loewi called it Vagusstoff, 'vagus substance'. It was later identified as acetylcholine, and in 1936 Loewi shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with the British scientist Henry Dale for discoveries about the chemical transmission of nerve impulses.

Loewi's two frog hearts hold this chapter in miniature. A change in what an organ does had a physical cause that could be isolated, carried from one heart to another and named. Physiological psychology studies behaviour in that spirit: through the nerves that carry messages, the cells and chemicals that pass them on, the drugs that interfere with them, and the brain systems behind sensation, movement, emotion, sleep, memory and language.

5.1 Biopsychology and the Organisation of the Nervous SystemLower yield · 21 Qs

Loewi's second frog heart slowed because a nerve had released a chemical. To see how such simple events add up to fear, memory and speech, you need a point of view that explains behaviour through the body, and a map of the machinery. This section supplies both; the structures inside the brain follow in section 5.2.

The biological perspective

The biological perspective, also called the neurobiological approach, holds that behaviour and mental processes are produced by the body: the brain and nervous system, hormones and genes. Every psychological event, from a memory to a mood, is assumed to have a physical basis in neural activity, so changing the body with a drug, a lesion or a hormone changes the mind.

Three neighbouring approaches (Chapter 1) sharpen the contrast. The psychophysical approach relates the physical intensity of a stimulus to the sensation it produces; the cognitive approach explains behaviour by mental processes such as perceiving, remembering and thinking; the neo-behaviouristic approach explains it by learned stimulus-response connections, with intervening variables such as habit strength inside the organism. The science built on this view, biopsychology, owes its emergence largely to Donald Hebb's The Organization of Behavior (1949), the first comprehensive theory of how thought and memory could arise from brain activity (section 5.9).

Exam trap

Asked which system the biological perspective links behaviour to, answer the body: the mind is the cognitive answer, the soul a dualist one and the unconscious the psychodynamic one. Behaviour "governed by the brain and nervous system" names the neurobiological approach.

Biopsychology as a neuroscience

Biopsychology is the scientific study of the biology of behaviour. For John P. J. Pinel and Steven J. Barnes (Biopsychology, 11th edition, 2021) it is a neuroscience: biopsychologists bring expertise in behaviour to what neuroanatomy, neurochemistry and neurophysiology discover. Their research falls into six divisions (Table 5.1).

Biopsychologists study both humans, who can report what they experience, and nonhuman animals, whose simpler brains allow comparison across species and experiments that would be unethical in people. In an experiment the researcher assigns subjects to conditions and varies one independent variable, so only an experiment shows cause. A quasi-experimental study compares groups already exposed to a condition in real life, such as heavy drinkers and abstainers, so confounding variables remain. A case study examines one person in depth (Chapter 9).

Key study: converging operations and Korsakoff's syndrome

Because each method has weaknesses, progress comes from converging operations: aiming several approaches at one problem so that each covers the others' weaknesses. Korsakoff's amnesia (section 5.9) was first blamed on alcohol from quasi-experiments with heavy drinkers; case studies of malnourished people who drank little, and experiments in which thiamine-deficient rats developed similar memory loss, then pointed to thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency.

Behind all this lies the mind-brain problem. René Descartes (1596 to 1650) proposed dualism: the body, brain included, is a machine obeying natural laws, while a nonphysical mind controls it, the two meeting, he suggested, at the pineal gland. Monism holds that only one kind of substance exists; its materialist form, the working view of biopsychology, treats mental events as brain activity.

The divisions of the nervous system

The nervous system is a system of twos (Figure 5.1). The central nervous system (CNS), the brain and spinal cord, lies inside the skull and spine; the peripheral nervous system (PNS) is all the nerves outside them. The PNS has two divisions. The somatic nervous system deals with the outside world: its afferent nerves carry sensory signals from skin, eyes and ears to the CNS, and its efferent nerves carry commands to the skeletal muscles, which are under voluntary control (afferent arrives, efferent exits). The autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulates the internal environment, controlling smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands largely involuntarily.

Exam trap

The parts of the ANS are the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions. "Central and peripheral" divide the whole nervous system, "voluntary and involuntary" describe somatic versus autonomic, and "cerebrospinal and cortical" is no division at all. The brain and spinal cord form the CNS.

The autonomic nervous system

Each division of the ANS reaches its targets through two neurons: a preganglionic neuron from the CNS synapses in an autonomic ganglion on a postganglionic neuron that runs to the organ.

The sympathetic division mobilises and spends energy. Walter Bradford Cannon (1871 to 1945) described its emergency role in 1915 as the fight-or-flight response: the heart races, pupils and airways widen, blood shifts to the muscles and the liver releases glucose. Its preganglionic neurons leave spinal segments T1 to L2, the thoracolumbar outflow; their short fibres end mostly in the sympathetic chain, a string of ganglia beside the spine, from which long postganglionic fibres run to the organs. Acetylcholine is the transmitter at the ganglia, noradrenaline (norepinephrine) at most targets. Some preganglionic fibres go straight to the adrenal medulla, the core of the adrenal gland, which acts like a sympathetic ganglion and releases adrenaline (epinephrine) and some noradrenaline into the blood. This sympathetic adrenal-medullary system is the fast arm of the stress response.

The parasympathetic division serves rest and digest: it slows the heart, narrows the pupils and promotes digestion, conserving and storing energy. Its preganglionic neurons leave the brainstem in cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X and the spinal cord at S2 to S4, the craniosacral outflow. Their long fibres end in ganglia near or inside the organ, and both synapses use acetylcholine. The vagus nerve (X) carries most of these fibres, supplying the heart, lungs and most of the gut.

Most organs receive both divisions (dual antagonistic innervation), so your heart rate reflects the balance of sympathetic acceleration and vagal braking; sweat glands and most blood vessels are sympathetic only, and in sexual response the two cooperate (Table 5.2). The enteric nervous system, a network of neurons in the gut wall, runs local reflexes of gut movement and secretion on its own.

Exam trap

Fight or flight is sympathetic; the fullest answer is "sympathetic nervous system plus adrenal medulla". Rest and digest and conserving energy are parasympathetic; allocating energy for expenditure is sympathetic. A stem naming the vagus and acetylcholine means parasympathetic, not the sympathetic adrenal-medullary system. The enteric system cannot slow the heart or narrow the pupils, and the pituitary-thyroid axis is endocrine, setting metabolic rate rather than the emergency response (section 5.7).

Grey matter, white matter and the spinal cord

Slice fresh brain or spinal cord and you see two tissues. Grey matter is mainly neuron cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons and synapses, where information is processed. White matter is mainly myelinated axons, the wiring between areas; myelin is a fatty insulating sheath (section 5.3), and its fat makes the tissue white. A cluster of cell bodies is a nucleus in the CNS but a ganglion in the PNS; a bundle of axons is a tract in the CNS but a nerve in the PNS. (The basal ganglia, despite the name, are CNS nuclei.)

In the spinal cord (Figure 5.2) a butterfly-shaped core of grey matter, with dorsal and ventral horns, is surrounded by white columns of ascending and descending tracts: the reverse of the cerebral cortex, which is grey outside. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves attach to the cord: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal. Each joins it by two roots. The dorsal root is purely sensory, its cell bodies sitting just outside the cord in the dorsal root ganglion; the ventral root is purely motor, its cell bodies lying in the ventral horn. This is the Bell-Magendie law, named after Charles Bell (1774 to 1842), who reported the motor role of the ventral roots in 1811, and François Magendie (1783 to 1855), who demonstrated both halves of the law in 1822.

Exam trap

White matter is myelinated axons; cell bodies and unmyelinated axons are grey, and dendrites are never myelinated. Cell bodies in the PNS form ganglia (nuclei in the CNS); tracts and nerves are bundles of axons.

Neurons by function and the reflex arc

Neurons fall into three functional classes. Sensory (afferent) neurons carry signals from receptors to the CNS. A motor neuron (efferent) carries commands from the brain and spinal cord to muscles and glands. An interneuron (connector or association neuron), by far the commonest kind, lies wholly within the CNS and links the two. Muscles and glands are effectors: targets, not neurons.

Together they form the reflex arc of an automatic response. Touch a hot pan and the signal runs: receptor in the skin, afferent neuron through the dorsal root, interneuron in the spinal grey matter, efferent neuron through the ventral root, effector muscle that pulls your hand away (Figure 5.2). The arc closes in the spinal cord, so you withdraw before you feel the pain. This withdrawal reflex is polysynaptic. The knee-jerk, a stretch reflex, is monosynaptic: the sensory neuron synapses directly on the motor neuron, with no interneuron.

Exam trap

The order is afferent neuron, interneuron, efferent neuron; no arc runs afferent to afferent or starts with an efferent. A cell carrying information from the brain to muscles and glands is a motor neuron: "effector cell" names the target and "connector neuron" the interneuron.

The twelve cranial nerves

Twelve pairs of cranial nerves leave the brain directly, numbered from front to back. They serve mainly the head and neck, except the "wandering" vagus, which reaches the chest and abdomen (Table 5.3). Three are sensory (I, II, VIII), five are motor (III, IV, VI, XI, XII) and four are mixed (V, VII, IX, X). Four carry parasympathetic fibres: III (pupil), VII (tears, saliva), IX (saliva) and X (heart, lungs, gut).

Protecting the CNS

The CNS is encased in bone and wrapped in three meninges: from outside in, the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid mater and the thin pia mater, which clings to the brain's surface. Between arachnoid and pia lies the subarachnoid space.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), made by the choroid plexus (networks of capillaries in the ventricles), fills the brain's four ventricles, the central canal of the spinal cord and the subarachnoid space. It flows from the two lateral ventricles, the largest, to the third ventricle, through the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth, and out into the subarachnoid space, and is absorbed through the arachnoid granulations into the venous sinuses of the dura. CSF cushions the brain, which floats in it. If its flow is blocked, for example at the aqueduct, the ventricles swell and compress the brain: hydrocephalus, treated with a draining shunt.

The blood-brain barrier exists because the endothelial cells of brain capillaries are sealed together by tight junctions, supported by the end-feet of astrocytes (star-shaped glial cells). Small fat-soluble molecules such as oxygen, alcohol and nicotine pass; glucose is carried across by active transport; large or water-soluble molecules, many toxins and many drugs are kept out (section 5.4). Weak spots include the area postrema in the floor of the fourth ventricle, which detects poisons in the blood and triggers vomiting.

Exam trap

The barrier is tight junctions plus astrocyte end-feet: not the pia mater (a meninx), microglia (immune scavengers) or oligodendrocyte myelin (around axons). CSF is made by the choroid plexus and absorbed by the arachnoid granulations, and the ventricles hold CSF, not blood, myelin or "synaptic fluid".

Directions and planes

Anterior (rostral) means toward the nose, posterior (caudal) toward the tail. Dorsal means toward the back or the top of the head, ventral toward the belly or the bottom of the head; because humans stand upright, this axis bends at the brainstem, so dorsal is up in the brain but back in the spinal cord. Medial means toward the midline, lateral away from it. Ipsilateral means on the same side, contralateral on the opposite side: each hemisphere controls the contralateral hand. A horizontal section is parallel to the ground, a frontal (coronal) section parallel to the face and a sagittal section parallel to the midline; the midsagittal section runs exactly between the hemispheres.

5.2 The Brain: Brainstem, Diencephalon and Cerebral CortexMedium yield · 27 Qs

A lesion the size of a pea can stop your breathing in one place and merely blur a corner of your vision in another. Location is everything. This section tours the brain from the bottom up: the plan that names its five divisions, the hindbrain and midbrain that keep you alive and awake, the diencephalon that relays and regulates, and the cortex that perceives, plans and thinks.

Five divisions from three swellings

The brain's tongue-twisting names come from its development. The embryonic nervous system begins as a fluid-filled neural tube, and three swellings appear at its front end: the forebrain (prosencephalon), midbrain (mesencephalon) and hindbrain (rhombencephalon). Before birth the forebrain divides into the telencephalon and diencephalon, and the hindbrain into the metencephalon and myelencephalon, giving the five divisions of Table 5.4. A memory aid: the telencephalon is on top, and the other four lie below it in alphabetical order.

Exam trap

The corpus callosum lies just above the thalamus, so it is often filed under the diencephalon. It is the white matter of the cerebral hemispheres, so it belongs to the telencephalon. The diencephalon is the thalamus and hypothalamus, the mesencephalon the midbrain, the metencephalon the pons and cerebellum.

The hindbrain: medulla, pons and cerebellum

The medulla oblongata is where the spinal cord widens into the brain. Much of it is tracts: on its front surface two ridges, the pyramids, carry the corticospinal motor tract, which crosses sides at the lower medulla (Section 5.6). It also holds the vital centres: the cardiovascular centre for heart rate and blood pressure, the respiratory centre that generates the rhythm of breathing, and reflex centres for swallowing, coughing, sneezing and vomiting. So medullary damage is the brain injury most likely to kill, and an overdose of alcohol or opioids kills by silencing its respiratory centre.

Above it, the pons (Latin "bridge") bulges from the front of the brainstem; its nuclei relay signals from the cortex across to the cerebellum. It also helps set the rhythm of breathing, and its reticular nuclei contribute to arousal, sleep and REM sleep (Section 5.8).

Behind the pons sits the cerebellum ("little brain"): about 10 percent of the brain's mass but more than half its neurons. It does not start movements; the primary motor cortex does. It makes them accurate, comparing the intended movement with the actual one and correcting the error. So it coordinates and smooths movement, keeps balance and posture, times rapid sequences and supports motor learning, such as the practised timing of a cricket stroke. Damage produces ataxia: clumsy, uncoordinated movement and a wide, staggering gait, like drunkenness. It does not produce language (Broca's area), store declarative memories (hippocampus and cortex) or regulate emotion (amygdala); its circuitry is in Section 5.6.

Exam trap

Heartbeat and respiration are modulated by the hindbrain (medulla and pons). The hypothalamus adjusts them for homeostasis but does not pace them; the amygdala is for fear; the cerebellum, though in the hindbrain, does not keep you breathing. Damage most likely to cause death: the medulla, not the pons, cerebellum or reticular formation (whose damage causes coma).

The midbrain and the reticular activating system

Two words organise the midbrain. The tectum ("roof") is its back surface, with two pairs of bumps: the superior colliculi turn your eyes and head towards a sudden flash (visual orienting), and the inferior colliculi relay hearing (superior for sight, inferior for sound). The tegmentum beneath holds the substantia nigra, whose dopamine neurons feed the basal ganglia and die in Parkinson's disease; the red nucleus, a motor nucleus; the periaqueductal grey, which suppresses pain and mediates opioid analgesia; and the ventral tegmental area, source of the dopamine reward pathway (Section 5.4). On the front, the cerebral peduncles are thick stalks of axons descending from the cortex.

Through the core of the whole brainstem, from medulla to midbrain, runs the reticular formation, a net-like web of about 100 small nuclei (reticulum, "little net"). Its ascending part, the reticular activating system (RAS), projects through the thalamus and directly to the cortex. It regulates arousal, wakefulness, attention and consciousness, filtering stimulation so that a parent sleeps through traffic yet wakes at a baby's cry. Destroy it and the result is coma. Its descending parts help control muscle tone and cardiac and respiratory reflexes.

Key study: Moruzzi and Magoun (1949)

Giuseppe Moruzzi and Horace Magoun stimulated the reticular formation of sleeping cats electrically. The cats woke, and their EEG changed from the slow, synchronised waves of sleep to the fast, desynchronised pattern of alertness. With brainstem transection and lesion findings, this led them to propose that low reticular activity produces sleep and high activity wakefulness. Later work showed that sleep is also actively generated elsewhere (Section 5.8).

Exam trap

RAS questions offer its neighbours. Fine motor coordination, balance and posture belong to the cerebellum, emotional memory to the amygdala, episodic memory to the hippocampus, motor planning to premotor and prefrontal cortex, reading comprehension to Wernicke's area and the angular gyrus. The RAS means arousal, wakefulness, attention and consciousness.

The diencephalon: thalamus and hypothalamus

The thalamus is the large two-lobed structure at the top of the brainstem, one lobe on each side of the third ventricle. It is the brain's relay station: every sense except smell synapses in a thalamic nucleus before reaching cortex. The lateral geniculate nucleus relays vision, the medial geniculate nucleus hearing (lateral for light, medial for music), and the ventral posterior nucleus touch, pain, temperature, body position and taste. Smell goes from the olfactory bulb directly to olfactory cortex. Nor is the thalamus a passive switchboard: each relay nucleus receives feedback from its cortex, and other nuclei relay motor loops (basal ganglia and cerebellum to motor cortex) and association loops (to prefrontal cortex).

Just below the front of the thalamus (hypo, "below") lies the almond-sized hypothalamus, master regulator of homeostasis, the maintenance of a stable internal environment. It controls hunger, thirst, body temperature, the sleep-wake cycle, sexual behaviour, and emotion and aggression, acting through the autonomic nervous system, through motivated behaviour and through the endocrine system. The pituitary gland hangs from its underside, and the hypothalamus directs it, with releasing hormones carried in local blood vessels to the anterior pituitary and axons to the posterior pituitary (Section 5.7). The pituitary is the "master gland", but the hypothalamus is its master: the control centre integrating the endocrine, autonomic and visceral systems.

Its nuclei are named here and taught later: the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the circadian clock above the optic chiasm; the lateral and ventromedial areas and arcuate nucleus in feeding (both Section 5.8); the preoptic area in temperature and sexual behaviour; and the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, which make oxytocin and vasopressin (Section 5.7). On its underside sit the two mammillary bodies, part of the limbic memory circuit and damaged in Korsakoff's syndrome (Section 5.9). Behind the thalamus, the epithalamus holds the pineal gland, which secretes melatonin at night.

Exam trap

"Relays every sense except smell" is the thalamus. "Homeostasis", "master regulator", "controls the pituitary" and "integrates the endocrine, autonomic and visceral systems" are the hypothalamus. The brainstem runs autonomic reflexes without integrating them with hormones; the spinal cord carries messages and simple reflexes; the pineal makes melatonin; the corpus callosum, basal ganglia and hippocampus relay no senses.

The limbic system and basal ganglia

Beneath the cortex lie two loose groupings. The limbic system is a ring of midline structures circling the thalamus (limbus, "border"): the hippocampus of the medial temporal lobe (forming new declarative memories and spatial maps), the almond-shaped amygdala in front of it (emotion, especially fear, and emotional memory), the cingulate cortex above the corpus callosum, the fornix (the main limbic tract, arching from hippocampus to mammillary bodies and septum), the septum and the mammillary bodies. Together they serve emotion, motivation and memory (Sections 5.7 and 5.9).

The basal ganglia are large nuclei deep in each hemisphere. The tail-like caudate nucleus and the putamen receive cortical input; both look striped, so together they are the striatum. Its output goes to the globus pallidus ("pale globe"). The subthalamic nucleus and the substantia nigra, whose dopamine input to the striatum fails in Parkinson's disease, are functional partners; older texts also counted the amygdala. The basal ganglia select and initiate voluntary movements, build habits and support procedural learning (pathways in Section 5.6).

Exam trap

"Which is NOT a basal ganglia structure?" The red nucleus, a motor nucleus of the midbrain tegmentum (source of the rubrospinal tract). The caudate and globus pallidus are core members; the subthalamic nucleus belongs to the circuit.

The cerebral cortex: layers and landmarks

The cerebral cortex ("bark") is a sheet of grey matter a few millimetres thick over the hemispheres, with white matter (myelinated axons) beneath. It is crumpled into ridges, gyri, and grooves, sulci; the deepest grooves are fissures. The longitudinal fissure separates the hemispheres. The central sulcus separates frontal from parietal lobe, with the precentral gyrus (primary motor cortex) in front and the postcentral gyrus (primary somatosensory cortex) behind. The lateral fissure (Sylvian fissure) separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes. The calcarine sulcus, on the medial surface of the occipital lobe, is lined by primary visual cortex (Figure 5.4).

About 90 percent of human cortex is neocortex ("new cortex", or isocortex), the most recently evolved part, with six layers numbered I to VI from the surface. Large pyramidal cells send long axons out of the area; small stellate cells are interneurons. Layer IV receives thalamic input, so it is thick in sensory cortex; layer V holds the large pyramidal output cells projecting to brainstem and spinal cord, so it is thick in motor cortex, where it contains the giant Betz cells. The neocortex is the seat of higher cognitive functions: perception, language, reasoning, planning and voluntary movement. The older allocortex has fewer layers: the hippocampus (three) and olfactory cortex.

Exam trap

The neocortex means higher cognitive functions. Basic reflexes belong to the spinal cord and brainstem, olfactory processing to the old olfactory cortex, autonomic regulation to the hypothalamus and medulla.

The lobes, association cortex and Brodmann's map

Fibre systems: wiring the parts together

The white matter comes in three kinds. Commissural fibres cross the midline to connect the hemispheres. The largest commissure is the corpus callosum, an estimated 200 million axons linking the left and right hemispheres; the smaller anterior commissure links the temporal lobes and olfactory regions. Association fibres stay within one hemisphere: short U-fibres join neighbouring gyri, and long bundles such as the arcuate fasciculus link the frontal and temporal lobes (Broca's and Wernicke's areas). Projection fibres connect cortex with lower centres in both directions, from thalamus to cortex and from cortex to brainstem and spinal cord. They funnel through the internal capsule between the thalamus and basal ganglia, so a small stroke there can paralyse the opposite side of the body. Three pairs of cerebellar peduncles link the cerebellum to the brainstem. Cutting the corpus callosum produces the split brain (Section 5.10).

5.3 Neurons, Neural Conduction, Synapses and NeurotransmittersMedium yield · 45 Qs

Every word you read here is carried by cells that work like tiny batteries and talk in chemical whispers. This section follows one message from start to finish: how a neuron is built, how it holds a charge at rest, how that charge flips into an action potential that races down the axon, and how chemicals carry the signal across the synapse.

The neuron: the basic unit

In the early 1870s the Italian physician Camillo Golgi (1843 to 1926) found that silver chromate stains a few neurons in a slice completely black, showing single cells in silhouette for the first time: the Golgi stain. Golgi held that the nervous system is one continuous net of fused fibres, the reticular theory. The Spanish histologist Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1852 to 1934), using Golgi's own stain, showed that it is built of separate cells that touch but do not fuse: the neuron doctrine. The rivals shared the Nobel Prize in 1906, and Cajal was right. The neuron is the basic structural and functional unit of the nervous system; a dendrite is only part of one, the brain is an organ made of billions of them, and glial cells support neurons rather than carry their messages.

Figure 5.5 shows the parts. The cell body (soma) holds the nucleus and keeps the cell alive. Branching dendrites, studded with tiny dendritic spines, receive most incoming synapses. The cone-shaped axon hillock joins the soma to the axon, the long fibre that carries signals away from the soma; action potentials begin here (strictly, Pinel notes, in the axon initial segment just beyond it). A fatty myelin sheath wraps many axons, broken at intervals by bare gaps, the nodes of Ranvier. The axon ends in fine branches, the telodendria, each tipped by an axon terminal (terminal button) whose synaptic vesicles store and release the transmitter.

A multipolar neuron (one axon, many dendrites) is by far the commonest type and includes motor neurons. A bipolar neuron has two processes, one at each end, as in the retina. A unipolar neuron has a single process that splits in two, as in most somatosensory neurons.

Exam trap

Transmitter is released from the axon terminals. The telodendria are the branches leading to them, the axon hillock is where the spike starts, and myelin only insulates. The basic unit is the neuron, not the dendrite, the brain or the glial cell.

The resting membrane potential

Push a fine electrode into a resting neuron and its inside reads about 70 millivolts more negative than the outside: the resting membrane potential, about -70 mV (range about -60 to -80 mV). The neuron is polarised because ions are unevenly spread: sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl-) ions are concentrated outside, potassium (K+) ions and negatively charged proteins inside.

Two forces act on each ion: the concentration gradient, from crowded to scarce, and electrostatic pressure, as like charges repel and opposite charges attract. Both push Na+ inwards, yet little enters, because at rest the membrane is far more permeable to K+ than to Na+: K+ channels are open, Na+ channels closed. K+ leaks out until the negative charge inside holds the rest back. The sodium-potassium pump, powered by ATP, exchanges three Na+ out for every two K+ in, preserving the gradients against the leak. The Nernst equation gives one ion's equilibrium potential; the Goldman equation combines several ions.

Key study: Hodgkin and Huxley's squid giant axon (1952)

Alan Hodgkin (1914 to 1998) and Andrew Huxley (1917 to 2012) recorded inside the giant axon of the squid, up to 1 mm across. In 1952 they described the action potential as a timed rise in Na+ and then K+ permeability, in equations that won a Nobel Prize in 1963. Criticism: the large, simple squid axon fits many mammalian neurons only roughly.

Exam trap

The resting potential is about -70 mV, inside negative: not -40 mV and never positive. It rests on high K+ permeability (not equal Na+ and K+ permeability) plus the pump. Open Na+ channels, Ca2+ influx and Cl- channels are distractors.

Postsynaptic potentials and the action potential

Transmitter binding nudges a neuron's membrane potential. A depolarisation (say from -70 to -67 mV) makes firing more likely and is an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP); a hyperpolarisation (to -72 mV) makes it less likely and is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP). Both are graded potentials, sized in proportion to the input, and they spread fast but decrementally, fading like ripples on a pond.

Whether a neuron fires depends on the sum of thousands of PSPs reaching the axon initial segment, a process called integration. In spatial summation, PSPs arriving together from different synapses add up (an EPSP and an IPSP cancel); in temporal summation, PSPs arriving in quick succession at one synapse add up. If the total reaches the threshold of excitation, about -65 mV in Pinel (many texts say -55 mV), an action potential follows.

The action potential (AP, spike) is a massive reversal of the membrane potential lasting about 1 millisecond (Figure 5.6). At threshold, voltage-gated Na+ channels open and Na+ rushes in, driving the membrane from -70 to about +50 mV. The Na+ channels then close and inactivate, while voltage-gated K+ channels (the delayed rectifiers) let K+ out, repolarising the membrane. They close slowly, so the membrane is briefly hyperpolarised before it returns to rest.

Unlike PSPs, action potentials obey the all-or-none law: once threshold is reached, the spike fires at full, constant size whatever the stimulus strength; below threshold, nothing fires. Squeeze a gun's trigger hard or gently and the bullet leaves at the same speed. A strong stimulus is signalled instead by firing more often: intensity is coded by frequency, the rate law.

Exam trap

Only action potentials obey the all-or-none law. EPSPs and IPSPs are graded, and the resting potential is a steady baseline that does not vary with the stimulus. Stronger stimuli give more spikes, not bigger ones; sub-threshold stimuli give no "miniature" spikes; and the law says nothing about neighbouring neurons firing together or one spike per second.

Refractory periods and conduction

For about 1 to 2 milliseconds after a spike begins, no stimulus, however strong, can fire another: the absolute refractory period, during which the voltage-gated Na+ channels are inactivated and cannot reopen until the membrane repolarises. In the relative refractory period that follows, K+ channels are still open and the membrane is hyperpolarised, so only a stronger than normal stimulus works. Two consequences follow. A spike cannot turn back onto the axon it has just left, so it travels one way, soma to terminals: orthodromic conduction (backward, antidromic travel is seen mainly under artificial stimulation). And firing is capped at about 1,000 spikes per second, intense stimulation refiring the cell as soon as the absolute period ends.

In an unmyelinated axon the spike is regenerated at each Na+ channel in turn: continuous conduction, which never weakens but is slow, about 1 m per second in thin axons. In a myelinated axon ions can cross only at the nodes of Ranvier, where voltage-gated Na+ channels are concentrated, so the spike is regenerated node by node and seems to jump: saltatory conduction (Latin saltare, to leap). The function of myelin is thus to increase conduction speed, up to about 100 m per second in the largest axons (Pinel cites up to 60 m per second for human motor neurons); wider axons are faster too. Multiple sclerosis destroys CNS myelin and slows or blocks conduction (Section 5.11).

Exam trap

The absolute refractory period reflects inactivated Na+ channels, not pump failure, inactivated K+ channels or Cl- influx. Myelin speeds conduction; it does not support the neuron, store transmitter or generate spikes. Saltatory conduction needs Na+ channels clustered at the nodes, not spread evenly along the axon.

How a synapse works

A synapse is the junction where one neuron passes its message to another across a narrow gap, the synaptic cleft, in four steps.

  1. Synthesis and packaging. Small-molecule transmitters are made and packed into vesicles in the terminal; neuropeptides are made in the soma and carried down the axon on microtubules.
  2. Release. The arriving action potential opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels; Ca2+ entry makes vesicles fuse with the membrane and spill their contents into the cleft: exocytosis.
  3. Binding. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.
  4. Deactivation. Reuptake or an enzyme ends the message (below).

Most synapses are axodendritic (often onto a spine) or axosomatic. Axoaxonic synapses facilitate or inhibit a single terminal button, and dendrodendritic synapses can often transmit both ways. Most are directed synapses, with release site and receptors side by side. At nondirected synapses, Pinel's string-of-beads synapses, transmitter is released from varicosities, swellings along the axon, and spreads to distant targets; most monoamine neurons work this way. Some neurons are linked by gap junctions, protein channels (connexins) joining their cytoplasm to form electrical synapses, faster than chemical ones. And one button often holds a small-molecule transmitter and a neuropeptide together: coexistence.

Exam trap

Release is triggered by Ca2+ influx, not by K+ efflux (which repolarises), closure of the pump, or Cl- influx (which makes IPSPs). Varicosities are non-directed synapses that release monoamines.

Receptors and deactivation

A transmitter acts by binding a receptor, a membrane protein shaped for it, and most transmitters have several receptor subtypes, so one chemical can send different messages to different places. An ionotropic receptor is a ligand-gated ion channel that opens at once (Na+ in gives an EPSP; Cl- in or K+ out an IPSP), so its effect is fast and brief. A metabotropic receptor is coupled to a G protein, which opens a nearby channel or triggers a second messenger such as cyclic AMP that can alter the cell's metabolism and even its genes; these receptors are more common, and their effects slower, longer lasting and more varied. Small-molecule transmitters bind both kinds, but virtually all neuropeptides bind only metabotropic receptors. Metabotropic receptors on the releasing neuron itself, autoreceptors, sense its own transmitter and cut release when levels are high.

Transmitter left in the cleft would jam the line. Reuptake by transporters, the commoner route, returns monoamines and amino acids to the button. In enzymatic degradation an enzyme breaks the transmitter apart: acetylcholine is deactivated solely by acetylcholinesterase in the cleft, and its choline is recycled. Monoamines are degraded by monoamine oxidase (MAO-A: serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine; MAO-B: dopamine and phenylethylamine) and by catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT).

Exam trap

Ionotropic receptors bind small transmitters only. The transmitter cleared exclusively by an enzyme is acetylcholine; dopamine, GABA and glutamate are taken back up. MAO never acts on GABA, glutamate, glycine or acetylcholine.

5.4 Drugs, Reward and PsychopharmacologyMedium yield · 40 Qs

Every drug in this section works through the synaptic machinery of Section 5.3: filter coffee, a peg of whisky and a lithium tablet differ only in which synapses they touch. The section runs from the principles of drug action and addiction, through recreational drugs and the reward circuit they hijack, to the drugs prescribed for schizophrenia, depression, mania and anxiety.

Psychoactive drugs and their route to the brain

A psychoactive drug is a substance that alters mood, cognition, perception or behaviour by acting on the nervous system; the term does not mean a drug-induced hallucination, a particularly active psychopath or the psychology of physical activity. Drugs are taken in four ways. Oral ingestion is easy and safe but unpredictable, because food in the stomach alters absorption. Injection (subcutaneous, intramuscular or intravenous) is fast, strong and predictable, but an intravenous overdose cannot be recalled. Inhalation through the lung capillaries (tobacco, cannabis, anaesthetics) makes the dose hard to regulate. Absorption through mucous membranes of the nose, mouth or rectum is how snorted cocaine enters.

From the blood a drug must cross the blood-brain barrier (Section 5.3), which small, fat-soluble molecules pass most easily. Most drugs are then deactivated by liver enzymes, a process called drug metabolism, whose products usually can no longer penetrate the barrier.

Agonists, antagonists and synaptic mechanisms

Drugs that facilitate the effects of a particular neurotransmitter are its agonists; drugs that reduce them are its antagonists. A drug can act at any step in a transmitter's life. Agonists may increase synthesis (L-dopa, the precursor of dopamine), increase release (amphetamine), block reuptake (cocaine, SSRIs), block degradation (MAO inhibitors) or activate receptors (nicotine, morphine). Antagonists may block synthesis, empty the vesicles so the transmitter leaks out and is destroyed (reserpine), block release, or occupy receptors without activating them, as receptor blockers do (chlorpromazine, naloxone). Presynaptic autoreceptors normally slow release, so a drug that blocks them increases release and one that activates them reduces it.

An allosteric modulator binds a site on the receptor other than the transmitter's own and changes the receptor's response to the transmitter; benzodiazepines are positive allosteric modulators of the GABA-A receptor. A partial agonist activates a receptor only partly, raising activity when the transmitter is scarce but lowering it when the transmitter is plentiful; buspirone at the serotonin 5-HT1A receptor is an example.

Tolerance, withdrawal and addiction

Drug tolerance is decreased sensitivity to a drug that develops through exposure to it: a given dose has less effect, or more drug is needed for the same effect. The dose-response curve shifts to the right (Figure 5.7). Metabolic tolerance comes from changes that reduce the amount of drug reaching its sites of action (a heavy drinker's liver clears alcohol faster); functional tolerance comes from changes that reduce the reactivity of those sites, such as fewer receptors, and accounts for most tolerance to psychoactive drugs. Exposure to one drug also brings tolerance to others that act by the same mechanism, called cross tolerance: a heavy drinker needs more of a benzodiazepine or barbiturate, because all three enhance the GABA-A receptor. Tolerance may develop to some effects but not others, and some effects may instead grow with repeated use: drug sensitisation (amphetamine's motor effects in rats).

When a drug that has been in the body for days is suddenly removed, a withdrawal syndrome follows whose effects are almost always opposite to the drug's: sleeping pills leave insomnia, alcohol leaves tremor, seizures and delirium tremens. The compensatory neural changes that produced tolerance are now unopposed. Suffering withdrawal on stopping defines physical dependence; psychological dependence is a compulsive craving for the drug's effects without bodily withdrawal. Neither defines drug addiction: habitual use that continues despite harm to health and social life and despite repeated efforts to stop.

Key study: Siegel's heroin overdose rats (1982)

Shepard Siegel treated each dose as a Pavlovian conditioning trial: the usual room and paraphernalia become conditioned stimuli that elicit compensatory responses opposite to the drug's effect, so tolerance is strongest where the drug is usually taken (conditioned drug tolerance). Siegel and colleagues (1982) made rats tolerant to heroin in one environment, then gave a large dose there or in a novel one. Many more died in the novel environment, where no compensatory responses opposed the drug, which may explain human overdoses in unfamiliar places. Criticism: some drug cues elicit drug-like rather than opposite responses.

Exam trap

Cross tolerance means exposure to one drug reduces the effect of another acting by the same mechanism; it is not "no addiction to any drug". Withdrawal is usually the opposite of the drug's effect, and physical dependence is not addiction: detoxified users still relapse.

Sedative-hypnotics: alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates

Sedatives calm and hypnotics induce sleep; both are broad classes covering alcohol, barbiturates, benzodiazepines and newer sleeping pills, not one chemical family. These drugs depress the central nervous system mainly by enhancing GABA, the brain's main inhibitory transmitter, at the GABA-A receptor, a chloride (Cl-) channel whose opening hyperpolarises the neuron.

Alcohol is a positive modulator, functionally an agonist, at the GABA-A receptor, and also blocks the NMDA glutamate receptor, so it raises inhibition and lowers excitation. Drinking in pregnancy causes fetal alcohol syndrome (Chapter 6), and the thiamine deficiency of chronic drinkers causes Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome (Section 5.9).

The benzodiazepines form one chemical class: diazepam (Valium), alprazolam (Xanax), flunitrazepam (Rohypnol), triazolam (Halcion) and chlordiazepoxide. They are used as anxiolytics, hypnotics and anticonvulsants. The older barbiturates (phenobarbital) bind another site on the same receptor. As Figure 5.8 shows, a benzodiazepine increases the frequency of Cl- channel opening and works only when GABA is present, so its effect has a ceiling; a barbiturate increases the duration of each opening and at high doses opens the channel by itself. Hence barbiturates, and benzodiazepines taken with alcohol, are dangerous in overdose, whereas a benzodiazepine overdose alone is rarely fatal and is reversed by the antagonist flumazenil. Withdrawal from any of these drugs is rebound excitation (anxiety, insomnia, tremor, seizures) and can kill.

Exam trap

Frequency goes with benzodiazepines and duration with barbiturates, never the reverse. Both act on GABA-A, not GABA-B or NMDA receptors, and the receptor benzodiazepines modulate binds GABA, not glutamate, acetylcholine or glycine (the spinal cord's inhibitory transmitter). Valium, Xanax, Rohypnol and Halcion are all benzodiazepines; "sedatives" and "hypnotics" are too broad.

Opioids, stimulants, cannabis and hallucinogens

Opium is the dried sap of the opium poppy's seed pods; its natural alkaloids are morphine, codeine, thebaine and papaverine. Heroin (diacetylmorphine) is not one of them: it is made by adding two acetyl groups to morphine, a semisynthetic opioid that enters the brain faster. Methadone and fentanyl are fully synthetic. Opioids act mainly at mu opioid receptors, the receptors for the body's endorphins (Section 5.3): they relieve pain, cough and diarrhoea, bring euphoria, and in overdose suppress breathing. Naloxone (short acting, the emergency reversal of overdose) and naltrexone (long acting, oral) are opioid antagonists. Reward circuits and theories of addiction

Key study: Olds and Milner's 'pleasure centre' (1954)

In 1954, at McGill University, James Olds and Peter Milner let rats press a lever that delivered brief electrical stimulation through an electrode in their own brains. With electrodes in the septal area and along the medial forebrain bundle, rats pressed thousands of times an hour, ignoring food, until exhausted. This intracranial self-stimulation seemed to reveal sites that mediate the pleasure of food, water and sex: 'pleasure centres'. Later work found no single centre; self-stimulation depends largely on dopamine axons running in the medial forebrain bundle.

Those axons form the mesolimbic pathway, from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) of the midbrain to the nucleus accumbens in the ventral striatum and other limbic sites. It is the core reward pathway of addiction: natural rewards and almost every addictive drug raise dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, directly (cocaine, amphetamine) or indirectly (opioids, alcohol, nicotine), and dopamine antagonists reduce self-stimulation. The reward transmitter is dopamine, not serotonin, noradrenaline or acetylcholine. With chronic use, control of drug taking shifts to the dorsal striatum (habit), and weakened prefrontal control makes stopping harder.

Physical-dependence theories said addicts take drugs to escape withdrawal, but detoxified users relapse months later, and cocaine causes little withdrawal distress. Positive-incentive theories replaced them: people take drugs for their anticipated pleasure. Yet addicts often crave a drug they no longer enjoy. The incentive-sensitisation theory of Terry Robinson and Kent Berridge (1993) explains this: in addiction-prone people repeated use sensitises the drug's incentive value ('wanting') while tolerance develops to its pleasure ('liking'). Mesolimbic dopamine signals wanting more than liking.

Exam trap

The reward pathway is mesolimbic (VTA to nucleus accumbens), not nigrostriatal (movement), tuberoinfundibular (prolactin) or the raphe serotonergic pathway. Olds and Milner's sites were the septal area and medial forebrain bundle, not the cerebellar tonsils, the lateral geniculate nucleus (a visual relay) or the globus pallidus (a motor nucleus). The cerebellar vermis and vestibular nuclei serve balance, not addiction.

5.5 Research Methods, Behaviour Genetics and the Developing BrainMedium yield · 25 Qs

Every fact in this chapter came from a method, and each method sees the brain differently: its shape, its activity, or the timing of that activity. This section surveys those methods, then asks how genes are inherited and studied, which genetic errors derail development, and how the brain builds and keeps rewiring itself.

Imaging the living brain

Ordinary X-rays show little of the soft brain, so contrast X-ray techniques inject a substance that absorbs X-rays differently: in cerebral angiography a radio-opaque dye in a cerebral artery outlines the blood vessels, revealing vascular damage and, by displaced vessels, tumours. Computed tomography (CT), introduced in the early 1970s by Godfrey Hounsfield, passes X-rays through the head from many angles and computes horizontal slices. It is fast and shows fresh blood, so it is the first scan after acute head injury. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) uses a strong magnetic field and radio waves: hydrogen atoms then emit signals that differ between structures. With the highest spatial resolution (precision about where), MRI gives the most detailed view of brain structure. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), an MRI method, follows water diffusing along axons to map the white matter tracts.

CT and plain MRI are structural; functional imaging shows activity. Positron emission tomography (PET) injects a radioactive tracer such as 2-deoxyglucose (FDG in human PET), which active neurons absorb like glucose but cannot metabolise, so the scan maps metabolic activity. Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) maps blood flow with a gamma-emitting tracer, more cheaply but less sharply. Functional MRI (fMRI) needs no injection: it detects the BOLD signal (blood-oxygen-level-dependent; Seiji Ogawa, 1990), which rises as active regions draw oxygenated blood. Its spatial resolution is good but its temporal resolution (precision about when) only moderate, because blood flow takes seconds to respond. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) measures cortical oxygenation with light shone through the scalp; it is portable and suits infants. Table 5.14 compares the methods.

The functional techniques are PET and SPECT (and fMRI), not CT or plain MRI, so "all of the above" is wrong. For brain structure after head injury choose MRI; CT is the quick emergency scan for bleeding. EEG, PET and fMRI record activity, not structure.

Recording electrical activity

Imaging shows where; electrical recording shows when. The electroencephalogram (EEG), first reported in humans by Hans Berger in 1929, records through scalp electrodes the summed electrical activity of cortical neurons. The brain's counterpart of the electrocardiogram (ECG), it has the highest temporal resolution, in milliseconds, but poor spatial resolution, and it is the standard test for epilepsy and sleep (wave bands in Section 5.8). Magnetoencephalography (MEG) records the magnetic fields of the same currents: as fast, better at localising, far costlier. In animals, a microelectrode records one neuron's action potentials (single-unit recording) or a larger electrode the firing of many neighbours (multiple-unit recording).

The response to a single click is lost in the background EEG, so the EEG after hundreds of clicks is averaged (signal averaging): random activity cancels and the time-locked event-related potential (ERP) remains, named by polarity (P or N) and latency in milliseconds. In the oddball paradigm, a rare target among frequent standard stimuli evokes a large positive wave about 300 ms later, the P300, first described by Samuel Sutton and colleagues in 1965. It reflects attention allocation and stimulus categorisation, and it is smaller and later in schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease (Table 5.15).

The electromyogram (EMG) records muscle tension and the electrooculogram (EOG) eye movements. Skin conductance (electrodermal activity, the galvanic skin response) rises as sympathetic arousal activates the sweat glands. Heart rate and blood pressure are other autonomic indices; the polygraph records several at once and, as a "lie detector", detects arousal, not lies.

Lesions, stimulation and genetic tools

Invasive methods, used mostly in animals, begin with stereotaxic surgery: a stereotaxic atlas gives each structure's coordinates, and a stereotaxic instrument holds the head and guides an electrode or cannula to them. A lesion destroys tissue to reveal its function by what is lost: aspiration sucks away cortex, radio-frequency current heats small subcortical areas, knife cuts sever tracts, and cryogenic blockade (cooling) makes a reversible lesion. Neurotoxins are selective: kainic acid kills cell bodies but spares passing axons, and 6-hydroxydopamine destroys dopamine and noradrenaline neurons. Bilateral lesions usually have far larger effects than unilateral ones.

Conversely, electrical stimulation or chemical stimulation (a microinjected transmitter or agonist) can evoke behaviour. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) applies continuous current through implanted electrodes, chiefly in Parkinson's disease. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) induces current in the cortex from a coil on the scalp, a non-invasive "virtual lesion"; repeated TMS treats depression. Optogenetics inserts genes for light-sensitive ion channels into one type of neuron, so light switches those neurons on or off. Microdialysis samples extracellular fluid in behaving animals to measure transmitter release. In humans, neuropsychological tests (Chapter 2) also map damage.

Genetic methods alter genes. A gene knockout mouse lacks a working gene; a knock-in replaces or adds one; a transgenic animal carries another species' gene, such as a human disease gene. CRISPR-Cas9, developed by Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier in 2012 (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2020), uses a guide RNA to lead the Cas9 protein to a chosen DNA sequence, where it edits the gene or switches it on or off. Other tools do not edit: the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of Kary Mullis copies DNA millions of times, gel electrophoresis separates DNA or proteins by size, and Western blotting detects a particular protein.

Mendel, chromosomes and epigenetics

Gregor Mendel (1822 to 1884), a monk who crossed pea plants and published in 1866, found that each trait depends on a pair of inherited factors, now called genes, one from each parent. The alternative forms of a gene are alleles: two alike make an organism homozygous (DD or dd), two different heterozygous (Dd). The genotype is the alleles carried, the phenotype the trait shown. A dominant allele is expressed even in a heterozygote; a recessive trait appears only in the homozygote, as the Punnett square in Figure 5.9 shows. Blending occurs only in incomplete dominance (red and white snapdragons give pink) and codominance (blood group AB shows both), so with a stated dominant allele "blue-green" is wrong.

Genes lie on chromosomes, strands of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) whose base sequence codes for proteins. Human cells have 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes and the sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. Meiosis makes eggs and sperm with 23 single chromosomes, and crossing over (paired chromosomes swapping segments) makes each one unique. A mutation is an accidental change in a gene. X-linked recessive sex-linked traits, such as red-green colour blindness, are commoner in males, who have no second X to mask them. Epigenetics studies changes in gene expression without any change in the DNA sequence: DNA methylation usually silences genes, and histone modification loosens or tightens the packaging of DNA, making genes more or less readable. Experience, such as early stress or maternal care, alters these marks.

Separating heredity and environment

Family studies ask whether risk rises with closeness of kinship, but relatives share homes as well as genes. Twin studies compare monozygotic twins (MZ), from one fertilised egg that splits, who have the same heredity and share virtually all their genes, with dizygotic twins (DZ), who share about 50 percent like other siblings. The concordance rate is the percentage of pairs in which, when one twin has a disorder, the other has it too: for schizophrenia, about 48 percent in MZ and 17 percent in DZ twins in the pooled data of Irving Gottesman (1991). Adoption studies ask whether adoptees resemble their biological parents (genes) or adoptive parents (environment).

Key study: The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart

From 1979, Thomas Bouchard and colleagues (main report in Science, 1990) gave adult MZ and DZ twins separated in infancy about 50 hours of intelligence and personality tests. MZ twins reared apart were about as alike as those reared together, and more alike than DZ twins, on almost every measure. Critics note that adoptive homes vary less than homes in general.

Exam trap

Twins reared apart combine twin and adoption logic (Pinel even calls the Minnesota study an adoption study), but the keyed answer is twin studies, because comparing MZ with DZ pairs defines the design. Choose adoption studies only if twin studies is not offered.

Linkage studies trace a DNA marker inherited along with a disorder through affected families, locating the gene nearby. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) compare hundreds of thousands of variants across the genome in thousands of patients and controls, usually finding many genes of tiny effect. Heritability is the proportion of variation in a trait, in a given population and environment, due to genetic differences; it describes populations, not individuals, and changes when environments change.

Diagnoses are too broad to map onto genes, so Gottesman and Todd Gould (2003) popularised the endophenotype: a measurable, heritable intermediate trait between genes and symptoms, through which genetic risk produces a disorder. P50 gating, P300 and smooth-pursuit eye-tracking deficits in schizophrenia are examples, also found in unaffected relatives. By contrast, the phenotype is the observable symptoms, the genotype the genes, and molecular genetics the study of DNA itself. The diathesis-stress model is taught in Chapter 8.

Phenylalanine accumulates and damages the developing brain, but newborn screening and a low-phenylalanine diet prevent the intellectual disability. Building the nervous system

After fertilisation the zygote divides into a solid ball (the morula) and then a hollow ball that implants (the blastocyst); these stages (Chapter 6) contain no nervous tissue. The nervous system begins at gastrulation, in the third week, when the embryo forms three germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm. Induced by the mesoderm beneath, a patch of ectoderm becomes the neural plate, which folds into the neural groove, whose lips fuse into the neural tube, forerunner of the brain and spinal cord. Failures of this neurulation cause spina bifida (tail end) and anencephaly (head end).

Development then proceeds in phases: proliferation in the ventricular zone lining the tube, migration of young neurons along radial glial cells, aggregation into structures, and axon growth led by growth cones. Roger Sperry, who rotated frogs' eyes and found that regenerating optic axons regrew to their original targets, proposed the chemoaffinity hypothesis (1963): each target releases a chemical label that attracts its axon. After synapses form, far more neurons exist than survive; in apoptosis (programmed cell death) those that fail to obtain neurotrophins from their targets die. Rita Levi-Montalcini discovered the first, nerve growth factor (Nobel Prize 1986). In synapse rearrangement, surviving axons take over the vacated sites.

Exam trap

The nervous system begins forming at the gastrula stage. Zygote, morula and blastocyst come earlier.

Pruning, myelination and lifelong plasticity

The young brain overproduces synapses; childhood synaptic density far exceeds the adult level. Synaptic pruning (neural pruning) then eliminates unused connections and keeps those in use: "use it or lose it". It is heaviest in childhood and continues in the prefrontal cortex through adolescence, partly carried out by microglia, which engulf unwanted synapses. Myelination also continues into early adulthood, the prefrontal cortex last, one reason judgement and impulse control mature late.

Exam trap

Pruning is the elimination of unused connections, a process of neuronal development and plasticity. It is not memory consolidation, not long-term potentiation or any strengthening of pathways (both Section 5.9), not neurotransmitter release (Section 5.3), not the growth of new neurons and not an increase in brain size.

Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function throughout life with experience, learning and injury; it is not a protective coating (skull, meninges or myelin), memory loss or cell death. In the 1960s Mark Rosenzweig and colleagues found that rats reared in enriched environments developed a thicker, heavier cortex than rats in barren cages. Plasticity peaks in critical periods: when David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel sewed one eye of a young kitten shut (monocular deprivation), the visual cortex permanently lost its responses to that eye, whereas adult cats were barely affected. Adults still make new neurons (adult neurogenesis) in the hippocampal dentate gyrus and, in rodents, the olfactory bulb; Peter Eriksson and colleagues confirmed it in the human hippocampus in 1998. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) supports synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival (it prevents apoptosis) and hippocampal neurogenesis, not action potential firing (Section 5.3) or myelin synthesis. Exercise and antidepressants raise it; chronic stress lowers it (Section 5.7).

5.6 Sensory Pathways and the Motor SystemLower yield · 22 Qs

Section 1.3 followed light, sound and touch to the sense organs. This section follows their signals into the brain, then traces motor commands back out to the muscles.

Principles of sensory systems

Every sensory system begins with transduction, the conversion of physical energy into neural signals by receptors. A neuron's receptive field is the area of the sensory surface (a patch of retina or skin) in which a stimulus changes its firing. The systems are hierarchical: receptors, thalamic relay nuclei, primary sensory cortex (input mainly from the thalamus), secondary sensory cortex (input mainly from primary cortex of the same sense) and association cortex (input from several senses). Each level contains areas with different jobs (functional segregation), and information flows along several routes at once (parallel processing).

Refractive errors

The cornea and lens must focus the image on the retina; their failures are refractive errors, optical rather than neural faults. In myopia (near-sightedness) distant objects blur because the eyeball is too long, so their image focuses in front of the retina; a concave lens corrects it. In hypermetropia (far-sightedness) a short eyeball focuses near objects behind the retina; a convex lens corrects it. Astigmatism is an irregularly curved cornea that blurs some orientations, and presbyopia the stiffening of the ageing lens that brings reading glasses after about 40. Visual field defects, by contrast, are blind areas left by pathway damage (5.6.4).

Retinal circuits and lateral inhibition

The retina is built inside out (the inverted retina): light passes the ganglion, amacrine, bipolar and horizontal cells, in that order, before reaching the rods and cones at the back. Signals return along the through pathway, receptors to bipolar cells to ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve, while horizontal and amacrine cells carry signals sideways. Where the axons leave, at the optic disc, there are no receptors: the blind spot, filled in by completion.

Stephen Kuffler (1953) found that cat ganglion cells have circular receptive fields with an opposing centre and surround: an on-centre cell fires to light on its centre and is inhibited by light on its surround, and an off-centre cell does the reverse. Such cells signal contrast, not even illumination.

The mechanism is lateral inhibition, the suppression of a neuron by its active neighbours, analysed by Haldan Keffer Hartline in the eye of the horseshoe crab Limulus (Nobel Prize 1967). At a light-dark border, a receptor just on the bright side has dark neighbours, receives less inhibition and signals "brighter still"; one just on the dark side receives extra inhibition and signals "darker still". The result is the illusory bright and dark strips called Mach bands (Ernst Mach, 1865).

Exam trap

Mach bands come from lateral inhibition, not the rod-cone dichotomy (dim-light versus colour vision), the opponent process (colour afterimages) or temporal summation (adding weak signals over time).

From retina to striate cortex

At the optic chiasm, fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross; temporal fibres do not. Because nasal hemiretinas see the outer visual fields, the left visual field reaches the right hemisphere. Each optic tract ends in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus, the visual relay (hearing uses the medial geniculate nucleus, touch the ventral posterior nucleus, and smell no thalamic relay).

The LGN has six layers. Layers 1 and 2 are magnocellular: large, fast, colour-blind cells with large receptive fields, for motion, contrast and depth. Layers 3 to 6 are parvocellular: small, slower cells for colour and fine detail. Koniocellular cells lie between the layers. Optic radiations carry the output to the primary visual cortex (V1, striate cortex, area 17) around the calcarine fissure. V1 is retinotopic, and the tiny fovea claims about a quarter of it.

Field loss therefore locates damage (Figure 5.10). An optic nerve lesion blinds one eye. A pituitary tumour pressing on the chiasm cuts the crossing fibres: bitemporal hemianopia. Damage behind the chiasm (tract, radiations or V1) causes contralateral homonymous hemianopia, the same opposite half-field lost in both eyes. A posterior cerebral artery stroke of V1 often spares central vision (macular sparing), because the occipital pole also draws blood from the middle cerebral artery. A small V1 lesion leaves a scotoma, an island of blindness often hidden by completion; in blindsight, studied by Lawrence Weiskrantz in the patient D.B. (1974), people point accurately at stimuli in the scotoma that they deny seeing.

The LGN relays vision only. Magnocellular is motion, parvocellular colour and detail; the two differ, and neither projects only to V4. Hemianopia with macular sparing means V1, not the chiasm, optic tract or optic nerve.

Visual cortex and the two streams

In V1, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel (Nobel Prize 1981; Section 1.4) found simple cells, responding to a line of one orientation in one exact position, and complex cells, responding to that orientation anywhere in a larger field, often only when it moves. V2 (area 18) elaborates contours and feeds specialised areas: V4 for colour (damage causes cerebral achromatopsia, a grey world) and V5, or MT, for motion (damage causes akinetopsia, movement seen as still frames).

Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin (1982) grouped these areas into two streams leaving V1. The dorsal stream runs to the posterior parietal cortex: "where" things are. The ventral stream runs to the inferotemporal cortex: "what" they are, faces included (the fusiform face area, Section 5.11). Melvyn Goodale and David Milner (1992) recast the dorsal stream as "how", vision for guiding action such as grasping, and the ventral stream as vision for conscious perception.

Exam trap

The streams are not colour against motion (motion is dorsal) or hearing against vision, and they diverge from V1 rather than converging on it.

Hearing, touch, pain, smell and taste

The hair cells of the organ of Corti transduce mechanically: basilar membrane movement bends their stereocilia, opening cation channels through which potassium from the endolymph flows in and depolarises the cell. Inner hair cells supply almost all auditory nerve signals; outer hair cells amplify the vibration. The pathway runs from the cochlear nuclei to the superior olives on both sides, where the two ears' signals first meet and are compared to locate sounds, then to the inferior colliculi, the medial geniculate nucleus and the primary auditory cortex in Heschl's gyrus (areas 41 and 42), which is tonotopic (each frequency has its own place). Conductive deafness arises in the outer or middle ear, sensorineural deafness in the hair cells or auditory nerve.

The sensorimotor hierarchy and mirror neurons

The motor system is a hierarchy run in reverse, from goals down to muscles. At the top, posterior parietal cortex integrates where the body and nearby objects are (damage causes apraxia and neglect, Section 5.11), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex decides to initiate voluntary responses. Secondary motor cortex, the premotor cortex and supplementary motor area (area 6), programmes patterns of movement and fires just before movement starts. Primary motor cortex (area 4, precentral gyrus), the main cortical exit, is laid out as a motor homunculus with outsized hands and mouth. Brainstem nuclei and spinal circuits execute. Primary, secondary and supplementary areas thus all control movement. The levels are functionally segregated and parallel, output is guided by sensory feedback, and practice hands control down to lower levels.

Key study: Rizzolatti's mirror neurons (1990s)

Giacomo Rizzolatti and colleagues found neurons in area F5 of macaque ventral premotor cortex that fired both when the monkey grasped an object and when it watched an experimenter grasp it: mirror neurons, active for performing and for observing the same action. Humans show a mirror system in premotor cortex, Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus) and the inferior parietal lobule, proposed to support imitation, action understanding and empathy. Criticism: human single-cell evidence is scarce, and the "broken mirror" theory of autism is debated.

Exam trap

Mirror neurons fire for doing and watching, not one alone, and not in REM sleep. The system is not in the hippocampus, amygdala or cerebellum.

Basal ganglia and cerebellum

Neither the basal ganglia nor the cerebellum sends commands to the muscles; both modulate the cortex through the thalamus. In the basal ganglia, the striatum (caudate and putamen) receives cortical input, and the output nuclei, the internal globus pallidus (GPi) and substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr), constantly inhibit the thalamus (Figure 5.11).

In the direct pathway, cortex excites striatal D1 neurons, which inhibit GPi and SNr; the thalamus, released, excites the cortex and movement is facilitated. In the indirect pathway, striatal D2 neurons inhibit the external globus pallidus (GPe), freeing the subthalamic nucleus (STN) to excite GPi; the thalamus is inhibited and movement suppressed. Dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta excites the direct and inhibits the indirect pathway. Its loss causes the slow, scarce movement of Parkinson's disease, a hypokinetic disorder; loss of indirect-pathway neurons causes the chorea of Huntington's disease, a hyperkinetic one (Section 5.11). The basal ganglia thus perform action selection, initiate and gate voluntary movement, and learn habits, motor sequences and rewarded actions. Depth perception, memory consolidation and cardiorespiratory control belong to the visual cortex, hippocampus and medulla.

The cerebellum compares intended with actual movement and corrects the error: climbing fibres from the inferior olive carry error signals to its Purkinje cells. It coordinates and times movement, maintains balance, and supports motor adaptation and eyeblink conditioning (Section 5.9). Damage causes ataxia (clumsy, unsteady movement), intention tremor (worse as the hand nears its target) and dysmetria (over- or undershooting).

5.7 The Endocrine System, Stress and the Emotional BrainHigh yield · 46 Qs

A bus swerves towards you on a Mysuru road. Before you have thought a word, your heart pounds and your palms are wet, and an hour later you are still shaky: nerves reacted in milliseconds, but hormones in your blood kept you on edge for hours. This section follows the hormones from the hypothalamus to the glands and the stress systems they build, then the brain circuits that turn a threat into fear.

Glands, ducts and hormones

Glands come in two kinds. Exocrine glands are duct glands: they deliver their secretions through ducts onto a surface or into a cavity, and those secretions are not hormones. Sweat, salivary, tear and digestive glands are exocrine. Endocrine glands are ductless: they release hormones, chemical messengers carried by the blood to distant cells that have receptors for them. The pancreas is both: most of it sends digestive enzymes down a duct, while islands of cells within it release insulin into the blood.

Hormones belong to three chemical classes. Amino acid derivatives are made in a few steps from one amino acid (adrenaline, thyroxine, melatonin). Peptide and protein hormones are short and long chains of amino acids (insulin, oxytocin, vasopressin, growth hormone, ACTH). Steroid hormones are made from cholesterol: the gonadal hormones (testosterone, oestrogens, progesterone) and the adrenal cortex hormones (cortisol, aldosterone). Most hormones act on receptors in the cell membrane, but steroids, small and fat-soluble, also enter the cell, bind receptors in the cytoplasm or nucleus and change gene expression directly, so their effects are the most diverse and long-lasting.

Exam trap

Exocrine glands are duct glands; endocrine glands are ductless. "Primary" is no class of gland, and sweat, saliva and tears are never hormones.

The hypothalamus and the pituitary

The pituitary gland, a pea-sized gland hanging from the base of the brain, is called the master gland because most of its hormones are tropic hormones, which make other glands release theirs; it also releases growth hormone, which regulates growth. Yet the master gland has a master: the hypothalamus above it (Section 5.2), which integrates the endocrine, autonomic and visceral systems. In an emergency the hypothalamus supervises the fight-or-flight response, driving the sympathetic nervous system and the pituitary at once.

The pituitary is really two glands, controlled in two different ways (Table 5.21). The anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis) is glandular tissue that releases growth hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), the gonadotropins (follicle-stimulating hormone, FSH, and luteinising hormone, LH) and prolactin, yet no nerve fibres reach it. How, then, does the hypothalamus command it? Geoffrey Harris (1913 to 1971) proposed in 1955 that hypothalamic neurons release releasing and release-inhibiting hormones into a network of blood vessels, the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system, which carries them down the pituitary stalk; cutting the portal veins halted anterior pituitary release until they regrew. In the late 1960s Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally separately isolated one, thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), sharing the 1977 Nobel Prize.

The posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) is neural tissue, an outgrowth of the hypothalamus, and makes nothing itself. Oxytocin and vasopressin are synthesised in neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus, carried down their axons and released from the posterior pituitary into the blood. Vasopressin, or antidiuretic hormone (ADH), makes the kidneys reabsorb water; its failure causes diabetes insipidus, with copious dilute urine and constant thirst. Oxytocin contracts the uterus in labour, ejects milk during suckling and promotes bonding.

Oxytocin and vasopressin are made in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, not the suprachiasmatic nucleus (body clock), the arcuate nucleus (feeding; the dopamine that inhibits prolactin) or the mammillary bodies. Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin ejects it.

The other endocrine glands

Three facts about the remaining glands (Table 5.22) recur: the adrenal glands run the stress response, the thyroid sets the metabolic rate and the pancreas controls blood glucose.

Each adrenal gland sits on a kidney and is two glands in one. The outer adrenal cortex makes steroids: cortisol, the chief glucocorticoid and the stress hormone; aldosterone, for salt and water balance; and adrenal androgens. The inner adrenal medulla, a modified sympathetic ganglion, releases adrenaline and noradrenaline when sympathetic nerves fire. Adrenaline and epinephrine are two names for one hormone (Latin and Greek for "on the kidney"), as noradrenaline is norepinephrine.

The thyroid gland releases thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which set the body's metabolic rate and energy expenditure; the thyroid itself is controlled by TSH. In the islets of Langerhans of the pancreas, beta cells release insulin, which lowers blood glucose by letting cells take it up, and alpha cells release glucagon, which raises it. Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune destruction of the beta cells, usually in childhood. Type 2 diabetes, far commoner and widespread in India, is a disorder of insulin resistance: it runs in families, but it is most strongly associated with poor diet, obesity and a sedentary lifestyle.

Two stress systems, feedback and allostasis

The hypothalamus answers a threat through two systems (Figure 5.12). In the fast sympathetic adrenal-medullary system (SAM), sympathetic nerves fire and the adrenal medulla pours adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood within seconds: the fight-or-flight response named by Walter Bradford Cannon (1871 to 1945; the autonomic wiring is in Section 5.1).

The slower hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA axis) works by hormones. The paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) into the portal blood, the anterior pituitary answers with ACTH, and ACTH in the general circulation makes the adrenal cortex release cortisol over minutes. Cortisol mobilises glucose and dampens inflammation and immunity. It also switches the axis off by negative feedback on the hypothalamus (less CRH) and the anterior pituitary (less ACTH), helped by the hippocampus, whose dense glucocorticoid receptors let it restrain the axis. The thyroid and gonadal axes work the same way. Hans Selye's general adaptation syndrome, what follows when the axis stays switched on, is taught in Section 1.11.

Brief cortisol surges help; chronic ones harm. Prolonged high cortisol shrinks hippocampal dendrites, and a damaged hippocampus brakes the axis less well, so cortisol climbs further. Chronic stress also suppresses immunity and slows wound healing. The study of such links between mind, nervous system and immune system is psychoneuroimmunology.

Cannon also named the goal of these systems: homeostasis (popularised in The Wisdom of the Body, 1932), keeping the internal state stable around fixed set points, as a thermostat holds a room's temperature. Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer (1988) argued that the brain also predicts demands and shifts the set points in advance, raising blood pressure as you stand and cortisol before an examination. They called this allostasis, "stability through change": the brain regulating its own stress responses by controlling hormone release. Bruce McEwen and Eliot Stellar (1993) added allostatic load, the cumulative wear and tear when these responses are overused or fail to switch off: high blood pressure, abdominal fat, insulin resistance and hippocampal damage.

Exam trap

The HPA chain is hypothalamus (CRH), anterior pituitary (ACTH), adrenal cortex (cortisol). ACTH comes from the anterior pituitary, not from the adrenal gland it targets; the posterior pituitary and adrenal medulla are not in the axis, and "pineal, pituitary, thyroid" is no axis; the cerebellum plays no part. Cortisol feeds back on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, not the pineal, posterior pituitary, adrenal medulla or vagal motor nucleus (parasympathetic output). "The brain regulating its own stress responses by controlling hormone release" is allostasis, not homeostasis (fixed set points), neuroplasticity (rewiring with experience, Section 5.5) or circadian rhythm (Section 5.8).

Clinical link

In melancholic depression the HPA axis is often overactive: in the dexamethasone suppression test, a night dose of synthetic glucocorticoid fails to suppress next morning's cortisol (Chapter 8). Because endocrine disease mimics depression and anxiety, hormones are checked before a psychiatric diagnosis.

Emotion and the autonomic nervous system

Fear and anger bring one broad sympathetic pattern: heart rate, blood pressure and breathing rise, the pupils dilate, blood shifts to the muscles, glucose enters the blood and sweating increases. Sweat conducts electricity, so skin conductance rises and skin resistance falls (the galvanic skin response). Pupil constriction is parasympathetic. The polygraph records heart rate, blood pressure, breathing and skin conductance together; it detects arousal, not lies. Whether each emotion has its own autonomic pattern divides the James-Lange, Cannon-Bard and Schachter-Singer theories (Section 1.11).

Exam trap

Odd one out among signs of sympathetic activation: pupil constriction. "Lowered skin resistance" is sympathetic: it is the same event as raised skin conductance.

From sham rage to the limbic system

Which part of the brain makes emotion? In 1929 Philip Bard found that decorticate cats, their cortex removed, raged at the lightest touch, arching their backs, hissing and baring their teeth, but excessively and at no target. He called this sham rage. It survived removal of everything down to the hypothalamus but vanished once the hypothalamus went too. The hypothalamus organises rage; the cortex inhibits and directs it.

In 1937 James Papez (1883 to 1958) proposed a circuit for emotion (Figure 5.13). The Papez circuit runs from the hippocampus through the fornix to the mammillary bodies, by the mammillothalamic tract to the anterior thalamic nucleus, on to the cingulate cortex, through the cingulum to the entorhinal (parahippocampal) cortex and back to the hippocampus. Emotion, he argued, is expressed through the hypothalamus and experienced through the cortex. The loop is now thought to serve memory more than emotion (Section 5.9), but it began the search for an emotional brain.

Paul MacLean (1913 to 2007) enlarged the circuit in 1952, adding the amygdala, septum and other structures, and named it the limbic system (Latin limbus, border, because it rings the thalamus at the inner edge of the cortex); later it became the middle layer of his triune brain. Its members are the hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate gyrus, fornix, septum and mammillary bodies, with the hypothalamus and anterior thalamus closely tied in. It is the classic "seat of emotion", regulating emotion, motivation and memory.

Exam trap

"Seat of emotion", "emotion and motivation", "emotional behaviour": limbic system; offered single parts (hippocampus, thalamus, hypothalamus) beside it, choose the system. Not the reticular formation (arousal), hindbrain (breathing, heart rate), whole forebrain (too broad), pituitary (hormones), cerebellum (motor coordination), corpus callosum (joins the hemispheres), frontal lobe (voluntary movement, decisions), thalamus and sensory cortex (sensory processing), visual cortex (acuity) or Broca's area (syntax). Hunger, thirst and hormone regulation are hypothalamic, so Amity 2023 keys "regulation of emotions", not "both".

The amygdala and fear

The amygdala (Greek for almond), a cluster of nuclei in each anterior medial temporal lobe, is the key emotional structure: it detects threat, gives events emotional salience, triggers fear and defensive aggression, and learns what predicts danger. Its role was mapped by fear conditioning: after a tone has preceded mild shocks, the tone alone makes a rat freeze, heart racing. Signals enter through the lateral nucleus, where tone and shock are associated, and leave through the central nucleus for the hypothalamus (autonomic and hormonal responses) and the periaqueductal grey of the midbrain (freezing). Amygdala lesions abolish conditioned fear.

Joseph LeDoux (born 1949) traced two routes in (Figure 5.14). The low road, from the sensory thalamus straight to the amygdala, is fast and crude: you jump back from a coiled shape before you know what it is. The high road, from the thalamus through the sensory cortex to the amygdala, is slower but accurate: it tells you the shape is a rope.

Key study: Patient S.M. (Adolphs and colleagues, 1994)

Does the human amygdala matter for emotion? Ralph Adolphs and colleagues tested S.M., whose amygdalae had been destroyed on both sides by Urbach-Wiethe disease, a rare genetic disorder that calcifies the amygdala and nearby medial temporal lobe. She recognised faces and most expressions but not fear, and could not produce a fearful expression herself. Later work found she barely feels fear, reaching for snakes in a pet shop (Justin Feinstein and colleagues, 2011). Criticism: one rare patient.

In affective blindsight, described by Beatrice de Gelder and colleagues in 1999, patients blinded in part of the visual field by damage to the primary visual cortex guess well above chance which emotion a face shown there expresses, while insisting they see nothing. The signal is thought to travel subcortically, from the superior colliculus through the pulvinar of the thalamus to the amygdala. "Affect neglect", "emotional neglect" and "motivational blindsight" are not established terms.

Exam trap

Fear and aggression, emotion processing, threat detection, fear conditioning: amygdala. Not the hippocampus (conscious recall, declarative memory, Section 5.9), thalamus (relay), hypothalamus (executes the amygdala's orders), frontal lobe (verbal working memory), brainstem (vital functions), cerebellum and basal ganglia (starting movement, motor sequence learning) or suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian rhythm). "Slower, more accurate identification" is the high road; conscious vision and voluntary motor output are cortical.

Klüver-Bucy syndrome and other influences

What happens to an animal without its amygdalae? In 1937 and 1939 Heinrich Klüver and Paul Bucy removed both anterior temporal lobes, amygdalae included, in rhesus monkeys. This Klüver-Bucy syndrome follows mainly from loss of the amygdala. 5.8 Sleep, Biological Rhythms, Hunger, Thirst and SexMedium yield · 31 Qs

Why does a student who revised all night still feel alert in the evening, hungry at lunchtime however large breakfast was, and thirsty after salty chaat? The answers lie in small clusters of neurons, mostly in the hypothalamus.

Biological rhythms and the master clock

A circadian rhythm (Latin circa diem, about a day) repeats about every 24 hours, like the sleep-wake cycle; ultradian rhythms are shorter (the 90-minute sleep cycle) and infradian rhythms longer (the menstrual cycle). Circadian rhythms are entrained, locked to the world, by zeitgebers ("time givers"), above all light. Without cues, in a cave or windowless laboratory, they persist as free-running rhythms slightly longer than 24 hours (about 24.2 in humans). Jet lag and shift work put the clock out of step with the day; eastbound flights, which demand a phase advance, are the harder.

Curt Richter found in 1967 that large medial hypothalamic lesions disrupted the daily cycles of eating, drinking and activity in rats; smaller lesions located the clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), just above the optic chiasm. SCN lesions abolish the circadian timing of sleep but not its total amount.

Key study: the SCN transplant (Ralph and colleagues, 1990)

Martin Ralph and colleagues transplanted SCN tissue from fetal mutant hamsters with a 20-hour free-running cycle into normal adults whose 25-hour rhythms SCN lesions had abolished. Rhythms returned with the donor's 20-hour period (and the reverse transplant gave 25 hours): the SCN sets the period. It is the master clock, though most body cells carry clock machinery that it keeps in step.

Light reaches the SCN by the retinohypothalamic tract, from retinal ganglion cells containing the photopigment melanopsin, not from rods or cones. The clock itself is a molecular loop: clock genes such as period, timeless and clock are transcribed, and their proteins build up and switch off their own transcription over about 24 hours, as Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young showed in fruit flies (Nobel Prize, 2017).

The SCN drives the pineal gland, a small endocrine gland of the epithalamus, to secrete melatonin at night, the "hormone of darkness"; light suppresses it. Melatonin promotes sleepiness, and taken at bedtime in the new time zone it eases jet lag.

Exam trap

The master circadian pacemaker is the SCN of the hypothalamus, not the paraventricular or ventromedial nucleus or mammillary bodies, and no centre for hunger, temperature, anterior pituitary release, memory, language or pain (Table 5.25). Yet RML 2022 ("which gland regulates circadian rhythm") and RML 2023 ("which structure regulates the body's internal clock") keyed the pineal gland, which turns the clock's signal into melatonin. Choose the SCN whenever it is offered; for a gland, or "hypothalamus" against "pineal", these keys want the pineal. The pituitary (Section 5.7), cerebellum and thalamus keep no clock.

The NREM stages and a REM period make a cycle of about 90 minutes, four to six a night (Section 1.5).

The switches for sleep, waking and dreams

In 1936 Frederic Bremer cut cats' brainstems between the colliculi (the cerveau isolé, "isolated forebrain"), and the cortex showed almost constant slow-wave sleep; a cut at the bottom of the brainstem (the encéphale isolé, 1937) left the sleep-wake cycle intact. Something between the cuts keeps the forebrain awake. Giuseppe Moruzzi and Horace Magoun (1949) found that stimulating the brainstem reticular formation woke sleeping cats: it is the reticular activating system.

It is now seen as several ascending arousal systems named by transmitter: noradrenaline (locus coeruleus), serotonin (raphe nuclei), histamine (tuberomammillary nucleus), acetylcholine (basal forebrain and pons) and orexin, or hypocretin (lateral hypothalamus, in its posterior, wake-promoting part). Slow-wave sleep begins when GABA neurons of the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO) become active and inhibit these systems. As the two sides inhibit each other, Clifford Saper (2001) likened them to a flip-flop switch: one side wins, so we change state quickly (Figure 5.15).

The chemical of drowsiness is adenosine, a by-product of the brain's energy use that builds up during waking and falls during sleep: it is the homeostatic sleep pressure, and caffeine keeps you awake by blocking its receptors. Orexin, acetylcholine and serotonin promote arousal instead. Alexander Borbély's two-process model (1982) joins this process S, rising with time awake, to the circadian process C from the SCN.

REM has its own switch: REM-on neurons of the sublaterodorsal nucleus in the pons and REM-off neurons of the ventrolateral periaqueductal grey inhibit each other. In REM the sublaterodorsal nucleus drives medullary neurons that inhibit motor neurons, producing atonia.

In the activation-synthesis hypothesis of J. Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley (1977; Section 1.5), the cortex weaves a dream from random pontine activity. In REM the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is relatively inactive, so dreams lack organisation, planning, logic and a sense of time; the amygdala is active, so they are emotional; and the extrastriate visual association cortex is active while the striate (primary visual) cortex is quiet, so we see vivid images without input from the eyes. The somatosensory cortex plays no special part.

Sleep disorders and why we sleep

In narcolepsy the orexin neurons of the lateral (posterior) hypothalamus are lost. Its signs are excessive daytime sleepiness with sleep attacks; cataplexy, a sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion, in full consciousness; sleep paralysis; hypnagogic hallucinations while falling asleep; and sleep-onset REM. All but the sleepiness are REM breaking into waking.

In REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD), REM atonia is lost and patients act out their dreams; it often precedes Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia by years (Section 5.11). Sleepwalking (somnambulism), night terrors and bedwetting are NREM parasomnias, arising from partial arousal out of N3 early in the night; nightmares are frightening REM dreams, mostly late in the night. In sleep apnoea, breathing stops repeatedly during sleep. Insomnia and its treatment are in Chapters 7 and 8.

Exam trap

Sleepwalking and night terrors: N3, not REM, N1 or N2 (a body in REM is paralysed). RBD is loss of REM atonia, not an inability to enter REM, not bedwetting in N3 and not daytime sleepiness without night-time symptoms.

Why sleep at all? Recuperation theories say sleep restores what waking depletes; adaptation theories say it evolved to keep animals inactive and safe when they need not forage. Sleep also aids memory consolidation (Section 5.9).

Hunger: set points and positive incentives

Most people assume hunger works like a thermostat. On this set-point assumption, eating starts when energy falls below a set point and stops when it is restored: Jean Mayer's glucostatic theory (1953) made blood glucose the regulated quantity, and the lipostatic theory made it body fat. John Pinel and colleagues argue instead for positive-incentive theory: we eat mainly for the anticipated pleasure of food, shaped by taste, learning and company, and body weight drifts to a settling point.

The circulating hormone that triggers hunger is ghrelin, from the empty stomach. Satiety signals come from the gut and from fat. In 1973 James Gibbs, R. C. Young and Gerard Smith injected cholecystokinin (CCK), released by the duodenum as food arrives, into hungry rats, which then ate smaller meals: the classic satiety peptide. Insulin, released by the pancreas as blood glucose rises, is both a satiety and a body-fat signal.

Key study: the ob/ob mouse and leptin (1994)

Mice of the ob/ob strain overeat and grow up to three times normal weight. In 1994 Jeffrey Friedman's group cloned the mutated gene: expressed only in fat cells, it codes a hormone they named leptin, which ob/ob mice lack, and leptin injections cut their eating and fat. Released in proportion to fat stores, leptin is the long-term regulator of body weight and fat. Yet obese humans usually have high leptin and do not respond to injections (leptin resistance). The long-term fat signal is leptin, not the hypothalamus (a brain area, not a hormone), oestrogen or cortisol.

The hypothalamus and the arcuate nucleus

Section 1.10 told how two lesion studies built the dual-centre hypothesis. In 1940 Albert Hetherington and Stephen Ranson destroyed the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) of rats, which overate (hyperphagia) and grew grossly obese, first in a dynamic phase of rapid gain, then in a static phase holding the new weight. In 1951 the Indian physiologist Bal Krishan Anand and John Brobeck found that lateral hypothalamus (LH) lesions caused aphagia, a fatal refusal to eat; Philip Teitelbaum and Alan Epstein (1962) showed that it comes with adipsia, a refusal to drink, and that tube-fed rats partly recover. Eliot Stellar (1954) made the LH the feeding centre and the VMH the satiety centre.

The model has since been revised: VMH lesions raise blood insulin, so food is turned into fat and the rats overeat because they are getting fat, not the reverse; cut fibres from the paraventricular nucleus explain much of the syndrome; and LH lesions cause broad motor and sensory deficits. The nuclei are now parts of a network (Table 5.25) whose hub is the arcuate nucleus.

Learned influences on eating, and thirst

What is on the plate, and who is at the table, matter too. In the portion size effect, people eat more when served larger portions: Barbara Rolls and colleagues (2002) served lunches of macaroni and cheese in different sizes, and the bigger the portion, the more people ate, without feeling fuller. In sensory-specific satiety, a food loses its appeal as you eat it while other foods stay attractive, so a new dish restarts eating. A small first course increases hunger (the appetiser effect), and a cafeteria diet of varied, palatable foods made rats eat 84 per cent more calories. Through social facilitation we eat more with others than alone, though we also eat less when companions do. Prader-Willi syndrome is in Section 5.5, eating disorders in Chapter 8.

Thirst has two triggers. In osmotic thirst the salt concentration of body fluid rises, as after a salty meal: water leaves the cells, and hypothalamic osmoreceptors sense this cellular dehydration, start thirst and trigger the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) so the kidneys keep water. In hypovolaemic thirst blood volume falls, as after bleeding: the kidneys release renin, which forms angiotensin II, and angiotensin II acts on the subfornical organ to start drinking. When ADH fails, in diabetes insipidus, the kidneys pass large volumes of dilute urine and the person is constantly thirsty.

Hormones, sexual development and sexual response

The organisational effects of hormones act early in development and permanently shape body and brain; activational effects act in adulthood, only while the hormone is present. Charles Phoenix and colleagues (1959) drew this distinction after female guinea pigs exposed to testosterone before birth mounted like males when given testosterone as adults.

The default course of a fetus is female. The SRY gene on the Y chromosome turns the primitive gonads into testes, which secrete testosterone, masculinising the body, and Müllerian-inhibiting substance, which makes the female ducts degenerate; Alfred Jost found that fetuses deprived of their gonads develop female ducts whatever their chromosomes. From puberty the pituitary gonadotropins, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone, make the gonads secrete androgens (chiefly testosterone), oestrogens (oestradiol) and progestins (progesterone). Both sexes make all three, in different amounts; hence Pinel's attack on the mamawawa, the men-are-men-and-women-are-women assumption that the sexes are separate, opposite categories.

In congenital adrenal hyperplasia, the adrenal glands of XX fetuses make excess androgens, partly masculinising girls' genitals; in androgen insensitivity syndrome, XY individuals with testes but unresponsive androgen receptors develop a female body without a uterus. The medial preoptic area, whose sexually dimorphic nucleus is several times larger in male rats (Roger Gorski, 1978), is critical for male sexual behaviour, the VMH for female. In 1991 Simon LeVay reported that INAH3, a nucleus of the anterior hypothalamus, was smaller in gay than in heterosexual men, nearer the size in women; the finding has not been consistently replicated, and reading it as a "female-like" brain is mamawawa thinking.

William Masters and Virginia Johnson (1966) observed volunteers in the laboratory and described a four-phase sexual response cycle: excitement, plateau (arousal levels off near its peak), orgasm and resolution, with a male refractory period after orgasm. Helen Singer Kaplan (1979) put desire first, in a three-phase model of desire, excitement and orgasm.

Exam trap

Masters and Johnson: excitement, plateau, orgasm, resolution. A sequence that starts with plateau, or inserts desire anywhere, is not theirs: desire first is Kaplan, and the refractory period lies within the male resolution phase rather than being a phase of its own.

5.9 The Biology of Learning, Memory and AmnesiaHigh yield · 47 Qs

Every time you learn something, your brain physically changes. Where, and how? The answers come from patients whose memory broke in revealing ways, from animal lesion studies, and from the synapse itself. Section 1.7 treated memory as a psychological process; here the subject is its biology.

Amnesia: forward and backward in time

Clinically, amnesia is a pathological loss of memory, far beyond ordinary forgetting. It usually follows brain damage or disease (head injury, stroke, surgery, encephalitis, thiamine deficiency); more rarely its cause is psychological, as in dissociative amnesia (Chapter 8). It is a disorder of memory, not a behavioural disturbance, a depressed mood or impaired perception.

Amnesias come in two directions in time from the onset (Figure 5.17). The first, anterograde amnesia, is the inability to form new long-term memories after the injury or onset: the patient chats normally, yet ten minutes later the conversation is gone. The second, retrograde amnesia, is the loss of memories formed before it. It usually has a temporal gradient: recent memories are lost most completely while remote ones survive, a pattern Théodule Ribot (1839 to 1916) described in Les maladies de la mémoire (1881), hence Ribot's law. The gradient shows that memories grow sturdier with age, through consolidation (5.9.4). In source amnesia you remember a fact but not where or when you learned it, a lapse common in old age and after frontal lobe damage.

Exam trap

Anterograde means forward from the injury: no new memories. It is not "temporary memory loss" (transient global amnesia, Table 5.27), a visual deficit, or an inability to speak or recognise faces. Losing memories from before a concussion or stroke, or failing to retrieve old memories, is retrograde amnesia; dissociative amnesia is the psychological kind.

The search for the engram

If learning changes the brain, the change must be somewhere. In 1904 the German zoologist Richard Semon (1859 to 1918) named it the engram: the physical trace an experience leaves in the brain. It is not a process of forgetting, a retrieval failure or a type of explicit memory.

Its most determined hunter, Karl Spencer Lashley (1890 to 1958), trained rats on mazes and then removed different parts of their cortex, expecting one lesion to erase the habit. None did. In Brain Mechanisms and Intelligence (1929) he stated two principles: mass action (the deficit depended on how much cortex was removed, not where) and equipotentiality (within a functional area, any part could do the work of any other). His review "In search of the engram" (1950) concluded that memories are not held in one place but distributed throughout the cortex.

His former student Donald Olding Hebb (1904 to 1985) explained why in The Organization of Behavior (1949). A memory is held by a cell assembly, a network of neurons linked because they fired together during an experience; spread across the cortex, it survives any single lesion in part. The links form at a Hebbian synapse: when neuron A repeatedly helps to fire neuron B, the connection from A to B strengthens ("cells that fire together wire together", a later summary). Long-term potentiation, found in 1973, bore him out (5.9.7). The Montreal neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield (1891 to 1976), stimulating the temporal lobes of awake epileptic patients, sometimes evoked vivid recollections, now regarded as reconstructions rather than stored replays.

Exam trap

Lashley placed memories throughout the cortex: not in specific localised regions, the hippocampus alone or only the frontal and temporal lobes. The engram is the physical trace of a memory; the term is Semon's, the search Lashley's.

H.M. and the medial temporal lobes

Section 1.7 told H.M.'s story; here is the neural detail. Henry Gustav Molaison (1926 to 2008) had severe epilepsy that drugs could not control, apparently arising in both medial temporal lobes. In 1953, when he was 27, the neurosurgeon William Beecher Scoville (1906 to 1984) performed a bilateral medial temporal lobectomy, removing the inner parts of both temporal lobes: most of the hippocampus, the amygdala and adjacent cortex. The seizures eased. Scoville and the psychologist Brenda Milner (born 1918) reported the cost in 1957.

H.M. was left with severe anterograde amnesia for declarative memory (explicit memory: consciously recalled facts and events). He never learned his new address or recognised doctors he met after surgery, and he reread magazines with fresh interest. He had some retrograde amnesia for the few years before surgery. Spared were his intelligence (above average), language, personality, working memory (a normal digit span of about six) and remote memories, such as people and events from his childhood.

Most surprising, he could learn skills. Tracing a star while watching his hand only in a mirror, ten times a day on three days, he made fewer errors each day, yet each day denied ever doing the task. He also improved at rotary pursuit (keeping a stylus on a moving target), identified fragmented drawings sooner on the incomplete-pictures test (priming) and acquired a conditioned eyeblink: all implicit (nondeclarative) memory.

Three lessons followed. The medial temporal lobes consolidate new declarative memories but do not store remote ones, since his old memories survived. Short-term and long-term memory are separable. Explicit and implicit memory dissociate.

Key study: Scoville and Milner (1957)

Their report on H.M. and other patients established medial temporal amnesia as a distinct syndrome. Criticism: the lesion also took the amygdala and nearby cortex, so the case alone could not show which structure mattered.

Exam trap

H.M.'s most profound deficit was anterograde amnesia for declarative memory, not retrograde amnesia or a loss of procedural, semantic or working memory. Improving mirror drawing shows preserved procedural memory, not episodic, declarative or semantic memory. The surgery removed the medial temporal lobes, not prefrontal, parietal (angular gyrus) or occipital (lingual gyrus) cortex. It did not abolish motor coordination, selectively erase semantic memory, or cause "global retrograde amnesia with intact new learning", the reverse of his case.

The hippocampus: consolidation and space

Which structure mattered? Patient R.B., described by Stuart Zola-Morgan, Larry Squire and David Amaral (1986), suffered ischaemia (interrupted blood supply to the brain) at 52, when a pump failed during heart bypass surgery. His anterograde amnesia was milder than H.M.'s, and after his death the only obvious damage was to pyramidal cells in field CA1 of the hippocampus. Hippocampal damage alone, then, causes anterograde amnesia: a man who since a road accident remembers his past but not what he said ten minutes ago has probably injured his hippocampus.

The hippocampus is needed for consolidation, the conversion of fragile new memories into stable long-term ones, especially episodic and spatial declarative memories. In standard consolidation theory, it binds the scattered cortical pieces of an experience and replays them, partly during sleep (5.8), until cortical connections can hold the memory alone; remote memories then no longer need it. This explains both H.M.'s intact childhood and Ribot's gradient.

It is also the brain's map. In 1971 John O'Keefe (born 1939) and Jonathan Dostrovsky found place cells in the rat hippocampus, each firing only when the rat is in one particular spot; O'Keefe and Lynn Nadel argued in The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map (1978) that the hippocampus builds a cognitive map of the environment. May-Britt Moser (born 1963) and Edvard Moser (born 1962) in 2005 found grid cells in the entorhinal cortex, the hippocampus's main input, each firing in a regular triangular grid of locations, a coordinate system for space. The three shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Eleanor Maguire and colleagues (2000) found enlarged posterior hippocampi in London taxi drivers, larger the longer they had driven.

K.C., Kent Cochrane (1951 to 2014), studied by Endel Tulving (1927 to 2023), suffered diffuse brain damage including the medial temporal lobes in a motorcycle accident in 1981. He lost episodic, autobiographical memory for his whole life and could not imagine his future, yet his semantic knowledge, skills (chess, card games) and working memory survived: episodic and semantic memory are separable. Clive Wearing (born 1938), a British musician, lost both hippocampi and more to herpes simplex encephalitis in 1985; left with a memory span of seconds, he could still play the piano and conduct.

Exam trap

The hippocampus forms and consolidates new declarative memories (especially episodic and spatial); remote memories end up in the wider cortex. It does not control temperature (hypothalamus), balance (cerebellum), speech (Broca's area, 5.10), procedural memory (basal ganglia, cerebellum), smell transduction (olfactory receptors, 5.6) or voluntary movement (motor cortex). Failure to form new memories after head injury points to the hippocampus, not the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, cerebellum, thalamus, hypothalamus, pituitary, brainstem, reticular formation (arousal) or spinal cord (reflexes).

Memory systems and the amygdala

Memory, then, is several systems, each with its own structures (Table 5.26). Skills and habits depend on the cerebellum, basal ganglia and motor cortex, spared in H.M. but damaged in Parkinson's and Huntington's disease, which impair skill learning. Korsakoff's syndrome and its look-alikes

The second classic site of amnesia is the medial diencephalon. Korsakoff's syndrome (Korsakoff's psychosis), described in the 1880s by the Russian neuropsychiatrist Sergei Korsakoff (1854 to 1900), is an amnesic disorder caused by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1), classically in chronic alcoholism with a poor diet: alcohol supplies calories without vitamins and impairs thiamine absorption. It also occurs in malnourished non-drinkers, so the vitamin, not the alcohol, is the main cause. Thiamine is needed to metabolise glucose, so energy-hungry neurons die first.

It usually follows Wernicke's encephalopathy, described by Carl Wernicke (1848 to 1905) in 1881: an acute emergency with a triad of confusion (disorientation, lapses of attention), ataxia (loss of muscle coordination) and ophthalmoplegia (paralysis of eye movements), often with nystagmus (jerky eye movements). Attention and coordination are both impaired. Untreated survivors settle into Korsakoff's amnesia, and the continuum is called Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.

Damage centres on the mammillary bodies and the mediodorsal (dorsomedial) thalamus, a diencephalic amnesia, though it is diffuse. The result is dense anterograde amnesia, severe retrograde amnesia with a temporal gradient reaching back decades, confusion, apathy, poor insight and confabulation: detailed, plausible but false stories that fill memory gaps, told sincerely without intent to deceive. Malingering, by contrast, is deliberate faking for gain; delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of consciousness; confusion is muddled thinking without invented stories. Thiamine supplements for heavy drinkers help prevent it. Treatment is high-dose thiamine given before any glucose (a glucose load can precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy); it halts damage, but established amnesia is largely permanent.

Exam trap

Korsakoff's: vitamin B1 (thiamine), not B12, niacin, B6, folate or vitamin D, hypoxia or lead. Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome impairs both attention and muscle coordination. Memory loss with confabulation and nystagmus in a heavy drinker is Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, not Alzheimer's, vascular or frontotemporal dementia. Sincere invented memories are confabulation, not malingering, delirium or confusion.

Memory at the synapse: Aplysia and LTP

Learning was first traced to particular synapses in a sea slug. Eric Kandel (born 1929) chose Aplysia because its large neurons can be identified one by one. A touch to its siphon makes it withdraw its gill. Repeated harmless touches weaken the reflex (habituation) because sensory neurons release less transmitter onto motor neurons; a tail shock strengthens it (sensitisation) because interneurons release serotonin onto the sensory terminals, raising cyclic AMP and transmitter release. Long-term forms need the transcription factor CREB, protein synthesis and new synapses. Kandel shared the 2000 Nobel Prize.

In mammals the breakthrough came in 1973: Timothy Bliss (born 1940) and Terje Lømo (born 1935) stimulated the perforant path into the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus in anaesthetised rabbits. After a brief burst of high-frequency (tetanic) pulses, single test pulses produced larger responses for hours, and in later work for weeks or months. This long-term potentiation (LTP) is a lasting strengthening of synaptic transmission after high-frequency stimulation. Four properties fit a memory mechanism: longevity (it lasts); input specificity (only the stimulated synapses strengthen); cooperativity (several inputs must be active together); and associativity (a weak input active with a strong one is potentiated too, a cellular version of pairing a conditioned with an unconditioned stimulus).

The secret lies in the receptors for glutamate, the brain's main excitatory transmitter (Figure 5.18). AMPA receptors carry ordinary fast excitation. The NMDA receptor is special: at resting potential its channel is plugged by a magnesium ion (Mg2+), so glutamate alone lets almost nothing through. Only when glutamate is bound and the postsynaptic membrane is already depolarised is the magnesium expelled, letting calcium (Ca2+) flood in. The NMDA receptor is thus a coincidence detector of presynaptic and postsynaptic activity: Hebb's rule in molecular form. (Glycine must also bind as a co-agonist but cannot open it alone; the receptor is excitatory and postsynaptic, so it does not trigger vesicle release.)

In the dendritic spine, calcium activates CaMKII (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II), which strengthens AMPA receptors and inserts extra ones, so the same glutamate release produces a larger response. Late LTP, lasting days or more, needs CREB-driven gene expression, new proteins and growth of more and larger dendritic spines; LTP does not involve fewer spines, glutamate depletion or neuron death (apoptosis). Richard Morris and colleagues (1986) tied it to behaviour: rats given AP5, an NMDA receptor blocker, showed no hippocampal LTP and were badly impaired at learning where a platform lay hidden in the Morris water maze, a pool of opaque water.

Exam trap

LTP is a lasting strengthening after high-frequency stimulation, not a weakening (that is LTD), neuron death or myelination, and it is linked to memory, not thermoregulation, circadian rhythm or sleep. Its induction depends on NMDA receptors, not AMPA receptors alone, muscarinic M1 or GABA-A receptors. Magnesium blocks the NMDA channel, not the AMPA channel.

LTD and the dopamine teaching signal

Synapses that could only strengthen would saturate. LTP's counterpart, long-term depression (LTD), is a lasting weakening of synaptic transmission. Serena Dudek and Mark Bear (1992) produced it in hippocampal field CA1 with prolonged low-frequency stimulation, about 1 Hz for 15 minutes. LTD there also needs NMDA receptors, but the modest, sustained calcium rise activates phosphatases (enzymes that remove phosphate groups) rather than kinases, and AMPA receptors are pulled out of the membrane. A large, fast calcium surge gives LTP; a small, slow one gives LTD. LTD is not cell death, presynaptic GABA release, magnesium block of AMPA receptors or glutamate release without postsynaptic effect; high-frequency stimulation with NMDA receptors blocked produces neither LTP nor LTD.

Learning also needs a signal saying what is worth learning. Wolfram Schultz recorded from dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra in monkeys learning that a light predicted juice. Before learning, the neurons burst when juice arrived unexpectedly. After learning, they burst at the light and ignored the expected juice; if the juice was withheld, firing paused when it should have come. Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague (1997) identified this as a reward prediction error: reward received minus reward expected, positive for a pleasant surprise, zero for a predicted reward, negative for a disappointment. It is the surprise term of the Rescorla-Wagner model (Chapter 1), and the dopamine released strengthens the striatal synapses that were just active. Drug reward is covered in 5.4.

Exam trap

The prediction-error signal comes from dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area, not serotonin release, GABA inhibition or acetylcholine release. LTD needs prolonged low-frequency stimulation; LTP needs brief high-frequency bursts.

5.10 Lateralisation, the Split Brain and LanguageMedium yield · 28 Qs

In 1861 a Paris surgeon found that a man who could say only one syllable had damage on the left side of his brain. That finding raised the two questions of this section: why do the hemispheres do different jobs, and where is language produced and understood?

Discovering the dominant hemisphere

Cerebral lateralisation is the tendency of a function to depend more on one cerebral hemisphere than on the other. In 1836 Marc Dax, a French country doctor, reported that none of his 40 or so patients with speech loss had damage confined to the right hemisphere, but nobody took notice. In 1861 Paul Broca (1824 to 1880) described Louis Victor Leborgne, nicknamed 'Tan' because it was almost the only syllable he could utter. Leborgne understood speech; at autopsy his lesion lay in the left inferior frontal cortex, and by 1864 Broca had seen the same in more patients. Because language, and later skilled movement, proved to depend on the left hemisphere, it was called the dominant hemisphere or major hemisphere, and the right the minor hemisphere. The second label is outdated.

Two tests reveal the language hemisphere of a living person. In the Wada test (sodium amytal test) of Juhn Atsushi Wada (1949), the barbiturate sodium amytal is injected into one carotid artery, anaesthetising that hemisphere for a few minutes; if it holds language, the patient falls mute. It is used before brain surgery. The harmless dichotic listening test plays different words or digits to the two ears at once. Doreen Kimura (1961) found that most people report more right-ear items, a right-ear advantage for words, because each ear's stronger pathway runs to the opposite hemisphere and the left hemisphere handles language; for melodies the advantage passes to the left ear.

About 90 percent of people are right-handed, and handedness is loosely tied to language. Language is in the left hemisphere in about 95 percent of right-handers and in most left-handers (roughly 70 percent); the rest have it on the right or in both hemispheres.

Exam trap

The left hemisphere is the major (dominant) hemisphere. 'Spatial and pattern sense' and 'centre of musical ability' describe the right hemisphere, and 'minor hemisphere' is the old name for the right. Most left-handers are still left-dominant for language.

One right-hemisphere skill lies inside language itself. Prosody is the melody of speech: the intonation and emotional tone that let 'Oh, great' mean delight or sarcasm. Emotional prosody depends on right-hemisphere regions homotopic to the language areas, that is, in their mirror-image positions. Elliott Ross (1981) described the aprosodias: damage opposite Broca's area leaves speech flat and monotonous, and damage opposite Wernicke's area leaves the patient deaf to the emotion in other voices, while words and grammar are spared.

The hemispheres differ in structure too. Norman Geschwind (1926 to 1984) and Walter Levitsky (1968) found the planum temporale, auditory cortex behind Heschl's gyrus that includes part of Wernicke's area, larger on the left in about two thirds of 100 brains. Popular talk of logical 'left-brained' and creative 'right-brained' people is a myth. Lateralisation is a matter of degree (the right-ear advantage is slight), every complex skill, creativity included, uses both hemispheres together, and imaging finds no 'left-brained' individuals.

Exam trap

The function most reliably lateralised to the right is visuospatial processing and prosody; phonological processing, grammatical syntax and verbal memory are left-hemisphere. Speech intonation depends on right-hemisphere regions homotopic to the language areas, not Broca's area, the primary motor cortex or the occipital lobe.

The split brain

The most striking evidence came from cutting the corpus callosum, the largest commissure linking the hemispheres (5.2). In 1953 Ronald Myers and Roger Wolcott Sperry (1913 to 1994) cut both the corpus callosum and the optic chiasm of cats, so each eye fed one hemisphere. A cat trained on a visual task with one eye patched had to relearn it from scratch with the other eye, as if it had two brains: the corpus callosum transfers learning between hemispheres.

From the 1960s surgeons cut the corpus callosum in people with intractable epilepsy, to stop seizures spreading between hemispheres. This corpus callosotomy (often called commissurotomy) reduced the seizures and left patients almost unchanged in daily life. Sperry and his student Michael Gazzaniga (born 1939) then tested them, work that won Sperry the Nobel Prize in 1981.

Key study: Sperry and Gazzaniga's split-brain patients (1960s)

Method: the left visual field projects to the right hemisphere and the right field to the left (5.6). In tachistoscopic presentation the patient fixates a central dot while a picture is flashed to one side for about a tenth of a second, too fast for an eye movement. Each hand, hidden from view, reports to and is controlled by the opposite hemisphere. Result: objects flashed to the right visual field were named at once. Objects flashed to the left field reached only the non-speaking right hemisphere: the patient said he saw nothing, yet his left hand picked out the object by touch (Figure 5.19). Neither eye nor field was blind. Criticism: the patients were few, and long-standing epilepsy may have reorganised their brains.

Four conclusions followed. Each hemisphere perceives, learns and remembers independently: shown two pictures at once, each hand fetches the object its own hemisphere saw. Language is not bilateral: in right-handers speech comes from the left hemisphere, and the right cannot speak, though it understands simple words. The right hemisphere is better at spatial tasks. And each hemisphere completes what it sees: shown chimeric figures, photographs joining halves of two different faces, patients of Jerre Levy, Colwyn Trevarthen and Sperry (1972) reported one whole face, naming the right-field half but pointing to the left-field half.

In Gazzaniga's best-known demonstration, one patient's left hemisphere saw a chicken claw and his right hemisphere a snow scene; his right hand chose a chicken, his left a shovel. Asked why, he said at once that the shovel was for cleaning out the chicken shed. Gazzaniga called this the left-hemisphere interpreter, which invents plausible reasons for behaviour whose real cause it does not know. A few patients show intermanual conflict: one man's left hand would switch off a television programme he was enjoying.

Outside the laboratory both hemispheres share the same eyes, ears and body, and some information crosses through the brainstem or by cross-cueing (one hemisphere signalling to the other with a frown). Cutting the corpus callosum therefore does not kill, blind or abolish language; its cost is difficulty coordinating activities that need both hemispheres.

Exam trap

An object in the left visual field cannot be named but can be selected with the left hand; the patient is not blind in that eye or field and has not forgotten the object. The Nobel Prize went to Roger Sperry, not to Karl Spencer Lashley (1890 to 1958; the engram, 5.9), Wilder Graves Penfield (1891 to 1976; cortical stimulation mapping and the homunculus, 5.6) or Donald Olding Hebb (1904 to 1985; cell assemblies, 1949).

Broca's area and Wernicke's area

Lateralisation tells you which side; localisation tells you where within it. Broca's area lies in the left inferior frontal gyrus, in the pars opercularis and pars triangularis (areas 44 and 45, 5.2), just in front of the face area of the primary motor cortex. This frontal-lobe area serves speech production: programming articulation and assembling grammatical sentences. It is not the seat of comprehension, memory consolidation (the hippocampus, 5.9) or motor coordination (the cerebellum, 5.6).

In 1874 Carl Wernicke (1848 to 1905) described patients who spoke fluently but understood little, with damage further back. Wernicke's area is the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere (area 22), the classic centre for comprehending spoken and written language. In front of it, in Heschl's gyrus, the primary auditory cortex registers sounds without understanding them. The arcuate fasciculus, a bundle of association fibres, joins the two language areas. Behind Wernicke's area, where the temporal, parietal and occipital lobes meet, the angular gyrus (area 39) links a word's seen form to its sound and meaning, so it is vital for reading. The usual decoys have other jobs: the occipital pole holds primary visual cortex, the postcentral gyrus primary somatosensory cortex, the right parietal lobe spatial attention, and the anterior cingulate attention and error monitoring.

Exam trap

Ria speaks slowly and laboriously in keywords that make sense. 'Frontal lobe' is true but too broad, and a temporal-lobe (Wernicke's area) lesion leaves speech fluent: the answer is Broca's area, also the site after a left frontal stroke with non-fluent speech. For comprehension the answer is Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus), never the inferior frontal gyrus, Heschl's gyrus or the angular gyrus.

The Wernicke-Geschwind model and the dual streams

In 1965 Geschwind revived the ideas of Broca, Wernicke and Dejerine as the Wernicke-Geschwind model, a left-hemisphere circuit of seven parts (Figure 5.20). To repeat a heard word, the primary auditory cortex passes the sound to Wernicke's area, where it is understood; the arcuate fasciculus carries it to Broca's area, which selects an articulation programme; and the face area of the motor cortex drives the speech muscles. To read aloud, the primary visual cortex sends the written word to the angular gyrus, which converts it into its auditory code for Wernicke's area, and the same output path follows. Damage to each box or link predicts a particular aphasia.

The model's limits are serious. Removing Broca's area alone often leaves no lasting aphasia, real lesions are large and include white matter, and almost every aphasic patient has both expressive and receptive problems. Modern research favours the dual-stream model of Gregory Hickok and David Poeppel (2007). A ventral stream along the temporal lobe maps speech sounds onto meaning in both hemispheres; a dorsal stream from the temporoparietal junction to the frontal lobe, including the arcuate fasciculus, maps sounds onto articulation and is strongly left-lateralised. That is why damage to the dorsal route can spare understanding but ruin repetition.

Aphasia and its syndromes

Aphasia is an acquired disturbance of language function due to organic brain disease, most often a left-hemisphere stroke, and is the common term for all types of language loss. Dysphasia is often used for partial loss, dyslexia is a disorder of reading, and 'lexphasia' is not a term. Aphasia is a disorder of language, not of intellect, abstract thinking or memory, which fail in dementia and amnesia (5.9, 5.11). Clinicians classify aphasias by fluency, comprehension, repetition and naming (Table 5.29).

Broca's aphasia (expressive or non-fluent aphasia) is primarily a deficit of speech production. Speech is slow, effortful, poorly articulated and telegraphic, made of content words with the small grammatical words missing (agrammatism): 'Yes ... Monday ... Dad and Dick ... Wednesday nine o'clock ... doctors ... and ... teeth.' The keywords make sense, comprehension is relatively preserved, repetition is impaired, and the patient knows it and is frustrated. Right hemiparesis is common because the motor cortex lies next door. Wernicke's aphasia (receptive or fluent aphasia) is the mirror image: fluent, grammatical speech that is empty of meaning, full of paraphasias (wrong words or sounds) and neologisms (invented words), sometimes reaching word salad, with severely impaired comprehension, poor repetition and little awareness.

Reading, writing and speech beyond aphasia

Alexia is an acquired loss of reading and agraphia of writing. Joseph Jules Dejerine (1849 to 1917) linked alexia with agraphia to the left angular gyrus in 1891, and in 1892 described alexia without agraphia (pure alexia) after damage to the left occipital cortex and the splenium, the rear of the corpus callosum. His patient could write but not read what he had written: words seen by the intact right visual cortex could not cross to the left-hemisphere language areas. Such patients often cannot name colours either. Acalculia is an acquired loss of calculation. Gerstmann syndrome (Josef Gerstmann, 1920s) follows left angular gyrus damage and combines finger agnosia, left-right confusion, agraphia and acalculia.

Not every speech problem is aphasia. Dysarthria is faulty articulation from weak or uncoordinated speech muscles, and apraxia of speech is faulty programming of articulatory movements; in both, language itself is intact. Developmental dyslexia and other language-based learning difficulties are present from childhood despite adequate intelligence and teaching. Their core weakness is phonological, linked to the left perisylvian language network including Broca's area rather than to the occipital lobe (vision), cerebellum (coordination) or amygdala (emotion); the disorders are in Chapter 8. Finally, deaf signers with left-hemisphere damage develop sign-language aphasias of the Broca and Wernicke types, while right-hemisphere damage spares signing: the left hemisphere is specialised for language, not for sound.

5.11 Brain Damage, Neurodegeneration and Neuropsychological SyndromesMedium yield · 32 Qs

Much of what we know about the healthy brain comes from damaged ones: what a patient loses, and what survives, shows what the damaged part did. This section covers how brains are damaged, the major neurological diseases and the classic neuropsychological syndromes.

Causes of brain damage

Brain damage has six main causes. A brain tumour (neoplasm) is a mass of cells growing independently of the rest of the body. About 20 percent are meningiomas, encapsulated in their own membrane between the meninges, harmful only by pressure and almost always benign. Most others are infiltrating, growing diffusely into brain tissue and usually malignant; gliomas, from glial cells, are the commonest. About 10 percent are metastatic, seeded through the blood from cancers elsewhere, often the lungs.

A stroke is a sudden-onset cerebrovascular disorder. Around the dead tissue (the infarct) lies the penumbra, tissue that may recover or die over the next few days and that treatment tries to save. Cerebral haemorrhage is bleeding from a ruptured vessel, often an aneurysm. Cerebral ischaemia is a blocked blood supply, from thrombosis (a plug formed on the spot), embolism (a plug carried from elsewhere) or arteriosclerosis (walls narrowed by fat deposits). Starved neurons release excess glutamate, which overactivates NMDA receptors and floods neighbouring neurons with Na+ and Ca2+, killing them over a day or two: excitotoxicity (Section 5.3).

A closed-head traumatic brain injury leaves the skull intact. A contusion (bruised brain) forms where the brain strikes the skull, at the site of the blow (coup) and often on the opposite side (contrecoup injury). Concussion, now called mild traumatic brain injury, disturbs consciousness without structural damage, but repeated ones produce chronic traumatic encephalopathy, the dementia of boxers and American football players. The confused period after a head injury in which new memories are not stored is post-traumatic amnesia; its length is the best single guide to severity.

Infection causes encephalitis (brain inflammation): herpes simplex encephalitis attacks the medial temporal lobes (Clive Wearing, Section 5.9), bacteria cause meningitis, neurosyphilis causes general paresis (psychosis and dementia) and HIV can cause dementia. Neurotoxins include lead, mercury, alcohol (Korsakoff's syndrome, Section 5.9) and antipsychotic drugs (tardive dyskinesia, Section 5.4). Genetic causes include recessive genes, the dominant gene of Huntington's disease and the extra chromosome 21 of Down syndrome. Whatever the cause, neurons die by necrosis, passive swelling and bursting within hours that inflames nearby tissue, or by apoptosis, programmed shrinkage over a day or two without inflammation.

Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a disorder of recurrent, spontaneous seizures arising from chronic brain dysfunction; a single seizure from a high fever does not count. Neurons discharge in synchronous bursts, often because GABA inhibition fails, and the EEG shows high-amplitude spikes. Many patients first sense an aura (a strange smell, déjà vu), which betrays where the seizure starts. Generalised seizures involve the whole brain, focal (partial) seizures only part of it (Table 5.30). Simple focal seizures are sometimes called Jacksonian after John Hughlings Jackson (1835 to 1911), whose Jacksonian march is jerking that spreads from fingers to arm to face as the discharge crosses the motor homunculus. Complex focal seizures usually begin in the temporal lobe (temporal lobe epilepsy) and bring automatisms such as fumbling with buttons. The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification of 2017 grades focal seizures by awareness instead: focal aware (simple) and focal impaired awareness (complex).

Parkinson's and Huntington's diseases

Parkinson's disease, described in 1817 by James Parkinson (1755 to 1824) as the "shaking palsy", affects 1 to 2 percent of people over 65. Its signs are resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia (slowness) or akinesia (difficulty starting movement), postural instability, a masked face and a shuffling gait; loss of smell and depression may come years earlier, and dementia often follows. Dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta die, so the nigrostriatal pathway no longer delivers dopamine to the striatum (Figure 5.21); in 1960 Oleh Hornykiewicz found the patients' striatum almost empty of dopamine. Surviving nigral neurons hold Lewy bodies, clumps of alpha-synuclein. L-dopa, the dopamine precursor, which unlike dopamine crosses the blood-brain barrier, and dopamine agonists relieve symptoms for some years; deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus helps when drugs fail. In 1982 young drug users in California who injected a "synthetic heroin" contaminated with MPTP developed permanent parkinsonism (reported by J. William Langston in 1983); MPTP destroys nigral dopamine neurons in monkeys too, giving the best animal model of the disease.

Exam trap

Parkinson's disease is dopamine loss from the substantia nigra pars compacta. It is not degeneration of the locus coeruleus (noradrenaline), raphe nuclei (serotonin), spinal motor neurons (motor neuron disease, polio), hippocampal pyramidal cells (memory) or cholinergic basal forebrain (Alzheimer's), nor a serotonin, acetylcholine or GABA deficiency. The subthalamic nucleus is the stimulation target, not the lesion.

Huntington's disease, described by George Huntington in 1872, is rarer (about 1 in 7,500): chorea (jerky, dance-like involuntary movements), psychiatric symptoms and cognitive decline ending in dementia, beginning at about 40. An autosomal dominant expanded CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene on chromosome 4 (Section 5.5) kills the GABA neurons of the striatum, caudate first. Parkinson's is hypokinetic, Huntington's hyperkinetic.

Alzheimer's disease and other dementias

Dementia is an acquired, usually progressive decline of global intellect (memory, abstraction, judgement) that disrupts daily life; its criteria belong to Chapter 8, and "senile dementia" and "atrophy" are vague older labels. The commonest cause is Alzheimer's disease, described in 1906 by Alois Alzheimer (1864 to 1915) in his patient Auguste Deter. Onset is insidious and progression gradual: recent episodic memory fails first, then language (anomia), visuospatial and executive functions. The brain shows amyloid plaques (beta-amyloid, outside cells), neurofibrillary tangles (tau, inside cells) and neuron loss that begins in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus. Death of cholinergic neurons in the nucleus basalis of Meynert leaves the cortex short of acetylcholine, so treatment uses cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and memantine, an NMDA receptor antagonist. The APOE e4 allele raises risk; rare early-onset families carry APP or presenilin mutations, and because APP lies on chromosome 21, almost everyone with Down syndrome has plaques and tangles by 40.

Exam trap

Acetylcholine deficiency means Alzheimer's disease, not "senile dementia", "atrophy" or Korsakoff's syndrome (thiamine deficiency, Section 5.9). Alzheimer's is not a loss of GABA (Huntington's), noradrenaline or histamine (Section 5.3).

Multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune attack on the myelin that oligodendrocytes make in the central nervous system. Hard scars, sclerotic plaques, form in the white matter, disseminated in time and space. It usually starts in early adulthood with a relapsing-remitting course of visual disturbance, weakness, numbness and fatigue, and is commoner in women and far from the equator. Contrast alpha-synuclein deposits (Parkinson's), beta-amyloid plaques (Alzheimer's) and degeneration of lower motor neurons (polio, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, which kills upper motor neurons too).

Face-recognition loss after a stroke is an agnosia, not aphasia, apraxia or ataxia, and prosopagnosia beats the broader "visual agnosia". The lesion is fusiform, not in the primary motor cortex (movement), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (executive function) or cerebellar vermis (balance).

Apraxia (Hugo Liepmann, 1900) is inability to perform learned purposeful movements despite intact strength, sensation, coordination and comprehension. In ideomotor apraxia the patient cannot mime an act on command (show how you use a comb) though he does it naturally; in ideational apraxia he cannot sequence the steps of an act such as brushing his hair. Constructional apraxia spoils copying and block building, dressing apraxia putting on clothes. The lesion is usually left parietal or frontal.

Exam trap

"Impairment in motor performance and coordination" is dyspraxia (developmental coordination disorder; Amity 2023 key). Apraxia is acquired loss of skilled movement with coordination intact; ataxia is cerebellar incoordination; anomia is failure to name, alexia to read.

Hemispatial neglect

After right posterior parietal damage, patients with hemispatial neglect ignore the left side of space and of their own body. They eat from the right of the plate, shave only the right side of the face, bisect lines to the right and miss left targets on cancellation tests (Figure 5.22). In extinction, a left stimulus is noticed alone but missed when paired with a right one, and anosognosia is common. Left neglect is far commoner and more severe than right neglect, because the right hemisphere attends to both sides of space. Neglect is inattention, not blindness: in homonymous hemianopia the patient knows half the visual field is lost and compensates. Bilateral damage to primary visual cortex causes cortical blindness (bilateral homonymous hemianopia), sometimes denied in Anton's syndrome; hemiplegia (one-sided paralysis) follows motor cortex or internal capsule damage.

Exam trap

Right parietal lesion: left neglect. Anterograde amnesia (Section 5.9), expressive (Broca's) aphasia (Section 5.10), anosmia, prosopagnosia and bilateral homonymous hemianopia are distractors.

Phineas Gage and the prefrontal syndromes

Key study: Phineas Gage (1848)

In 1848 an explosion drove a tamping iron through the left cheek and frontal lobe of Phineas Gage (1823 to 1860), a 25-year-old railway construction foreman in Vermont. His speech, memory and intelligence survived, but the reliable foreman became impulsive, profane and poor in judgement, and soon lost his job. His physician John Martyn Harlow reported the case in 1848 and, in 1868, that friends found him "no longer Gage". In 1994 Hanna Damasio and colleagues reconstructed the injury from his skull and placed it in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Criticism: the record is thin, and Gage later drove stagecoaches in Chile for several years before dying in San Francisco, so he may have partly recovered.

Gage is the classic evidence that the frontal lobes govern personality and social behaviour. Damage to different prefrontal regions produces different frontal lobe syndromes (Table 5.33). Dorsolateral damage causes the dysexecutive syndrome: executive functions (planning, sequencing, set-shifting and inhibition, using working memory) break down, and the patient shows perseveration, repeating a response after it has stopped working. In the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test of David Grant and Esta Berg (1948) cards are sorted by colour, shape or number, the rule being learned only from feedback and changed without warning; in 1963 Brenda Milner showed that frontal patients keep sorting by the old rule. Planning is tested by the Tower of London of Tim Shallice (1982), inhibition by the Stroop test, and verbal fluency by naming words that begin with a given letter.

The Wisconsin test detects dorsolateral dysfunction, not hippocampal (long-term declarative memory), motor cortex or cerebellar damage, colour perception (visual cortex) or reflexes (spinal cord). Orbitofrontal damage gives disinhibition, not pure aphasia, cortical blindness or hemiplegia. Impaired executive function is the hallmark of the frontal lobe syndrome, not of obsessive-compulsive disorder, catatonic schizophrenia (Chapter 8) or temporal lobe epilepsy.

Recovery and plasticity after damage

When an axon is cut, its distal segment breaks up within days (anterograde degeneration, or Wallerian degeneration, after Augustus Waller, 1850), while retrograde degeneration creeps back to the cell body. Peripheral axons often regenerate, guided by Schwann cells; central axons rarely do, blocked by oligodendrocyte inhibitors and glial scars. Instead, healthy neighbouring axons grow branches into vacated synapses (collateral sprouting), and neighbouring cortex takes over lost territory (cortical reorganisation). Recovery is best with small lesions and young patients, and people with high cognitive reserve (roughly, education and intelligence) compensate better. In constraint-induced movement therapy (Edward Taub, 1993) a stroke patient's good arm is restrained while the weak arm is trained intensively, enlarging its cortical representation. Batteries such as the Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska and NIMHANS battery chart the losses (Chapter 2).

Loewi's Two Frog Hearts, Revisited

Every section of this chapter has passed through Loewi's laboratory. The nerve he stimulated was the vagus, the main parasympathetic nerve, whose rest-and-digest outflow slows the heart (5.1). The slowing was a chemical message crossing a synapse: acetylcholine binding to receptors and then broken down by acetylcholinesterase (5.3). Drugs act by imitating, boosting or blocking such messages (5.4), and Loewi's logic of changing one thing and measuring another runs through the methods of 5.5. The opposite state, the pounding heart of fear, belongs to the sympathetic division and the adrenal medulla (5.7). His idea surfaced at night, out of sleep (5.8). And when Alzheimer's disease strips the cortex of acetylcholine, the drugs prescribed are cholinesterase inhibitors, which keep Vagusstoff working longer (5.11).

Chapter summary

5.1 Biopsychology and the Organisation of the Nervous System. The biological perspective explains behaviour through the body, brain and nervous system. Biopsychology (Pinel) has six divisions and progresses by converging operations; Descartes's dualism opposes monism. The CNS is the brain and spinal cord; the PNS has somatic (voluntary) and autonomic divisions. The sympathetic division (fight or flight, Cannon 1915; T1 to L2; adrenal medulla) spends energy; the parasympathetic (rest and digest; craniosacral; vagus; acetylcholine) conserves it. A reflex arc runs afferent neuron, interneuron, efferent neuron. White matter is myelinated axons; PNS cell-body clusters are ganglia; dorsal roots are sensory, ventral roots motor. Twelve cranial nerves, the meninges, CSF and the blood-brain barrier complete the map.

5.2 The Brain: Brainstem, Diencephalon and Cerebral Cortex. The embryonic forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain become five divisions: telencephalon (cortex, limbic system, basal ganglia, corpus callosum), diencephalon (thalamus, hypothalamus), mesencephalon, metencephalon (pons, cerebellum) and myelencephalon (medulla). The medulla holds the vital centres for breathing and heart rate; the cerebellum coordinates movement and balance; the reticular activating system maintains arousal (Moruzzi and Magoun, 1949). The thalamus relays every sense except smell, and the hypothalamus governs homeostasis through the pituitary. Six-layered neocortex, about 90 percent of cortex, serves higher cognition across frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes, mapped by Brodmann (1909); commissural, association and projection fibres connect it.

5.3 Neurons, Neural Conduction, Synapses and Neurotransmitters. The neuron is the basic unit of the nervous system (Cajal's neuron doctrine; Nobel 1906, shared with Golgi); glia include astrocytes, microglia and the myelin makers, oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS). The resting potential, about -70 mV, rests on K+ permeability and the pump (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in). Summed EPSPs reaching threshold fire an all-or-none action potential (Na+ in, K+ out, peak about +50 mV), then refractory periods. Myelin gives fast saltatory conduction. Ca2+ influx triggers release; ionotropic and metabotropic receptors respond; reuptake or enzymes clear the transmitter. Glutamate excites, GABA inhibits, acetylcholine drives muscle, monoamines arise in brainstem nuclei, and endorphins control pain.

5.4 Drugs, Reward and Psychopharmacology. Psychoactive drugs act as agonists or antagonists at synapses. Tolerance (metabolic, functional, cross and conditioned, as in Siegel's 1982 heroin study) and withdrawal are distinct from addiction. Benzodiazepines raise the frequency and barbiturates the duration of GABA-A channel opening; heroin is a semisynthetic opioid; THC acts at CB1 receptors. Olds and Milner (1954) found self-stimulation sites in the septal area, and the mesolimbic pathway (VTA to nucleus accumbens) is the core reward circuit. Antipsychotics block D2, and nigrostriatal blockade causes extrapyramidal effects ending in tardive dyskinesia. SSRIs work after 2 to 4 weeks; lithium (Cade 1949) is the gold-standard mood stabiliser; disulfiram blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase.

5.5 Research Methods, Behaviour Genetics and the Developing Brain. Structural imaging (contrast X-ray, CT, MRI, DTI) shows anatomy; functional imaging (PET, SPECT, the BOLD signal of fMRI, fNIRS) shows activity, and paired-image subtraction revealed the default mode network, active at rest. EEG has the best temporal resolution, and averaging it yields ERPs such as the P300 to oddball targets. Lesions, stimulation (DBS, TMS, optogenetics), microdialysis, knockouts and CRISPR-Cas9 manipulate the brain. Mendelian inheritance and epigenetics underlie twin, adoption, linkage and GWAS designs, heritability and endophenotypes. PKU, Huntington's, fragile X, Down, Turner, Klinefelter and Prader-Willi syndromes show genetic errors. The neural tube forms at gastrulation; pruning, myelination and neuroplasticity continue for years.

5.6 Sensory Pathways and the Motor System. Sensory systems transduce energy, build receptive fields and climb from receptors through thalamic relays to primary, secondary and association cortex. In the inverted retina, lateral inhibition sharpens edges and creates Mach bands. Nasal fibres cross at the optic chiasm; the LGN's magnocellular layers carry motion, its parvocellular layers colour and detail, to V1, whose damage gives contralateral homonymous hemianopia, often with macular sparing. Dorsal 'where/how' and ventral 'what' streams leave V1. Hair cells, two somatosensory pathways and PAG analgesia complete the senses. Corticospinal fibres cross at the pyramidal decussation; mirror neurons fire for doing and watching; the basal ganglia select actions and the cerebellum corrects errors.

5.7 The Endocrine System, Stress and the Emotional Brain. Endocrine glands are ductless and release hormones into the blood; exocrine glands use ducts. Hormones are amino acid derivatives, peptides or steroids. The hypothalamus controls the pituitary, the master gland: releasing hormones reach the anterior lobe (GH, ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH, prolactin) through portal blood, while oxytocin and vasopressin, made in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, travel down axons to the posterior lobe. The adrenals run stress: the fast SAM system releases adrenaline, and the slower HPA axis (CRH, ACTH, cortisol) is braked by negative feedback. Allostasis is stability through change. Sham rage, the Papez circuit and the limbic system led to the amygdala, the hub of fear.

5.8 Sleep, Biological Rhythms, Hunger, Thirst and Sex. Circadian rhythms are timed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), entrained by light through the retinohypothalamic tract, which drives night-time melatonin from the pineal gland. Sleep EEG runs from beta and alpha waking through theta (N1) and spindles (N2) to delta slow-wave sleep (N3); REM is paradoxical, with a waking-like EEG and atonia. Sleep begins when the VLPO inhibits the arousal systems; adenosine builds sleep pressure, and orexin, lost in narcolepsy, holds waking. Ghrelin starts hunger, CCK and leptin bring satiety, VMH lesions cause obesity and LH lesions aphagia, and arcuate NPY neurons trigger eating. Masters and Johnson described excitement, plateau, orgasm and resolution.

5.9 The Biology of Learning, Memory and Amnesia. Amnesia is a pathological loss of memory: anterograde (no new memories after the onset) or retrograde (loss of earlier memories, worst for recent ones, Ribot's law). Lashley's search for Semon's engram found memories distributed across the cortex, which Hebb explained with cell assemblies. H.M.'s bilateral medial temporal lobectomy (1953) showed that the hippocampus consolidates new declarative memories but not skills; R.B., place and grid cells, K.C. and Clive Wearing refined the picture, and the amygdala strengthens emotional memories. Korsakoff's syndrome follows thiamine deficiency and damages the mammillary bodies and mediodorsal thalamus. At the synapse, NMDA-dependent LTP strengthens and LTD weakens connections, while dopamine signals reward prediction errors.

5.10 Lateralisation, the Split Brain and Language. Dax (1836) and Broca (1861, patient Leborgne, 'Tan') tied language to the left, dominant hemisphere; the Wada test (1949) and Kimura's dichotic listening right-ear advantage (1961) confirm it in about 95 percent of right-handers and most left-handers. The right hemisphere leads in visuospatial processing, faces, music and emotional prosody. Split-brain studies (Myers and Sperry 1953; Sperry and Gazzaniga, Nobel 1981) showed each hemisphere working independently. Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus, areas 44 and 45) serves speech production; Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus, area 22) comprehension. Aphasia is acquired language loss from organic brain disease: Broca's, Wernicke's, conduction, global, transcortical and anomic types.

5.11 Brain Damage, Neurodegeneration and Neuropsychological Syndromes. Brain damage comes from tumours (encapsulated meningiomas, infiltrating gliomas, metastases), strokes (haemorrhage or ischaemia, with glutamate excitotoxicity in the penumbra), closed-head injury (contusions, contrecoup, chronic traumatic encephalopathy), infections, neurotoxins and genes; neurons die by necrosis or apoptosis. Epilepsy (tonic-clonic, absence, focal seizures) is neurological but not neurodegenerative. Parkinson's disease is dopamine loss from the substantia nigra pars compacta; Huntington's disease kills striatal GABA neurons; Alzheimer's disease brings plaques, tangles and acetylcholine loss. Multiple sclerosis is autoimmune demyelination. Agnosia (prosopagnosia), apraxia, right parietal left neglect and the prefrontal syndromes (Gage, Wisconsin Card Sorting Test) complete the section.

From the past papers

  1. Parts of the autonomic nervous system are: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Sympathetic and parasympathetic (b) Central and peripheral (c) Voluntary and involuntary (d) Cerebrospinal and cortical

    Answer: (a) Sympathetic and parasympathetic. These are the two main divisions of the ANS (many books add the enteric system of the gut wall). Central and peripheral divide the whole nervous system, voluntary and involuntary describe somatic versus autonomic control, and cerebrospinal and cortical is not a division at all.

  2. Which of the following is the correct path of a reflex arc? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Afferent neuron to interneuron to afferent neuron (b) Afferent neuron to afferent neuron to interneuron (c) Afferent neuron to interneuron to efferent neuron (d) Efferent neuron to interneuron to the brain

    Answer: (c) Afferent neuron to interneuron to efferent neuron. The signal enters the spinal cord by a sensory (afferent) neuron, crosses an interneuron in the grey matter and leaves by a motor (efferent) neuron to the effector. An arc must end in an efferent neuron and closes in the spinal cord, not the brain; the knee-jerk even skips the interneuron.

  3. White matter is primarily composed of (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Myelinated axons. (b) Myelinated dendrites. (c) Cell bodies. (d) Unmyelinated axons.

    Answer: (a) Myelinated axons. The fat of the myelin sheath makes the tissue look white. Cell bodies and unmyelinated axons make up grey matter, and dendrites are not myelinated.

  4. The reticular activating system (RAS), located in the brainstem, is crucial for the regulation of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Fine motor coordination (b) Arousal, wakefulness, and attention (c) Emotional memory (d) Balance and posture

    Answer: (b) Arousal, wakefulness, and attention. The RAS, the ascending part of the reticular formation in the brainstem core, keeps the cortex awake and alert; its destruction causes coma. Fine motor coordination and balance and posture belong to the cerebellum, emotional memory to the amygdala.

  5. Which brain structure would most likely result in death if damaged? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Cerebellum (b) Medulla (c) Reticular formation (d) Pons

    Answer: (b) Medulla. The medulla oblongata holds the vital centres for breathing, heart rate and blood pressure, so its damage is the most likely to kill. Cerebellar damage causes ataxia and reticular damage coma; the pons helps set the breathing rhythm but is not the main vital centre.

  6. Which brain structure serves as the primary control center for integrating the endocrine, visceral, and autonomic nervous systems? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Cerebellum (b) Hypothalamus (c) Thalamus (d) Brainstem

    Answer: (b) Hypothalamus. It controls the autonomic nervous system and directs the pituitary, so it integrates neural and hormonal control of the body for homeostasis. The thalamus is the sensory relay, the cerebellum coordinates movement, and the brainstem carries out autonomic reflexes without integrating them with the endocrine system.

  7. Which of the following is an inhibitory neurotransmitter? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Norepinephrine (b) GABA (c) Dopamine (d) Epinephrine

    Answer: (b) GABA. GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter of the brain: at GABA-A receptors it opens Cl- channels and produces IPSPs. Noradrenaline, dopamine and adrenaline are catecholamines that modulate arousal, movement and reward; none is the standard inhibitory transmitter.

  8. Which of the following is NOT a type of glial cell? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Microglia (b) Astrocytes (c) Oligodendrocytes (d) Myelin sheath

    Answer: (d) Myelin sheath. Myelin is the fatty insulation that glial cells make (oligodendrocytes in the CNS, Schwann cells in the PNS); it is not a cell. Microglia (immune cells), astrocytes and oligodendrocytes are all glia.

  9. Which of the following is not a neurotransmitter? (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Dopamine (b) Serotonin (c) Nor-epinephrine (d) Adrenalin

    Answer: (d) Adrenalin (as keyed). Adrenaline is the odd one out because it acts mainly as a hormone of the adrenal medulla. Strictly it is also a catecholamine neurotransmitter, released by a few adrenergic brainstem neurons, so treat this as an odd-one-out question; dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline are classic brain monoamine transmitters.

  10. Valium, Xanax, Rohypnol, and Halcion are: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Benzodiazepines (b) Barbiturates (c) Sedatives (d) Hypnotics

    Answer: (a) Benzodiazepines. Diazepam, alprazolam, flunitrazepam and triazolam all belong to this one chemical class, which raises the frequency of GABA-A chloride channel opening. Barbiturates are a different class (they raise the duration of opening), and 'sedatives' and 'hypnotics' are broad functional labels that also cover alcohol and barbiturates.

  11. Which of the following substances is NOT a natural alkaloid compound? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Morphine (b) Heroin (c) Codeine (d) Papaverine

    Answer: (b) Heroin. Heroin (diacetylmorphine) is semisynthetic, made by adding two acetyl groups to morphine. Morphine, codeine, papaverine and thebaine occur naturally in opium, the dried sap of the poppy.

  12. A 45-year-old man with schizophrenia has been on antipsychotic medication for years. He presents with repetitive, involuntary, and purposeless movements of his mouth, tongue, and face, including lip-smacking and grimacing. This condition is most likely: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Akathisia (b) Acute Dystonia (c) Tardive Dyskinesia (d) Parkinsonism

    Answer: (c) Tardive Dyskinesia. Involuntary orofacial movements after months or years of neuroleptic treatment are the late, often irreversible extrapyramidal effect. Acute dystonia (spasms) comes within hours to days, akathisia (inner restlessness, pacing) within days to weeks, and parkinsonism (tremor, rigidity, slowness) within weeks.

  13. Which neuroscientific technique provides the highest temporal resolution for studying neural activity? (RML 2025)

    (a) fMRI (b) PET scan (c) EEG (d) CT scan

    Answer: (c) EEG. EEG records the brain's electrical activity directly, so it follows changes millisecond by millisecond. fMRI depends on a blood-flow response that takes seconds, PET on tracer uptake over a minute or more, and CT is a still structural image with no temporal resolution at all.

  14. In Phenylketonuria (PKU), the body fails to convert phenylalanine into which substance due to a deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Urea (b) Tyrosine (c) Tryptophan (d) Histidine

    Answer: (b) Tyrosine. Phenylalanine hydroxylase converts phenylalanine into tyrosine; in autosomal recessive PKU the enzyme is missing, so phenylalanine accumulates and damages the brain unless a low-phenylalanine diet starts at birth. Urea is a waste product of protein breakdown, tryptophan the precursor of serotonin and histidine of histamine.

  15. A male child presents with tall stature, small testes, and learning difficulties. Karyotype analysis shows an extra X chromosome. This condition is : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Klinefelter syndrome (b) Turner syndrome (c) Prader-Willi syndrome (d) Down syndrome

    Answer: (a) Klinefelter syndrome. An extra X in a male (47,XXY) gives tall stature, small testes, infertility and learning difficulties. Turner syndrome (45,X) gives a short, infertile female; Prader-Willi syndrome is a paternal deletion at 15q11 to q13; Down syndrome is trisomy 21.

  16. The Mach bands phenomenon is best explained by : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Rod-cone dichotomy in the fovea (b) Opponent-process theory of color vision (c) Lateral inhibition in the retina (d) Temporal summation in photoreceptors

    Answer: (c) Lateral inhibition in the retina. At a light-dark border, cells on the bright side receive less inhibition from their dark neighbours and cells on the dark side receive more, so the edge is exaggerated into a brighter and a darker strip. The rod-cone dichotomy concerns dim-light versus colour vision, the opponent process colour coding and afterimages, and temporal summation the adding of weak signals over time.

  17. Difficulty in seeing objects at a distance is called : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Myopia (b) Hypermetropia (c) Astigmatism (d) Visual Field Defects

    Answer: (a) Myopia. In myopia (near-sightedness) the eyeball is too long, so distant objects focus in front of the retina and blur; a concave lens corrects it. Hypermetropia blurs near objects, astigmatism comes from an irregular cornea, and visual field defects are blind areas caused by damage to the visual pathway, not focusing errors.

  18. Which of the following areas of the brain is involved in controlling movements of the body? (Amity 2023)

    (a) Primary (b) Secondary (c) Supplementary (d) All of the above

    Answer: (d) All of the above. Movement is controlled by a hierarchy: primary motor cortex (area 4, precentral gyrus) is the main cortical output, and secondary motor cortex, the premotor cortex and supplementary motor area, programmes the patterns of movement it executes. No single area does the job alone.

  19. In carrying out the 'fight or flight' response, the role of supervisor is assigned to the: (RML 2024)

    (a) Adrenal gland (b) Pituitary gland (c) Hypothalamus (d) Parasympathetic nervous system

    Answer: (c) Hypothalamus. It integrates the endocrine and autonomic systems and drives both the sympathetic nervous system and the pituitary. The pituitary and the adrenal gland carry out its orders, and the parasympathetic system calms the body instead.

  20. Which cluster of symptoms is seen in Kluver-Bucy syndrome? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Agnosia, aphasia, hyper-orality, hyperphagia, hypersexuality (b) Agnosia, avolition, alogia, asexuality (c) Hypersexuality, altered emotional response, hyperorality, hyperalertness (d) Hypersexuality, hypo-sensitivity, delayed reaction

    Answer: (a). Bilateral anterior temporal lobe and amygdala damage gives psychic blindness (agnosia), hyperorality, hyperphagia and hypersexuality, with aphasia in human cases. Avolition and alogia are negative symptoms of schizophrenia, and the animals become placid, not hyperalert.

  21. What is the term for the brain's process of regulating its own responses to stress by controlling hormone release? (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Neuroplasticity (b) Homeostasis (c) Allostasis (d) Circadian rhythm

    Answer: (c) Allostasis. Sterling and Eyer (1988) defined it as stability through change: the brain anticipates demands and adjusts hormone release. Homeostasis is Cannon's stability around fixed set points, neuroplasticity is rewiring with experience, and circadian rhythm is the 24-hour cycle.

  22. A lesion of the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) produces: (Recall set)

    (a) Loss of circadian rhythms (b) Aphagia and adipsia (c) Diabetes insipidus (d) Hyperphagia and obesity

    Answer: (d) Hyperphagia and obesity. The VMH was the classic satiety centre (Hetherington and Ranson, 1940). Aphagia and adipsia follow lateral hypothalamic lesions (Anand and Brobeck, 1951), circadian rhythms depend on the suprachiasmatic nucleus, and diabetes insipidus follows failure of ADH from the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei.

  23. Which gland regulates the circadian rhythm in the human beings? (RML 2022)

    (a) Hypothalamus (b) Cerebellum (c) Pituitary gland (d) Pineal gland

    Answer: (d) Pineal gland. The clock itself is the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, but the question asks for a gland, and the pineal gland turns the clock's signal into night-time melatonin. The cerebellum coordinates movement and the pituitary is the master gland of other hormones; neither keeps a clock.

  24. Masters and Johnson's sexual response cycle is distinct from Kaplan's model. Which of the following correctly reflects Masters and Johnson's sequence? (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Excitement, Plateau, Orgasm, Resolution (b) Plateau, Excitement, Orgasm, Resolution (c) Excitement, Desire, Plateau, Orgasm, Refractory (d) Desire, Arousal, Plateau, Orgasm, Resolution

    Answer: (a) Excitement, Plateau, Orgasm, Resolution. Masters and Johnson (1966) described these four phases, with a male refractory period after orgasm. Putting desire first is Helen Singer Kaplan's (1979) change, and no model starts with plateau.

  25. A person suffered a head injury in a road accident several years ago. Since the accident, he is unable to form new memories. He can remember many things which he did before the accident, but he cannot remember what he said ten minutes ago. The part of the brain which is most probably injured is: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Brain stem (b) Hippocampus (c) Spinal cord (d) Reticular formation

    Answer: (b) Hippocampus. Failing to form new memories while old ones survive is anterograde amnesia, the signature of hippocampal damage (H.M., R.B.). The brainstem and reticular formation serve vital functions and arousal, and the spinal cord carries reflexes and sensorimotor signals; none consolidates memory.

  26. According to Karl Lashley's research on memory storage, where did he conclude that memories are primarily stored in the brain? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) In specific localized brain regions dedicated to particular memories (b) Distributed throughout the entire brain cortex (c) Exclusively in the hippocampus (d) Only in the frontal and temporal lobes

    Answer: (b) Distributed throughout the entire brain cortex. No cortical lesion erased a maze habit in Lashley's rats; the deficit depended on how much cortex was removed (mass action), not where (equipotentiality). He found no localised engram, and the hippocampus's role in forming memories emerged only later, with H.M.

  27. Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome results in: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Lapse of attention (b) Loss of muscle coordination (c) Both a & b (d) None

    Answer: (c) Both a and b. Thiamine deficiency first causes Wernicke's encephalopathy: confusion with lapses of attention, ataxia (loss of muscle coordination) and ophthalmoplegia with nystagmus. Survivors develop the amnesia of Korsakoff's syndrome, so the combined syndrome affects attention and coordination as well as memory.

  28. The left hemisphere is: (RML 2024)

    (a) Concerned to a large extent with spatial and pattern sense (b) The minor hemisphere (c) The major hemisphere (d) Centre of musical ability

    Answer: (c) The major hemisphere. The left hemisphere is dominant for language in about 95 percent of right-handers and most left-handers, so it was called the major or dominant hemisphere. Spatial and pattern sense and musical ability are right-hemisphere strengths, and 'minor hemisphere' is the old label for the right.

  29. Cutting through a person's corpus callosum causes the person to: (RML 2024)

    (a) Die (b) Become blind (c) Be unable to speak or understand language (d) Have difficulty coordinating certain kind of activities

    Answer: (d) Have difficulty coordinating certain kinds of activities. Corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy leaves patients alive, sighted and able to speak (the left hemisphere still speaks); the cost is that the hemispheres cannot share information, which shows up in tasks needing both.

  30. Ria has difficulty enunciating words correctly and speaks in a slow and laboured way; her speech often makes sense, but it includes only keywords. Which part of her brain is damaged? (RML 2022)

    (a) Wernicke's area (b) Broca's area (c) Temporal lobe (d) Frontal lobe

    Answer: (b) Broca's area. Slow, effortful, telegraphic speech that still makes sense is Broca's (non-fluent) aphasia. 'Frontal lobe' is true but too broad; Wernicke's area and the temporal lobe produce fluent, empty speech with poor comprehension.

  31. Prosopagnosia results in an inability to : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Recognize faces (b) Distinguish tastes (c) Express feelings through facial expressions (d) Recognize objects

    Answer: (a) Recognize faces. The source key gives (b), which is wrong: prosopagnosia is a visual agnosia for faces after fusiform gyrus damage, and loss of taste is ageusia. Loss of facial expression is a motor sign (the masked face of Parkinson's disease), and failure to recognise objects is object agnosia.

  32. A patient presents with an inability to perform coordinated, purposeful movements, despite having intact motor function, sensation, and comprehension of the task. When asked to brush their hair, they cannot sequence the movements. This deficit is best described as: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Agnosia (b) Ataxia (c) Apraxia (d) Alexia

    Answer: (c) Apraxia. Loss of learned purposeful movement with strength, sensation, coordination and comprehension intact is apraxia (here ideational: the steps cannot be sequenced). Agnosia is a failure of recognition, ataxia is cerebellar incoordination and alexia is loss of reading.

  33. Which of the following neurological conditions is NOT classified as a neurodegenerative disorder? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Parkinson's disease (b) Alzheimer's disease (c) Epilepsy (d) Huntington's disease

    Answer: (c) Epilepsy. Epilepsy is recurrent seizures from synchronous neural discharge, an episodic disorder with normal function between attacks. Parkinson's (substantia nigra dopamine neurons), Alzheimer's (cortical and hippocampal neurons) and Huntington's (striatal GABA neurons) are progressive losses of neurons.
