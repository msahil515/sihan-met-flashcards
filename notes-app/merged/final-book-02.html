London, 1884. Among the displays on diet, dress and sanitation at the International Health Exhibition in South Kensington stood a long, narrow room unlike anything else in the show. For threepence a visitor could walk along a counter of instruments while attendants measured height, arm span, weight, breathing power, strength of squeeze, keenness of sight and hearing, colour sense and speed of reaction. The figures went onto a card: the visitor kept one copy and the laboratory kept the other. By the time the exhibition closed, 9,337 people had passed through Francis Galton's Anthropometric Laboratory.

Galton was not running a sideshow. He had argued in Hereditary Genius (1869) that ability is inherited, and he believed that an able mind showed itself in keen senses and quick reactions. Measure the senses accurately enough, in enough people, and you would be measuring intelligence. His data were superb: taken the same way for every visitor and from thousands of people, they showed how human traits spread around an average, and to analyse them Galton worked out the idea of correlation. At the University of Pennsylvania, James McKeen Cattell built the same kinds of measure into a battery and in 1890 gave it a name: the mental test.

Then, in 1901, Cattell's student Clark Wissler asked the obvious question. Did the tests predict anything that mattered? At Columbia University they barely related to students' grades. Careful, precise measurement had captured the wrong thing. That gap, between measuring something consistently and measuring what matters, runs through this whole chapter, and at its end we return to Galton's counter of instruments.

2.1 Psychological Testing: Nature, History, Types and EthicsLower yield · 11 Qs

Later sections each study one kind of test or one property of tests. This one builds their frame: what a test is, where testing came from, how tests are sorted and judged, how clinicians reason from scores, and testing ethics.

What a test is

Anne Anastasi (1908 to 2001), author of the standard text Psychological Testing (1954), defined a psychological test as "essentially an objective and standardized measure of a sample of behavior". Objective: the score does not depend on who gives or scores it. Standardised: everyone meets the same items, instructions, time and scoring. Sample: the test observes a small, chosen part of behaviour and infers the rest, as a doctor judges your blood from one drop. Lee J. Cronbach (1916 to 2001) stressed comparison: a test is "a systematic procedure for comparing the behavior of two or more persons".

Measurement assigns numbers to attributes by rules (Ravi scored 42); evaluation judges the number against a standard (42 is a pass); testing obtains a behaviour sample with a standardised instrument; and assessment, the widest, answers a referral question by integrating tests with interview, observation, history and records. A test yields a score; an assessment yields a reasoned conclusion about a person.

The psychometric approach measures individual differences with standardised tests, and its logic of fixed items, norms, reliability and validity serves intelligence, symptoms of psychopathology and neurological or cognitive deficits alike. Levels of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) are taught in Chapter 9.

From Chinese examinations to the MMPI

Testing is older than psychology: China examined candidates for public office under the Han dynasty (206 BC to AD 220), and under the T'ang dynasty written civil-service examinations became the rule. Modern testing has two nineteenth-century roots, one clinical: the French psychiatrist Jean-Étienne Esquirol (1772 to 1840) separated mental retardation from mental illness, grading retardation by the person's use of language, and Édouard Séguin (1812 to 1880), who trained retarded children through sensory and motor exercises, left us the form board, a performance task of fitting shapes into matching holes.

The other root is individual differences. Francis Galton (1822 to 1911) argued in Hereditary Genius (1869) that ability is inherited and, believing that all knowledge enters through the senses, measured sensory discrimination and reaction time in his London Anthropometric Laboratory of 1884 (the scene that opened this chapter). His idea of correlation became, in the hands of Karl Pearson (1857 to 1936), the product-moment correlation coefficient. Galton is called the father of mental testing and of psychometrics. James McKeen Cattell (1860 to 1944), who had worked with Wundt and Galton, coined the term mental test in his 1890 paper "Mental Tests and Measurements"; his tests, too, were sensory and motor. In 1901 Clark Wissler (1870 to 1947) found that they barely correlated with Columbia students' grades. Measuring the senses precisely was not measuring intelligence.

The breakthrough came in Paris, where Alfred Binet (1857 to 1911) and Théodore Simon tested judgement, comprehension and reasoning instead (Table 2.1). From the 1960s, the period of accountability, American courts heard challenges to tests as biased against minorities and women. Indian tests (Kamat 1934, Bhatia 1955, Malin 1969, the NIMHANS and PGIMER batteries) appear in Sections 2.6, 2.7 and 2.11.

Kinds of tests

Four ways of sorting tests matter most; Table 2.2 gives the rest.

Scoring. On an objective test a fixed key leaves no room for the scorer's judgement, so any two scorers agree: multiple-choice items, rating scales and self-report inventories such as the MMPI are objective. On a subjective test (an essay, an interview rating, a projective test such as the Rorschach) the scorer judges each free response. "Projective" names a technique of ambiguous stimuli (Section 2.10); "introjective" is no kind of test (introjection is a defence mechanism, Chapter 4).

Time. A speed test has easy items of similar difficulty under a time limit so strict that few finish; the score is how many are done, as in a clerical checking test. A power test gives generous time, or none, but its items climb in difficulty until some are too hard for anyone; the score reflects the hardest level mastered (Figure 2.1). Most real tests are speeded tests, a blend of both.

What is asked. Cronbach, in Essentials of Psychological Testing (1949), separated tests of maximum performance, which ask you to do your best under standard conditions (intelligence, aptitude and achievement tests: the ability tests), from tests of typical performance, which ask how you usually feel or act (personality, interest and attitude measures). An IQ test is an ability test.

Purpose. An intelligence test measures general mental ability; an aptitude test, the potential to learn a specific skill; an achievement test, what has already been learned; a personality test, traits, emotions, attitudes and motives; an interest inventory, liking for activities and occupations; a neuropsychological test, brain-behaviour functions in suspected brain damage; and a diagnostic test, specific weaknesses such as reading errors.

Characteristics of a good test

A good test has five characteristics:

  • Objectivity: freedom from the examiner's judgement in administration, scoring and interpretation, so that different examiners agree.
  • Reliability: consistency of scores over occasions, forms and scorers (Section 2.3). Consistency is a criterion of good measurement; high cost, low reliability and subjectivity mark poor measurement.
  • Validity: measuring what the test claims to measure (Section 2.4). Reliability is necessary but not sufficient: head size can be measured consistently, yet it is a worthless intelligence test.
  • Norms: a representative sample's performance, against which a raw score is read (Section 2.5).
  • Practicability (usability): reasonable time, length, cost and ease of scoring.

Standardisation means fixing uniform procedures for administration and scoring (the same instructions, materials, time limits and key for everyone) and establishing norms, so that scores are comparable across people and occasions. It is a precondition of validity, not a guarantee of it; it does not remove cultural bias; and it has nothing to do with computer administration.

Exam trap

RML 2024 keyed objectivity as the most important characteristic of a psychological test, following Indian texts such as A. K. Singh's that list it first: without it the score reflects the examiner. Most textbooks, and the 2014 Standards ("the most fundamental consideration"), name validity, since an objective test of the wrong thing is useless. On RML papers expect objectivity; elsewhere choose validity. Subjectivity and rationality are not test characteristics.

Building, using and misusing tests

A test is built in the eight steps of Figure 2.2, which Sections 2.2 to 2.5 take in turn (A. K. Singh counts seven, folding item analysis into the try-out).

Tests serve selection (choosing applicants), classification and placement (assigning people to categories or programmes), diagnosis and treatment planning, self-knowledge and guidance, programme evaluation and research. Their limitations: they can invade privacy; a label such as "retarded" or "gifted" may stick after the person has changed; they sample only limited aspects of behaviour; test anxiety depresses some scores; and fixed keys penalise creative examinees whose unusual answers earn no credit.

Clinical judgement and its errors

Clinicians treat assessment information as samples, correlates or signs. A sample directly resembles the behaviour of interest: how a client tackles a frustrating puzzle samples how she meets frustration. A correlate has a statistically demonstrated link with something outside the test, as a high depression-scale score goes with clinicians' ratings of depression. A sign is read as an indicator of a hidden state, as when a Rorschach response is taken to signal disordered thinking.

Interpretation can be nomothetic, placing the person against norms for everyone (her anxiety is at the 95th percentile), or idiographic, tracing what is unique to her (what this worry means in her life); good assessment uses both. In Clinical versus Statistical Prediction (1954) Paul E. Meehl (1920 to 2003) compared clinical prediction, combining the data in the clinician's head, with actuarial prediction, combining them by a formula derived from past cases. The formula usually equalled or beat the clinician, and later reviews agree.

Judgement also errs systematically. Confirmatory bias covers ignoring information that does not support one's hypothesis, ignoring information that does not fit one's stereotypes, and reading ambiguous information as support (confirmation bias in general: Chapter 1). Illusory correlation is perceiving a relation that is not there (Key study). Overconfidence grows with more data even when accuracy does not, and neglect of base rates over-diagnoses rare conditions (Section 2.4). The Barnum effect is accepting vague, universally true descriptions as uniquely accurate: in 1949 Bertram Forer (1914 to 2000) tested his students, then gave each the same "personal" sketch, largely from an astrology book ("You have a great need for other people to like and admire you"); they rated it 4.26 out of 5 for accuracy. Meehl named the effect in 1956 after the showman P. T. Barnum.

Key study: Illusory correlation (Chapman and Chapman, 1967 and 1969)

Clinicians believed Draw-a-Person signs, such as large eyes meaning suspiciousness, that research did not support. Loren Chapman and Jean Chapman showed naive students drawings, each paired at random with a symptom statement. The students "found" the very relations clinicians reported, though none existed, because the pairs were already linked in their minds (eyes go with suspicion). In 1969 they found the same for Rorschach signs: popular invalid signs were seen and valid ones missed.

The safeguards are multimethod assessment (tests, interview, observation, records), multiple informants (client, family, teachers), actuarial aids and a deliberate search for disconfirming evidence. Assessment ends in a written report (referral question, background, tests, behaviour, results, interpretation, recommendations) and feedback the client can understand. Interviewing, the mental status examination and behavioural assessment are in Chapter 7.

All three statements describe confirmatory bias: answer "All of the above". Assessment information is conceived as samples, correlates or signs; the other triads offered (factual, hypothetical or imaginary; predictions, estimates or suggestions; examples, prototypes or categories) are invented.

Ethics and responsible test use

Tests can harm as well as help. The rules are the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing of the American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association and National Council on Measurement in Education (AERA, APA and NCME; 2014 edition), Section 9 (Assessment) of the APA Ethics Code and, in India, the code of the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI), where clinical psychologists register (ethics and law in full: Chapter 7). The main duties:

  • Competence. Use only tests you are trained for. Publishers sell by test-user qualification levels: Level A (achievement and proficiency tests) needs only the manual; Level B (group intelligence, aptitude and interest tests) needs training in test construction and statistics; Level C (individual intelligence tests, clinical personality inventories, projective techniques) needs an advanced degree and supervised experience.
  • Informed consent. Explain purpose, nature, fees, who sees results and the limits of confidentiality, unless law or a court orders the testing.
  • Confidentiality and release of test data. Results go only to the client, those the client authorises, or as law requires; raw data may be withheld to prevent harm or misuse.
  • Test security. Items, manuals and protocols are kept from the public; a leaked test measures nothing.
  • Current editions and relevant norms. Obsolete tests and outdated or foreign norms mislead.
  • Fairness. A test is biased when its scores mean different things for different groups (Section 2.4).
  • Disability. Accommodations (Braille or large print, a sign-language interpreter, extra time, a scribe) remove barriers unrelated to what is measured; the report records them.
  • Translation. A translated test is back-translated into the original language by an independent translator and the versions compared; reliability, validity and norms must then be re-established.
  • Explaining results and labelling. Clients are owed an understandable explanation; labels are used sparingly, because they stigmatise and can fulfil themselves.

2.2 Building a Test: Items, Item Analysis, IRT and Response BiasLower yield · 11 Qs

A test begins as a pile of draft items, and most will not survive. The constructor writes far more than needed, tries them out and keeps those of the right difficulty that separate people high and low on the trait. Then come the biases that test-takers and raters add.

Writing items

An item is the smallest scorable unit of a test, a question or task with a scoring rule. A good item measures only its trait, is unambiguous, gives no clues, is of suitable difficulty and discriminates.

Essay items (free-response items) tap organisation and evaluation but are scored slowly and subjectively. Objective items, once called new-type items, are scored alike by anyone. In supply type items the examinee produces the answer (short answer, completion); in selection type items he chooses it: true-false or alternative response (a 50 percent guessing chance), matching (more responses than stems, to block elimination), rearrangement (put steps in order) and the multiple-choice item, with a stem (the question), a key (the correct option) and distractors, wrong options that attract those who do not know.

The writing rules make the trait alone decide the answer: one idea per item ("I enjoy parties and sports" defeats someone who likes only one), clear wording, no double negatives, no clues such as a longer key or specific determiners (always and never suggest false), plausible distractors and, on personality scales, balanced keying (half the items keyed "agree", half "disagree").

A correction for guessing removes lucky hits: S = R - W / (n - 1), with R right, W wrong (omissions excluded) and n options. Item difficulty, ceiling and floor

Item analysis uses tryout data to decide which items to keep, revise or discard, judging each by its difficulty, its discrimination and its distractors.

The item difficulty index (p) is the proportion passing the item (120 of 200 gives 0.60), so a high p means an easy item. An item everyone passes or everyone fails tells no one apart. The variance of a pass-fail item, p × q, peaks at p = 0.50, which gives maximum variance and discrimination. Guessing raises the target: for multiple choice the optimum lies midway between chance and 1.00, about 0.62 for four options, 0.75 for true-false (texts aiming at maximum reliability give 0.74 and 0.85). Most tests keep p between 0.30 and 0.70.

A ceiling effect occurs when a test is too easy: most respondents score near the maximum, so it cannot discriminate among high performers or register further gains; scores are negatively skewed (Figure 2.3). A floor effect is the reverse: the test is too difficult, scores pile up near the minimum (positively skewed) and low performers cannot be told apart. Both shrink the spread of scores, lowering reliability and validity.

Exam trap

"The test is too difficult for everyone" is a floor effect. A ceiling effect does not mean "scores are normally distributed" (they are negatively skewed) or "perfect reliability": no reliability coefficient exceeds 1, and bunched scores lower it.

Item discrimination and distractors

The item discrimination index (D) asks whether high scorers on the whole test did better on this item. Take the top and bottom 27 percent by total score, the split that Truman Lee Kelley (1884 to 1961) showed in 1939 best balances extremeness against group size; then D = pupper - plower, ranging from -1 to +1. If 40 of 50 upper and 15 of 50 lower examinees pass, D = 0.80 - 0.30 = 0.50 (and p = 0.55).

Robert L. Ebel's guide: 0.40 and above, very good; 0.30 to 0.39, reasonably good; 0.20 to 0.29, marginal; below 0.20, poor. A negative D means the lower group did better: the item is miskeyed, ambiguous (able examinees see a second defensible answer) or otherwise faulty, and must be revised or discarded.

Exam trap

D = -0.20 does not mean "well-functioning", "too easy" or "at ideal difficulty": difficulty is p, not D. A negative D says the item works backwards; check the key first.

Discrimination can also be an item-total correlation: point-biserial r (pass-fail item against a continuous total), biserial r (the pass-fail split assumed to cut a normal ability), phi (two true dichotomies) or tetrachoric r (two artificial dichotomies). Since the item is part of the total, the correlation is inflated, most in short tests; the corrected form uses the other items. The item's correlation with an outside criterion, times its standard deviation, is the item validity index.

Distractor analysis checks each option: a working distractor draws more of the lower group; a non-functioning one draws almost nobody; one drawing the upper group signals ambiguity or miskeying. Plausible distractors make an item harder and usually more discriminating. Difficulty caps discrimination: at p = 1.00 or 0, D must be 0, and D can approach 1 only near p = 0.50, though an ambiguous item can sit at p = 0.50 with D near zero. These indices assume a power test; in a speed test the last items are reached only by fast workers, so their p and D reflect speed, not content.

Item response theory

All this is classical test theory (CTT; X = T + E, Section 2.3), whose p and D depend on the sample: an item with p = 0.90 among postgraduates may have p = 0.30 in Class 8. Item response theory (IRT), or latent trait theory, models each item instead. It assumes an unobservable latent trait, θ (theta), and expresses the probability of a correct (or keyed) response as a function of the person's θ and the item's parameters.

Graphed, this is the item characteristic curve (ICC): the probability of a correct response plotted against the examinee's level on the latent trait, not against time taken, number of examinees or reliability. It is an S-shaped ogive (Figure 2.4). Its location on θ is the difficulty, b (where the probability reaches 0.50, or halfway between c and 1); its slope there is the discrimination, a; its lower asymptote is the pseudo-guessing parameter, c, the chance that a very low-θ examinee still succeeds.

The Rasch model, or one-parameter logistic model (1PL), published in 1960 by the Danish mathematician Georg Rasch (1901 to 1980), estimates difficulty only. The two-parameter model adds discrimination and the three-parameter model adds guessing; Allan Birnbaum set out these logistic models in 1968, building on the work of Frederic M. Lord (1912 to 2000) from 1952. IRT assumes unidimensionality (one trait) and local independence (at a fixed θ, responses to different items are unrelated). An item information function shows how precisely an item measures at each θ, peaking near b; summed, they give the test information function.

When the model fits, item parameters are sample-invariant and θ does not depend on which items were given, since persons and items share one scale. This allows computerised adaptive testing (CAT), which picks each next item to suit the current estimate of θ and so needs far fewer items; item banking; test equating across forms; and detection of differential item functioning (DIF), or item bias, when equally able people from two groups have different ICCs for an item (Table 2.4).

Agreeing regardless of content is acquiescence. Social desirability depends on content; central tendency is choosing the middle; halo is a rater's error, not a test-taker's.

Rating scales and rater errors

When another person judges the trait, bias moves to the rater. Rating scales may be numerical (numbered points), graphic (a line to mark), descriptive (a phrase per level), percentage (a percentile band), standard or man-to-man (known people as anchors), cumulated points or checklist (ticked statements summed) or forced-choice (equally favourable statements, curbing leniency). The behaviourally anchored rating scale (BARS) of Patricia Cain Smith and Lorne M. Kendall (1963) anchors each point with a concrete behaviour. Ranking orders people; paired comparison compares every pair.

The halo effect (halo error), named by Edward Lee Thorndike (1874 to 1949) in 1920 from officers' ratings of soldiers, is judging a person on the basis of one attribute or a general impression that colours every other rating: a clinician who sees anxiety improve rates depression improved too. 2.3 Reliability: Consistency and the Standard Error of MeasurementHigh yield · 64 Qs

Step on a bathroom scale three times in a minute, read 61, 64 and 58 kilograms, and you stop trusting it. A psychological test is a scale for something invisible, so the first question about any score is the same: measured again, would it come out the same? What follows separates score from error, estimates the error four ways, and puts an honest band around one person's score.

True scores, error and the meaning of reliability

In 1904 Charles Spearman proposed the model on which classical test theory rests: every observed score is a true score plus error, X = T + E. The true score is the average a person would earn over endless independent testings. The model assumes that errors are random, averaging zero; that they are uncorrelated with true scores; and that errors on one test are uncorrelated with errors on another. The variances therefore add: observed variance = true variance + error variance.

The reliability coefficient (rxx) is the share of observed-score variance that is true-score variance: rxx = σ2T / σ2X = 1 - σ2E / σ2X. If scores vary with variance 100, of which 80 is true and 20 error, reliability is .80. It runs from 0 (all error) to 1 (no error). The domain-sampling model sees a test's items as a sample from a large domain, such as all the words a spelling test could use; the score on the whole domain is the universe score. The more items sampled, the closer the test score comes to it, so longer tests are more reliable.

Only random error (a lapse of attention, a lucky guess, a noisy hall), which moves scores unpredictably, lowers reliability. A systematic error moves every score the same way, like a scale that always reads two kilograms heavy; being consistent, it leaves reliability intact but biases the scores and so threatens validity. Reliability therefore means consistency, dependability and freedom from measurement error. It is distinct from validity (measuring what the test claims) and sensitivity (detecting a condition or a change), both in Section 2.4, from generalizability (findings extending to other people and settings), and from fairness to groups (Section 2.1). A standardised test has uniform administration and scoring (Section 2.1); a normed test is read against a reference group (Section 2.5).

Exam trap

Reliability is "freedom from measurement error, consistency and dependability": all of the above. A study is reliable when its measures are stable on different occasions. "Conducted by a reputable researcher" is trust in a person, "findings can be generalised to other settings" is external validity, and "methods stated clearly enough to be repeated" is replicability (Chapter 9).

Stability and equivalence: test-retest and parallel forms

In test-retest reliability the same test is given to the same people on two occasions and the scores are correlated, giving the coefficient of stability. Giving a new anxiety scale to students twice, two weeks apart, and correlating the scores is the classic case. It detects time-sampling error: changes in health, mood and conditions between sittings. Too short an interval lets memory and practice inflate the coefficient; too long lets real change lower it, so a fortnight to a few weeks is usual. The method suits traits expected to stay stable over the interval, such as intelligence and personality, not rapidly changing states such as current mood, where a low coefficient reflects true change.

Exam trap

Test-retest reliability means the test gives consistent results over time. Correlating two halves is split-half reliability; two raters scoring one protocol is inter-rater reliability; correlating with a future criterion, or "measuring what it claims", is validity (Section 2.4); the client doing better the second time is a practice effect. It fits stable traits, not mood states, single-item measures or open-ended interviews.

A second method, alternate-forms reliability (parallel-forms reliability), uses two versions built to one blueprint: the same number, kind, content and format of items, equal difficulty, and equal means and variances. Given in one session, their correlation is a coefficient of equivalence, which detects content-sampling error; given weeks apart, it is a coefficient of stability and equivalence, the most demanding estimate. Parallel forms avoid remembered answers and suit retesting and speed tests, but they are costly to write, rarely truly equivalent, and practice on the type of task still carries over.

Split-half reliability and the Spearman-Brown formula

Can reliability come from one sitting? Methods that do so estimate internal consistency reliability: how well the parts of a test agree with each other within one administration. In split-half reliability one test is scored as two halves, which are then correlated. First half against second half is a poor split, since warming up, fatigue and rising difficulty make the halves unequal; an odd-even split, or random halves matched for content and difficulty, is better. The major challenge is dividing the test into two nearly equivalent halves, and different splits give different answers.

The half correlation describes a test half as long, so it underestimates the whole. Spearman and William Brown published the correction independently in 1910. The Spearman-Brown prophecy formula steps the half correlation up: rfull = 2rhalf / (1 + rhalf), so halves correlating .60 give 1.20 / 1.60 = .75. Its general form predicts reliability when length changes by a factor k (new length over old): rnew = kr / [1 + (k - 1)r]. Adding parallel items (k above 1) raises reliability, because true-score parts add up while random errors partly cancel; shortening (k below 1) lowers it. Odd-even splitting suits a power test: graded items, generous time. On a pure speed test of easy items the score is how far a person gets, so whoever reaches item 60 has about 30 odd and 30 even right. The halves match by construction and the correlation nears 1.00 spuriously, as do KR-20 and alpha from one timed sitting. Speed tests need test-retest, parallel forms or separately timed halves, each given with half the time and stepped up with Spearman-Brown. Projective and many neuropsychological tests lack comparable, objectively scored items to split. Every internal consistency method shares one limitation: a single administration says nothing about stability over time.

Exam trap

The limitation of internal consistency methods: they measure consistency within a single administration only. Needing several items and falling with mixed content are conditions of use, so a mock offering "all of the above" still keys the single administration.

Kuder-Richardson formulas and coefficient alpha

Why trust one arbitrary split? In 1937 G. Frederic Kuder and Marion Richardson derived an internal consistency coefficient from item statistics. KR-20 suits items scored dichotomously (right or wrong, yes or no) that may vary in difficulty: KR-20 = [k / (k - 1)] × [1 - Σpq / σ2X], where k is the number of items, p the proportion passing an item, q = 1 - p, and σ2X the variance of total scores.

The product pq is the item's variance: an item passed by 30 percent has pq = .30 × .70 = .21, and the maximum is .25 at p = .50. Items measuring one thing covary, so total variance far exceeds the summed item variances. For 20 items with Σpq = 3.2, mean 12 and variance 16: 3.2 / 16 = .20, 1 - .20 = .80, and × 20 / 19 gives .84. KR-21 assumes equal item difficulty, replacing Σpq with M(k - M) / k, so it needs only k, mean and variance: KR-21 = [k / (k - 1)] × [1 - M(k - M) / (kσ2)]. For the same test, 12 × 8 = 96, 96 / (20 × 16) = .30, 1 - .30 = .70, and × 20 / 19 gives .74. When item difficulties differ, KR-21 is lower than KR-20: a quick, conservative estimate.

Lee Cronbach (1951) generalised KR-20 to items with several score points, such as Likert ratings. The resulting coefficient alpha is α = [k / (k - 1)] × [1 - Σσ2i / σ2X]: five Likert items with variances summing to 6 and total variance 20 give 1.25 × .70 = .875. For 0 or 1 items σ2i = pq, so alpha equals KR-20. Cronbach showed that alpha is the mean of all possible split-half coefficients (each by Rulon's or Flanagan's formula); averaging over every split increases the accuracy of the estimate. Cyril Hoyt (1941) reached the same value by analysis of variance of persons by items: r = (MSpersons - MSresidual) / MSpersons.

For alpha, as for any reliability coefficient, about .70 is acceptable for research and group comparisons, decisions about an individual patient need .80 to .90 or more, and .40 is poor: the items do not hang together. Alpha rises with test length, very high values can mean redundant items, and a high alpha does not prove that a test measures a single trait.

Exam trap

Cronbach's alpha measures internal consistency reliability: how well a scale's items hang together. It is not validity of any kind (content, construct, concurrent, predictive or criterion), not test-retest, parallel-forms or inter-rater reliability, and not a variance or regression statistic. KR-20 differs from alpha only in being limited to dichotomous items, whatever their content.

Agreement between scorers

Scorer or inter-rater reliability is the degree to which two or more clinicians agree when independently scoring or interpreting the same test responses. It matters wherever judgement enters, since scorer disagreement becomes error in the score: projective protocols, essays, behaviour observation and psychiatric diagnosis. Agreement of two tests on one construct is convergent validity, predicting future behaviour is predictive validity, and items relating to each other is internal consistency.

Simple percentage agreement flatters raters, since some agreement is chance. Jacob Cohen (1960) corrected for it with Cohen's kappa for categorical judgements: κ = (Po - Pe) / (1 - Pe). Two clinicians classify 100 patients: both say depressed for 40 and not depressed for 30, so observed agreement Po = .70. Clinician A calls 50 depressed and B calls 60, so chance agreement Pe = (.50 × .60) + (.50 × .40) = .50, and κ = .20 / .50 = .40. Seventy percent raw agreement is only fair beyond chance. J. Richard Landis and Gary Koch (1977) labelled kappa below 0 poor, .00 to .20 slight, .21 to .40 fair, .41 to .60 moderate, .61 to .80 substantial and .81 to 1.00 almost perfect, so .85 is almost perfect. The standard error of measurement

A reliability coefficient describes a group. A psychologist facing Ravi, whose IQ came out at 110, needs to know how far his true score might lie from 110. The standard error of measurement (SEM), the standard deviation of the errors one person's scores would show over many retestings, answers that: SEM = SD × √(1 - rxx).

With SD 15 and reliability .91, SEM = 15 × √.09 = 15 × .30 = 4.5; with reliability .80, SEM = 15 × √.20 = about 6.7. At r = 1 the SEM is 0; at r = 0 it equals the SD. The true score lies within ±1 SEM of the observed score about 68 percent of the time and within ±1.96 SEM 95 percent of the time, so Ravi's true IQ is probably 105.5 to 114.5 (68 percent) or 101.2 to 118.8 (95 percent), as Figure 2.6 shows.

A difference score is unreliable when its two tests correlate highly: rdd = [(rxx + ryy) / 2 - rxy] / (1 - rxy), so reliabilities .90 and .80 with a correlation of .70 give .15 / .30 = .50, because the shared true score cancels and the errors remain. A composite score from several positively correlated tests is usually more reliable than its parts.

The index of reliability, √rxx, is the correlation between observed and true scores (√.81 = .90) and caps validity (Section 2.4). The estimated true score, M + rxx(X - M), regresses towards the mean: an IQ of 130 with reliability .90 gives 100 + .90 × 30 = 127, because extreme scores contain some luck. Spearman's (1904) correction for attenuation, rxy / √(rxx ryy), estimates the correlation between perfectly reliable measures: a predictor and criterion correlating .36, with reliabilities .81 and .64, give .36 / (.90 × .80) = .36 / .72 = .50 (Section 2.4). Divide by the root; multiplying (.26) or dropping the root (.69) are the usual slips.

What raises or lowers reliability

Reliability belongs to scores from a particular group under particular conditions, not to a test for all time.

  • Length. More parallel items raise it, as Spearman-Brown predicts.
  • Group heterogeneity. A wide range of ability raises r; restriction of range (testing only admitted students) lowers it.
  • Item difficulty and discrimination. Items near p = .50 with high item-total correlations add most; items everyone passes or fails add nothing (Section 2.2).
  • Guessing. It adds random error, most on true-false items.
  • Speededness and interval. Speed inflates single-session estimates; a longer retest interval lowers test-retest coefficients.
  • Scoring and conditions. Objective keys raise reliability; subjective scoring, ambiguous items, noise, fatigue and low motivation lower it.

Cronbach and Goldine Gleser (1965) applied the bandwidth-fidelity dilemma from information theory to testing: a test covering many traits briefly (wide bandwidth) measures each with low precision (fidelity), while a narrow test measures one trait precisely but says little else.

A G study estimates these variance components; a D study (decision study) forecasts dependability under other designs, such as two raters instead of one. The result is a generalizability coefficient, of which classical reliability is a special case. 2.4 Validity and the Accuracy of Test DecisionsMedium yield · 32 Qs

A bathroom scale that always reads two kilograms light is perfectly consistent and consistently wrong. Section 2.3 asked whether a test measures consistently; this section asks whether it measures the right thing, and then follows its scores into the decisions they drive.

What validity means

The classic definition, associated with Henry Edward Garrett (1894 to 1973), is that validity is the extent to which a test measures what it claims (purports) to measure. Validity is specific to a purpose and a population (a test valid in one culture need not be valid in another), it is a matter of degree, and every new use needs new evidence.

The American Psychological Association's Technical Recommendations of 1954 named content, predictive, concurrent and construct validity, and the 1966 Standards merged predictive and concurrent into criterion-related validity, giving the classical trinity of content, criterion-related and construct validity. In 1989 Samuel Messick replaced the trinity with a unified view of validity: one integrated judgement of how well evidence and theory support the interpretations and uses of scores, so validity belongs to an inference drawn from scores, not to the test itself. The 2014 Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing list five sources of evidence: test content, response processes, internal structure, relations with other variables, and the consequences of testing.

Face validity and content validity

Face validity is the appearance of validity: on superficial inspection a test seems to measure what it claims, because its questions intuitively seem relevant, as when a depression questionnaire asks "I feel sad most of the day". It is judged by test takers and lay people, not by experts or by any statistic, so it is not validity in the technical sense. It still earns rapport and acceptance, but an obvious purpose makes faking good or faking bad easy, which is why some inventories use subtle items (Section 2.9).

Content validity is how adequately the items sample the whole domain: a depression scale should cover mood, interest, sleep, appetite, energy, guilt, concentration and suicidal thinking, not sadness alone. The developer drafts a table of specifications (test blueprint), a grid of content areas against objectives that fixes the number of items in each cell, and experts then judge every item. C. H. Lawshe quantified their judgement in 1975 with the content validity ratio, CVR = (ne − N/2) / (N/2), where ne of N experts rate an item essential; it runs from −1 to +1. Item validity means each item belongs to the domain; sampling validity means the items together cover all of it. The two threats are construct under-representation (part of the domain left out) and construct-irrelevant variance (scores also reflecting something else, as when a mathematics test set in English partly measures reading). Content validity is the main evidence for achievement tests.

Exam trap

Face validity has nothing to do with faces: "facial expressions used for diagnosis" and "a scale of emotional responding" are invented distractors, and "a hypothetical attribute that may not be directly observable" defines a construct (2.4.5). Face validity is judged by lay people; content validity by experts against a blueprint.

Criterion-related validity: concurrent and predictive

A criterion is an independent, external measure of the attribute, such as a psychiatrist's diagnosis, a gold-standard scale, grades or job ratings, and criterion-related validity is the correlation between test and criterion. Its two types differ only in timing.

In concurrent validity test and criterion are measured at about the same time. The classic design gives a new intelligence test together with an established one; likewise, a new 20-item depression questionnaire that correlates r = 0.85 with a gold-standard scale completed the same day shows concurrent validity. A high correlation with an established measure of the same attribute shows that the test reflects present status. In predictive validity the criterion is measured later, as when entrance scores are correlated with first-year marks. Selection tests need it, and it usually runs lower than concurrent validity because people change in the interval. A criterion recorded before the test gives postdictive validity.

The correlation is the validity coefficient; its square, the coefficient of determination, is the share of criterion variance the test explains (r = 0.60 explains 36 percent). The standard error of estimate, SEest = SDy √(1 − r2), is the error of an individual prediction: with r = 0.60 and a criterion SD of 10 marks it is 8 marks. It is not the standard error of measurement (Section 2.3).

Exam trap

Concurrent and predictive are the two criterion-related types; face, content, factorial and construct validity are not criterion based. RML 2024 keyed "predictive validity" as the one NOT criterion based: that key is wrong. Of its options, face validity (appearance only) and factorial validity (construct evidence) are not criterion based; face validity is the safer mark. RML 2021 defined concurrent validity as "scores correlate highly with scores from tests that measure the same attribute"; "two tests done at the same time" is only the timing, "two clinicians agree" is inter-rater reliability, and split-half or retest correlations are reliability (Section 2.3).

Criteria, cut-offs and cross-validation

A coefficient is no better than its criterion, and finding a good one is the criterion problem. A good criterion is relevant, reliable (an unreliable one caps the coefficient, 2.4.6), free from bias and available at reasonable cost. In criterion contamination the criterion is influenced by knowledge of the predictor scores, or includes predictor-related content, so the correlation is inflated artificially: supervisors who know employees' selection-test scores tend to rate high scorers highly. The cure is blind ratings. Criterion deficiency is the opposite flaw, a criterion that omits important parts of performance, and plain random error in the criterion lowers rather than inflates the coefficient.

Restriction of range also lowers validity: grades exist only for students the entrance test admitted, and their narrow band of scores underestimates the test. Items chosen because they correlated with the criterion in one sample capitalise on chance, so the coefficient falls in a fresh sample. Re-checking it there is cross-validation, and the fall is shrinkage, largest with small samples and many items tried for few kept.

An expectancy table shows the chance of criterion success at each band of test scores. A cut-off score divides those selected or diagnosed from the rest, and since no test is perfectly valid every cut-off makes some errors (2.4.8). Incremental validity (Lee Sechrest, 1963) is the prediction a test adds beyond measures already available, such as school marks. Synthetic validity, a term of Lawshe's, infers validity for a job too small to study directly from known validities for its components.

Construct validity

What is the criterion for anxiety? Many attributes have none. A construct is a hypothetical or inferred attribute, such as intelligence, anxiety or extraversion, that cannot be directly observed but is used to explain behaviour. Construct validity, introduced in the 1954 recommendations and set out by Lee Joseph Cronbach (1916 to 2001) and Paul Everett Meehl (1920 to 2003) in 1955, is the degree to which a test measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure. They placed each construct in a nomological network, the web of lawful relations the theory predicts with other constructs and with behaviour. Validation then tests whether scores behave as the network predicts.

Convergent validity is a high correlation with other measures of the same construct; discriminant validity (divergent validity) is a low correlation with measures of theoretically unrelated constructs. A new resilience scale should correlate highly with an established resilience measure and weakly with an unrelated trait. Donald Thomas Campbell (1916 to 1996) and Donald W. Fiske showed in 1959 how to test both at once.

Key study: Campbell and Fiske's multitrait-multimethod matrix (1959)

Does a score reflect the trait, or only the way it was measured? Campbell and Fiske measured two or more traits, each by two or more methods (self-report, peer rating, observation), and intercorrelated everything in a multitrait-multimethod matrix. The monotrait-heteromethod values (same trait, different methods) should be high, showing convergence, and higher than the heterotrait-monomethod values (different traits, same method). When different traits correlate highly merely because both were self-reported, the matrix has exposed method variance, which proved common in the published matrices they examined. Criticism: the criteria are judged by eye, without a single statistical test, so confirmatory factor analysis is now used to model the matrix.

Factorial validity is shown when items load on the expected factor in exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis, evidence of internal structure (the technique is in Chapter 9). In the known-groups method (contrasted groups) the test separates groups known to differ, such as depressed inpatients and community volunteers. Scores should also change with development as the theory expects and move with experimental interventions (anxiety scores falling after therapy). Internal consistency (Section 2.3) supports a construct claim but cannot establish it alone. Consequential validity, Messick's addition, weighs the social consequences of using the scores; ecological validity is how well test performance represents everyday functioning, a key question in neuropsychology. The validity of research designs (internal, external, statistical conclusion and the construct validity of a manipulation) belongs to Chapter 9.

Exam trap

Construct validity was keyed as "the degree to which a test measures what it claims to measure" (RML 2025) and as "the level to which the theoretical construct is related to other similar measures" (RML 2021); in the 2021 paper "scores correlate highly with tests of the same attribute" defined concurrent validity. The 2025 wording is Garrett's general definition, which fits because the unified view treats all validity as construct validity. The distractors are reliability (consistency across administrations or among items), content validity (the whole domain) and inter-rater reliability (rater agreement). For discriminant validity choose the weak correlation with an unrelated construct; a high correlation with an established measure of the same construct is convergent, "covers all facets" is content and "predicts future coping" is predictive.

Reliability and validity together

A test can be reliable without being valid: a scale with high internal consistency but low criterion validity consistently measures something, but not the intended construct. It cannot be valid without being reliable, because scores that are mostly random error correlate with nothing. Reliability is therefore necessary but not sufficient for validity; consistency does not make a test valid, and validity is more than consistency.

A validity coefficient cannot exceed √(rxx × ryy), the square root of the product of the test and criterion reliabilities. With a perfectly reliable criterion this validity ceiling is √rxx, the index of reliability: rxx = 0.81 allows at most 0.90. Run in reverse, the logic gives the correction for attenuation of Charles Edward Spearman (1863 to 1945), published in 1904, which estimates the correlation if both measures were perfectly reliable: rc = rxy / √(rxx × ryy). An observed 0.36 with reliabilities of 0.81 and 0.64 becomes 0.36 / 0.72 = 0.50.

One apparent conflict remains. Very high homogeneity maximises internal consistency but narrows coverage, so a broad criterion is often predicted better by a heterogeneous test with moderate inter-item correlations: the bandwidth-fidelity trade-off of Section 2.3 seen from the validity side.

Exam trap

The TRUE statement (RML 2024) is "a test can be reliable without being valid". "If a test measures what it is intended to measure, then by definition it is reliable" is the near-miss: a valid test must be reliable, but not by definition. Validity need not come "first chronologically". The ceiling is the square root of reliability, not reliability itself, its square or reliability minus the SEM.

Sensitivity, specificity and predictive values

Once a test is used to decide (depressed or not, select or reject), validity shows up as right and wrong calls. Crossing the test result with the true state, set by a gold-standard diagnosis, puts every person in one of four cells (Figure 2.7): a true positive, a false positive (positive without the condition), a false negative (the condition missed) or a true negative.

Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN) is the true positive rate: the proportion of people who have the condition whom the test correctly identifies. Specificity = TN / (TN + FP) is the proportion of people without the condition whom the test correctly clears. Both describe the test itself at a given cut-off. A screen with sensitivity 90 percent and specificity 70 percent calls 90 percent of depressed people depressed and 70 percent of healthy people healthy. A high false positive rate means normal people are diagnosed as disordered; false negatives leave disordered people undetected. The proportion of all decisions that are correct is the hit rate.

The clinician, though, holds a result, not a diagnosis, and needs the rows. The positive predictive value, PPV = TP / (TP + FP), is the probability that a positive result is correct, and the negative predictive value, NPV = TN / (TN + FN), the probability that a negative one is. Both depend heavily on the base rate (prevalence) of the condition in the people tested, as Meehl and Albert Rosen showed in 1955 (Table 2.10).

An over-strict diagnosis produces false negatives, not false positives.

Cut-offs, ROC curves and selection

Sensitivity and specificity hold only for one cutting score. Lower the cut-off and sensitivity rises while specificity falls; raise it and the reverse happens. The choice weighs the costs of the two errors: missing a suicidal patient is worse than a false alarm, so screens use lenient cut-offs. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC curve), borrowed from signal detection theory, plots sensitivity (true positive rate) against 1 − specificity (false positive rate), one point for each possible cut-off (Figure 2.8). A useless test follows the diagonal; a better one bows toward the top-left corner. The area under the curve (AUC) summarises discrimination: 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect, and it equals the probability that a randomly chosen case scores higher than a randomly chosen non-case.

In selection, the selection ratio is the proportion of applicants who can be accepted (50 posts for 500 applicants gives 0.10). H. C. Taylor and J. T. Russell published the Taylor-Russell tables in 1939, giving the percentage of selectees who will succeed for a given validity coefficient, selection ratio and base rate. Even a modest validity helps a great deal when the selection ratio is low, and little when almost everyone must be accepted or the base rate is extreme. Utility analysis, developed by Cronbach and Goldine Gleser (1957), adds costs and gains: a test is worth using when better decisions repay the cost of testing.

2.5 Norms, Standard Scores and Interpreting a ScoreMedium yield · 32 Qs

A ten-year-old answers 34 reasoning items correctly. Is that good? Until you know how hard the items were and how other ten-year-olds do, the raw score (items passed or points earned) means nothing; it needs a frame of reference. There are two such frames, and the commoner one, comparison with other people, rests on norms, on derived scores and on the normal curve that ties them together.

Norm-referenced and criterion-referenced scores

In norm-referenced interpretation a score is compared with those of a norm group or standardisation sample, a representative sample of the people the test is meant for. "Higher than 90 per cent of her peers", "5th in his class" and "1 standard deviation (SD) above the mean" are norm-referenced: they rank a person among others, as selection requires. In criterion-referenced interpretation the score is compared with a fixed standard, whatever others do: "85 per cent of the addition problems correct, meeting the mastery cut-off". Robert Glaser drew the distinction in 1963. A criterion-referenced test asks what a person can do, so a whole class can pass it, or no one.

A domain-referenced test samples items from a defined domain (all two-digit additions, say), so the percentage correct estimates how much of the domain the person commands; a mastery test adds a cut-off and reports pass or fail, like a driving test. Norms are not standards: they describe how people do perform, not how they should, so matching the norm of a poorly taught school is not being good enough. An ipsative score compares a person with no one else: it expresses one trait relative to the same person's other traits, as in forced-choice inventories (Section 2.9), and cannot be compared across people.

Exam trap

Of the usual options, only "85 per cent correct, meeting the mastery cut-off" is criterion-referenced; percentile standing, class rank and "1 SD above the mean" are norm-referenced. Norm-referencing compares with a representative standardisation sample, not with a cut-off, an absolute criterion or the person's own earlier scores.

Standardisation and the norm sample

The process of standardisation means giving and scoring a test identically for everyone (same instructions, materials, time limits and scoring rules) and giving it so to a large sample whose scores become the norms. The test maker defines the target population, the people the test is meant for, and samples it: simple random sampling is ideal but rarely practical, so stratified sampling (quotas matching the census on age, sex, region, residence and socio-economic status) or cluster sampling (whole schools chosen at random) is used (Section 9.3). Good norms are large, representative, recent (the Flynn effect, a rise of about 3 intelligence quotient (IQ) points a decade, makes old norms too easy; Section 1.9), relevant to the person tested and fully described in the manual. Hence national norms, local norms (one region or school) and subgroup norms (by age, sex, grade, language or occupation). American norms compare an Indian child with American children, so Indian versions of Western tests were renormed (Section 2.6).

Standardisation lets the clinician do all three of the following: see where a client's score falls on the test's distribution of scores; estimate from it whether the client might meet the diagnostic criteria for a disorder (an IQ about 2 SD below the mean is one criterion for intellectual disability, Section 2.7); and estimate how probable that diagnosis is. Computers now score tests against the norm tables and write narrative reports (as for the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory-2, MMPI-2), saving time and errors, though not replacing clinical judgement.

Age and grade norms; ratio and deviation IQ

The oldest derived scores express performance as the age or school grade at which it is typical. An age-equivalent norm is the average performance at each age; Alfred Binet's 1908 scale grouped items by age, and the age level a child reached was the mental age (MA). A grade-equivalent norm does the same for school grades, interpolating in-between values. Both mislead. Growth is uneven (faster at 2 than at 14), so a year is not a fixed unit; traits such as personality do not grow with age; grade equivalents in different subjects are not comparable; and a Class 4 child with an arithmetic grade equivalent of 7.2 has not mastered Class 7 work, only scored on a Class 4 test as an average Class 7 pupil is estimated to.

William Stern divided MA by chronological age (CA) in 1912, and Lewis Madison Terman multiplied by 100 in the 1916 Stanford-Binet: the ratio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100, so MA 8 and CA 10 give 80. It fails for adults, because mental age stops rising in the late teens (a 40-year-old with an MA of 16 would score 40). David Wechsler's Wechsler-Bellevue scale (1939) introduced the deviation IQ, a standard score comparing a person with same-age peers, with a mean of 100 and an SD of 15 at every age (Sections 1.9 and 2.6).

Exam trap

MA 8 and CA 10 give 80; 125 inverts the ratio (CA ÷ MA). The deviation IQ is not "only for adults", is not less reliable and is not built from unstandardised raw scores: it is a standard score against age peers.

Percentile ranks

The percentile rank (PR) is the percentage of the norm group scoring below a given raw score (some books say "at or below"). If 70 per cent of the sample scored below Meera's 65, her PR is 70 and her score is the 70th percentile (P70). It is not the percentage correct, a raw score: 70 per cent correct could be the 30th percentile on an easy test and the 99th on a hard one. The quartiles cut the distribution into four (Q1 = P25; Q2 = P50, the median; Q3 = P75) and deciles into ten (D5 = P50).

Percentiles are ordinal, with unequal units. Scores crowd around the middle of a normal distribution, so a small raw difference there jumps many percentile points while a large one in the tails moves few: the 50th to the 60th percentile is a quarter of an SD (IQ 100 to 104), the 90th to the 99th more than a whole SD (IQ 119 to 135). So percentiles exaggerate differences near the middle, hide them in the tails and should not be averaged; equal-unit standard scores solve this.

The z score

A standard score states how far a score lies from the mean in SD units. The basic one is the z score: z = (X − M) ÷ SD, with X the raw score and M and SD those of the norm group. z scores have a mean of 0 and an SD of 1; z = +1.0 is exactly one SD above the mean, and negative values lie below it. Their great use is comparing results from different distributions. Arjun scores 60 in psychology (mean 50, SD 5) and 70 in statistics (mean 60, SD 10). The raw scores favour statistics, but psychology is z = +2.0 and statistics z = +1.0, so psychology was his better paper.

Look-alikes answer other questions (Sections 9.8 and 9.9). The variance (SD squared) and the semi-interquartile range, (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2, measure the spread of a whole distribution; the coefficient of variation, (SD ÷ M) × 100, compares spread across distributions in different units; the standard error is the SD of a sampling distribution, such as that of sample means (the standard error of measurement is in Section 2.3); the median, not z, describes the centre of a skewed distribution. Only z places one person within a distribution.

Exam trap

The measure of how far a data point lies from the mean in SD units, which lets scores from different distributions be compared, is the z score. It is not a measure of variability or of the central tendency of a skewed distribution.

The normal curve: from z to percentile

Why does z = +1 always mean the 84th percentile? Because test scales are built on the normal curve, the symmetrical bell in which mean, median and mode coincide, and its areas are fixed: about 34.1 per cent of cases lie between the mean and 1 SD on each side, 13.6 per cent between 1 and 2 SD and 2.1 per cent between 2 and 3 SD. So about 68 per cent fall within ±1 SD, 95 per cent within ±2 SD and 99.7 per cent within ±3 SD (Figure 2.9). The z table of areas under the curve is derived from the normal distribution and gives the proportion of cases below any z.

The landmarks: z = 0 is the 50th percentile; +1 the 84th and −1 the 16th; +0.5 about the 69th; +1.5 about the 93rd; +2 about the 98th (97.7) and −2 the 2nd; +3 the 99.9th. With IQ (mean 100, SD 15), 100 is the 50th percentile, 85 the 16th, and 130, two SDs above the mean, about the 98th, so only about 2 in 100 score higher. On a Binet-type scale with an SD of 16, an IQ of 136 is z = 36 ÷ 16 = 2.25; the z table gives .9878 below it, so about 98.8 per cent score lower (98.7 if the entry is truncated).

Exam trap

±2 SD holds about 95 per cent (exactly 95 within ±1.96, so 97.5 per cent lies below +1.96); ±1 SD holds 68 and ±3 SD 99.7. The z table comes from the normal distribution, not from the SD, t scores or random sampling. The 84th percentile is z = +1, not +1.5 (93rd) or +2 (98th).

Linear and normalised scores; the T score

z scores carry signs and decimals, so other standard scores rescale them: new score = new mean + (new SD × z). This is a linear transformation (new = a + b × old), as are z itself and the deviation score X − M; it keeps the raw distribution's shape, so skewed raw scores stay skewed. A normalised standard score (an area, or non-linear, transformation) instead turns each raw score into its percentile rank, looks up the z that cuts off that percentage of the normal curve and rescales it, forcing a normal shape. Stanines, stens, the C scale, normalised T scores and most modern intelligence-test scales are normalised, which is justified only with large samples and when the skew lies in the test, not the trait.

The T score of William A. McCall (1922) has a mean of 50 and an SD of 10: T = 50 + 10z, usually 20 to 80 (±3 SD). Computed from z it is linear; McCall's own scale was normalised. T scores are the metric of the MMPI and most personality inventories, including the Revised NEO Personality Inventory (NEO-PI-R; Section 2.9). T 70, 2 SD above the mean (about the 98th percentile), was the original MMPI's clinical cut-off; the MMPI-2 uses T 65, 1.5 SD above (about the 93rd).

Exam trap

T scores have a mean of 50 and an SD of 10, not 100 and 15 (IQ), 0 and 1 (z) or 500 and 100 (CEEB). T 70 = +2 SD, T 65 = +1.5 SD, T 60 = +1 SD.

Stanines, stens and the C scale

The stanine ("standard nine"), developed by the United States Army Air Forces in World War II, runs from 1 to 9 with a mean of 5 and an SD of about 2. Stanines are assigned by rank: the lowest 4 per cent of the norm group get 1, the next 7 per cent 2, and so on in the proportions 4, 7, 12, 17, 20, 17, 12, 7 and 4 per cent. Each band but the open-ended 1 and 9 is half an SD wide. Stanine 5 spans z = −0.25 to +0.25, the 40th to 60th percentile, and holds 20 per cent of cases; stanine 4 covers about the 23rd to 40th percentile, and 9 begins at the 96th (Figure 2.9). Built from percentile ranks, stanines are a normalised, non-linear transformation. Stanines 1 to 3 are below average, 4 to 6 average and 7 to 9 above average; single digits are easy to record but coarse.

The sten ("standard ten") runs from 1 to 10 with a mean of 5.5 and an SD of 2. It has no middle band: stens 5 and 6 lie just below and above the mean, and 4 to 7 cover the middle 68 per cent. Stens are the scale of Raymond Bernard Cattell's Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire (16PF, 1949), not of the Wechsler scales, the Stanford-Binet or the Rorschach. J. P. Guilford's C scale (1954) extends the stanine to 11 units (0 to 10; mean 5, SD 2) by splitting off about the extreme 1 per cent at each end.

Exam trap

Stanine 5 holds 20 per cent, not 17 (stanines 4 and 6). Of z, 50 + 10z, X − M and the stanine, only the stanine is a non-linear transformation.

Scaled scores, deviation IQs and CEEB scores

Each Wechsler subtest is reported as a scaled score (mean 10, SD 3, range 1 to 19), and the index scores and Full Scale IQ as deviation IQs (mean 100, SD 15). A scaled score of 10 is exactly average, the 50th percentile; 7 is 1 SD below (about the 16th percentile, like an IQ of 85), which the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, fourth edition (WAIS-IV), calls low average; 13 is 1 SD above (84th); 4 is 2 SD below (2nd), the borderline region. The Stanford-Binet used an SD of 16 from Form L-M (1960) through the fourth edition (1986), returning to 15 in the fifth (2003), and Cattell's Culture Fair Intelligence Test also uses 16. So IQ 130 is z = +2.0 (97.7th percentile) on a Wechsler scale but +1.875 (97th) on an SD-16 scale: compare tests through z. A CEEB score (College Entrance Examination Board) has a mean of 500 and an SD of 100, as on the older SAT and Graduate Record Examinations (GRE). Tables 2.11 and 2.12 summarise them all.

2.6 Intelligence Tests: Binet, Wechsler, Non-verbal and Indian ScalesMedium yield · 35 Qs

In 1904 the French education ministry asked which Paris schoolchildren could not keep up without special help. The answer, a list of graded tasks, founded psychology's most used family of tests. This section follows it through the Binet, Wechsler and Kaufman scales, the non-verbal tests and the Indian adaptations; theories of intelligence and the IQ itself were introduced in Section 1.9.

What an intelligence test can and cannot do

An individual test is given by one examiner to one person, who can be watched and questioned (the Stanford-Binet, the Wechsler scales); a group test is given to many people at once. A verbal test uses language in its items; a non-verbal test keeps language out of the items; a performance test asks the person to handle materials, such as blocks or a form board, usually against the clock.

What does a good IQ test offer? High test-retest reliability, high internal consistency (both above .90 for the major full-scale IQs) and good validity for predicting school achievement (it correlates about .5 with grades). It does not offer a good prediction of behaviour in general: honesty, persistence and social skill depend on personality, motivation and circumstance.

Intelligence tests diagnose intellectual disability and giftedness, guide educational placement, set a neuropsychological baseline and document eligibility for a disability allowance, but not for housing benefit (an income question), suicide risk (a clinical risk assessment, Chapter 7) or, with an adult scale, a child's special educational needs. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) is an intelligence test, not a personality, aptitude (predicting future success), attitude or achievement (measuring past learning) test (Sections 2.8 and 2.9), and it does not look for neurological soft signs, subtle non-localising signs such as clumsiness.

Exam trap

"An IQ test does NOT provide ..." is answered by "a good predictor of behaviour". High test-retest reliability, high internal consistency and good validity (for school achievement) are exactly what it does provide.

The Binet scales

Alfred Binet (1857 to 1911) and the physician Theodore Simon (1872 to 1961) published the first practical intelligence test in 1905: 30 items in order of difficulty. In 1908 they grouped the items by the age at which most children pass them, creating the first age scale; the highest level a child reaches is the mental age. The 1911 revision, the year Binet died, extended it to adults.

Lewis Madison Terman (1877 to 1956) standardised it at Stanford University as the Stanford-Binet (1916), using William Stern's ratio IQ (mental age ÷ chronological age × 100): the original Stanford-Binet used the ratio IQ, not a deviation IQ, percentile rank or T-score (Section 2.5). Later editions (Table 2.13) moved away from mental age: parallel Forms L and M with Maud Merrill (1937); Form L-M (1960), with a deviation IQ of SD 16; and the SB4 (1986) of Robert L. Thorndike, Elizabeth Hagen and Jerome M. Sattler, a point scale (each subtest scored by items passed, whatever their age level) reporting Standard Age Scores, SD 16.

The Stanford-Binet Fifth Edition (SB5, 2003) of Gale H. Roid covers ages 2 to 85 and over, with mean 100 and SD 15. Its five factors, Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing and Working Memory, are each tested in a verbal and a non-verbal domain: 10 subtests yielding Full Scale, Verbal and Nonverbal IQs. Two routing subtests (Object Series/Matrices and Vocabulary) set the starting level for the rest. Within a subtest the examiner finds a basal level, below which all items are assumed passed, and a ceiling level, where failures stop testing.

Wechsler, the deviation IQ and the WAIS-IV

David Wechsler (1896 to 1981), chief psychologist at Bellevue Hospital in New York, found the Stanford-Binet unsuited to the adults he tested. It had little appeal for adults; it put too much emphasis on word manipulation; it stressed speed over accuracy; and it relied on mental age, which stops growing in the late teens, so an adult's ratio IQ falls with every birthday. His Wechsler-Bellevue Intelligence Scale (1939) answered each flaw: a point scale with a Performance scale of non-verbal tasks beside the Verbal scale, scored by a new deviation IQ, also called the standard IQ because it is a standard score.

The deviation IQ compares the actual score with the expected mean score for the person's age group: IQ = 100 + 15 × (score minus age-group mean) ÷ age-group SD. Scoring at your age group's mean gives 100 at any age; one SD above gives 115. Wechsler defined intelligence (1944) as "the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment".

Exam trap

Wechsler's new method was the deviation ("standard") IQ, not a mental-age, physical-age or age-relative formula IQ; mocks write it as actual score ÷ expected mean score for age. His target flaws were no appeal for adults, word manipulation, speed over accuracy and mental age, never "children", "physical age" or "accuracy over speed".

The adult line runs from the WAIS (1955; 6 verbal and 5 performance subtests) through the WAIS-R (1981) and WAIS-III (1997), which still gave Verbal, Performance and Full Scale IQs, to the WAIS-IV (2008; ages 16:0 to 90:11), which dropped the first two. It yields a Full Scale IQ (FSIQ) from 10 core subtests and four index scores, composites for narrower abilities: Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Perceptual Reasoning (PRI), Working Memory (WMI) and Processing Speed (PSI) (Figure 2.10). Five supplemental subtests can replace a spoiled core subtest. The VCI reflects verbal reasoning and acquired knowledge; the PRI, reasoning with visual, non-verbal material. The Working Memory Index, built on holding and manipulating digits and mental sums, reflects attention, concentration and mental control; the Processing Speed Index, how quickly simple visual material is scanned and acted on. The optional General Ability Index (GAI, VCI plus PRI) estimates reasoning with less weight on memory and speed; the Cognitive Proficiency Index (CPI) pairs WMI and PSI. The WAIS-5 (2024) splits perceptual reasoning into Visual Spatial and Fluid Reasoning: five primary indices.

Exam trap

The WAIS-IV gives four indices (VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI) beside the FSIQ, not two, three or five; the GAI is an optional summary. Core subtests: 3 verbal, 3 perceptual. "VIQ, PIQ, FSIQ and Memory Quotient" mixes the WAIS-III with the Wechsler Memory Scale (Section 2.11); "crystallised, fluid, visual, auditory (or quantitative)", "VIQ, PIQ, memory, attention" and "cognitive, behavioural, emotional, adaptive" are not WAIS-IV indices.

Kaufman, Das-Naglieri and Woodcock-Johnson

Alan S. Kaufman and Nadeen L. Kaufman built their tests on theory. The Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (K-ABC, 1983) used Alexander Luria's sequential processing (handling items in serial order) and simultaneous processing (integrating many at once), and scored achievement apart from ability. The KABC-II (2004, ages 3 to 18) lets the examiner choose a Luria model or a Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model. The Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test (K-BIT, 1990; KBIT-2, 2004, ages 4 to 90) is a quick screen with a Verbal or Crystallised scale and a Nonverbal or Fluid scale (matrices). Reading, mathematics, quantitative and analytical scales belong to achievement and scholastic aptitude tests, such as the ACT, SAT and GRE admission tests (Section 2.8); an "EQ scale" belongs to emotional intelligence (Section 1.9).

The Indian-born J. P. Das and Jack A. Naglieri turned Luria's three functional units of the brain into the PASS theory (Planning, Attention, Simultaneous and Successive processing), measured by their Cognitive Assessment System (1997). The Woodcock-Johnson Tests of Cognitive Abilities (Richard W. Woodcock and Mary Bonner Johnson, 1977) are, in current editions, built on CHC theory.

Non-verbal and culture-fair tests

Vocabulary items reward schooling and language as much as intelligence. A culture-free test would leave culture out entirely; none exists, since even a pattern on paper assumes familiarity with pictures and timing. A culture-fair test merely minimises language, reading and specific knowledge, so that people from different backgrounds start more nearly level.

The classic is Raven's Progressive Matrices by John Carlyle Raven (1902 to 1970). Each item is a pattern with a piece missing. The Standard Progressive Matrices (1938) has 60 items in five sets, A to E, growing progressively harder; the Coloured version (1947) serves children of 5 to 11, elderly people and disabled people; the Advanced version serves able adults. Raven aimed at Spearman's g through eductive ability, seeing relations in confusion, as against recalling learned information. The test measures fluid, non-verbal reasoning by abstract pattern completion with minimal language, and is one of the strongest single markers of g.

Exam trap

Raven's measures fluid intelligence; it is culture-fair, not culture-free, and does not measure crystallised, verbal or social intelligence, reading comprehension or memory alone. Offered "general intelligence" beside "fluid", choose fluid: Raven's marks g, but its content is fluid and non-verbal. "Both" is wrong.

Raymond Bernard Cattell (1905 to 1998) named his test the Culture Free Intelligence Test (1940), then renamed it Culture Fair. Its three Scales are graded by age and ability; Scales 2 and 3 use four figural subtests: series, classification, matrices and conditions. Other tests remove speech from the task: the Leiter International Performance Scale of Russell Graydon Leiter (1929; Leiter-3, 2013) gives its instructions by pantomime, for deaf or autistic children; the Columbia Mental Maturity Scale needs only pointing to the drawing that does not belong, suiting children with cerebral palsy; and the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test of Lloyd M. Dunn (1959) measures receptive vocabulary as the person points to the picture the examiner names.

The simplest non-verbal test is a pencil and the request "draw a man". Florence Laura Goodenough (1886 to 1959) noticed that children add details to drawings in a regular developmental order. Her Draw-a-Man test (1926) credits up to 51 details as a non-verbal estimate of intellectual maturity and visual-motor ability; Dale B. Harris revised it in 1963. Because it asks so little, it works with a client too disorganised for verbal or performance subtests. Karen Machover's Draw-a-Person uses the same request projectively, to read personality (Section 2.10).

Exam trap

A disorganised client who cannot manage verbal or performance tasks is asked to draw a person: the domain targeted is visual-motor integration and cognitive estimation (Goodenough), not projective personality assessment (Machover), verbal comprehension or processing speed.

Group testing arrived with the First World War. In 1917 a committee led by Robert Mearns Yerkes (1876 to 1956) built the Army Alpha, a verbal test for literate recruits, and the Army Beta, a non-verbal one for illiterate and non-English-speaking recruits. Arthur Otis, Terman's student, devised the multiple-choice group format the Alpha borrowed (today's Otis-Lennon School Ability Test). The Primary Mental Abilities tests of Louis Leon Thurstone (1887 to 1955) score seven primary abilities separately instead of giving one IQ.

Performance tests and Indian scales

India needed tests that did not lean on literacy or on one language, so its psychologists normed performance tests and adapted Western scales (Table 2.16). In the Kohs Block Design test (Samuel C. Kohs, 1920; book 1923) the person arranges coloured cubes to copy a printed design; Wechsler borrowed it as Block Design. In the Alexander Pass-Along test (W. P. Alexander, 1932) blocks are slid along a tray, never lifted, to reach a target pattern. The Seguin Form Board, a teaching device of Edouard Seguin (1812 to 1880) for children with intellectual disability, became a test in Henry Herbert Goddard's version: a child replaces 10 shapes over three trials, and the shortest time converts to a mental age. In the Gesell Drawing Test of Arnold Gesell (1880 to 1961) a child copies geometric figures mastered at typical ages.

C. M. Bhatia assembled Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence (1955), standardised on Indian children. Its five subtests are Kohs Block Design, Alexander Pass-Along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory (digits, the one non-performance task) and Picture Construction; the Seguin Form Board is not among them. Needing no reading or writing, it is the test of choice for an illiterate child or adult from a rural area, because it minimises cultural and educational bias; the WISC-IV and SB5 carry Western verbal content, and MISIC is half verbal and assumes schooling. Being visual, it cannot serve a patient with cortical blindness.

Exam trap

For an illiterate rural 10-year-old, choose Bhatia's Battery over the WISC-IV, SB5 or MISIC. Its population is illiterate or rural Indians, adolescents and adults included, not urban English speakers or preschoolers only.

The adaptations follow their parents. The Binet-Kamat Test (V. V. Kamat, 1934; revised 1967) adapted the 1916 Stanford-Binet; it is a heavily verbal age scale giving a mental age and ratio IQ. Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC; Arthur J. Malin, Nagpur, 1969) adapted the WISC for ages 6 to 15, so it can be given from 6 years, and yields Verbal and Performance Quotients and a Full Scale IQ. The Wechsler Adult Performance Intelligence Scale (WAPIS; P. Ramalingaswamy, 1974) adapted the WAIS performance subtests for Indian adults. Pramila Phatak of Baroda produced Indian norms for the Draw-a-Man test, and Raven's matrices also have Indian norms. Pearson's Indian editions of the WISC-IV and WAIS-IV are newer and poorly documented.

A mock keys "WAIS-IV India edition" as the Indian WAIS common in clinics. Learn WAPIS (Ramalingaswamy) as the classic Indian adult adaptation; the BKT and MISIC are children's tests, so "all of the above" fails.

2.7 Assessing Development, Adaptive Behaviour and DisabilityLower yield · 21 Qs

An intelligence test asks how well a person reasons when tested. Two other questions matter as much. Has this infant reached the milestones expected for her age? And how well does a child or adult actually manage everyday life? Their answers decide who is diagnosed with intellectual disability and who in India is certified as disabled.

Beyond IQ: quotients, adaptive behaviour and informants

An infant cannot follow test instructions, so you measure how far she has come along the expected path. A developmental scale lists milestones at the ages most children reach them. The level reached is the developmental age (DA), and the developmental quotient is DQ = DA / CA × 100, CA being chronological age. A social maturity scale gives a social age (SA) and a social quotient, SQ = SA / CA × 100: if 6-year-old Ravi (72 months) manages daily life like a typical child of 54 months, his SQ is 75. Modern scales replace these crude ratios with deviation scores, mean 100 and SD 15 (Section 2.5).

Why measure everyday competence apart from IQ? Because one child scoring 65 dresses, shops and takes the bus alone, while another scoring 75 cannot look after herself. The American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (AAIDD, formerly the AAMD) defines adaptive behaviour as the conceptual (language, money, number), social (relationships, rules, avoiding victimisation) and practical (self-care, safety, travel, work) skills that people learn and perform in everyday life. Since it is what a person habitually does, not what he can do at his best, it is usually rated by an informant, a parent, caregiver or teacher, which also suits infants and the severely disabled.

A screening test is short, is given to many children and only flags those needing a closer look; a diagnostic test is longer and individual, and yields a level on which a diagnosis can rest. And because symptoms vary across contexts (the boy restless in a noisy classroom may be calm at home), clinicians use multi-informant assessment, combining parents, teachers, the child and clinic observation.

Key study: Achenbach's cross-informant correlations (1987)

Thomas M. Achenbach and colleagues pooled studies in which several people rated the same children. Similar informants, such as two parents, agreed moderately (mean correlation about 0.60); different kinds, such as parent and teacher, only weakly (about 0.28). Disagreement, they argued, reflects real differences between settings, so each informant adds valid information: the logic of Achenbach's parallel parent Child Behavior Checklist, Teacher's Report Form and Youth Self-Report. Criticism: it does not say whose report should decide.

Exam trap

Multi-informant assessment is recommended because symptoms may vary across contexts, not because parents over-report, teachers add nothing or clinic observation alone is insufficient.

Infant and preschool developmental scales

Arnold Gesell (1880 to 1961) of the Yale Clinic of Child Development filmed thousands of infants and turned the timing of their behaviour into norms (1925, 1940). His Gesell Developmental Schedules place observed behaviour at an age level in four fields: motor, adaptive (solving problems with objects, not adaptive behaviour in the Vineland sense), language and personal-social, each giving a DQ.

Nancy Bayley (1899 to 1994) built the leading diagnostic infant test, the Bayley Scales of Infant Development (BSID, 1969). The BSID-II (1993) gave a Mental Development Index (MDI) and a Psychomotor Development Index (PDI). The Bayley-III (2006), renamed Infant and Toddler Development, and the Bayley-4 (2019) have five scales: cognitive, language and motor, tested with the child, and social-emotional and adaptive behaviour, reported by the caregiver. Its range since 1993, the first month to 42 months, is its signature.

For screening, the Denver Developmental Screening Test of William K. Frankenburg and Josiah B. Dodds (1967; Denver II, 1990) covers birth to 6 years in four sectors: personal-social, fine motor-adaptive, language and gross motor. The result is normal, suspect or untestable, never a quotient. The McCarthy Scales of Children's Abilities (Dorothea McCarthy, 1972; 2½ to 8½ years) yield a General Cognitive Index. Virginia Apgar's newborn score (1953) and T. Berry Brazelton's Neonatal Behavioral Assessment Scale (1973) are taught in Chapter 6.

The Indian Developmental Screening Test (DST) of J. Bharath Raj (Mysore, 1977, 1983) places 88 milestones on Indian norms from birth to 15 years and gives a DQ. The Developmental Assessment Scales for Indian Infants (DASII) of Pramila Phatak and colleagues (Baroda) restandardised the Bayley on Indian infants from birth to 30 months, giving motor and mental DQs. The Trivandrum Developmental Screening Chart of M. K. C. Nair and colleagues (1991), built from the Baroda norms, screens children under 2 in minutes.

Exam trap

The scale for birth to 42 months across cognitive, language and motor domains is the Bayley, not the WPPSI (from 2½ years), the Stanford-Binet (from 2; both Section 2.6) or the McCarthy (from 2½). Do not confuse Bharath Raj's Indian DST with the American Denver, or the DASII (the Indian Bayley) with the TDSC (a Kerala screening chart).

Social maturity: the Vineland Social Maturity Scale

Intelligence tests missed something institution staff saw daily: residents of the same mental age differed hugely in self-care. Edgar Arnold Doll (1889 to 1968), director of research at the Vineland Training School in New Jersey, measured that difference with the Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS, 1935), the first standardised measure of social competence, or adaptive behaviour.

Its 117 items run in the order in which they are usually achieved, grouped year by year through childhood and in wider bands up to "25 and above", so the scale spans birth to adulthood. They fall into eight categories: self-help general, self-help eating, self-help dressing, self-direction, occupation, communication, locomotion and socialisation. The scale is not given to the person: an informant who knows him well is interviewed, habitually performed items are credited under standardised scoring rules, and the total converts to a social age and an SQ.

The Indian version, Malin's VSMS, is by Arthur J. Malin of Nagpur, who also adapted the WISC as MISIC (Section 2.6). It keeps the eight categories but has 89 items for birth to 15 years on Indian norms, and its SQ is used with IQ in Indian disability certification.

Exam trap

The VSMS was devised by Doll, not Malin (the Indian adaptation), Bharath Raj (the DST), C. M. Bhatia (a performance battery), Terman, Wechsler or Binet. It yields social age and social quotient, not mental age and IQ, developmental age and DQ, or emotional age and EQ. Its items run past 25 years, so "applicable from 0 to 25 years only" is false; age-graded items set out in age-level groups (the "separate forms for different age groups" of the options) and standardised scoring are genuine features. Malin VSMS: 89 items, SQ; Bharath Raj DST: 88 items, DQ; both 0 to 15 years.

Adaptive behaviour scales and structured interviews

Sara S. Sparrow, David A. Balla and Domenic V. Cicchetti rebuilt Doll's scale as the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (1984; Vineland-II, 2005; Vineland-3, 2016); the current edition covers birth to 90 years. They measure adaptive functioning in daily life in four domains, communication, daily living skills, socialisation and motor skills (for young children), plus an optional maladaptive behaviour domain; the first three form the Adaptive Behavior Composite (mean 100, SD 15). Given as a caregiver interview or parent and teacher forms, the Vineland supplies the adaptive half of an intellectual disability diagnosis. The older AAMD Adaptive Behavior Scale (Kazuo Nihira and colleagues, 1969) rates adaptive skills in Part One and maladaptive behaviour in Part Two.

The Vineland belongs to a family of clinician-administered instruments with the K-SADS (Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia; Joaquim Puig-Antich, 1978), a semi-structured psychiatric interview of parent and child, and the ADI-R and ADOS-2 for autism (below). Exam trap

The Vineland measures adaptive behaviour, not IQ or verbal IQ, personality, psychopathology, psychotic symptoms, neurological soft signs, memory deficits or suicidal ideation. The Vineland-3, K-SADS, ADI-R and ADOS-2 are interviews and observation schedules, not EEG paradigms, projective techniques or self-report cognitive tests.

Assessing intellectual disability

DSM-5 (2013) diagnoses intellectual disability (formerly mental retardation) on three criteria. (A) Deficits in intellectual functions such as reasoning and learning, confirmed by clinical assessment and individual standardised testing: about 2 SD below the mean, an IQ of about 65 to 75 allowing for measurement error (Section 2.3). (B) Deficits in adaptive functioning in conceptual, social and practical domains. (C) Onset in the developmental period. The disorder itself is in Chapter 8.

Severity (mild, moderate, severe, profound) is graded by adaptive functioning, not IQ, since that decides the support needed. Older systems used IQ bands (Table 2.18): ICD-10's mild 50 to 69, moderate 35 to 49, severe 20 to 34, profound below 20, and DSM-IV-TR's overlapping bands. ICD-11 (in force 2022) renamed the condition disorders of intellectual development, adding a provisional level.

Old educational labels set expectations: mild was educable (academics to about sixth grade by the late teens), moderate trainable (about second grade), severe and profound custodial. So an IQ of about 50, at the mild-moderate border, legitimately predicts learning to about fifth grade, not lifelong institutional care, constant supervision or high-school graduation. And borderline intellectual functioning (IQ about 71 to 84) is not intellectual disability, and an FSIQ of 70 without adaptive deficits is no diagnosis.

Global developmental delay or intellectual disability?

What if the child is 3 and cannot sit through an IQ test? DSM-5 reserves global developmental delay for children under 5 years who miss milestones in several areas but are too young for reliable standardised testing; a DQ describes them until they are reassessed. From 5 years the diagnosis is intellectual disability. The key difference is the age of assessment. So the label is inappropriate for a 7-year-old with an FSIQ of 65 and severe adaptive impairment, who meets criteria for intellectual disability, but fits a child too young to test or one whose test or adaptive data are incomplete (Figure 2.11).

Exam trap

Global developmental delay versus intellectual disability turns on the age of assessment (under 5 versus 5 and above), not on social deficits, language delay alone or motor impairment.

Disability certification in India

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 (RPWD Act) recognises 21 disabilities, up from 7 under the 1995 Act, among them intellectual disability, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder and mental illness. Forty percent or more of a specified disability is a benchmark disability, which opens reservations and other benefits. Guidelines notified under the Act in 2018 set out how each disability is assessed.

For intellectual disability, IQ comes from Indian or Indian-normed tests (Binet-Kamat, MISIC, WAPIS, Bhatia's battery, the Seguin Form Board, Raven's matrices: Section 2.6), the social quotient from the VSMS, and a DQ for children under 5, who are certified with global developmental delay. A medical board that includes a clinical psychologist certifies. The percentages widely quoted for each IQ band (Table 2.18) come from earlier guidelines; check them against the current notification.

A specific learning disability (dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia; Chapter 8) is assessed and certified only from 8 years of age, since before then ordinary variation in learning to read, write and calculate cannot be told apart from a disorder. An intelligence test shows that general ability is not the problem, and the NIMHANS Index for Specific Learning Disabilities, with curriculum-based tests, measures the deficits. Disability from mental illness is rated with IDEAS, the Indian Disability Evaluation and Assessment Scale (Section 2.11).

Exam trap

The minimum age for assessing specific learning disability under the RPWD guidelines is 8 years, not 7, 10 or 14.

Assessing autism: ISAA, CARS and the diagnostic pair

Two rating scales dominate Indian autism assessment (the disorder is in Chapter 8). In the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) of Eric Schopler, Robert J. Reichler and Barbara Rochen Renner (North Carolina; 1980, manual 1988; CARS-2, 2010), the clinician observes the child, hears the parents and rates 15 areas, such as relating to people and imitation, from 1 (normal for age) to 4 (severely abnormal). Totals run from 15 to 60; 30 and above indicates autism.

The Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism (ISAA) was developed in 2009 by the National Institute for the Mentally Handicapped, Secunderabad, partly from CARS items. Its 40 items cover six domains (social relationship and reciprocity, emotional responsiveness, speech-language and communication, behaviour patterns, sensory aspects, cognitive component), each rated 1 (rarely) to 5 (always) from observation and parent interview. Totals run from 40 to 200: below 70 is not autism, and higher scores grade mild, moderate or severe autism with a disability percentage. ISAA is the instrument prescribed for certifying autism in India.

Diagnosis pairs the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R; Michael Rutter, Ann Le Couteur and Catherine Lord, 2003), a 93-item parent interview, with the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS-2; Lord and colleagues, 2012), structured play and conversation in one of five modules. The M-CHAT-R/F (Diana Robins and colleagues, 2009) screens toddlers of 16 to 30 months with 20 parent questions. Not autism tools: the Children's Global Assessment Scale (CGAS; David Shaffer, 1983), a single 1 to 100 rating of overall functioning, and the Child Behavior Checklist (Section 2.11).

Exam trap

IHBAS papers of 2020 and 2024 key CARS for severity and disability in autism, even for "the Indian tool" with ISAA offered. CARS is American; the Indian instrument prescribed for certifying autism disability is ISAA, which drew on CARS. Choose ISAA when offered. DAAS, PAAS, CGAS and the CBCL are distractors.

2.8 Aptitude, Achievement, Creativity, Interest and Attitude MeasuresLower yield · 8 Qs

Two Class 10 students in Mysuru both score 85 percent in mathematics. The marks show what each has learnt, but not what each could still learn, how original each is, what each enjoys or how each feels about a career in engineering. Each of those questions has its own family of tests.

Aptitude, achievement and intelligence

An achievement test measures what a person has already learnt: the knowledge and understanding gained in a specific academic area, usually through instruction. An aptitude test looks forward, estimating how well a person will learn or succeed in a specific area in future, such as mechanical, musical or clerical work. An intelligence test samples general ability and gives one broad score (Section 2.6). Achievement looks back and aptitude looks ahead, so achievement tests rest mainly on content validity and aptitude tests on predictive validity (Section 2.4).

Anne Anastasi (1908 to 2001) stressed that the difference is one of degree: all ability tests measure developed ability, and they lie on a continuum by how specific and how controlled the underlying learning is. Course-based achievement tests (a term of algebra) sit at one end; broad achievement tests, verbal aptitude and intelligence tests, and non-verbal tests follow; culture-fair tests, drawing on the least controlled everyday experience, sit at the other.

A teacher-made test serves one class and has no norms; a standardised achievement test has tried-out items, fixed administration and norms. By purpose, a survey test gives an overall level of attainment, a diagnostic test pinpoints specific weaknesses (exactly which kinds of subtraction a child gets wrong), and a prognostic test forecasts success in a later course.

Clinicians use individual batteries: the Wide Range Achievement Test (Joseph Jastak, 1946; reading, spelling and arithmetic), the Peabody Individual Achievement Test (Lloyd Dunn and Frederick Markwardt, 1970; many items answered by pointing), the Wechsler Individual Achievement Test (1992) and the Woodcock-Johnson Tests of Achievement (Richard Woodcock and Mary Bonner Johnson, 1977). The last two are co-normed with ability tests to assess the ability-achievement discrepancy: achievement well below what measured intelligence predicts has traditionally signalled a specific learning disability (Section 2.7).

Exam trap

The primary purpose of an achievement test is to measure understanding and knowledge in specific academic areas. "Future success potential" is aptitude, "cognitive abilities related to brain function" is neuropsychological testing (Section 2.11), and "unconscious conflicts" belong to projective techniques (Section 2.10).

Aptitude batteries and special aptitude tests

One intelligence score told a careers counsellor little. After Louis Leon Thurstone (1887 to 1955) split intelligence into primary mental abilities (Section 1.9), the 1940s produced the multiple aptitude battery: separately normed subtests that give a profile instead of one number. The Differential Aptitude Tests (DAT) of George K. Bennett, Harold G. Seashore and Alexander G. Wesman (1947), built to guide secondary school students, has eight subtests: verbal reasoning, numerical ability, abstract reasoning, clerical speed and accuracy, mechanical reasoning, space relations, spelling and language usage. Verbal reasoning plus numerical ability indexes scholastic aptitude, and J. M. Ojha adapted the DAT for India. The General Aptitude Test Battery (GATB) of the United States Employment Service (1947) measures nine aptitudes, from general learning ability to finger and manual dexterity, to match people to occupations, and the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery (1968) serves US military recruitment.

A special aptitude test measures one narrow talent; an early classic is the Measures of Musical Talents of Carl Seashore (1866 to 1949), published in 1919 (Table 2.19). Admission tests such as the SAT, ACT and GRE are scholastic aptitude tests predicting success in college or graduate study.

Tests in the guidance programme

These batteries serve guidance, the organised help a school gives students in educational and vocational choices. A guidance programme's tools are the case study (an in-depth picture of one pupil from all sources), the interview (a face-to-face conversation to gather information and advise), the cumulative record (a running file of marks, test scores, health and activities kept over the years), observation, and tests of intelligence, aptitude, achievement and interest. Counselling then draws on them all.

Exam trap

Guidance textbooks call counselling "the heart of the guidance programme", so choose counselling whenever it is offered. RML 2022 offered only case study, interview, cumulative record and aptitude test, and keyed the aptitude test, which no standard text supports: an aptitude test only predicts trainability. With exactly those four options, choose the interview, counselling's chief instrument and itself called the heart of the counselling process (Section 7.10), and expect some keys to differ.

Creativity and divergent thinking

Intelligence tests reward convergent thinking, narrowing information down to one correct answer. In his presidential address to the American Psychological Association in 1950, Joy Paul Guilford (1897 to 1987) tied creativity to divergent thinking, producing many varied answers to an open problem, the operation he called divergent production in his Structure of Intellect model (Section 1.9). His Alternate Uses task asks for uses of an object such as a brick. If Kavya writes wall, house, doorstop, paperweight and red powder for rangoli, her fluency (number of ideas) is five; her flexibility (number of categories: building, weighting, pigment) is three; her originality is the statistical rarity of each idea (rangoli powder scores, a wall does not); and elaboration is the detail she adds.

Ellis Paul Torrance (1915 to 2003) standardised such tasks in the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (1966), with verbal and figural (picture-completing) forms. Sarnoff Mednick (1962), in his associative theory, held that creative people reach remote associations easily. His Remote Associates Test (RAT) is a timed paper-and-pencil test of about 40 minutes in which the examinee finds one word linking three cue words: cottage, Swiss, cake gives "cheese". Michael Wallach and Nathan Kogan (1965), testing children in relaxed, game-like conditions, found creativity largely independent of intelligence. The Indian tests are the verbal and non-verbal Tests of Creative Thinking of Baqer Mehdi (1973) and the six Passi Tests of Creativity (B. K. Passi, 1979).

Exam trap

"A timed, 40-minute paper-and-pencil test" is the RAT. Each item has one right answer, so its format is convergent, unlike the TTCT. The Alternate Uses task is Guilford's, not Torrance's.

Interest inventories and value scales

An interest inventory asks which activities, subjects and occupations a person likes. On the empirical route an item joins an occupational scale if people in that occupation answer it differently from people in general, whatever its content; on the homogeneous route items are grouped by content into interest areas.

Edward Kellogg Strong Jr. (1884 to 1963) took the empirical route in the Strong Vocational Interest Blank (1927), revised by David Campbell as the Strong-Campbell Interest Inventory (1974) and now the Strong Interest Inventory. G. Frederic Kuder (1903 to 2000) took the homogeneous route in the Kuder Preference Record (1939): the person marks the most and least liked activity in each forced-choice triad, and the answers score ten areas (outdoor, mechanical, computational, scientific, persuasive, artistic, literary, musical, social service, clerical). As each choice raises one area by lowering another, the scores are ipsative: they show relative strengths within the person, not standing against others (Section 2.5). The later Kuder Occupational Interest Survey compares people with occupational groups.

John L. Holland (1919 to 2008), in a theory first published in 1959 and set out fully in 1973, sorted people and work environments into six RIASEC types (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional) arranged on a hexagon: adjacent types are alike, opposite ones (Realistic and Social) least alike. His self-scored Self-Directed Search (1971) gives a three-letter code matched to occupations.

For values, the Study of Values of Gordon Allport (1897 to 1967) and Philip Vernon (1931; with Gardner Lindzey from 1951; revised 1960) measures six values from the types of Eduard Spranger (1882 to 1963): theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political and religious. Milton Rokeach (1973) had people rank 18 terminal values (end states such as freedom) and 18 instrumental values (conduct such as honesty).

Thurstone and Likert scales

An attitude is an evaluative disposition towards an object such as a group or an idea (its components and theories are in Chapter 3). An attitude scale infers it from responses to statements, combined into one score from unfavourable to favourable. The classic scales differ in how the statements are chosen and how the score is formed.

Thurstone and Ernest J. Chave (1929), measuring attitude towards the church, devised the method of equal-appearing intervals. Judges sorted over a hundred statements into 11 piles from most unfavourable to most favourable, without giving their own opinion. A statement's scale value is the median pile; its ambiguity is Q, the interquartile range of the placements, and high-Q statements are dropped. About 20 statements with evenly spread values form the scale. The respondent ticks those they agree with and scores the median (or mean) of their values: agreeing with statements valued 7.2, 8.1 and 8.4 gives 8.1, a favourable attitude.

This is laborious. Rensis Likert (1903 to 1981) offered a short cut in 1932, the method of summated ratings. A Likert scale is a set of closed-ended statements, each with a fixed number of ordered alternatives, classically five from strongly agree to strongly disagree, scored 5 to 1 (reversed for unfavourable statements). Respondents rate their own agreement on this symmetric continuum, and item scores are summed with equal weights. No judges are used: items are kept by item analysis if high and low total scorers answer them differently (Section 2.2). Easier to build and usually as reliable, Likert scales dominate questionnaires; strictly ordinal, their scores are usually treated as interval. Figure 2.12 contrasts the two methods.

Exam trap

A summated rating scale is a Likert scale, never a Thurstone, Guttman or equal-appearing interval scale. Likert items are closed-ended with a fixed number of ordered alternatives, neither open-ended nor dichotomous. Judges pre-scaling statements is Thurstone; respondents rating their agreement, summed with equal weights, is Likert; a sentence-completion stimulus belongs to projective techniques (Section 2.10). Likert is a type of attitude scale, whereas nominal, ordinal and interval are levels of measurement (Chapter 9).

Guttman, Bogardus and the semantic differential

Louis Guttman (1916 to 1987) built a one-dimensional cumulative scale by scalogram analysis (1944). Statements are ordered so that endorsing a stronger one implies endorsing every weaker one ("I would accept a de-addiction centre in my city", "in my locality", "on my street"), so the total score reveals the exact pattern of answers. Fit is judged by the coefficient of reproducibility, which should be 0.90 or more.

The social distance scale of Emory Bogardus (1882 to 1973), published in 1925, is generally regarded as the oldest attitude scale. For each group the respondent marks the closest relation they would accept, on seven steps: close kinship by marriage, club chums, neighbours, co-workers, fellow citizens, visitors only, and exclusion from the country. In the semantic differential of Charles Osgood, George Suci and Percy Tannenbaum (1957), a concept such as "the police" is rated on seven-point bipolar adjective pairs (good to bad, strong to weak, fast to slow), which factor into evaluation, potency and activity; evaluation is the attitude proper.

In paired comparisons each object is paired with every other and one of each pair is chosen; Thurstone's law of comparative judgement (1927) converts the choices into scale values, and rank ordering is a quicker variant. Allen Edwards and Franklin Kilpatrick (1948) combined Thurstone judging, Likert item analysis and Guttman scaling in the scale-discrimination technique. Table 2.20 compares the main scales.

Implicit and indirect measures

Direct scales invite socially desirable answers, and some bias lies outside awareness. In the Implicit Association Test (IAT) of Anthony Greenwald, Debbie McGhee and Jordan Schwartz (1998), words or pictures are sorted on a computer with two keys into paired categories, such as "flower or pleasant" versus "insect or unpleasant", and then with the pairing reversed. In the bogus pipeline of Edward Jones and Harold Sigall (1971), people answer more honestly when wired to a machine they believe detects true feelings. Physiological indices (skin conductance, pupil size, facial muscle activity) show how strongly a person reacts, rarely in which direction.

Exam trap

The IAT measures implicit, unconscious attitudes and stereotypes through reaction times. It does not measure verbal ability, hidden mathematical ability or dream content.

2.9 Objective Personality InventoriesMedium yield · 38 Qs

In 1917, as the United States entered World War I, its army needed a quick way to spot recruits likely to break down under fire, and there were far too few psychiatrists to interview them all. Robert Sessions Woodworth (1869 to 1962) put the psychiatrist's screening questions on paper, and the personality inventory was born. Ever since, psychologists have argued over how to choose the questions and how far to trust the answers.

Objective tests and Cattell's three kinds of data

An objective personality test fixes the items, the answer format (true or false, yes or no, a rating) and the scoring key, so any two scorers get the same result. Rating scales and personality inventories (long self-report questionnaires scored on several scales) are objective; projective tests (Section 2.10) use ambiguous stimuli, free responses and interpretive scoring.

Raymond Bernard Cattell (1905 to 1998), whose trait theory is taught in Chapter 4, accepted a trait only if it appeared in three kinds of data:

  • L-data (life record data): actual behaviour in everyday life, from school performance, work and criminal records, and ratings by observers who know the person.
  • Q-data (questionnaire data): self-report ratings of one's own characteristics, attributes and interests.
  • T-data (objective test data): behaviour in a controlled test situation whose purpose the person cannot see, and so cannot easily fake, such as a laboratory task scored for a trait he does not know is being measured.
Exam trap

Cattell's scheme has exactly three letters, L, Q and T: there is no P-data (projective) or Z-data. School and criminal records are L-data; self-report questionnaires are Q-data; projective-test performance and Q-sort responses are not L-data. Q-data is not the Q-sort (2.9.7).

Four ways to build an inventory

The logical-content or rational strategy reasons out how people with a trait (anxiety, say) describe themselves and writes items whose content plainly reflects it, trusting face validity. Woodworth's Personal Data Sheet (built in 1917, published in 1919 and often dated 1920), the first personality inventory, asked 116 yes-no questions about symptoms and gave one score of emotional instability; Hugh M. Bell's Adjustment Inventory (1934) scored home, health, social and emotional adjustment. Its weakness: respondents see what each item is after, and the method assumes insight and honesty.

The theoretical strategy writes items for the constructs of a personality theory: the Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (Henry Murray's needs), the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (Carl Jung's types) and the Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory (Theodore Millon's theory).

Empirical criterion-group keying ignores content. A large item pool goes to a criterion group (say, patients diagnosed with depression) and a normal control group; only the items the two groups answer differently survive, whatever they say, and the scale is cross-validated on fresh groups. If depressed patients happened to answer "true" to "I like mechanics magazines" more often than controls, that item would join the depression scale. The MMPI, the California Psychological Inventory and the Strong Vocational Interest Blank (Section 2.8) were built this way.

The factor-analytic strategy factor-analyses the correlations among many items, keeps the items that load on each factor and names the factor afterwards: the 16PF, the Eysenck inventories, the NEO-PI-R, the Guilford-Zimmerman Temperament Survey (J. P. Guilford and Wayne Zimmerman, 1949; 300 items, ten traits such as sociability and restraint) and the Comrey Personality Scales (Andrew L. Comrey, 1970; 180 items, eight factors such as trust and orderliness). Modern inventories, such as the NEO-PI-R and the MCMI, use combined strategies.

Against response sets (Section 2.2) and deliberate faking, test makers use rapport and confidentiality, forced-choice items pairing statements of equal social desirability, disguised items, and validity scales and correction keys.

Exam trap

The MMPI was derived by finding items that discriminate between criterion and control groups, not by the rational method or by assuming traits and writing questions for them. Comrey's 180-item test is factor-analytic. "Correlational" and "experimental" are research designs (Chapter 9), and criterion-referencing is a way of interpreting scores (Section 2.5), not of choosing items.

The MMPI and its validity scales

Around 1940, at the University of Minnesota Hospitals, the psychologist Starke R. Hathaway (1903 to 1984) and the neuropsychiatrist J. Charnley McKinley (1891 to 1950) set out to aid psychiatric diagnosis. They gave over a thousand statements to patient groups with known diagnoses and to about 700 "Minnesota normals", mostly hospital visitors, and kept for each scale only the items that separated that patient group from the normals. The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI, 1943) had 550 different statements (566 printed, 16 repeated), answered true, false or cannot say. It is "multiphasic" because its items cover physical health, mood, family, sexual, religious and social attitudes, fears and unusual experiences.

The MMPI is therefore an objective personality inventory of personality and psychopathology, used mainly for psychiatric diagnosis. It is not a projective test, intelligence test, aptitude battery, interest inventory or neuropsychological screening battery, nor a tool for educational, career or physical-health assessment, and it does not measure unconscious drives (projective tests), the Big Five (the NEO) or leadership potential (the CPI). Because self-reports can mislead, it carries validity scales:

  • Cannot Say (?): items left blank or marked both ways; 30 or more make the record doubtful.
  • L (Lie) scale: small faults nearly everyone admits, of the kind "I get angry sometimes". Answering "false" to them asserts a degree of personal virtue rarely seen in our culture, so a high L marks a naive attempt to look good by denying common minor faults.
  • F (Infrequency) scale: items fewer than one normal person in ten endorses. A high F flags random or careless responding, over-reporting of symptoms (faking bad) or severe psychopathology; on the MMPI-2 an F above T 100 suggests random responding or gross exaggeration.
  • K (Correction) scale: added by Paul E. Meehl (1920 to 2003) and Hathaway in 1946 to catch subtle defensiveness. A fraction of K is added to clinical scales 1, 4, 7, 8 and 9 (the K-correction) to compensate for defensive responding.

The MMPI-2 added VRIN (Variable Response Inconsistency: contradictory answers to similar item pairs, a sign of random responding), TRIN (True Response Inconsistency: answering "true", or "false", regardless of content), F(B) (Back F, for later items), F(p) (Infrequency-Psychopathology, items rare even among psychiatric inpatients, which separates faking bad from real disorder) and S (Superlative Self-Presentation).

Exam trap

The three classic validity scales are L, F and K (four if Cannot Say is counted, as some texts do); VRIN, TRIN and S came later. L is naive faking good, K is subtle defensiveness plus a correction, and F is rare responding (random answers, faking bad or severe pathology). A very high F never means faking good, defensiveness or an excellent profile.

Indian context

H. N. Murthy's Multiphasic Questionnaire (MPQ; 1964) is a short (100-item) Indian adult inventory built from MMPI-type items, with a Lie scale and seven clinical scales such as hysteria, depression, anxiety, paranoia and schizophrenia. An elevated MPQ Lie scale indicates all of these: a tendency to fake and manipulate, social desirability, and mistrust (a guarded person who admits no flaw to the examiner).

Factor-analytic inventories: 16PF, Eysenck and NEO

Cattell turned his source traits into the Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire (16PF), first published in 1949; the fifth edition (1993) has 185 three-choice items for ages 16 and over. It scores 16 primary factors found by factor analysis and labelled by letter (A warmth, B reasoning, C emotional stability, E dominance, and so on to Q4 tension), plus five global, second-order factors (extraversion, anxiety, tough-mindedness, independence, self-control) that approximate the Big Five. Scores are stens (standard tens: 1 to 10, mean 5.5, SD 2). Factor B is a brief reasoning scale, but the 16PF measures personality traits, not fluid, crystallised or general intelligence (Section 2.6).

Exam trap

The 16PF is Raymond B. Cattell's: Kelly is to the Rep test as Cattell is to the 16PF. Hans Eysenck built the EPQ, Costa and McCrae the NEO-PI-R, Henry Murray the TAT, Gordon Allport the Study of Values, William Sheldon a body-type scheme, and Freud no inventory.

Hans Jurgen Eysenck (1916 to 1997), at the Maudsley Hospital, London, built three inventories. The Maudsley Personality Inventory (1959; 48 items) scored extraversion (E) and neuroticism (N); the Eysenck Personality Inventory (1964, with Sybil B. G. Eysenck; 57 items) added a Lie scale; and the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ, 1975; 90 items) added psychoticism (P), giving PEN plus L. The EPQ-R followed in 1985.

Paul T. Costa Jr. and Robert R. McCrae built the NEO Personality Inventory on the Five-Factor Model (Chapter 4). The NEO-PI-R (1992) has 240 items on a five-point agree-disagree scale and scores five domains, Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness and Neuroticism (OCEAN), each with six facets (30 in all), as T scores. The 60-item NEO-FFI scores domains only; the NEO-PI-3 (2005) simplified the wording. The NEO is not based on Cattell's 16 factors, Eysenck's PEN, Murray's needs or MBTI types (personality-disorder scales belong to the MCMI), and OCEAN is neither the four humours (sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic; Chapter 4) nor achievement, affiliation and power motives (Chapter 1). Shorter Big Five measures include the 44-item Big Five Inventory (Oliver John and colleagues, 1991); the HEXACO inventory of Kibeom Lee and Michael Ashton adds a sixth factor, Honesty-Humility.

Theory-based, clinical and Indian inventories

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) of Katharine Cook Briggs (1875 to 1968) and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers (1897 to 1980), first issued in 1943, is directly based on Carl Gustav Jung's theory of psychological types. Forced-choice items place people on four dichotomies, extraversion-introversion, sensing-intuition, thinking-feeling and judging-perceiving, giving 16 types such as INTP. It is popular in counselling and team building but psychometrically weak: continuous scores that bunch near the middle are forced into types, and many people get a different type on retest weeks later.

The Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS) of Allen L. Edwards (1953, manual 1954; revised 1959) measures 15 needs from Henry A. Murray's list, such as achievement, order, affiliation, dominance and aggression. To control social desirability, every item is a forced choice between two statements matched for desirability (225 pairs: 210 different and 15 repeated for a consistency score). The scores are therefore ipsative: each need is strong only relative to the person's other needs, so people cannot be compared directly.

The California Psychological Inventory (CPI) of Harrison G. Gough (1921 to 2010), published in 1956 (manual 1957), is the normal person's MMPI: largely criterion-keyed, with many MMPI items, but scoring folk concepts, everyday trait ideas found in all cultures, such as dominance, sociability, responsibility and self-control. It began with 480 items (now 434) and predicts achievement, leadership and delinquency. The Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory (MCMI) of Theodore Millon (1928 to 2014), first published in 1977, follows Millon's theory and the DSM, measuring personality disorders and clinical syndromes in patients (175 items; 195 in the MCMI-IV of 2015). It reports base rate scores, anchored to how common each disorder is in clinical samples rather than to normal T scores.

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) of Charles D. Spielberger (1927 to 2013) and colleagues (1970; Form Y 1983) has 40 items: 20 ask how you feel right now (state anxiety, passing) and 20 how you generally feel (trait anxiety, a stable proneness). Its purpose is to differentiate state from trait anxiety. Measuring psychoticism, extraversion and neuroticism is the EPQ's job; "normal and abnormal personality" fits broad inventories such as the MMPI and CPI; checking response validity is the job of validity scales. The older Taylor Manifest Anxiety Scale of Janet A. Taylor (1953) used 50 MMPI items judged to express overt anxiety.

Idiographic and situational methods

Some methods study how one person construes the world. In the Role Construct Repertory Test (Rep test, 1955) of George A. Kelly (1905 to 1967), the person names people who fill roles in his life (mother, a liked teacher, someone disliked). Shown three at a time, he says how two are alike and differ from the third; each answer ("generous versus selfish") is a bipolar personal construct, and a repertory grid of figures by constructs maps his construct system (theory in Chapter 4).

In the Q-sort, set out by William Stephenson (1902 to 1989) in 1953, a person sorts statements, such as self-descriptions, into piles from least to most characteristic, with a fixed number per pile forming a forced, quasi-normal distribution (Figure 2.14). Since every sort has the same mean and spread, it shows which statements are more characteristic of this person: within-person, not between-person, variation. Carl Rogers (1902 to 1987) had clients sort for the real self and the ideal self; Butler and Haigh (1954) found the mean real-ideal correlation rose from about zero before client-centred therapy to about .3 after it (.34 at the end, .31 at follow-up). Jack Block (1924 to 2010) built the 100-item California Q-set for observers. The Q-sort complements inventories; it never replaced the MMPI.

On an adjective checklist, such as Gough's Adjective Check List of 300 adjectives, the person ticks every word that applies. Situational tests watch behaviour in staged real-life tasks. In the Character Education Inquiry, Hugh Hartshorne and Mark A. May (1928) gave schoolchildren natural-seeming chances to cheat, lie and steal; honesty in one situation predicted honesty in another only weakly. The US Office of Strategic Services (OSS) selected World War II agents through stressful staged tasks; in the leaderless group discussion candidates tackle a problem with no appointed leader while assessors rate who leads, as India's Services Selection Board still does.

2.10 Projective TechniquesHigh yield · 44 Qs

Hand someone a symmetrical smudge of ink and ask, "What might this be?" One person sees two dancers, another a crushed bat, a third "blood, because it is red". The blot is the same, so the differences must come from the viewers. That idea produced clinical psychology's most famous and most disputed tests. This section explains it, sorts the tests into five families, works through the Rorschach and the Thematic Apperception Test, then the smaller techniques and the evidence on their worth.

The projective hypothesis

The objective inventories of Section 2.9 ask clear questions and score fixed answers. A projective technique reverses that logic. It presents an ambiguous, unstructured stimulus (an inkblot, a vague picture, half a sentence, a blank page), gives minimal instructions and accepts any answer. Because the stimulus supplies so little structure, whatever structure appears in the response is assumed to come from the person. This is the projective hypothesis: faced with an ambiguous stimulus, a person projects needs, conflicts, unconscious processes and a characteristic perceptual style into the response, usually without knowing it.

The word comes from Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939), for whom projection was a defence that attributes one's own unacceptable impulse to someone else (Section 4.3). Testing borrowed a broader sense: everyone reads their inner world into an unclear outer one. Lawrence K. Frank coined the term projective methods in 1939, likening them to an X-ray that shows inner structure without opening the body.

Projective tests share four features: an ambiguous stimulus; an open response, with almost unlimited possible answers; a disguised purpose, so the person cannot tell how answers will be read; and global interpretation of the whole personality rather than one trait. They therefore claim to reveal unconscious processes, not intellectual attainment, parenting style or career aptitude. Their main advantage follows from the disguise: a person who cannot tell what a "good" answer is cannot easily fake one, so the tests avoid the self-report biases of inventories (social desirability, faking, defensiveness).

Exam trap

The advantage is avoiding self-report bias. "They let people use the defence of projection" twists the theory; superior retest reliability and numerical data are strengths of objective inventories. Beside the EPQ, 16PF, MMPI-2, NEO-PI-R, MBTI or MCMI-III, only the Rorschach and TAT are projective: the rest are objective self-report inventories (Section 2.9). Intelligence tests and direct observation are not projective either.

Lindzey's five families

Gardner Lindzey (1959) sorted the many techniques by the kind of response they demand (Figure 2.15). In association techniques the person reports the first word, image or percept the stimulus calls up. In construction techniques the person builds a product, usually a story with a past, present and future. In completion techniques the person finishes an incomplete stimulus. In choice or ordering techniques the person picks or ranks items by preference, a step towards objective testing. In expression techniques the person produces something freely (a drawing, a painting, play), and the manner of production counts as much as the product.

Frank (1939) had grouped methods by what the person does with the material: constitutive (structuring formless material, as in finger painting), constructive (building a directed product), interpretive (giving material personal meaning, as in the TAT), cathartic (releasing emotion, as in play) and refractive (personality refracted through a conventional medium, as in handwriting). The categories overlapped, and Lindzey's replaced them.

Exam trap

The TAT is construction, the Rorschach association, sentence completion completion and Draw-A-Person expression. RML 2021 keyed the word association test as "not a projective test". That key is wrong: Lindzey lists word association as an association technique, and Galton's word association (1879) is usually called the first projective technique. By the standard classification all four options (word association, Rorschach, TAT, sentence completion) are projective, so no option is correct. Learn word association as projective; if the identical item returns, the paper's key was word association.

The Rorschach: ten cards and how they are given

Hermann Rorschach (1884 to 1922), a Swiss psychiatrist, published the test in Psychodiagnostik (1921). From many blots he kept the ten that best separated psychiatric groups, then died the next year, aged 37. The Rorschach Inkblot Test has ten bilaterally symmetrical cards (not 12, 15 or 20), always shown in the same order. Five are achromatic, black and grey (I, IV, V, VI, VII); two are black and red (II, III); three are multicoloured (VIII, IX, X), as Figure 2.16 shows. Hence the textbook phrase "five black-and-white and five coloured".

Administration has three phases. In the response phase (free association, or performance proper) the examiner asks "What might this be?", records every response verbatim with its reaction time, and does not prompt. In the inquiry phase, after all ten cards, the examiner goes back through each response to clarify where on the blot it was seen (location) and what made it look that way (determinants), using non-leading questions ("What made it look like that?"). Optional testing the limits follows: the examiner asks directly, for example, whether the person can see a common percept they missed. A longer reaction time, rejection or confusion on the coloured cards than on the achromatic ones is colour shock, read as a sign of emotional disturbance.

The Rorschach assesses personality, cognition and perception: how a person organises an ambiguous field, how they think, and whether thinking is disordered. Rorschach built it as a diagnostic aid, so it is used to detect thought disorder, help identify mental illness and infer unconscious intentions. It does not interpret dreams, record conscious desires, measure IQ, memory or brain size, or diagnose physical illness.

Exam trap

The inquiry clarifies each response without leading questions; cards are never shuffled, responses are recorded from the first card, and inquiry is not "scoring only". Ten cards, Hermann Rorschach, 1921: not 20 by Henry Murray, 15 by John Exner or 5 by Carl Jung, and no blank card (that is TAT card 16). Five chromatic and five achromatic, not three and seven. RML 2025 keyed "five black and white and five multi-colored cards": the five-five split is right but the wording is loose, since the achromatic cards are black and grey and only VIII, IX and X are fully multicoloured (II and III are black and red). It is still the only correct option; "only black and white", "only coloured" and "ten multicoloured" are wrong.

Scoring and reading a Rorschach record

Each response is coded first for its location, the part of the blot used: W the whole blot, D a common detail, Dd an unusual detail, and S white space (always coded with another location, as WS or DS). In Exner's system many W responses, especially well-organised ones, reflect organisational and integrative effort, the drive to pull a complex field into one meaningful whole. Developmental quality grades that integration: synthesised (+), ordinary (o) or vague (v). The form quality rating shows how well the percept fits the blot's actual shape, from good or ordinary to minus (distorted). It is the main index of reality testing: many minus responses mean perception driven by inner preoccupation rather than by the stimulus, as in psychosis. Content codes what was seen: H (whole human), Hd (human detail), A (animal), An (anatomy), Bl (blood) and others. A popular response (P) is one given very often, such as a bat or butterfly on Card V, and shows conventional perception; an original response is rare, a sign of creativity when well formed and of bizarre thinking when not.

Two further signs matter. An absence of human responses suggests poor interpersonal orientation, little interest in or understanding of people (not apathy or lack of empathy as such). A confabulatory response (DW) generalises from one detail to the whole blot ("a crab, because of these claws, so the whole thing is a crab"), and indicates impaired reality testing, not a regressed ego, cognitive immaturity or lack of empathy.

Exam trap

Shape alone is F; "blood because it is red", with no form, is pure C. FC and CF mix form and colour; Cn only names the colour. M is human movement only (FM animal, m inanimate); C' is achromatic.

Scoring systems: Exner, R-PAS and Holtzman

Rorschach left no full scoring system, and five American ones grew up in parallel: those of Samuel Beck, Bruno Klopfer, Marguerite Hertz, Zygmunt Piotrowski, and David Rapaport with Roy Schafer. Their codes differed, so research could not be compared. John E. Exner (1928 to 2006) combined their best-supported elements in his Comprehensive System (1974). It standardised administration, required at least 14 responses for a valid record, and tallied the codes into a Structural Summary of ratios and indices compared with norms. Lambda compares pure F responses with all others; a high Lambda suggests a simplifying, avoidant style. Experience Balance (EB) weighs human movement against colour: an introversive person (more M) works things out internally, an extratensive person (more colour) through feeling and action. Constellations flag specific risks: PTI (Perceptual-Thinking Index, formerly the Schizophrenia Index, SCZI), DEPI (depression), S-CON (suicide), CDI (coping deficit) and HVI (hypervigilance). The system gives no IQ score.

After Exner's death, Gregory Meyer and colleagues published the Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS, 2011). It keeps only research-supported variables, asks for two or three responses per card ("prompt and pull") so records are comparable in length, and reports standard scores against international norms. Wayne Holtzman had earlier solved the length problem differently: the Holtzman Inkblot Technique (1961) has two parallel forms of 45 cards each and allows one response per card, so every record is the same length and alternate-form reliability can be computed.

Exam trap

There is no "Murray system" of Rorschach scoring: Henry Murray gave us the TAT. The Comprehensive System is Exner's (not Beck's, Klopfer's or Piotrowski's) and scores the Rorschach, not the MMPI, TAT or Wechsler scales.

The Thematic Apperception Test

The Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) was introduced in 1935 by Christiana Morgan (1897 to 1967) and Henry Alexander Murray (1893 to 1988) at the Harvard Psychological Clinic; Murray's manual followed in 1943. To apperceive is to perceive in the light of past experience. The theory is Murray's need-press theory of psychogenic needs (Explorations in Personality, 1938; Section 4.6), not personal constructs (George Kelly), operant conditioning (B. F. Skinner) or cognitive distortions (Aaron Beck). For each picture of people in an ambiguous social scene, the person tells a dramatic story: what led up to it, what is happening, what the characters think and feel, and how it ends. The full set has 31 cards: 30 pictures plus blank card 16, on which the person imagines a scene. Classically 20 cards were given over two sessions; in practice a clinician selects about 5 to 12 per session.

The interpreter assumes the storyteller identifies with the hero and lends the hero their own needs, such as need for achievement (n Ach), affiliation (n Aff) and aggression (n Agg). The press is the force of the environment on the hero: alpha press as it really is, beta press as the person perceives it. A recurring need-press combination is a thema, and the outcome (success, failure, rescue) shows the person's expectations. Leopold Bellak later systematised this scoring, and David McClelland scored stories for the achievement motive (1953). The TAT therefore reveals unconscious thoughts, conflicts and motives, and a client's characteristic ways of thinking, feeling and behaving in close relationships; when stories about ambiguous pictures are wanted, it is the test of choice. It is a projective personality test, not an intelligence, achievement or neuropsychological test, and it does not measure vocabulary or memory.

Unlike the Rorschach, the TAT uses ambiguous social scenes with characters, not symmetrical inkblots, and asks for stories, not percepts; it shows the content of motives and relationships more than the structure of thought. Its descendants adapt the pictures to the teller. The Children's Apperception Test (CAT; Leopold and Sonya Sorel Bellak, 1949) has 10 cards of animals in human situations for children of 3 to 10, who identify readily with animals; the CAT-H (1965) uses human figures and the CAT-S is a supplement. The Bellaks' Senior Apperception Technique (1973) serves older adults, and the Roberts Apperception Test for Children (1982) adds normed scoring. Gerald Blum's Blacky Pictures (1950) show a dog family in scenes built around Freud's psychosexual stages, and Edwin Shneidman's Make-A-Picture-Story test (MAPS, 1947) lets the person place cut-out figures on backgrounds before telling the story.

Exam trap

TAT: Murray and Morgan, 1930s, a construction technique. Not Freud, Jung, Skinner, Rorschach, Exner or Bellak (Bellak made the CAT). The CAT differs mainly in its animal figures designed for children; it is not objective, not an intelligence test and uses no inkblots.

Indian context

Uma Chowdhury adapted the TAT (1960) and the CAT for India, with Indian figures, dress and settings. Udai Pareek adapted the Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Study (below); do not swap them.

Completion and verbal techniques

The oldest projective method is the word association test. Francis Galton tried it on himself in 1879. Carl Gustav Jung made it clinical (1910) with 100 stimulus words, timing each reply and watching for complex indicators (a delayed reaction, no reply, repeating the stimulus word, an odd answer) that betray an emotionally charged complex (Section 4.4). Grace Kent and Aaron Rosanoff published a 100-word list in 1910 with frequency norms from 1,000 normal adults, so a reply could be scored as common or idiosyncratic.

The sentence completion method grew from Hermann Ebbinghaus's completion method: the person finishes stems such as "My father..." or "I regret...". The Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank (Julian Rotter and Janet Rafferty, 1950) has 40 stems, each rated for conflict or positive adjustment and summed into one adjustment score. The Sacks Sentence Completion Test (Joseph Sacks and Sidney Levy, 1950) has 60 items covering 15 attitude areas in four groups: family, sex, interpersonal relationships and self-concept.

The Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Study (Saul Rosenzweig, 1945; some Indian texts give later dates) shows 24 cartoon-style frames of everyday frustrations; the respondent writes what the frustrated character says in an empty speech balloon. Replies are scored for direction of aggression: extrapunitive (blame turned outward), intropunitive (blame turned on oneself) or impunitive (blame avoided, the frustration glossed over); and for type of reaction: obstacle-dominance (the obstacle stands out), ego-defence (protecting the self) or need-persistence (seeking a solution). The Group Conformity Rating shows how often the person gives the commonest reply.

Expressive and choice techniques

In Karen Machover's Draw-A-Person test (1949) the person draws a person, then one of the other sex. Her body-image hypothesis holds that the drawing reflects the drawer's self-concept and unconscious attitudes, so size, placement, erasures and omitted parts are read as signs; it is not a test of fine motor skill alone, academic achievement or brain lesions. Contrast Florence Goodenough's Draw-a-Man, which counts details as an index of intellectual maturity (Section 2.6). John Buck's House-Tree-Person test (1948) asks for a house (home and family), a tree (deeper feelings about the self) and a person (self-concept and relationships), first in pencil (achromatic phase), then in crayon (chromatic phase), each followed by questioning. In the Kinetic Family Drawing (Robert Burns and S. Harvard Kaufman, 1970) the child draws everyone in the family doing something, revealing perceived closeness and conflict. Elizabeth Koppitz listed emotional indicators in children's figure drawings, such as a tiny figure or gross asymmetry. Max Hutt read the copying of the Bender designs projectively (Section 2.11).

The Szondi Test (Leopold Szondi, 1947) is the classic choice technique: from 48 photographs of psychiatric patients, in six sets of eight, the person picks the faces they like and dislike most; its genetic theory is unsupported. Margaret Lowenfeld's World Technique (a world built in a sand tray) and Mosaic Test (patterns from coloured pieces), the Hand Test (Edward Wagner, 1962: "What might this hand be doing?", read for acting-out tendencies), play, finger painting and graphology (handwriting analysis, with little validity) complete the expressive family.

Exam trap

House-Tree-Person is John Buck's, not Aaron T. Beck's (cognitive therapy), David D. Burns's (author of Feeling Good) or Karen Machover's (Draw-A-Person). Kinetic Family Drawing is Robert Burns's, a different Burns.

How good are projective tests?

The case against them is psychometric. Many techniques have competing scoring systems, so two clinicians may not be giving the same test. Inter-scorer reliability is acceptable only with structured systems such as Exner's or Rotter's; retest reliability is weak, because stories and percepts change between occasions. Global, intuitive interpretation has poor validity, and even scored variables show doubtful incremental validity: they add little to an interview and an inventory. Defenders reply that some Rorschach indices, notably of thought disorder and perceptual accuracy, hold up, and that the tests generate rich clinical hypotheses; they remain in clinical training and practice in India. Key study: Illusory correlation (Chapman and Chapman, 1967, 1969)

Loren Chapman and Jean Chapman asked why clinicians trusted signs that research did not support. They paired figure drawings with symptom statements at random and showed them to students, who "discovered" the links clinicians reported, such as odd eyes with suspiciousness, though the real correlation was zero. 2.11 Neuropsychological Assessment and Clinical Rating ScalesMedium yield · 40 Qs

A retired engineer forgets appointments and loses words mid-sentence. Is it depression, early dementia or a small stroke, and which abilities are gone? Neuropsychological tests profile what the brain can still do; clinical rating scales measure how severe symptoms are and whether treatment helps.

What neuropsychological assessment measures

Neuropsychological assessment infers how the brain is working from behaviour: each standardised task samples one function, and a characteristic pattern of failures points to the damaged system. Its domains are orientation and consciousness, attention and concentration, processing speed, memory, language, visuospatial and constructional skill, executive functions (planning, shifting, inhibiting and monitoring goal-directed behaviour) and motor and sensory function; mood is checked too, because it colours every score. Opinions, attitudes and reactions are not neuropsychological domains. The purposes are diagnosis, localisation, a baseline for measuring change, rehabilitation planning and judgments of capacity. Localisation itself is in Section 5.11.

A fixed battery gives every patient the same tests scored against norms (Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska); a flexible battery adds tests to a core to fit the referral question. The process approach of Edith Kaplan (1924 to 2009) in Boston holds that how a patient fails matters as much as the score (a piecemeal copy and an incomplete one may score alike). Its roots lie in the qualitative method of Alexander Romanovich Luria (1902 to 1977), who used no norms but watched exactly where a function broke down.

A score needs a standard. The normative comparison (nomothetic) standard compares the patient with healthy people of the same age and education. The individual comparison standard compares present performance with the person's own estimated premorbid level, which one Indian paper calls the 'ideometric' approach: an engineer who now scores average has declined. David Wechsler (1896 to 1981) noticed that some subtests 'hold' with age and illness (Vocabulary, Information, Picture Completion, Object Assembly) while others 'don't hold' (Digit Span, Similarities, Digit Symbol, Block Design). His deterioration index or quotient, (hold minus don't hold) divided by hold, estimates how far brain or psychiatric illness has eroded cognitive and intellectual ability. The National Adult Reading Test (Hazel Nelson, 1982) predicts premorbid IQ from reading aloud 50 irregular words such as 'debt', a skill that survives early dementia. Such profiles replaced the old screening question 'organic or functional?', once answered with a single test such as the Bender-Gestalt.

Exam trap

Estimating the impact of brain or psychiatric illness on intellectual ability means the deterioration quotient. Distractors: a mediation index belongs to statistics (Chapter 9), sense of coherence to Aaron Antonovsky's salutogenesis (Section 7.9), theory of mind to social cognition (Chapter 6). The 'ideometric' approach weighs premorbid functioning against current status; comparison with population norms is the normative approach, and neither quantitative scoring nor emotional well-being is meant.

Neuropsychological batteries

The classic fixed battery began with Ward Halstead's studies of frontal-lobe patients (1947) and was validated by his student Ralph Reitan (1922 to 2014). The Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery is used for comprehensive neuropsychological assessment: to detect and localise brain dysfunction and gauge its severity. Its tests are the Category Test (abstract reasoning and concept formation); the Tactual Performance Test (the blindfolded patient fits blocks into a form board, then draws it from memory, giving time, memory and localisation scores); the Seashore Rhythm Test; the Speech-Sounds Perception Test; the Finger Tapping (Finger Oscillation) Test of motor speed; and the Trail Making Test, Parts A and B. Reitan added the Reitan-Indiana Aphasia Screening Test, a sensory-perceptual examination, grip strength and lateral dominance. A Halstead Impairment Index (the proportion of key scores in the impaired range) of 0.5 or more suggests brain damage.

The Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery gave Luria's methods American norms: Anne-Lise Christensen compiled them (1975), and Charles Golden, Thomas Hammeke and Arnold Purisch standardised them (1978; manual 1980). Form I has 269 items scored 0 to 2 in 11 clinical scales, from motor and rhythm to memory and intellectual processes; high T scores mean impairment.

The Indian NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery (Bangalore; adult manual by Shobini Rao, D. K. Subbakrishna and K. Gopukumar, 2004, plus a children's battery) is a set of individually normed tests with Indian norms by age, sex and education: colour trails, digit vigilance, N-back, WCST, Tower of London, Stroop, verbal fluency, auditory verbal learning and a complex figure. The PGI Battery of Brain Dysfunction (PGIMER, Chandigarh; Dwarka Pershad and colleagues) combines the PGI Memory Scale, verbal and performance intelligence tests (the latter built on C. M. Bhatia's, Section 2.6), the Nahor-Benson perceptual test and the Bender-Gestalt. The PGI Memory Scale (Pershad and N. N. Wig, 1977) has 10 subtests: remote and recent memory, mental balance, attention and concentration, delayed and immediate recall, retention for similar and for dissimilar pairs, visual retention and recognition.

Exam trap

The Halstead-Reitan assesses brain function, not personality, vocational interest, attachment, self-reported depression or unconscious conflict, and it is not a projective test. The Rorschach is never part of it (the WAIS and MMPI are often given alongside). Luria did not write the Luria-Nebraska. PGI means Chandigarh; NIMHANS means Bangalore.

Memory and visual-motor tests

The Wechsler Memory Scale (Wechsler, 1945) began as seven brief subtests summed into a memory quotient. The WMS-IV (2009) gives five indices: Auditory Memory, Visual Memory, Visual Working Memory, Immediate Memory and Delayed Memory. Verbal and Performance IQ, like crystallised and fluid scores, belong to intelligence tests (Section 2.6); Mechanical Reasoning is a Differential Aptitude Tests subtest (Section 2.8).

In the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (André Rey, Geneva) the examiner reads 15 words over five trials, then an interference list, then asks for the first list again immediately and after a delay; a flat learning curve or heavy delayed loss signals a memory disorder. The California Verbal Learning Test (1987) uses a 16-word 'shopping list' of four categories, showing whether the person groups words by meaning. Visual memory is tested by the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure, a detailed drawing first copied (planning and construction) and later drawn from memory, and by the Benton Visual Retention Test (Arthur Benton, 1946), in which 10 designs are each shown briefly and drawn from memory.

The classic visual-motor test is the Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test (1938) of Lauretta Bender (1897 to 1987). She adapted nine figures (A and 1 to 8) from designs the Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer (1880 to 1943) had used to study perceptual organisation. Copying them requires perceiving each design as a whole and reproducing it by hand, so the test assesses visual-motor integration. Its errors screen for organic brain damage and neurological dysfunction in adults and for developmental immaturity in children, scored by Elizabeth Koppitz's developmental system (1963). The Bender-Gestalt II (2003) has 16 designs and a recall phase. Max Hutt also read the drawings projectively, a secondary use.

Simplest of all is digit span. Repeating digits in the order heard (forward span, about seven in adults) measures attention span. Repeating them in reverse (backward span) forces the person to hold and rearrange the digits at once, so it measures working memory (Section 1.7), not long-term, sensory or procedural memory.

Exam trap

The Bender does not test verbal intelligence, vocational interest, personality, unconscious conflict, depression, schizophrenia, ADHD, reading, auditory or working memory, verbal abstraction or premorbid intelligence (that is the NART). Bender (1938) means copying nine figures; Benton (1946) means drawing ten designs from memory.

Executive function, attention, thinking and language

The most widely used test of executive function is the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) of David Grant and Esta Berg (1948), with a clinical manual by Robert Heaton (1981). The patient sorts 128 response cards under four stimulus cards by colour, form or number, told only 'right' or 'wrong', and after ten correct sorts the rule changes without warning (Figure 2.17). A healthy person notices the run of 'wrong' and shifts; a frontal patient keeps sorting by the old rule. The scores are perseverative errors, categories achieved and failure to maintain set. The WCST measures set-shifting and cognitive flexibility and is most sensitive to dysfunction of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

Key study: Milner and the frontal lobes (1963)

In Montreal Brenda Milner gave the WCST to patients having brain surgery for epilepsy. Those with dorsolateral frontal excisions achieved few categories and made many perseverative errors, some while saying the rule had changed; temporal and posterior cases did not. Criticism: perseveration also follows damage elsewhere and occurs in schizophrenia, so the test is sensitive to frontal damage but not specific to it.

Exam trap

The 'Minnesota Number Ordering Test' and 'Manhattan Card Sorting Test' do not exist. The WCST does not measure memory, language, reading, visual acuity, mood or manual dexterity, and its region is the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, not the hippocampus, primary motor cortex or cerebellum.

The Trail Making Test, from the Army Individual Test Battery (1944), has two parts: Part A joins 25 numbered circles in order (attention and speed), and Part B alternates numbers and letters (1, A, 2, B and so on), adding set-shifting. In children with ADHD it assesses inattention; impulsivity, over-activity and social skills are better judged from continuous performance errors and rating scales. The Stroop Colour-Word Test (John Ridley Stroop, 1935) asks for the ink colour of conflicting colour words ('RED' printed in green), measuring response inhibition. The Tower of London (Tim Shallice, 1982), moving beads on three pegs to a goal in the fewest moves, measures planning, as does the Tower of Hanoi. Verbal fluency asks for words beginning with a letter in a minute (the Controlled Oral Word Association or FAS test) or from a category such as animals.

Sustained attention is tested by the Continuous Performance Test (H. Enger Rosvold and colleagues, 1956): in a long stream of letters the person responds only to a target, so omissions reflect inattention and commissions impulsivity. The Test of Everyday Attention measures sustained, selective and divided attention through everyday tasks such as map searching. Neither shows which side of the body is affected; Benton's Finger Localization Test does. The patient, without looking, identifies fingers the examiner touches, and consistently more errors on one hand point to the opposite hemisphere.

Concept formation, and with it thought disorder, is assessed by sorting. In the Object Sorting Test of Kurt Goldstein (1878 to 1965) and Martin Scheerer (1941) the patient groups everyday objects and explains why they belong together; concrete, bizarre or loose groupings expose disordered thinking directly. The Rorschach reveals it only indirectly (Section 2.10), the TAT taps needs and conflicts, and the Bender visual-motor skill. Proverbs and similarities test abstract against concrete thinking in the same way.

Language tests include the Boston Naming Test (60 line drawings), the Token Test of Ennio De Renzi and Luigi Vignolo (1962), which checks comprehension with commands such as 'touch the small red circle', and the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination and Western Aphasia Battery. The aphasias are in Section 5.10. Table 2.25 gathers the tests by domain.

Bedside screening and staging

A short screen settles whether cognition is impaired at all. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) of Marshal Folstein, Susan Folstein and Paul McHugh (1975) takes about ten minutes and is scored out of 30: orientation (10), registration (3), attention by serial sevens (5), recall (3), and language and copying (9). A score of 23 or below (cut-off 23/24) suggests impairment. It screens for and tracks global cognitive impairment and dementia, not personality, depression or IQ. Because it is verbal and education-bound, Mary Ganguli built the Hindi Mental State Examination (1995) for rural, largely illiterate older Indians.

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) of Ziad Nasreddine (2005) is also out of 30 and takes about ten minutes, but adds executive tasks (a short trail, fluency, abstraction), cube copying and clock drawing; 26 and above is normal. It is therefore more sensitive than the MMSE to mild cognitive impairment. Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination III extends the idea to 100 points.

Staging scales grade severity. The Glasgow Coma Scale of Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett (1974) rates level of consciousness from the best eye opening (1 to 4), verbal response (1 to 5) and motor response (1 to 6), totalling 3 to 15; 8 or below means severe injury or coma. In dementia the Clinical Dementia Rating grades six domains of cognition and daily function from 0 to 3, the Global Deterioration Scale (Barry Reisberg, 1982) describes seven stages, and the Hachinski Ischemic Score separates vascular from Alzheimer-type dementia (Section 8.10).

Exam trap

The Glasgow Coma Scale measures consciousness, not attention, memory or intelligence, and its minimum is 3, not 0. The MoCA's advantage over the MMSE is sensitivity to mild impairment, not brevity, and neither is reserved for neurologists.

Rating scales for depression and anxiety

Rating scales grade symptom severity and track change. What matters first is who completes them. In a self-report inventory the patient rates himself; in a clinician-rated scale a trained interviewer rates the patient from interview and observation. Structured diagnostic interviews (SCID, PSE) are in Section 7.2, and life-event scales such as Holmes and Rahe's in Section 1.11.

The Beck Depression Inventory of Aaron Temkin Beck (1921 to 2021), first published in 1961, is the most used self-report depression measure. The BDI-II (1996) has 21 items, each rated 0 to 3 for the past two weeks, so totals run from 0 to 63: 0 to 13 minimal, 14 to 19 mild, 20 to 28 moderate, 29 to 63 severe. For a patient with two months of sadness, anhedonia and worthlessness, the BDI is the instrument for further evaluation, not the Rorschach (projective), the MMPI (broad personality, Section 2.9) or the WAIS (IQ). Clinician-rated alternatives are the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (Max Hamilton, 1960) and the Montgomery-Asberg scale, which is sensitive to treatment change; the Zung scale (1965) and PHQ-9 are brief self-report screens, and Beck's Hopelessness Scale and Scale for Suicide Ideation assess suicide risk. For anxiety, the clinician-rated Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale pairs with the self-report Beck Anxiety Inventory and GAD-7. The Fear Survey Schedule of Joseph Wolpe and Peter Lang (1964) lists feared situations; the SUDS rating used in exposure is in Section 7.4.

Exam trap

RML 2025 keyed the BDI as 'typically used for neuropsychological assessment' against a Likert scale (an attitude-scaling format, Section 2.8), the 16PF (a personality inventory, Section 2.9) and the Peabody Individual Achievement Test (an achievement test, Section 2.8). The BDI is a self-report depression inventory, not a neuropsychological test; neuropsychologists add it to see whether low mood is depressing test scores. Choose it only because the others are further off.

Scales for OCD, psychosis, substances, children and forensic work

The Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) of Wayne Goodman and colleagues (1989) is the standard clinician-rated measure of OCD severity. After a symptom checklist, ten core items rate obsessions (five) and compulsions (five) on the same five dimensions, time, interference, distress, resistance and control, each 0 to 4, for a total of 0 to 40. It does not rate depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, PTSD or suicidal ideation.

For psychosis the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (John Overall and Donald Gorham, 1962) covers general psychopathology, and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) of Stanley Kay, Abraham Fiszbein and Lewis Opler (1987) measures symptom severity in schizophrenia with 30 items: 7 positive, 7 negative and 16 general psychopathology. Nancy Andreasen's SANS and SAPS rate negative and positive symptoms separately; the Young Mania Rating Scale rates mania. General screens such as the General Health Questionnaire (David Goldberg, 1972) and Leonard Derogatis's SCL-90-R detect distress without making a diagnosis. For substance use there are the WHO's Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), CAGE (Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener), the Michigan Alcoholism Screening Test (MAST), the CIWA-Ar for alcohol withdrawal, the SADQ for dependence severity and the Fagerstrom Test for nicotine dependence.

The Clinical Global Impression rates severity and improvement from 1 to 7; the Global Assessment of Functioning, dropped in DSM-5, gave way to the WHO's WHODAS 2.0. In India, disability from mental illness is certified with IDEAS, the Indian Disability Evaluation and Assessment Scale (Indian Psychiatric Society, 2002). Children are rated with the Conners Rating Scales (ADHD), Thomas Achenbach's Child Behavior Checklist and its Indian adaptation, Savita Malhotra's Childhood Psychopathology Measurement Schedule (PGIMER); the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (1987) screens new mothers. In forensic work the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised of Robert Hare (1980, revised 1991) rates 20 items 0 to 2 from interview and case records. Galton's Anthropometric Laboratory, Revisited

Look again at Galton's counter with this chapter behind you. Every visitor met the same instruments, instructions and recording, so his procedures were standardised and objective (Section 2.1). Measures such as height and grip strength are highly reliable (Section 2.3), yet Wissler showed that sensory and motor tests of this kind had almost no criterion validity for academic success (Section 2.4): consistency is necessary, not sufficient. Galton's thousands of cards were among the first large norms, and his spread of scores around an average foreshadowed standard scores (Section 2.5). Binet's turn to judgement and reasoning founded the intelligence tests of Section 2.6, and every later test has had to prove what Galton assumed. His belief in inherited ability, which fed eugenics, is one reason testing now carries rules of fairness.

Chapter summary

2.1 Psychological Testing: Nature, History, Types and Ethics. A psychological test is an objective, standardised measure of a sample of behaviour (Anastasi); assessment integrates tests with interview, observation and records. Testing runs from Chinese examinations through Galton's laboratory (1884), Cattell's 'mental tests' (1890) and Wissler's disproof (1901) to Binet and Simon (1905), the Army Alpha and Beta, Woodworth's inventory and the MMPI (1943). Tests are objective or subjective, speed or power, of maximum or typical performance. A good test is objective, reliable, valid, normed and practicable; standardisation fixes procedures and norms. Clinicians read data as samples, correlates or signs; actuarial prediction usually beats clinical judgement, which suffers confirmatory bias, illusory correlation and the Barnum effect.

2.2 Building a Test: Items, Item Analysis, IRT and Response Bias. Items are essay or objective (supply or selection type), and guessing is corrected by S = R - W/(n - 1). Item analysis gives difficulty p (proportion passing; ideal 0.50, about 0.62 for four options) and discrimination D = p upper - p lower on Kelley's 27 percent groups; a negative D flags a miskeyed item. Ceiling and floor effects stop a test discriminating at the top or bottom. In IRT the item characteristic curve plots success against the latent trait θ (b, a, c; Rasch 1PL to 3PL), and sample-invariant parameters allow adaptive testing. Acquiescence, social desirability and halo (Thorndike, 1920) bias scores.

2.3 Reliability: Consistency and the Standard Error of Measurement. Reliability is the consistency of a measure: dependability and freedom from measurement error. In Spearman's classical test theory (1904) every observed score is a true score plus random error, and reliability is the ratio of true-score to observed-score variance. Test-retest correlation gives the coefficient of stability, for stable traits; parallel forms give the coefficient of equivalence. Single-sitting internal consistency methods are split-half (stepped up by the Spearman-Brown formula, so .60 becomes .75), Rulon and Flanagan, KR-20 and KR-21 for right-wrong items, and Cronbach's alpha (1951). Cohen's kappa measures rater agreement beyond chance. The standard error of measurement, SD × √(1 - r), bands one person's score.

2.4 Validity and the Accuracy of Test Decisions. Validity is the extent to which a test measures what it claims to measure; since Messick (1989) it is a unified judgement about the interpretations and uses of scores. Face validity is mere appearance; content validity is adequate sampling of the domain (blueprint, Lawshe's CVR). Criterion-related validity is concurrent (criterion measured now) or predictive (criterion measured later). Construct validity (Cronbach and Meehl, 1955) rests on convergent and discriminant evidence and the multitrait-multimethod matrix (Campbell and Fiske, 1959). A test can be reliable without being valid, and validity cannot exceed the square root of reliability. Sensitivity and specificity describe the test; predictive values depend on the base rate.

2.5 Norms, Standard Scores and Interpreting a Score. A raw score means nothing without a frame of reference. Norm-referenced interpretation compares a person with a representative standardisation sample; criterion-referenced interpretation (Glaser, 1963) compares with a fixed mastery standard. Good norms are large, representative, recent and relevant. The ratio IQ (MA ÷ CA × 100) gave way to Wechsler's deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15). Percentile ranks are ordinal. The z score (mean 0, SD 1) and the normal curve (68, 95, 99.7 per cent within 1, 2, 3 SD) link T scores (50, 10), stanines (5, 2), stens (5.5, 2), scaled scores (10, 3) and CEEB scores (500, 100).

2.6 Intelligence Tests: Binet, Wechsler, Non-verbal and Indian Scales. Intelligence tests are reliable and predict school achievement but not behaviour in general. Binet and Simon (1905) built the first test; Terman's Stanford-Binet (1916) used the ratio IQ, and the SB5 (Roid, 2003) measures five factors in verbal and non-verbal domains. Wechsler's Bellevue scale (1939) introduced the deviation IQ; the WAIS-IV (2008; 16:0 to 90:11) gives an FSIQ and four indices (VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI) from 10 core subtests, and the WISC-V covers 6:0 to 16:11. Raven's Progressive Matrices measures fluid reasoning and is culture-fair. In India, Bhatia's performance battery suits illiterate people, while the Binet-Kamat, MISIC and WAPIS adapt the Stanford-Binet, WISC and WAIS.

2.7 Assessing Development, Adaptive Behaviour and Disability. Development and everyday competence are scored apart from IQ as DQ (developmental age / CA × 100) and SQ (social age / CA × 100), from several informants because symptoms vary across contexts. Infant scales include the Bayley (1 to 42 months), Gesell's schedules, Denver II and the Indian DST, DASII and TDSC. Doll's VSMS (1935; 117 items; SQ) and Malin's Indian version (89 items) measure social maturity; the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, adaptive functioning. DSM-5 intellectual disability needs IQ and adaptive deficits, with severity graded adaptively; global developmental delay is for under-5s. Under the RPWD Act 2016, learning disability is certified from 8 years, autism with ISAA.

2.8 Aptitude, Achievement, Creativity, Interest and Attitude Measures. An achievement test measures what has been learnt in a specific academic area; an aptitude test predicts future learning in a specific area, and Anne Anastasi placed both on one continuum. Multiple aptitude batteries (the DAT with eight subtests, 1947; the GATB with nine aptitudes) serve guidance. Guilford's divergent thinking is scored for fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration (TTCT; Mednick's RAT). Strong keyed interests empirically, Kuder used ipsative forced-choice triads and Holland proposed RIASEC. Thurstone's equal-appearing intervals use judges and median scale values; Likert's summated ratings sum respondents' agreement ratings; Guttman's cumulative scale needs reproducibility of 0.90; Bogardus measured social distance; the IAT measures implicit attitudes by reaction time.

2.9 Objective Personality Inventories. An objective personality test fixes items, answer format and scoring. Cattell accepted three data sources: L-data (life records), Q-data (questionnaires) and T-data (objective tests). Inventories are built rationally (Woodworth's Personal Data Sheet, 1917), theoretically (EPPS, MBTI, MCMI), by empirical criterion keying (MMPI, CPI) or by factor analysis (16PF, EPQ, NEO-PI-R, GZTS, Comrey). The MMPI (Hathaway and McKinley, 1943) assesses personality and psychopathology with validity scales L, F and K and ten clinical scales; the MMPI-2, MMPI-2-RF (RC scales) and MMPI-3 followed. The Rep test and Q-sort study one person at a time.

2.10 Projective Techniques. Projective techniques rest on the projective hypothesis (term coined by Frank, 1939): faced with an ambiguous stimulus, people project needs, conflicts and unconscious processes into their answers, which resists self-report bias. Lindzey (1959) grouped them as association, construction, completion, choice or ordering and expression. The Rorschach (1921) has 10 cards (5 achromatic, 2 black-red, 3 multicoloured), a response and inquiry phase, and codes for location, determinants and form quality; Exner's Comprehensive System (1974) and R-PAS (2011) standardised it. The TAT (Morgan and Murray, 1935; 31 cards) is read for needs and press. Sentence completion, the Rosenzweig P-F, figure drawings and the Szondi complete the family, all psychometrically weak.

2.11 Neuropsychological Assessment and Clinical Rating Scales. Neuropsychological assessment infers brain function from behaviour across attention, memory, language, visuospatial, executive, motor and sensory domains, using fixed batteries (Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska), flexible batteries or Kaplan's process approach, and comparing scores with norms or with the person's own premorbid level (Wechsler's deterioration quotient, the NART). India has the NIMHANS and PGI batteries. Key tests are the WMS-IV, Rey AVLT, the Bender-Gestalt (visual-motor integration), backward digit span (working memory) and the WCST (set-shifting, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex). The MMSE, MoCA and Glasgow Coma Scale screen and stage. Rating scales are self-report (BDI) or clinician-rated (Hamilton scales, Y-BOCS, PANSS, PCL-R).

From the past papers

  1. The primary difference between a "speed test" and a "power test" in psychological assessment is that a speed test: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Has items of increasing difficulty, while a power test has items of uniform difficulty. (b) Has a strict time limit that prevents most test-takers from completing all items, while a power test has items so difficult that no one can answer them all correctly. (c) Measures reaction time, while a power measures cognitive ability. (d) Is administered individually, while a power test can be administered in groups.

    Answer: (b). A speed test uses easy items under a time limit so strict that few finish; a power test allows generous time, but its items grow so hard that no one passes them all. Option (a) reverses the two, (c) confuses a timed test with reaction time, and (d) confuses timing with individual versus group administration.

  2. Which of the following is the most important characteristic of a psychological test? (RML 2024)

    (a) Objectivity (b) Subjectivity (c) Validity (d) Rationality

    Answer (keyed): (a) Objectivity. RML follows Indian texts such as A. K. Singh, which list objectivity first: a score must not depend on who gives or scores the test. Most textbooks and the 2014 Standards call validity the most fundamental property, so on other papers choose (c). Subjectivity is a defect, and rationality is not a test characteristic.

  3. Which of the following refers to the notion of the confirmatory bias? (RML 2021)

    (a) Clinicians ignore information that does not support their hypothesis (b) Clinicians ignore information that does not support their stereotypes. (c) Clinicians interpret ambiguous information as supporting their hypothesis. (d) All of the above

    Answer: (d) All of the above. Confirmatory bias is the tendency to notice, weigh and interpret information so that it confirms what one already believes; ignoring contrary evidence, ignoring evidence against one's stereotypes and reading ambiguity as support are all forms of it.

  4. The improvement in anxiety may sometimes spuriously improve depression. This is called: (RML 2024)

    (a) Halo effect (b) Ceiling effect (c) Hawthorne effect (d) Therapeutic effect

    Answer: (a) Halo effect. One attribute (the improved anxiety) colours the rating of another, so the depression rating improves although the depression has not. A ceiling effect is a test too easy to register further gains, the Hawthorne effect is change because people know they are being studied, and a therapeutic effect is real improvement from treatment.

  5. A response style or bias, in which there is tendency to agree with test items regardless of their contents is called: (RML 2024)

    (a) Halo effect (b) Central tendency effect (c) Social desirability effect (d) Acquiescence effect

    Answer: (d) Acquiescence effect. Acquiescence (yea-saying) is agreeing regardless of content and is controlled by balanced keying. Social desirability depends on content (the answer that looks good), central tendency is choosing the middle of the scale, and halo is a rater's error, not a test-taker's.

  6. The tendency of the rater/interviewer to judge an individual on the basis of one single attribute is referred to as : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) Similarity error (b) Central tendency error (c) Contrast error (d) Halo error

    Answer: (d) Halo error. Named by Thorndike (1920), halo lets one attribute or a general impression colour every other rating. Similarity error favours people like the rater, central tendency error rates everyone average, and contrast error rates others as opposite to the rater.

  7. Which of the following is assessed by Cronbach's alpha? (RML 2021)

    (a) Concurrent validity (b) Inter-rater reliability (c) Test retest reliability (d) Internal consistency

    Answer: (d) Internal consistency. Alpha (Lee Cronbach, 1951) estimates how well a scale's items hang together within one administration. Concurrent validity needs an outside criterion, inter-rater reliability needs two scorers (kappa or the intraclass correlation), and test-retest reliability needs two occasions.

  8. A test developer administers a new anxiety scale to a group of college students and, two weeks later, administers the same scale again to the same group. She then correlates the two sets of scores. This process is used to establish: (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Split-half reliability (b) Inter-rater reliability (c) Test-retest reliability (d) Parallel forms reliability

    Answer: (c) Test-retest reliability. Same scale, same people, two occasions, scores correlated: the coefficient of stability. Split-half correlates two halves of one sitting, inter-rater reliability compares scorers, and parallel forms would need a second, equivalent version of the scale.

  9. Odd-even method of splitting the test to estimate split-half reliability is MOST appropriate for : (RML 2022)

    (a) Projective tests (b) Neuropsychological tests (c) Power tests (d) Speed tests

    Answer: (c) Power tests. With graded items and generous time, odd and even halves are fair parallel samples. On a speed test the halves match by construction and the coefficient is spuriously high, and projective and many neuropsychological tests lack comparable, objectively scored items to split.

  10. To determine whether a newly developed intelligence test truly accurately measures intelligence or not, it is necessary to administer the test with one that has been in the field for some time. Which of the following needs to be looked for? (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Construct validity (b) Concurrent validity (c) Content validity (d) Predictive validity

    Answer: (b) Concurrent validity. Correlating a new test with an established test given at about the same time is the concurrent type of criterion-related validity. Predictive validity needs a criterion measured later, content validity concerns sampling of the domain, and construct validity rests on a network of evidence rather than one established test.

  11. Which of the following statements is TRUE? (RML 2024)

    (a) A test can be valid without being reliable (b) A test can be reliable without being valid (c) If a test measures what it is intended to measure, then by definition it is reliable (d) If a test gives consistent scores when administered repeatedly then it is valid

    Answer: (b) A test can be reliable without being valid. A test can consistently measure the wrong thing. It cannot be valid without being reliable, but option (c) overstates this as true by definition, and consistency alone (d) never proves validity.

  12. A high false positive rate in diagnosis means : (IHBAS 2025)

    (a) Diagnosis is too strict (b) Normal people are diagnosed as disordered (c) Diagnosis has low reliability (d) Disordered people go undetected

    Answer: (b) Normal people are diagnosed as disordered. A false positive is a positive call on someone without the condition, the mark of low specificity. Undetected disordered people (d) are false negatives, which an over-strict diagnosis (a) produces; reliability (c) is a different property.

  13. Which statistical measure tells us how far individual data points are dispersed from the mean expressed in standard deviation units? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Variance (b) Z-score (Standard score) (c) Coefficient of variation (d) Standard error

    Answer: (b) Z-score (Standard score). z = (X − M) ÷ SD states how many SDs one score lies from the mean, so scores from different distributions can be compared. Variance (SD squared) describes the spread of a whole distribution, the coefficient of variation compares spread across distributions, and the standard error is the SD of a sampling distribution.

  14. IQ if any body has a mental age of 8 and a chronological age of 10 is: (RML 2020)

    (a) 108 (b) 125 (c) 80 (d) 92

    Answer: (c) 80. Ratio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100 = (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80. The tempting 125 inverts the ratio (CA ÷ MA); a mental age below the chronological age must give an IQ below 100.

  15. Which of the following is a non-linear transformation of raw scores? (Recall set)

    (a) z-score (b) 50 + 10z (c) Stanine (d) Deviation score (X − M)

    Answer: (c) Stanine. Stanines are assigned from percentile ranks in fixed proportions (4, 7, 12, 17, 20, 17, 12, 7, 4 per cent), a normalising, area transformation that forces a normal shape. z, the T score 50 + 10z and the deviation score X − M all apply one multiply-and-add rule to every score, so they are linear and keep the raw distribution's shape.

  16. In the WAIS-IV (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale - Fourth Edition), how many core subtests are there in the Verbal Comprehension Index and the Perceptual Reasoning Index respectively? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) 3 verbal, 4 performance (b) 3 verbal, 3 performance (c) 4 verbal, 3 performance (d) 2 verbal, 4 performance

    Answer: (b) 3 verbal, 3 performance. The VCI core is Similarities, Vocabulary and Information (Comprehension is supplemental); the PRI core is Block Design, Matrix Reasoning and Visual Puzzles (Figure Weights and Picture Completion are supplemental). Counting a supplemental subtest as core produces the tempting 4.

  17. A 10-year-old child from a rural area, with no formal schooling, is brought for an intellectual assessment due to academic struggles. The child is illiterate and has had minimal exposure to formal testing materials. Which of the following tests would be the MOST appropriate to use to minimize cultural and educational bias? (Real PYQ 2024)

    (a) Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence (b) Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC-IV) (c) Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale (5th ed.) (d) Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC)

    Answer: (a) Bhatia's Battery. Its five performance subtests need no reading and carry Indian norms. The WISC-IV and SB5 rest on Western verbal content and norms, and MISIC, though Indian, is half verbal and assumes schooling.

  18. An IQ test does NOT provide which of the following? (RML 2021)

    (a) High test retest reliability (b) Good predictor of behaviour (c) High internal consistency (d) Good validity

    Answer: (b) Good predictor of behaviour. IQ tests have high test-retest reliability, high internal consistency and good validity for predicting school achievement, but how a person behaves in general depends on personality, motivation and circumstance.

  19. Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS) is a measure of : (RML 2022)

    (a) Mental age and Intelligence quotient (b) Social age and social quotient (c) Developmental age and developmental quotient (d) Emotional age and emotional quotient

    Answer: (b) Social age and social quotient. Doll's VSMS converts the items an informant credits into a social age, and SQ = SA / CA × 100. Mental age and IQ come from intelligence tests, developmental age and DQ from developmental scales such as Gesell's or the DST.

  20. A key differentiation between global developmental delay and intellectual disability is (JEMAS 2025)

    (a) Age of assessment (under 5 years for GDD, 5 years and above for ID) (b) Presence of social deficits (c) Language delay alone (d) Motor impairment

    Answer: (a) Age of assessment. DSM-5 reserves global developmental delay for children under 5 who are too young for reliable standardised testing and must be reassessed; from 5 years the diagnosis is intellectual disability. The deficits are of the same kind, so social, language or motor problems do not separate them.

  21. Name of the Indian tool to assess the severity and disability of children with autism : (IHBAS 2024)

    (a) CGAS (b) Childhood Autism Rating Scale (c) CBCL (d) ISAA

    Answer: (d) ISAA (the paper keys (b) CARS). The Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism (NIMH Secunderabad, 2009; 40 items) is the Indian instrument prescribed for certifying autism and its disability percentage; CARS is the American scale of Schopler and colleagues on which ISAA partly drew. CGAS rates global functioning and the CBCL general behaviour problems.

  22. Summated rating scale is also known as : (RML 2022)

    (a) Likert scale (b) Thurstone scale (c) Guttmann scale (d) Equal appearing interval scale

    Answer: (a) Likert scale. Likert (1932) had respondents rate their agreement with each statement and summed the ratings, hence summated ratings. The Thurstone scale and the equal-appearing interval scale are one and the same judge-scaled method, and the Guttman scale is cumulative.

  23. The Implicit Association Test is designed to measure: (RML 2022)

    (a) Verbal ability (b) Unconscious stereotyping (c) Hidden mathematical ability (d) Dream content

    Answer: (b) Unconscious stereotyping. Greenwald, McGhee and Schwartz (1998) built the IAT to reveal implicit attitudes and stereotypes from reaction times in a sorting task. It is not an ability test of any kind, and dream content is not its target.

  24. What is the primary purpose of achievement tests in psychological assessment? (RML 2025)

    (a) To measure future success potential (b) To assess cognitive abilities related to brain function (c) To measure understanding and knowledge in specific academic areas (d) To evaluate unconscious conflicts and emotions

    Answer: (c) To measure understanding and knowledge in specific academic areas. Achievement tests look back at what has been learnt. Future potential is the aim of aptitude tests, brain-related abilities of neuropsychological tests, and unconscious conflicts of projective techniques.

  25. Which of the following statements is TRUE with respect to Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)? (RML 2022)

    (a) It utilizes rational method of test construction (b) It is constructed by assuming specific personality traits and formulating questions to answer them (c) It is derived by finding items that discriminate between criterion and control groups (d) It is true with respect to both a and b

    Answer: (c) It is derived by finding items that discriminate between criterion and control groups. Hathaway and McKinley kept only items that separated patient groups from normal controls, whatever their content (empirical criterion keying). Options (a) and (b) describe the rational, logical-content strategy of Woodworth's Personal Data Sheet.

  26. According to Raymond Cattell's classification of personality data sources, which of the following is NOT recognized as a legitimate data type? (Real PYQ 2025)

    (a) Q-data (Questionnaire data) (b) L-data (Life record data) (c) T-data (Objective test data) (d) P-data (Projective data)

    Answer: (d) P-data. Cattell recognised only three sources: L-data (life records such as school and criminal records, and observer ratings), Q-data (self-report questionnaires) and T-data (objective tests in controlled situations whose purpose is hidden).

  27. Elevated Lie scale on Multiphasic Questionnaire (MPQ) is indicative of: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Tendency to fake and manipulate (b) Social desirability (c) Mistrust (d) All the above

    Answer: (d) All the above. A high Lie score means the person denies common minor faults: an attempt to fake a good impression, a pull towards social desirability, and a guarded mistrust that admits no flaw to the examiner.

  28. How many Rorschach cards are coloured? (RML 2022)

    (a) 10 (b) 5 (c) 3 (d) 4

    Answer: (b) 5. Two cards (II, III) are black with red and three (VIII, IX, X) are multicoloured, so five carry colour and five (I, IV, V, VI, VII) are achromatic. Three counts only the fully coloured cards; ten is the whole set.

  29. Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) is based on which technique of psychological testing? (RML 2022)

    (a) Expressive technique (b) Construction technique (c) Association technique (d) Completion technique

    Answer: (b) Construction technique. In Lindzey's scheme the person constructs a story about each picture. Association is the Rorschach and word association, completion is sentence completion and the Rosenzweig P-F, and expression is drawing and play.

  30. Absence of 'Human' Response on Rorschach Psychodiagnostics Test indicates: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Apathy (b) Poor interpersonal orientation (c) Lack of empathy (d) All the above

    Answer: (b) Poor interpersonal orientation. Human content reflects interest in and understanding of people, so its absence points to poor interpersonal orientation. Apathy and lack of empathy are not the standard reading, so 'All the above' is wrong as well.

  31. One of the most widely used tests to assess executive functioning: (Amity 2023)

    (a) Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (b) Minnesota Number Ordering Test (c) Manhattan Card Sorting Test (d) None of the above

    Answer: (a) Wisconsin Card Sorting Test. Grant and Berg's WCST (1948) measures set-shifting and cognitive flexibility through perseverative errors when the sorting rule changes unannounced, and is most sensitive to dorsolateral prefrontal dysfunction. The Minnesota and Manhattan tests are invented distractors, so 'none of the above' is wrong too.

  32. Which of the following is a clinician-rated questionnaire? (RML 2022)

    (a) Beck Depression Inventory (b) Beck Anxiety Inventory (c) Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (d) Both b and c

    Answer: (c) Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale. Max Hamilton's 14-item anxiety scale (1959) is completed by a trained clinician from interview and observation. Both Beck inventories are self-report: the patient rates his own symptoms, so (a), (b) and (d) are wrong.

  33. Impact of psychiatric illness on one's cognitive and intellectual abilities can be estimated on the basis of: (IHBAS 2020)

    (a) Deterioration quotient (b) Mediation index (c) Sense of coherence index (d) Theory of Mind index

    Answer: (a) Deterioration quotient. Wechsler compared 'hold' subtests (Vocabulary, Information, Picture Completion, Object Assembly) with 'don't hold' subtests (Digit Span, Similarities, Digit Symbol, Block Design) to estimate loss from illness. Mediation is a statistical concept, sense of coherence is Antonovsky's health concept, and theory of mind concerns understanding others' minds.
