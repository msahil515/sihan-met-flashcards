Ethics in Practice — the terms they test

Each principle + what it actually looks like in the clinic room · Built 2026-05-22

How they ask this. Either straight definition ("non-maleficence means ___") or a vignette ("a clinician does X, which principle is violated?"). The 4 big ones are Beauchamp & Childress's principles. The rest are the day-to-day rules that fall out of them. For each, learn the one-line definition and the in-practice example, because the vignette items only give you the scenario.

The 4 core principles (Beauchamp & Childress)

Mnemonic: "A-B-N-J" / "Auto Brings No Justice." Autonomy · Beneficence · Non-maleficence · Justice.

PrincipleOne lineIn practice
AutonomyRespect the client's right to decide for themselvesInformed consent · right to refuse treatment · not coercing
BeneficenceAct for the client's goodChoosing the treatment most likely to help · promoting wellbeing
Non-maleficence"First, do no harm" (primum non nocere)Not using unproven/harmful methods · staying within competence
JusticeFairness — treat like cases alike, distribute resources fairlyNo discrimination · fair access to care · equitable waitlists
The classic swap: beneficence = do good, non-maleficence = avoid harm. They're a pair but separate principles. "Withholding a harmful drug" = non-maleficence. "Giving a helpful one" = beneficence.

What falls under Autonomy

Informed consent: client understands the nature, risks, benefits, and alternatives, and agrees voluntarily and with capacity. Three pillars: disclosure · capacity · voluntariness.

Assent: agreement from someone who can't legally consent (a minor) — you still get their assent alongside the parent's consent.

Right to refuse / withdraw at any time without penalty.

Paternalism = overriding a competent person's choice "for their own good." It's the violation of autonomy. Only justified when the person lacks capacity.

Confidentiality and its limits

Confidentiality: not disclosing client information. Privacy = the client's right; confidentiality = your duty to protect it; privileged communication = the legal protection in court.

Limits / when you must break it: risk of serious harm to self or others · suspected child/elder abuse (mandatory reporting) · court order.

Duty to warn / protect (Tarasoff): if a client makes a credible threat against an identifiable person, the therapist must act to protect that person. This is where non-maleficence/beneficence to a third party overrides confidentiality. Very common vignette.

The other named terms

TermMeans
FidelityLoyalty, keeping promises, being trustworthy and reliable to the client
VeracityTruth-telling — being honest with the client (e.g. about diagnosis, prognosis)
CompetencePractising only within the limits of your training; refer out otherwise
Dual / multiple relationshipHaving a second role with a client (friend, business, romantic) — a boundary violation
Duty of careThe legal/ethical obligation to act in the client's best interest
NegligenceFalling below the accepted standard of care, causing harm (basis of malpractice)
Capacity / competence to consentWhether the person can understand and reason about the decision
VulnerabilityExtra protection owed to those who can't fully protect their own interests (children, severe MI)

Indian context (worth a line)

RCI (Rehabilitation Council of India) licenses clinical psychologists and has its own code of ethics. Mental Healthcare Act 2017 codifies advance directives, nominated representative, right to confidentiality, and consent for persons with mental illness, plus decriminalises suicide attempt. APA Ethics Code is the international reference (the 5 general principles: beneficence/non-maleficence, fidelity/responsibility, integrity, justice, respect for rights & dignity).

The traps

  1. Beneficence vs non-maleficence. Do good vs avoid harm. If the answer is about stopping/avoiding something harmful, it's non-maleficence.
  2. Privacy vs confidentiality vs privilege. Privacy = the right · confidentiality = your duty · privileged communication = court protection.
  3. Consent vs assent. Adults with capacity give consent; minors give assent (parent gives consent).
  4. Autonomy vs paternalism. Overriding a competent refusal "for their good" violates autonomy. Only ok if capacity is genuinely absent.
  5. Confidentiality is not absolute. Harm to self/others, abuse reporting, and court orders override it. Tarasoff is the headline case.
