HPA Axis — Stress Endocrinology

Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal axis · Built 2026-05-16

Why this matters. HPA shows up in MET + NIMHANS papers under at least four hats: (1) biopsych "draw the axis," (2) abnormal psych "HPA in MDD/PTSD/Cushing's," (3) stress/health psych "Selye + allostatic load," (4) assessment "dexamethasone suppression test." One page, all four.

Three things to know cold: CRH → ACTH → cortisol, the negative feedback loop, and which psychiatric disorder shows what kind of HPA dysregulation.

1. The axis in one diagram

STRESSOR (physical, psychological, immune)
  ↓
HYPOTHALAMUS (paraventricular nucleus, PVN)
  → releases CRH (Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone) + AVP (vasopressin, synergistic)
  ↓ (via portal blood, infundibular stalk)
ANTERIOR PITUITARY (corticotroph cells)
  → releases ACTH (Adrenocorticotropic Hormone) from POMC precursor
  ↓ (via systemic circulation)
ADRENAL CORTEX — zona fasciculata
  → releases CORTISOL (glucocorticoid) + small DHEA, weak aldosterone
  ↓
TARGET TISSUES: liver, muscle, fat, immune, brain

NEGATIVE FEEDBACK: cortisol inhibits both hypothalamus (↓CRH) and pituitary (↓ACTH).
This loop shuts the response off when the stressor ends. In MDD and PTSD it breaks.

1.1 Anatomy details to memorise

  • Paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of hypothalamus — the brain origin of stress.
  • CRH = 41-amino-acid peptide, half-life ~60 min. Co-secreted with AVP (potentiates).
  • Anterior pituitary corticotrophs make POMC → cleaved to ACTH + β-endorphin + MSH.
  • Adrenal cortex layers (outside in): Glomerulosa (aldosterone), Fasciculata (cortisol), Reticularis (androgens — DHEA). Memory: "GFR, salt-sugar-sex deeper you go".
  • Adrenal medulla (inner) makes adrenaline / noradrenaline via SAM axis — fast, "fight-or-flight". HPA is the slower, sustained arm.

3. Cortisol — what it actually does

  • Metabolic: ↑gluconeogenesis, ↑glycogenolysis, ↑lipolysis, ↑proteolysis (catabolic). Raises blood glucose.
  • Immune: suppresses inflammation, decreases lymphocytes, neutrophilia. That's why glucocorticoids are anti-inflammatory drugs.
  • Cardiovascular: maintains vascular tone, permissive for catecholamines.
  • CNS: alertness, attention. At chronic high levels: hippocampal atrophy, memory deficits, mood changes.
  • GI: ↑gastric acid (chronic stress → ulcers).
  • Bone: ↑resorption, ↓formation → osteoporosis with chronic excess.

3.1 Cortisol's diurnal rhythm

  • Peak: ~30 min after waking ("cortisol awakening response", CAR).
  • Trough: around midnight.
  • Tightly tied to SCN (suprachiasmatic nucleus) circadian rhythm via the hypothalamus.
  • Disrupted in shift workers, depression, chronic insomnia, jet lag.

4. HPA dysregulation in psychiatric disorders

4.1 Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

  • Hyperactive HPA axis is one of the most replicated biological findings in MDD.
  • Elevated baseline cortisol; blunted diurnal variation; flattened cortisol awakening response.
  • Enlarged adrenal glands on imaging.
  • Dexamethasone Suppression Test (DST): give 1 mg dex at 11 pm, measure cortisol next morning. In healthy subjects → cortisol suppressed. In ~50% of melancholic / psychotic depression → non-suppression. Sensitivity ~40-50%, specificity ~80-90%, not used diagnostically but historically big in NIMHANS papers (Carroll DST).
  • Mechanism: impaired glucocorticoid receptor feedback → cortisol cannot turn the axis off → sustained CRH drive. Reduces hippocampal BDNF, contributes to volume loss.
  • SSRIs and CBT both normalise HPA over weeks of treatment.

4.2 PTSD — paradox

  • PTSD shows low basal cortisol (often) but enhanced negative feedback (super-suppression on low-dose DST).
  • Elevated CRH in CSF.
  • Theory: hypersensitive glucocorticoid receptors → over-suppression of cortisol after stressor.
  • Contrast with MDD: depressed = hypercortisolaemic with failed feedback; PTSD = hypocortisolaemic with exaggerated feedback. Frequent MCQ trap.

4.3 Cushing's syndrome / disease

  • Excess cortisol from any cause.
  • Cushing's disease = pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma (most common endogenous cause).
  • Cushing's syndrome = the broader phenotype, often iatrogenic (exogenous steroids).
  • Physical: central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, hirsutism, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, thin skin, easy bruising.
  • Psychiatric: depression in 50-80%, irritability, anxiety, insomnia, occasional psychosis or mania. Treating the Cushing's often resolves the depression — pure example of endocrine psychiatry.

4.4 Addison's disease

  • Primary adrenal insufficiency — cortisol and aldosterone deficient.
  • Fatigue, weight loss, hyperpigmentation (high ACTH stimulates MSH), hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia.
  • Psychiatric: apathy, depression, occasional psychosis. Reverses with hydrocortisone replacement.

4.5 Anxiety disorders, panic, OCD

  • Findings are mixed. Panic disorder shows mild HPA activation during attacks but normal baselines.
  • GAD: subtle blunting of CAR, elevated evening cortisol.
  • OCD: no clear HPA signature — less examinable than mood / PTSD.

4.6 Schizophrenia

  • HPA hyperactivity at first episode, especially with poor outcome.
  • Cortisol rises before psychotic relapse in some prospective studies.
  • Linked to dopamine sensitisation theory ("threat → cortisol → mesolimbic DA").

4.7 Eating disorders

  • Anorexia nervosa: hypercortisolaemia, loss of diurnal variation, often DST non-suppression even without depression.
  • Restoration of weight partly normalises HPA.

4.8 Early life stress / abuse

  • Childhood maltreatment → durable HPA dysregulation in adulthood (often blunted reactivity + altered glucocorticoid receptor methylation).
  • Mediator for later depression, PTSD, addiction risk.
  • McGowan / Meaney rat studies — maternal licking ↑ glucocorticoid receptor in hippocampus → calmer adult. Classic epigenetics MCQ.

5. Stress theory — Selye and beyond

5.1 Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)

StageWhat happensHPA state
Alarm"Fight or flight": SAM activation first, then HPA kicks inCortisol rises sharply
ResistanceBody adapts, mobilises resources, performance can riseCortisol elevated and sustained
ExhaustionResources depleted, disease onset (ulcers, infection, depression)HPA dysregulation — high or low
Memory: A-R-E (Alarm, Resistance, Exhaustion). Selye called the non-specific response to demand "stress" and the trigger "stressor". His rat experiments (1936) launched the field.

5.2 Cannon — "fight or flight" (1915)

  • Walter Cannon coined "fight or flight" — the immediate SAM response.
  • Predates Selye; describes the acute phase only.
  • "Homeostasis" is also Cannon's term — keeping internal milieu constant.

5.3 Allostasis and allostatic load (McEwen, 1993+)

  • Allostasis = achieving stability through change (active adaptation, not just returning to a setpoint).
  • Allostatic load = the wear-and-tear from chronic activation of adaptive systems (HPA, SAM, immune).
  • Allostatic overload = the load becomes pathology: cardiovascular disease, depression, metabolic syndrome, immune dysfunction.
  • Updates Selye's "exhaustion" with a cumulative, multi-system model.
  • Bruce McEwen — high-yield name for any "modern stress theory" MCQ.

5.4 Lazarus & Folkman — cognitive appraisal (1984)

  • Stress is the relationship between person and environment appraised as exceeding resources.
  • Primary appraisal: is this event harmful/threatening/challenging?
  • Secondary appraisal: can I cope with it?
  • Coping styles: problem-focused vs emotion-focused.
  • Transactional model — bridges biology with cognition; what activates HPA depends on appraisal, not the event alone.

6. Measuring the HPA axis

  • Salivary cortisol — non-invasive, captures diurnal rhythm and CAR. Used in research.
  • 24-hour urinary free cortisol — best for Cushing's screening.
  • Midnight serum cortisol — should be low; non-suppression suggests Cushing's.
  • Dexamethasone Suppression Test (DST):
    • Low-dose (1 mg overnight) — screen for Cushing's; non-suppression abnormal.
    • High-dose (8 mg) — distinguishes pituitary Cushing's (suppresses) from ectopic ACTH (no suppression).
    • In psychiatry: Carroll DST in depression — non-suppression suggests melancholic depression.
  • CRH stimulation test — distinguishes hypothalamic vs pituitary defects.
  • Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) — laboratory stressor (mock interview + arithmetic). Reliably activates HPA in healthy controls; blunted in many psychiatric groups. Highly cited.

7. HPA + neuroplasticity — why chronic cortisol matters

  • Hippocampus is densely packed with glucocorticoid receptors. Chronic cortisol → dendritic atrophy in CA3, suppression of neurogenesis in dentate gyrus, hippocampal volume loss (MRI in MDD, PTSD, Cushing's).
  • Cortisol ↓ BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) → less synaptic plasticity. Antidepressants reverse this over weeks.
  • Prefrontal cortex also atrophies; amygdala often hypertrophies or hyperreacts → fear bias.
  • This biology underpins the "neurotrophic hypothesis of depression": stress → ↑cortisol → ↓BDNF → ↓hippocampal plasticity → depressive phenotype. Reversed by antidepressants and exercise.

9. Top 10 MCQ traps

  1. "Releasing hormone for cortisol" → CRH, not ACTH. ACTH is the pituitary hormone downstream of CRH.
  2. Adrenal cortex layers: GFR → glomerulosa (aldosterone) / fasciculata (cortisol) / reticularis (androgens). Medulla = adrenaline.
  3. MDD = hyper-cortisol with failed suppression. PTSD = hypo-cortisol with super-suppression. Don't mix these up.
  4. DST non-suppression has poor sensitivity — not diagnostic, only suggestive of melancholic depression.
  5. Cushing's disease = pituitary adenoma. Cushing's syndrome = any cause of hypercortisolism (often iatrogenic steroids).
  6. Addison's hyperpigmentation is from MSH (made from same POMC precursor as ACTH).
  7. "Stress" was coined by Selye, "fight or flight" by Cannon, "allostatic load" by McEwen, "cognitive appraisal" by Lazarus & Folkman.
  8. Cortisol awakening response peaks ~30 minutes after waking, not at waking.
  9. Chronic cortisol → hippocampal atrophy (memory complaints in depression, Cushing's).
  10. Glucocorticoid receptor methylation (Meaney) is the headline epigenetics finding linking early life care to adult stress reactivity.
