Originators — "name the thing + who gave it"

The "who proposed what" layer NIMHANS bolts on top of the clinical stuff. Named effects, complexes, personality concepts, social-psych theories, motivation theories and psychometrics terms, each pinned to the person who coined it. Built 2026-05-24 after your prof flagged Icarus complex, McCall's T and reactance as the kind of curveball they ask.

Why this page exists. Your professor said the exam asked: the Icarus complex and who gave it, McCall's T, and reactance. None are core clinical. Two tells, not one: "who coined this named effect / complex / concept" (Icarus, McCall's T), AND "here's a phenomenon described in words, name the term" (reactance was this kind: the scenario was described and reactance was the answer). 0. The three your prof named

Lock these cold.
TermWhoWhat it is
Icarus complexHenry MurrayA personality pattern of soaring ambition + craving for attention/admiration that ends in a fall (fascination with fire, ascent, falling, narcissism). Same Murray who gave the TAT, needs/press theory and personology.
McCall's T scoreWilliam McCallA standardised score with mean 50, SD 10 (no negatives, no decimals; MMPI uses it). Psychometrics, not clinical. Recognise it from the definition (that's how your prof asked it, not "who gave it"): they describe the score and the options are McCall's T vs other "t" things (Student's t, t-test, T-data). The trap is the shared letter: McCall's T = a standard score; Student's t = a test statistic (Gosset). Score with mean 50, SD 10 → McCall's T.
(Psychological) reactanceJack Brehm (1966)The motivational push-back when you feel a freedom is being threatened or removed: you want the forbidden thing more. Social psych / motivation. Recognise it from a scenario (that's how your prof asked it): "told you can't have X, you now want X more / do it anyway" → reactance. "Reverse psychology" and the forbidden-fruit effect are reactance in action.
Pattern to take away: personality theorists and their named complexes, social-psych theories with their author, and test/measurement jargon with the mean+SD. Everything below is that same layer, widened.

2. Personality theorists & their named concepts

TheoristThe concepts they ownWhat the headline idea actually says
Henry MurrayPersonology; needs & press; TAT; Icarus complex; nAch rootsPersonality runs on internal needs (achievement, affiliation...) pushed and pulled by environmental press (the pressures a situation applies). The TAT was his tool to read those needs off the stories people tell about ambiguous pictures.
Gordon AllportCardinal / central / secondary traits; functional autonomy; idiographic approachTraits are the real units of personality, ranked by reach: a cardinal trait dominates a whole life, central traits are your handful of defining ones, secondary are situational. Functional autonomy = a motive outliving its original cause (you keep fishing long after you stopped needing the food).
Raymond Cattell16PF; source vs surface traits; fluid vs crystallised intelligence; factor analysisUsed factor analysis to boil personality down to 16 source traits (the deep causes) sitting under the visible surface traits. Also split intelligence into fluid (raw reasoning, peaks young) and crystallised (built-up knowledge, holds with age).
Hans EysenckPEN model (Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism)Just three biologically-rooted dimensions, tied to nervous-system arousal: introverts are chronically over-aroused so they avoid stimulation, extraverts under-aroused so they chase it. Argued the basis is inherited.
Costa & McCraeBig Five / NEO-PI (OCEAN)The modern consensus: five factors (Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism) capture most of personality. Empirically derived, cross-cultural, the default trait model today.
Carl JungAnalytical psychology; collective unconscious, archetypes; introversion/extraversionBelow your personal unconscious sits a collective unconscious shared by all humans, stocked with archetypes (shadow, anima/animus, self) — inherited templates that surface in myths and dreams everywhere.
Alfred AdlerIndividual psychology; inferiority/superiority complex; style of life; birth order; social interestThe master motive is striving for superiority to overcome childhood inferiority feelings. Overdo the compensation and you get a superiority complex; your unique way of pursuing it is your style of life, shaped partly by birth order.
Karen HorneyBasic anxiety; moving toward/against/away from people; womb envy; neurotic needsNeurosis grows from basic anxiety (feeling small and alone in a hostile world), handled by three styles: moving toward (compliance), against (aggression) or away (withdrawal). Countered Freud with womb envy.
Erich FrommEscape from freedom; character orientationsFreedom is frightening, so people flee it into conformity, authoritarianism or destructiveness rather than bear the loneliness of being truly free. The healthy path is the productive orientation.
Harry Stack SullivanInterpersonal theory; the self-system; good-me / bad-me / not-mePersonality only exists in relationships, not inside one head. The self-system guards against anxiety; the self forms as good-me (rewarded), bad-me (punished) and the dissociated, dread-laden not-me.
George KellyPersonal construct theory; Rep grid; "man as scientist"Each person is a scientist running their own theory of the world through bipolar personal constructs (e.g. kind–cruel) used to predict events. Distress = your constructs failing to predict; the Rep grid maps them.
Julian RotterLocus of control (internal/external); social learning; expectancyBehaviour depends on your expectancy of a reward times how much you value it. Locus of control = whether you believe outcomes come from your own actions (internal) or from luck/fate/others (external).
Walter MischelPerson–situation debate; CAPS; the marshmallow testArgued traits predict behaviour poorly because situations matter so much (the person–situation debate). His marshmallow test showed kids' delay of gratification predicted later outcomes.
Albert BanduraSelf-efficacy; reciprocal determinism; observational learning; Bobo dollWe learn by watching others, no reinforcement needed (Bobo doll). Self-efficacy = belief you can pull off a task, which drives whether you try. Reciprocal determinism: person, behaviour and environment all shape each other.
Abraham MaslowHierarchy of needs; self-actualisation; peak experiences; Jonah complexNeeds stack in a pyramid (physiological → safety → love → esteem → self-actualisation); lower ones must be largely met before higher ones drive you. Self-actualisation = becoming all you can be.
Carl RogersSelf-concept; congruence; unconditional positive regard; fully functioning personPeople grow toward their potential when accepted without conditions. Trouble comes from the gap between the real self and ideal self; unconditional positive regard closes it and powers his client-centred therapy.
William SheldonSomatotypes: endomorph / mesomorph / ectomorphBody build predicts temperament: round endomorph = sociable/relaxed, muscular mesomorph = bold/energetic, thin ectomorph = restrained/private. Discredited now but still examined.
Ernst KretschmerBody-build → temperament types (precursor to Sheldon)Earlier version of the same idea: linked physique (pyknic, asthenic, athletic) to temperament and even to psychosis type. Sheldon built directly on him.
Swap-trap: womb envy = Horney (reply to Freud's penis envy). Inferiority complex = Adler, not Freud. Locus of control = Rotter. Self-efficacy = Bandura. Functional autonomy = Allport.

3. Social psychology theories & authors

AuthorWhat they gaveWhat the theory actually says
Leon FestingerCognitive dissonance; social comparison theoryHolding two clashing beliefs (or belief vs action) creates discomfort you're driven to reduce, usually by changing the attitude rather than the act. Social comparison: we judge ourselves by comparing to others when no objective yardstick exists.
Jack BrehmPsychological reactanceWhen a freedom is threatened or removed, you push back and want the forbidden option more. The engine behind reverse psychology and why bans can backfire. (Your prof's Q.)
Fritz HeiderAttribution theory; balance theoryStarted attribution: people act as naive scientists explaining behaviour by either the person (internal) or the situation (external). Balance theory: we want our likes/dislikes among people and things to stay consistent.
Harold KelleyCovariation model of attributionTo decide if a behaviour is caused by the person or situation, we weigh three things: consensus (do others do it too), consistency (does he always), distinctiveness (only with this target).
Jones & DavisCorrespondent inference theoryWe infer a stable trait from an act most confidently when the act was freely chosen, unexpected, and had few other reasons (non-common effects).
Lee RossCoined fundamental attribution errorWe over-blame other people's character and under-weight their situation when explaining their behaviour (but flip it for ourselves).
Muzafer SherifRealistic conflict theory; Robbers Cave; autokinetic norm formationGroup hostility comes from real competition over scarce resources, and only a superordinate goal needing both groups dissolves it (shown at the Robbers Cave summer camp).
Tajfel & TurnerSocial identity theory; minimal group paradigmPart of your self-esteem comes from group membership, so you favour your in-group even when groups are split on something trivial (the minimal group finding).
Solomon AschConformity (line judgement)People will give an obviously wrong answer to match a unanimous group. Normative conformity: going along to fit in, not because you believe it.
Stanley MilgramObedience to authority; six degrees / small worldOrdinary people will deliver what they think are lethal shocks if an authority figure tells them to. Obedience comes from the situation and the authority, not evil personalities.
Philip ZimbardoStanford prison; deindividuationAssigned roles and anonymity can make normal students turn cruel fast. Deindividuation = losing your sense of individual responsibility in a group/anonymous setting.
Irving JanisGroupthinkTight, pressured groups suppress dissent to keep harmony, producing bad decisions (illusion of unanimity, self-censorship). Explains fiascos like the Bay of Pigs.
Petty & CacioppoElaboration Likelihood ModelPersuasion takes two routes: the central route (you think hard about the argument, lasting change) when motivated/able, or the peripheral route (swayed by cues like attractiveness, weak change) when not.
Carl HovlandYale attitude-change approachMapped persuasion by who says it (source: credible, likeable), what is said (message), and to whom (audience).
Daryl BemSelf-perception theoryWe figure out our own attitudes by observing our behaviour, the same way we'd judge a stranger ("I keep reading about this, I guess I'm interested"). Rival explanation to dissonance.
Gordon AllportContact hypothesisContact between groups cuts prejudice, but only under conditions: equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support.
Adorno et al.Authoritarian personality (F-scale)A rigid personality type, measured by the F-scale, that submits to authority and is hostile to out-groups, proposed to explain susceptibility to fascism.
Norman TriplettFirst social-psych experiment → social facilitationCyclists rode faster against others than the clock. Social facilitation: the mere presence of others boosts performance on easy/well-learned tasks (and hurts hard ones).

3b. Compliance, influence & freedom (reactance, widened)

Reactance is the "threaten my freedom and I push back" theory. These sit right beside it: the techniques that get compliance, and the states that come from losing control.

TermWhoWhat it is
(Psychological) reactanceJack Brehm (1966)Threatened freedom → you want the forbidden thing more. (Your prof's Q.)
Learned helplessnessSeligman & MaierUncontrollable aversive events → you stop trying even when escape is possible. Model of depression. (Later: learned optimism, Seligman.)
6 principles of influenceRobert CialdiniReciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. Scarcity works because of reactance.
Foot-in-the-doorFreedman & FraserSmall request first → bigger one later succeeds.
Door-in-the-faceCialdini et al.Big request (refused) → moderate one looks reasonable.
Low-ball techniqueCialdini et al.Get agreement, then raise the cost.
Cognitive dissonanceLeon FestingerTension from holding clashing cognitions; you change attitude to resolve it.
Scenario tell: "told he can't do X, so he wants it more / does it anyway" = reactance (Brehm). "tried, failed repeatedly, gave up even when he could win" = learned helplessness (Seligman). Don't swap them.

4. Motivation & emotion theories

AuthorTheoryWhat it actually says
David McClellandNeed for achievement / affiliation / power (nAch); scored via TATThree learned social motives drive us in different mixes: achievement (master challenges), affiliation (be liked/belong), power (influence others). High-nAch people pick moderate, doable risks.
Clark HullDrive-reduction theoryA need creates a drive (tension); behaviour is motivated to reduce that drive and return to balance (homeostasis). Eating reduces the hunger drive.
Deci & RyanSelf-determination theory; intrinsic vs extrinsicWe're most motivated and well when three needs are met: autonomy (choice), competence (effectiveness), relatedness (connection). Dangling external rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation.
Mihaly CsikszentmihalyiFlowTotal absorption in a task when challenge and skill are both high and matched: time vanishes, self-consciousness drops. Too easy = boredom, too hard = anxiety.
Yerkes & DodsonArousal–performance law (inverted-U)Performance rises with arousal up to a point, then falls: too little and you're flat, too much and you choke. The optimum is lower for hard tasks than easy ones.
Bernard WeinerAttribution theory of motivationHow you explain success/failure drives future motivation, along three axes: locus (me vs outside), stability (lasting vs one-off), controllability. Blaming a stable, uncontrollable cause kills effort.
John AtkinsonAchievement-motivation modelAchievement behaviour is a tug-of-war between the hope of success and the fear of failure; which wins decides whether you approach or avoid the challenge.
Emotion theories (the trick is the order of events):
James–LangeBody first → emotionYou see the bear, your body reacts (heart pounds, you run), and you feel afraid because you notice those bodily changes. Emotion = reading your own physiology.
Cannon–BardBody + emotion simultaneouslyThe bodily response and the felt emotion fire at the same time, both triggered by the thalamus. You don't need the body feedback to feel the fear.
Schachter–SingerTwo-factor: arousal + labelYou feel general arousal, then look around for a cognitive label to explain it; the same racing heart becomes fear or excitement depending on context.
LazarusAppraisal firstA quick mental appraisal of the situation (threat or not) comes first and determines the emotion. Cognition precedes feeling.
ZajoncAffective primacyThe counter-claim: we can feel before we think, emotion can fire before any conscious appraisal (mere-exposure preferences are his evidence).

5. Psychometrics & measurement terms

TermOriginatorWhat it is
T score (mean 50, SD 10)William McCallA standard score rescaled so the mean is 50 and SD is 10, killing negatives and decimals. MMPI reports it. (This is the McCall's T your prof asked.)
Factor analysis, g, two-factor theoryCharles SpearmanInvented factor analysis and used it to argue intelligence is one general factor (g) plus task-specific factors (the two-factor theory).
Spearman–Brown prophecy formula; rank correlation (rho)SpearmanThe formula predicts how much reliability rises if you lengthen a test; rho correlates two sets of ranks.
Coefficient (Cronbach's) alphaLee CronbachThe standard index of internal consistency: how well a test's items hang together measuring one thing. Above ~0.7 is acceptable.
Construct validity (the paper)Cronbach & MeehlThe idea that a test is valid if it behaves the way the underlying construct theoretically should (relates to things it ought to, not to things it shouldn't).
KR-20 / KR-21 (dichotomous reliability)Kuder & RichardsonInternal-consistency formulas for tests scored right/wrong (a special case of alpha for dichotomous items).
Kappa (inter-rater); Cohen's d (effect size)Jacob CohenKappa = agreement between two raters, corrected for chance. Cohen's d = size of a difference between two means in SD units (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large).
Product-moment correlation (r); chi-squareKarl Pearsonr measures the strength/direction of a linear relationship between two interval variables (−1 to +1).
Correlation concept; regression to the meanFrancis GaltonFirst conceived correlation and noticed regression to the mean: extreme scores tend to be followed by ones closer to average.
Likert (summative agree–disagree) scaleRensis LikertThe familiar strongly-agree-to-strongly-disagree response scale; you sum item scores for a total. Treated as interval-ish.
Thurstone equal-appearing intervals; law of comparative judgment; PMALouis ThurstoneBuilt attitude scales with judged equal intervals, and argued against Spearman that intelligence is several primary mental abilities, not one g.
Guttman scalogram (cumulative scaling)Louis GuttmanA cumulative scale: agreeing with a strong item implies you'd agree with all the weaker ones, so a score pins your exact pattern.
Semantic differentialCharles OsgoodRate a concept between bipolar adjective pairs (good–bad, weak–strong); the dimensions reduce to evaluation, potency, activity.
Multitrait–multimethod matrix; convergent/discriminant validityCampbell & FiskeA grid checking that a test correlates with other measures of the same trait (convergent) but not with different traits (discriminant).
Item Response Theory / Rasch modelGeorg Rasch (IRT also Lord & Novick)Models the chance of a correct answer from the person's ability and the item's difficulty, so items and people sit on one scale (basis of adaptive testing).
Critical incident techniqueJohn FlanaganCollect real examples of especially effective/ineffective behaviour to define what a job actually requires (used in job analysis).
Ratio IQ (MA/CA×100)William Stern (popularised by Terman, Stanford-Binet)Old IQ = mental age ÷ chronological age × 100. Breaks down in adulthood since mental age plateaus, which is why deviation IQ replaced it.
Deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15)David WechslerModern IQ: your standing relative to your age group on a normal curve (mean 100, SD 15), not a mental-age ratio.

5b. Statistical tests & who gave them (Student's t, widened)

Student's t = William Sealy Gosset. "Student" is a pseudonym, not a person. Gosset worked at the Guinness brewery in Dublin and published the t-test in 1908 under the pen name "Student" because Guinness barred staff from publishing. So if the Q asks "who gave the t-test / who was Student," the answer is Gosset. That pseudonym fact is exactly the curveball NIMHANS loves.

What the t-test does: compares means when the sample is small and the population SD is unknown (you estimate it from the sample). That's the whole reason it exists separate from the z-test.

TestWho gave itWhat it's for
Student's t-test / t-distributionW. S. Gosset ("Student", 1908)Compare means, small n, σ unknown. Flavours: one-sample (df = n−1), independent two-sample (df = n₁+n₂−2), paired (before/after, same people).
ANOVA / F-testRonald Fisher (the F was named for Fisher by Snedecor)Compare 3+ group means at once. F = between-group var ÷ within-group var.
Chi-square (χ²)Karl PearsonAssociation/goodness-of-fit for categorical data.
Pearson rKarl PearsonLinear correlation, interval data.
Spearman rho (ρ)Charles SpearmanRank correlation (ordinal / non-normal).
Mann–Whitney UMann & WhitneyNonparametric twin of the independent t (two unrelated groups).
Wilcoxon signed-rankFrank WilcoxonNonparametric twin of the paired t.
Kruskal–Wallis HKruskal & WallisNonparametric twin of one-way ANOVA.
Friedman testMilton FriedmanNonparametric repeated-measures ANOVA.
McNemar testQuinn McNemarPaired nominal data (before/after yes-no).
t vs z: use z when the population SD is known and/or n is large; use t when SD is estimated from a small sample. As df → ∞ the t-distribution becomes the normal (z) curve. t is shorter and fatter-tailed than z.
Swap-traps: "Student" is Gosset, not a real student. 2 groups → t-test; 3+ groups → ANOVA (don't run repeated t-tests). Parametric → nonparametric pairs: independent t → Mann–Whitney, paired t → Wilcoxon, one-way ANOVA → Kruskal–Wallis. F-test/ANOVA = Fisher; chi-square = Pearson.

Conversion you can be asked: T = 50 + 10z. So z = +1 → T = 60; z = −2 → T = 30. MMPI clinical-range cut is usually T ≥ 65. Linear T = rescaled z (keeps the shape); normalised T = forced to a normal curve via percentile→z first. Both still mean 50, SD 10.
