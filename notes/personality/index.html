The one frame that organises the whole book. The book ends every chapter by scoring the theorist on six recurring Questions About Human Nature. Hold these six and you can predict any theorist's "view of the person" answer: (1) free will vs determinism · (2) nature vs nurture (heredity vs environment) · (3) past vs present (does childhood fix us, or does the here-and-now/future drive us) · (4) uniqueness vs universality · (5) equilibrium vs growth (is the basic motive to reduce tension and stay balanced, or to grow) · (6) optimism vs pessimism. Part One · The study of personality

Ch 1. Personality: what it is, and how the book studies it

Personality is the relatively enduring, distinctive pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviours that characterises a person and how they adapt to the world. The word comes from the Latin persona, the mask the Roman actor wore, which already hides the book's central tension: is personality the mask we show, or the real face behind it? Every theory in the book is one answer to four questions: what is personality made of, where does it come from, why do people differ, and can it change.

The book groups the theorists into approaches, and that grouping is the spine of the table of contents: the psychoanalytic (Freud); the neopsychoanalytic (Jung, Adler, Horney); the life-span (Erikson); the genetics/trait pioneer (Allport); the humanistic (Maslow, Rogers); the cognitive (Kelly); the behavioural (Skinner); the social-learning (Bandura); the trait approach (Cattell, Eysenck, the Five-Factor Model); and the facet / limited-domain theories (Rotter, Zuckerman, Seligman).

How the book assesses and researches personality

The book insists every theory must answer two practical questions, and the recurring "Assessment" and "Research" sections grade each theorist on them:

  • Assessment = how do you measure this theory's constructs? The methods recur: self-report inventories (objective, scored against norms, e.g. MMPI, 16PF, NEO), projective techniques (ambiguous stimuli, e.g. Rorschach, TAT), clinical interviews, behavioural assessment, and idiographic personal documents (diaries, letters). A good test must clear three bars: reliability (consistency), validity (measures what it claims), and standardised norms.
  • Research = how was the theory tested? Three designs recur: the clinical method (case studies, the dominant psychoanalytic tool, rich but unreliable and unrepresentative), the experimental method (manipulate a variable, the only route to cause and effect), and the correlational method (relate two measured variables, covariation only, never causation).
TRAP — method by camp. Case study / clinical method = the psychoanalysts (Freud, Jung, Adler). Correlational + factor analysis = the trait theorists (Cattell, Eysenck, FFM). Controlled experiment = the behaviourists and social-learning theorists (Skinner, Bandura) and Rogers, who pioneered experimental psychotherapy research. The method usually tells you the camp.
Recap Ch 1. Personality = the enduring, distinctive pattern of how a person thinks, feels and behaves. The book is organised by approach and grades every theorist on six human-nature issues plus Assessment and Research. Nomothetic (general laws, trait research) vs idiographic (the unique individual, case studies) is the master methodological split.

Part Two · The psychoanalytic approach

Ch 2. Sigmund Freud — psychoanalysis

The life (1856–1939). Born in Moravia, raised in Vienna, the adored eldest son of a young mother (the book notes this shaped his Oedipal theory). Trained as a neurologist; studied hysteria under Charcot in Paris and worked with Josef Breuer, whose patient Anna O. coined the "talking cure". A flirtation with cocaine, a long self-analysis after his father's death (the engine of The Interpretation of Dreams, 1900), decades of jaw cancer, and finally flight from the Nazis to London in 1938, where he died. The theory is, in part, Freud analysing Freud.

The problem he was solving. Patients had real symptoms (paralysis, blindness, fits) with no physical cause. Freud concluded the cause was mental and hidden: a painful idea pushed out of awareness, leaking out as a symptom. The whole edifice grows from that one move, that an unconscious drives behaviour we cannot explain.

Instincts: the engine

Behaviour is powered by instincts (drives), psychological representations of bodily needs. Two classes: Eros, the life instinct, whose energy is libido; and Thanatos, the death instinct, source of aggression. An instinct has a source, aim, object and impetus; cathexis is libido invested in an object.

The two maps of the mind

Topographic model (the iceberg): conscious (current awareness, the tip), preconscious (retrievable, just below), unconscious (repressed, inaccessible, the vast mass below the waterline that steers slips, symptoms and dreams).

Structural model (three agencies in constant negotiation):

  • Id — present at birth, wholly unconscious, runs on the pleasure principle (gratification now), thinks in illogical primary process. The screaming toddler.
  • Ego — runs on the reality principle, finds realistic ways to satisfy the id, delays gratification, uses rational secondary process thinking, and deploys the defences. The negotiator.
  • Superego — the internalised moral code, formed ~age 5 when the Oedipus complex resolves. Conscience (punishes with guilt) + ego-ideal (rewards with pride). The strict parent's voice.
Rider on a horse. Id = the horse's raw power; superego = the rulebook of where it may go; ego = the rider trying to get somewhere real without being thrown or paralysed. Anxiety is the ego's alarm that it is losing the negotiation; defences buy relief.

Anxiety and the defence mechanisms

Freud named three anxieties: reality anxiety (a real external threat), neurotic anxiety (fear the id will break loose), and moral anxiety (fear of the superego, i.e. guilt/shame). When anxiety threatens the ego, it distorts reality unconsciously: the defence mechanisms, catalogued by Anna Freud.

TRAP — the three "redirect" defences they swap: Displacement = redirect to a substitute target (yell at the dog). Sublimation = redirect into a socially acceptable activity (aggression → surgery). Projection = put your impulse onto another person. Read the stem for "target" vs "activity" vs "another person".
NIMHANS — Vaillant's hierarchy of defences. George Vaillant ranked defences by maturity: psychotic (denial of external reality, delusional projection, distortion) → immature (projection, acting out, passive aggression, fantasy, splitting, somatisation) → neurotic (repression, displacement, reaction formation, intellectualisation, rationalisation, undoing) → mature (sublimation, humour, altruism, suppression, anticipation). Trap: repression is unconscious + neurotic; suppression is the deliberate, conscious set-aside, and is mature, the only conscious defence.

Psychosexual stages

Libido settles on a different body zone at each age; how the conflict resolves shapes the adult, and getting stuck (over- or under-gratified) is fixation.

The Oedipus complex is the centrepiece: the boy desires his mother, sees his father as rival, fears castration anxiety, and resolves it by identifying with the father, thereby absorbing his morality (the superego). The female parallel, the Electra complex with penis envy, is the most criticised piece and was rejected outright by Horney.

Stage order mnemonic: Old (oral) Age Pensioners (phallic) Love (latency) Guinness (genital). And: the superego forms in the phallic stage, not at birth.

Assessment in Freud's theory

Three windows onto the unconscious: free association (say whatever comes, censorship off; blocks reveal resistance), dream analysis ("the royal road to the unconscious", separating the remembered manifest content from the disguised latent content, undoing the dream-work of condensation, displacement and symbolism), and Freudian slips / parapraxes (the tongue betraying the wish). Projective tests later operationalised the same logic.

Research on the theory

Built almost entirely on case studies (Anna O., Little Hans, Dora, the Rat Man, the Wolf Man), the clinical method at its richest and least controllable. Later controlled research gives a split verdict: the existence of unconscious processing and of some defences (especially displacement and projection) finds support; the psychosexual stages, the Oedipus complex and dream symbolism do not.

Questions about human nature (Freud's answers)

Strong determinism (we are run by instincts and childhood); nature and nurture, but biological instincts lead; the past rules (the first five years fix us); universality (the same stages and structures in everyone); equilibrium (the goal is to discharge tension, the pleasure principle); and deeply pessimistic about human nature. The dark end of all six issues.

Reflections (the criticisms)

Unscientific and largely unfalsifiable; built on a small, biased sample (neurotic, wealthy Viennese women); reliant on patients' fallible retrospective memory; overweighted on sex; and rigidly deterministic. Yet it is the foundation the entire book is reacting to: every later theorist defines themselves partly against Freud.

Recap Ch 2. Unconscious conflict → id/ego/superego → three anxieties → defences (Anna Freud) → five psychosexual stages → assessed by free association, dreams, slips → tested by case study → human nature: determinist, past-bound, pessimistic.

Part Three · The neopsychoanalytic approach

Ch 3. Carl Jung — analytical psychology

The life (1875–1961). Swiss pastor's son, solitary, drawn to dreams, myth and the occult. Freud's anointed "crown prince" after their famous 13-hour first conversation (1907); president of the psychoanalytic association; then the bitter break in 1913 over Freud's insistence that libido is sexual. The split triggered Jung's own years-long "confrontation with the unconscious" (the Red Book), which became the raw material of his theory.

Jung kept the unconscious but enlarged it and de-sexualised libido into general psychic energy. His map of the psyche:

  • Ego — the conscious mind.
  • Personal unconscious — your own repressed/forgotten material, organised into emotionally charged complexes (a "mother complex").
  • Collective unconscious — a deeper, inherited layer shared by all humanity, his single most testable idea, containing the archetypes: universal images. The key ones: persona (the social mask), shadow (the dark, denied side), anima/animus (the inner feminine/masculine), and the self (the unifying centre, symbolised by the mandala).

Attitudes & functions. Jung coined introversion vs extraversion (energy turned inward vs outward). He added four functions: the rational pair thinking and feeling, and the irrational pair sensing and intuiting. Two attitudes × four functions = his eight psychological types (later the seed of the MBTI). Individuation is the lifelong integration of all parts into the self, and Jung uniquely stressed midlife as its turning point.

Assessment & research

The word-association test (delays and odd responses expose complexes), symptom analysis, dream analysis (read in series, with amplification rather than free association), and comparative study of myth and symbol across cultures. The MBTI descends from his types but has weak psychometrics.

Questions about human nature

A balance of free will and determinism; past, present and future (uniquely teleological, we are pulled by aspirations and the self-to-be); nature and nurture; both unique and universal (archetypes are shared); strongly growth-oriented (individuation); cautiously optimistic.

Reflections

Criticised for mysticism, obscurity and untestable occult elements, but introversion/extraversion and the idea of complexes are permanent contributions.

Recap Ch 3. Jung: collective unconscious + archetypes (persona, shadow, anima/animus, self), introversion/extraversion + 4 functions → 8 types, individuation, midlife. De-sexualised libido. Teleological, growth-oriented.

Ch 4. Alfred Adler — individual psychology

The life (1870–1937). A sickly Viennese child (rickets, near-fatal pneumonia, run over twice in the street) who lived in the shadow of a healthy older brother, the autobiography behind inferiority and birth order. An ophthalmologist turned GP turned psychiatrist; an early member of Freud's circle who broke in 1911, rejecting the sexual emphasis for a social one. Coined "inferiority complex"; a huge influence on humanistic and cognitive therapy.

Adler said the one dynamic force in life is not pleasure but striving for superiority (really, striving for completion, competence and perfection). Every child begins small, weak and dependent and so feels inferiority; healthy growth means compensating for it. When it derails you get an inferiority complex (paralysing self-doubt, from organ inferiority, pampering, or neglect) or an over-compensating superiority complex. Other core ideas:

  • Style of life — the unique pattern each person builds by ~age 5 to pursue their goals.
  • Creative power of the self — we are not passive products of heredity and environment; we actively shape our own personality. (This is why Adler is a forerunner of humanism.)
  • Fictional finalism — we are pulled by future fictional goals (ideals like "honesty pays") and live "as if" they were true.
  • Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) — an innate, developable feeling of kinship with humanity; its presence is the mark of mental health.
  • Birth order — first-born (dethroned, conservative), second/middle (competitive, ambitious), youngest (pampered), only child (precocious, rivalrous with adults).

Assessment & research

Early recollections (your earliest memory reveals your style of life), dream analysis (about future problem-solving, not the past), and birth-order position. Research on social interest and early recollections is modest; birth-order findings are mixed.

Questions about human nature

Strong free will (the creative self); present and future over past (fictional goals pull us); nature and nurture but what you do with them matters most; uniqueness; growth; and firmly optimistic, the brightest of the psychoanalysts.

Reflections

Enormously influential (humanistic and cognitive therapy owe him a great deal) but the theory is loose, sometimes commonsensical, and hard to test.

Lock the trio: Freud → will to pleasure (sex); Adler → will to power (overcome inferiority); Jung → will to meaning/the collective unconscious. (Frankl later literally named the "will to meaning", see S3.) The single most-tested cluster in personality.

Ch 5. Karen Horney — neurotic needs and trends

The life (1885–1952). German; an unhappy childhood (felt unloved by a harsh father, clung to her mother) fed bouts of depression and her lifelong interest in how parenting breeds anxiety. Trained in psychoanalysis, emigrated to the US in 1932, and broke with orthodoxy to found her own institute, the first major theorist to challenge Freud's account of women from the inside.

Horney's foundation is basic anxiety: the feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world, rooted in a childhood lacking warmth and security. To cope, the child develops one of three neurotic trends (overuse of an otherwise-normal strategy), distilled from a longer list of 10 neurotic needs:

TrendThe moveThe neurotic version
Moving toward peopleCompliance, seeking affection & approvalThe "self-effacing" type, needs a partner to run their life
Moving against peopleAggression, seeking power, control, prestigeThe "expansive" type, must dominate and win
Moving away from peopleDetachment, seeking self-sufficiency & privacyThe "resigned" type, withdraws to avoid needing anyone

She also described the idealised self-image and the "tyranny of the should" (we tyrannise ourselves trying to match an impossible ideal self), and contrasted the real self with the idealised self. On gender she rejected penis envy, countering that men may feel "womb envy" (envy of women's capacity to bear life) and that apparent penis envy is really envy of men's social power.

Questions about human nature

Free will; the present (current character, though rooted in childhood); heavily nurture and culture over biology; growth/self-realisation; and optimistic (neurosis is learned, so it can be unlearned).

Reflections

Anticipated feminist psychology and the cultural turn; criticised because her data are clinical observations from a now-dated era.

Recap Chs 3–5. Jung = collective unconscious + archetypes. Adler = inferiority/superiority, birth order, social interest, creative self. Horney = basic anxiety → three trends (toward/against/away), womb envy. All three traded Freud's sex drive for, respectively, meaning, power, and social safety.

Part Four · The life-span approach

Ch 6. Erik Erikson — identity theory

The life (1902–1994). The theorist of identity who never knew his biological father and never took a degree, a Dane raised in Germany who felt an outsider everywhere (Jewish at school, Nordic-looking at temple). An artist who drifted into teaching at a Montessori-style school, was analysed by Anna Freud, emigrated to the US, and ended up a Harvard professor. His own "identity crisis" is the theory.

Erikson extended Freud in two decisive ways: development is lifelong (eight stages, birth to death, not five) and psychosocial rather than psychosexual (each stage is a social crisis). Growth follows the epigenetic principle (an inborn plan unfolding in fixed order). Each stage poses a tension between a healthy pole and an unhealthy one; resolving it yields a basic strength / virtue.

Erikson coined the term "identity crisis" and studied identity in culture (the Sioux and Yurok) and in history (his psychobiographies of Luther and Gandhi).

Assessment & research

Play therapy and play constructions, psychohistory, and dream analysis. The big research spin-off is James Marcia's identity statuses, which cross "crisis explored?" with "commitment made?": diffusion (neither), foreclosure (commitment without exploration), moratorium (exploring, not yet committed), achievement (committed after exploring).

Questions about human nature

A genuine balance of free will and determinism; past, present and future; nature and nurture (biology + society); universal stages but unique resolutions; growth; and optimistic (each crisis can be re-worked later).

TRAP — Freud vs Erikson stage pairing. Erikson's first four stages map onto Freud's (trust↔oral, autonomy↔anal, initiative↔phallic, industry↔latency), but Erikson's are psychosocial and run to eight across the whole lifespan; Freud's are psychosexual and stop at five by puberty. "Identity vs role confusion" and "intimacy vs isolation" are pure Erikson.

Part Five · The trait pioneer

Ch 7. Gordon Allport — motivation and personality

The life (1897–1967). A dutiful, orderly American academic whose most famous story is his one meeting with Freud: the 22-year-old Allport, to break the ice, described a boy on his train with a dirt phobia; Freud studied him and asked, "And was that little boy you?" Allport left convinced psychoanalysis digs for hidden meaning where none exists, and built a theory of the conscious, present, normal adult instead.

Allport defined traits as real neuropsychic structures that make many stimuli functionally equivalent and guide consistent behaviour. He organised them at three levels:

  • Cardinal traits — so dominant they define a whole life (rare; a "Scrooge", a "Machiavellian").
  • Central traits — the 5–10 core descriptors you'd put in a reference letter (honest, anxious, sociable).
  • Secondary traits / dispositions — situational preferences shown only sometimes.

His distinctive concepts: the proprium (his word for the self, developing through seven aspects from bodily self to propriate striving); functional autonomy (a motive can become independent of its origin, you ran to lose weight, now you run because you love it, so adult motives are not chained to childhood); and the lexical hypothesis (with Odbert he counted ~18,000 trait words: if a trait matters, language coins a word for it). Allport championed the idiographic (he called it morphogenic) study of the unique individual and, crucially, did not use factor analysis.

Assessment & research

Personal documents — most famously the case study Letters from Jenny (300+ letters analysed for her central traits) — plus the Study of Values (Allport-Vernon-Lindzey: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, religious) and expressive behaviour. He largely rejected the lab.

Questions about human nature

Strong free will; the present, not the past (functional autonomy frees us); the great champion of uniqueness (idiographic); nature and nurture; growth; and optimistic. There is even a discontinuity between the child (driven, Freudian) and the adult (autonomous, future-oriented).

Reflections

Made personality a respectable field and bridged academic and applied work, but functional autonomy's mechanism is left vague, and idiographic methods are hard to generalise from, so the theory generated little research.

TRAP — the "3" and the factor-analysis swap. Allport = 3 trait levels (cardinal/central/secondary), idiographic, NO factor analysis. Eysenck = 3 dimensions (PEN). Cattell = 16 factors and IS the factor-analysis man. Don't pin factor analysis on Allport.

Part Six · The humanistic approach (the "third force")

Humanism arose in the 1950s–60s as a revolt against the two ruling forces, psychoanalysis (which made us slaves of the unconscious) and behaviourism (which made us puppets of conditioning). Its creed: people have free will, are basically good, and are pulled forward by an innate drive toward growth and self-actualisation.

Ch 8. Abraham Maslow — needs-hierarchy theory

The life (1908–1970). A lonely, unhappy child of immigrant Jewish parents in Brooklyn (he openly loathed his mother). Began studying dominance and sexuality in monkeys with Harlow; after WWII and the birth of his children he turned to the healthy end of human nature and became the acknowledged "father of humanistic psychology" and the third force.

Maslow proposed that human needs stack in a hierarchy: lower needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher ones can motivate us. Bottom to top: physiological → safety → belongingness/love → esteem → self-actualisation. The lower four are deficiency needs (D-needs), felt as a lack you scramble to fill; the top is a being need (B-need) or growth need, felt as a pull. Late in life he added self-transcendence above self-actualisation.

He studied unusually healthy, accomplished people (Lincoln, Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt) and described the self-actualiser: realistic, accepting, spontaneous, problem-centred, autonomous, with deep but few relationships, a fresh appreciation of life, and peak experiences (moments of ecstasy and wholeness). The Jonah complex is the fear of one's own greatness that keeps people from self-actualising.

Assessment & research

Biographical analysis of his self-actualisers (the sample is the chief criticism, hand-picked and small) and the Personal Orientation Inventory (POI, Shostrom), which operationalised self-actualisation as a measurable trait.

Questions about human nature

Free will; present and future; needs are innate ("instinctoid", so nature) but expressed through nurture; uniqueness blooms at the top of the hierarchy; pure growth; and strongly optimistic.

Ch 9. Carl Rogers — person-centred theory

The life (1902–1987). Raised on a strict religious Illinois farm; left agriculture and then the ministry for psychology after a trip to China loosened his faith. Years of clinical work with troubled children convinced him that the client, not the expert, holds the key, hence client-centred therapy. The most research-minded of the humanists.

Rogers built everything on one motive, the actualising tendency (an innate push of every organism to maintain and enhance itself), and on the self / self-concept (your picture of who you are). He contrasted the real self with the ideal self: a rough match is congruence and well-being; a large gap is incongruence and distress. Why does incongruence develop? Because others love us conditionally, imposing conditions of worth ("I'll value you if you become a doctor"), which makes us betray our own organismic valuing process (the gut sense of what is right for us). The cure is unconditional positive regard (UPR). The goal of growth is the fully functioning person (open to experience, living existentially in the present, trusting the organism, free, creative). In therapy this distils to three core conditions: empathy, congruence/genuineness, and unconditional positive regard.

Assessment & research

Rogers pioneered psychotherapy research: he was the first to tape-record and transcribe sessions for analysis, and he used the Q-sort technique (sort cards describing the self, then the ideal self; the correlation between the two sorts is an objective measure of self-ideal congruence that should rise over successful therapy).

Questions about human nature

Free will; present and future; the actualising tendency is innate (nature) but conditions of worth are learned (nurture); uniqueness; growth; and optimistic.

TRAP — the two humanists they pair. Rogers = self-concept, real vs ideal self, congruence, conditions of worth, UPR, Q-sort, the three core conditions. Maslow = the hierarchy of needs, D- vs B-needs, self-actualisers, peak experiences, the POI. Both believe in growth; only Rogers gives you the therapy triad and the Q-sort.
Recap Chs 8–9. Maslow: needs hierarchy, self-actualisation, peak experiences, POI. Rogers: actualising tendency, self vs ideal self, conditions of worth → UPR, fully functioning person, Q-sort + recorded sessions. Third force = free will + innate goodness + growth.

Part Seven · The cognitive approach

Ch 10. George Kelly — personal construct theory

The life (1905–1967). A Kansas farm boy with an engineering and education background who ran travelling psychology clinics across Depression-era rural Kansas. The practical, no-dogma setting (no Freudian couch to be had) led him to a theory in which there is no fixed human nature to uncover, only the way each person construes the world.

Kelly's image is man-the-scientist: every person constantly forms hypotheses (personal constructs) about the world, tests them against events, and revises them. A construct is bipolar (kind–cruel, safe–dangerous). The philosophy behind it is constructive alternativism: there is always more than one way to construe any situation, so we are never prisoners of our biography, we can always re-construe. The theory is one fundamental postulate ("a person's processes are psychologically channelised by the ways in which they anticipate events") plus 11 corollaries (construction, individuality, organisation, dichotomy, choice, range, experience, modulation, fragmentation, commonality, sociality). Kelly even re-defined emotions cognitively: anxiety = events lie outside your constructs; threat = awareness of imminent comprehensive change to your constructs; guilt = acting against your core role construct; hostility = forcing reality to fit a failed construct.

Assessment & research

The Role Construct Repertory Test (Rep Test) and its grid form (the rep grid) elicit a person's own constructs; the self-characterisation sketch (write about yourself in the third person) feeds fixed-role therapy, where the client rehearses being a deliberately different character.

Questions about human nature

Free will (we choose our constructs); present and future (we are run by anticipation, so Kelly needs no separate "motivation", anticipation is the motive); both unique and common (the commonality corollary); growth-leaning; and optimistic. The environment matters only as we construe it.

Reflections

The pioneering cognitive theory of personality, decades ahead of its time, but criticised as cold and over-rational (it slights emotion, the unconscious, and development) and it spawned relatively little research outside the rep grid.

Part Eight · The behavioural approach

Ch 11. B. F. Skinner — reinforcement theory

The life (1904–1990). An American who wanted to be a novelist, failed ("the dark year"), and turned to psychology. The most influential, and most provocative, behaviourist: in Walden Two (a utopia engineered by reinforcement) and Beyond Freedom and Dignity he argued that "autonomous inner man" is an illusion we should discard.

Skinner's radical move: there is no inner "personality" to study at all. What we call personality is just a learned bundle of operant response patterns maintained by a history of reinforcement, so look at the environment, not inside the "black box". His machinery:

  • Operant conditioning — behaviour is shaped by its consequences. Reinforcement (positive = add something pleasant; negative = remove something aversive) increases a behaviour; punishment decreases it (and Skinner argued punishment is unreliable, it suppresses without teaching).
  • Schedules of reinforcement — fixed/variable × ratio/interval. Variable-ratio (the slot machine) produces the highest, most extinction-resistant responding.
  • Shaping — reinforcing successive approximations to build a complex behaviour.
  • Extinction — stop reinforcing and the behaviour fades; the superstition experiment showed accidental reinforcement can create "superstitious" behaviour in pigeons.

Assessment & research

Functional analysis (the A-B-C: Antecedent → Behaviour → Consequence) and direct observation, never a personality "test" of inner traits. The empirical base is strong: rat and pigeon labs, plus applied successes in behaviour modification and token economies.

Questions about human nature

Extreme determinism (the environment controls us absolutely); the present (current contingencies plus reinforcement history); pure nurture; universality (the laws of learning are the same for all species); neither equilibrium nor growth (behaviour is shaped from outside); pessimistic about free will but optimistic that we can engineer better behaviour.

Reflections

Rigorous, testable and hugely applicable, but criticised for extrapolating from animals to humans and for ignoring genetics, cognition and the inner life that Bandura would put back.

Part Nine · The social-learning approach

Ch 12. Albert Bandura — modeling / social-cognitive theory

The life (1925–2021). Canadian, from a tiny prairie town with a two-teacher school that taught him self-directed learning, a lived rehearsal of his later theme of human agency. Spent his career at Stanford; the most-cited living psychologist for years; renamed his approach "social-cognitive" to stress thought.

Bandura's correction to Skinner: we learn far more by watching than by being reinforced, and thought sits in the middle. His pillars:

  • Observational learning / modeling — we learn by watching models, no personal reinforcement needed (the Bobo doll studies: children who watched an adult hit the doll imitated the aggression). It runs through four processes: attention → retention → production (reproduction) → motivation. Vicarious reinforcement (seeing the model rewarded) raises imitation.
  • Reciprocal determinism — behaviour, personal/cognitive factors, and the environment all influence one another in a three-way loop; you are neither a free agent nor a passive product.
  • Self-efficacy — your belief in your ability to succeed at a specific task, which predicts whether you'll even try, persist, and recover. Its four sources: mastery experiences (the strongest), vicarious experience (seeing similar others succeed), verbal persuasion, and physiological/emotional state.
  • Self-regulation and the self-system (we set internal standards and judge ourselves against them); later, moral disengagement and collective efficacy.

Assessment & research

Direct behavioural observation and microanalytic self-efficacy scales. Strong experimental base: the Bobo doll series, and the demonstration that modeling (guided mastery) treats phobias better than insight.

Questions about human nature

Human agency within reciprocal limits (a middle position on free will); the present; nurture-leaning, but cognition is central; uniqueness possible; growth via agency; and optimistic (efficacy can be built).

TRAP — self-efficacy vs locus of control. Self-efficacy = Bandura = "can I do this task?" (task-specific competence belief). Locus of control = Rotter = "do I or outside forces control outcomes?" (source of control). Both sound like "belief about control"; the split is task-ability vs outcome-source.
Recap Chs 11–12. Skinner: no inner self, behaviour = operant patterns shaped by reinforcement schedules; functional analysis (A-B-C); determinist. Bandura: observational learning (Bobo doll, 4 processes), reciprocal determinism, self-efficacy (4 sources); agency + cognition put back in.

Part Ten · Advances in personality theory

Ch 13. The trait approach & the Five-Factor Model

Trait theorists got impatient with unprovable unconscious forces and asked a measurable question: what stable dispositions do people have, and how many basic ones are there? Their engine is factor analysis, a statistic that scans thousands of trait ratings and finds the few underlying dimensions ("factors") that explain the correlations.

Raymond Cattell (1905–1998)

The factor-analysis champion. He split surface traits (visible clusters of behaviour) from source traits (the deeper factors that cause them) and reduced personality to 16 source traits, which became the 16PF questionnaire. He drew on three data sources, L-data (life records), Q-data (questionnaires) and T-data (objective tests), and added dynamic traits (ergs = innate drives, sentiments = learned, attitudes), plus the famous distinction of fluid vs crystallised intelligence.

Hans Eysenck (1916–1997)

Argued for a few biologically based super-dimensions, the PEN model: Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism (he began with E and N on the EPI, then added P on the EPQ). His biological theory is elegant and testable: extraverts have chronically low cortical arousal (an under-active reticular activating system), so they seek stimulation to top up; neuroticism reflects an over-reactive limbic/autonomic system. Eysenck stressed strong heritability.

McCrae & Costa — the Five-Factor Model (OCEAN)

When researchers re-ran the trait data across languages and decades, five factors kept reappearing, the convergence that made the Five-Factor Model (championed by Costa & McCrae, measured by the NEO-PI-R) today's dominant model. (The lexical lineage: Galton → Allport & Odbert → Goldberg, who coined "Big Five".)

Questions about human nature (the trait camp)

More deterministic and biological than the humanists (especially Eysenck and McCrae-Costa, who stress genes); nature-heavy; the present (stable dispositions); universality (the same factors everywhere); neither growth nor equilibrium is the focus, traits simply are; relatively neutral on optimism.

TRAP — the trait numbers & theory-vs-description. Cattell = 16 (16PF, factor analysis). Eysenck = 3 (PEN, biological). FFM = 5 (OCEAN, NEO-PI-R). HEXACO = 6 (adds H). And: the Big Five is descriptive/atheoretical (says what traits exist); Eysenck's PEN is causal/biological (says why). Both contain Extraversion and Neuroticism, which is exactly why they get paired to trip you.

Ch 14. Facets of personality (the limited-domain theories)

The book's penultimate chapter collects theorists who illuminate one slice of personality rather than the whole person. Each is a high-yield "who studied X" item.

Julian Rotter — locus of control

From his social-learning theory, behaviour potential = expectancy × reinforcement value (how likely you think a reward is, times how much you want it). His famous slice is locus of control (measured by the I-E Scale): do you believe outcomes flow from your own actions (internal) or from luck, fate and powerful others (external)? Internals generally cope and achieve better.

Marvin Zuckerman — sensation seeking

A biologically based trait, the need for "varied, novel, complex and intense sensations and experiences", measured by the Sensation Seeking Scale (SSS) with four facets: thrill & adventure seeking, experience seeking, disinhibition, boredom susceptibility. Linked to dopamine and to risk-taking.

Martin Seligman — helplessness, optimism, positive psychology

The clearest example of a theorist who turned one finding into a whole programme. Learned helplessness (dogs that couldn't escape shock stopped trying even when escape became possible) became a model of depression; reformulated as explanatory / attributional style along three axes, internal–external, stable–unstable, global–specific (the pessimist explains bad events as internal-stable-global). That flipped into learned optimism, and Seligman went on to found positive psychology (the study of well-being: the PERMA model, signature character strengths).

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi — flow

Flow: the absorbed, timeless state of total engagement when a task's challenge exactly matches your skill, a cornerstone of positive psychology alongside Seligman.

TRAP — the facet owners. Locus of control = Rotter. Sensation seeking = Zuckerman. Learned helplessness / learned optimism / positive psychology = Seligman. Flow = Csikszentmihalyi. Self-efficacy is Bandura, not a facet theorist.

Part Eleven · Personality in perspective

Ch 15. Personality in perspective

The book's closing chapter steps back and asks what, after all the theories, we actually know. Its main threads, all examinable:

  • The person–situation debate, resolved by interactionism. Mischel (see Bandura's neighbours) showed behaviour is less cross-situationally consistent than pure trait theory assumed. The settled view is interactionism: stable traits and situations jointly produce behaviour, and people also select and shape the situations they enter.
  • Biological foundations. Twin and adoption studies put the heritability of major traits around 40–60%. Temperament appears early: Buss & Plomin (emotionality, activity, sociability, the EAS model) and Jerome Kagan (the inhibited vs uninhibited infant, a precursor of introversion). Thomas & Chess's easy/difficult/slow-to-warm-up temperaments belong here too.
  • Gender and culture. Personality expression and even which traits matter differ across individualist vs collectivist cultures; the FFM replicates broadly but not perfectly, and "indigenous" dimensions (e.g. the Indian Triguna, see S5) capture variance Western models miss.
  • The image of human nature. The book's parting note: the theories disagree most on the six human-nature questions, and which theory you find convincing says as much about your own assumptions as about the evidence.
Recap Chs 13–15. Trait approach (Cattell 16 / Eysenck 3 PEN / FFM 5 OCEAN / HEXACO 6) + facets (Rotter locus of control, Zuckerman sensation seeking, Seligman helplessness→optimism→positive psych, Csikszentmihalyi flow). Perspective: interactionism settles person vs situation; heritability ~40–60%; temperament (Buss-Plomin, Kagan); culture & gender matter.

Bandura's Bobo doll experiments (1961–63) — observational learning

Question: can aggression be learned simply by watching, with no reinforcement to the learner? What: nursery-school children watched an adult model either attack or ignore an inflatable Bobo doll (hitting it with a mallet, kicking it, shouting novel phrases); the children were then mildly frustrated and left alone with the doll. A later variant had the children see the model rewarded, punished, or face no consequence. Found: children who saw aggression imitated it, including novel acts and verbal aggression they had not seen before. Seeing the model rewarded raised imitation (vicarious reinforcement); seeing the model punished lowered performance but not learning — when later offered an incentive, the "punished" children reproduced the acts just as well. Matters: founded social-learning / social-cognitive theory and the crucial learning-vs-performance distinction; learning happens through observation alone, consequences only govern whether it is shown. Trap: the children learned the aggression regardless of the model's fate; consequences only changed whether they performed it. (Contrast Skinner, for whom learning requires the learner's own reinforcement.)

Bandura's self-efficacy & snake-phobia mastery studies

Question: what most powerfully raises a person's belief that they can do something, and does that belief drive behaviour? What: Bandura treated people with severe snake phobia by guided mastery / participant modeling (the therapist models touching the snake, then the client does it in graded steps), comparing it with watching a model only, and with symbolic/imaginal methods. Found: mastery experiences (actually performing the feared act successfully) produced the strongest, most generalised rise in self-efficacy and the biggest behaviour change, outperforming insight or vicarious methods alone. Matters: established the four sources of self-efficacy in rank order — mastery experiences (strongest) → vicarious experience → verbal persuasion → physiological/emotional state — and showed efficacy beliefs predict effort, persistence and recovery. The clinical lineage of exposure-based therapy. Trap: the strongest source of self-efficacy is mastery (performance accomplishment), not encouragement (verbal persuasion). Self-efficacy = Bandura, task-specific; do not confuse with Rotter's locus of control (outcome-source).

Rogers' Q-sort & self/ideal-self congruence research

Question: can the humanistic idea of "congruence" be measured objectively, and does therapy raise it? What: Rogers (with the Q-sort technique, devised by William Stephenson) had clients sort a deck of self-descriptive cards twice — once for the real / actual self ("how I am") and once for the ideal self ("how I'd like to be"). The correlation between the two sorts is an objective index of self-ideal congruence. He also tape-recorded and transcribed therapy sessions, a field first. Found: in successful client-centred therapy the real-self and ideal-self sorts moved closer together (congruence rose), and largely because the ideal became more realistic, not only because the real self "improved". Matters: Rogers turned a humanistic construct into a measurable, research-friendly variable and pioneered psychotherapy process research. Trap: a large real–ideal gap = incongruence (distress); a close match = congruence (adjustment). Q-sort = Rogers (Stephenson devised the tool).

Marcia's identity-status interview — operationalising Erikson

Question: can Erikson's "identity vs role confusion" be made measurable? What: James Marcia built a semi-structured Identity Status Interview that scores an adolescent on two axes: has the person undergone a crisis (active exploration of alternatives) and have they made a commitment? Found: crossing the two yields four identity statuses: diffusion (no exploration, no commitment), foreclosure (commitment without exploration — borrowed wholesale from parents), moratorium (in active crisis, exploring, not yet committed), achievement (committed after exploring). Matters: the single most-cited research operationalisation of an Eriksonian stage; achievement and moratorium are the more mature statuses. Trap: the trickiest pair is foreclosure (commitment, no exploration) vs moratorium (exploration, no commitment yet). Marcia ≠ Erikson: Erikson gave the stage, Marcia gave the four statuses.

Mischel — the person–situation debate & the marshmallow studies

Question (1968): how cross-situationally consistent is behaviour, really? What: Walter Mischel's book Personality and Assessment reviewed the evidence and argued trait scores predict behaviour only modestly across situations (the famous "personality coefficient" of about r ≈ 0.30), igniting the person–situation debate. Found / resolution: behaviour is more situation-dependent than pure trait theory assumed, but the settled answer is interactionism — stable dispositions and situations jointly produce behaviour, and Mischel's own CAPS model (cognitive-affective personality system) reframes consistency as stable "if–then" behavioural signatures (this person is aggressive when teased by peers but not when criticised by adults). The marshmallow / delay-of-gratification studies: preschoolers could eat one marshmallow now or wait alone for two; the seconds they could wait predicted later (correlational) outcomes — adolescent self-control and academic measures. Later large replications found the link is weaker and largely explained by background factors (affluence, home environment), a cautionary tale on over-claiming. Trap: Mischel = the person-situation critique and delay of gratification; the resolution of the debate is interactionism, not "situations win". Don't confuse delay of gratification (a cognitive self-regulation skill) with Freud's reality principle.

Friedman & Rosenman — Type A behaviour & coronary heart disease

Question: does a behaviour pattern predict heart disease? What: two cardiologists, Meyer Friedman & Ray Rosenman, classified men as Type A (competitive, hostile, impatient, time-urgent, achievement-driven) or Type B (relaxed, easygoing) and followed them prospectively (the Western Collaborative Group Study). Found: Type A men developed roughly twice the rate of coronary heart disease (CHD). Later analysis narrowed the toxic ingredient to hostility / anger rather than the whole pattern. Matters: a landmark in health/personality psychology; spawned Type C (cancer-prone, emotion-suppressing) and Type D ("distressed": negative affect + social inhibition, a cardiac risk marker — Denollet). Trap: Type A → heart disease; the most cardiotoxic component is hostility. Type = discrete category (the older, body-/pattern-based tradition), unlike a continuous trait.

Seligman — learned helplessness → learned optimism

Question: what does uncontrollable aversive experience do to motivation? What: Seligman & Maier gave dogs inescapable shock; later, in a box where escape was easy (just jump the barrier), these dogs failed to even try, unlike dogs that had earlier been able to escape. Found: learned helplessness — having learned that responses don't matter, the organism stops responding and shows deficits resembling depression. Reformulation (with Abramson): whether helplessness follows depends on the person's explanatory / attributional style along three axes — internal–external, stable–unstable, global–specific. The depressive/pessimist explains bad events as internal, stable, global ("it's me, it always happens, it ruins everything"). Matters: a leading cognitive model of depression; flipped into learned optimism and launched positive psychology (PERMA, signature strengths). Trap: the pessimistic style for bad events = internal + stable + global. Learned helplessness / optimism / positive psychology all = Seligman.

Rotter — locus of control & the I-E Scale

Question: do people differ in whether they believe they control their own outcomes? What: from his social-learning theory (behaviour potential = expectancy × reinforcement value), Julian Rotter built the Internal-External (I-E) Scale, a forced-choice self-report. Found: people array along a dimension from internal locus (outcomes flow from my own actions/ability) to external locus (outcomes flow from luck, fate, powerful others). Internals generally show better coping, achievement, health behaviour and stress resistance. Matters: one of the most-used constructs in clinical and health psychology. Trap: locus of control = Rotter, about the source of outcomes ("who controls what happens"); contrast Bandura's self-efficacy ("can I perform this task"). Both sound like "belief about control".

The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart (Bouchard) — heritability

Question: how much of personality is genetic? What: Thomas Bouchard's Minnesota study assessed identical (MZ) and fraternal (DZ) twins, crucially including pairs reared apart, on personality inventories and other measures. Found: MZ twins reared apart were strikingly similar on the major personality traits — heritability estimates of roughly 40–60% for the big trait dimensions — and shared environment contributed surprisingly little (the non-shared environment matters more than the shared family environment). Matters: the empirical backbone for the biological/trait camp (Eysenck, McCrae & Costa) and the "Part Eleven" perspective chapter's heritability figure. Trap: lock ~40–60% heritability; and note the counter-intuitive finding that the shared family environment explains little — siblings differ largely because of non-shared experiences.

Eysenck's factor-analytic & arousal studies

Question: are there a few biologically based super-dimensions, and what causes them? What: Hans Eysenck factor-analysed large samples to extract the PEN dimensions (Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism), then tested their biological basis with arousal measures (lemon-juice salivation tests, EEG, conditioning studies). Found: introverts show higher baseline cortical arousal (a more active reticular activating system) and so avoid extra stimulation, while extraverts are chronically under-aroused and seek stimulation; neuroticism reflects an over-reactive limbic/autonomic system. Traits showed high heritability. Matters: the model of a causal, biological trait theory (says why, not just what). Trap: Eysenck = 3 dimensions (PEN), biological, arousal-based; introverts are over-aroused (hence stimulation-shy). Contrast Cattell's 16 and the descriptive Big Five. Reward/punishment sensitivity in a stem = Gray (BIS/BAS), not Eysenck.

Cattell's lexical/factor work — the 16PF

Question: how many basic source traits underlie personality? What: Raymond Cattell started from the lexical pool, reduced thousands of trait words to clusters, and applied factor analysis to three data sources — L-data (life records), Q-data (questionnaires) and T-data (objective tests). Found: he distilled personality to 16 source traits (the deep causal factors) beneath the visible surface traits, operationalised as the 16PF questionnaire. He also separated fluid from crystallised intelligence. Matters: the prototypical factor-analytic trait programme and a direct ancestor of the Big Five (re-analyses of his variables kept yielding five higher-order factors). Trap: Cattell = 16 source traits, 16PF, factor analysis; surface trait (visible cluster) vs source trait (underlying cause).

McCrae & Costa — cross-cultural stability of the Five-Factor Model

Question: are the Big Five universal and stable? What: Paul Costa & Robert McCrae administered the NEO-PI-R across many languages and cultures and re-tested adults longitudinally. Found: the same five factors (OCEAN) replicated broadly across cultures, and individual trait levels are largely stable after about age 30 (with small normative shifts — neuroticism and openness tend to decline, agreeableness and conscientiousness to rise, with age, the "maturity principle"). Matters: evidence that the FFM taps something cross-culturally real and trait-like, feeding the Five-Factor Theory (heritable basic tendencies → culturally shaped characteristic adaptations). Trap: FFM = 5 (OCEAN), NEO-PI-R, descriptive, stable after ~30, cross-cultural. The Big Five describes what traits exist; Eysenck's PEN explains why.

Murray's TAT & McClelland's need for achievement

Question: can hidden motives ("needs") be read from imaginative fantasy? What: Henry Murray (with Christiana Morgan) built the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) — ambiguous picture cards the person tells stories about — as a window onto his system of needs (achievement, affiliation, power, etc.) meeting environmental press (his theory: personology). David McClelland then scored TAT stories for need for achievement (nAch) and linked high nAch to entrepreneurial striving and even economic growth, plus need for power and affiliation. Found: projective fantasy reliably elicits implicit motives that self-report misses. Matters: the projective-test lineage and the motive-measurement tradition. Trap: TAT = Murray (& Morgan); need for achievement run hard by McClelland. TAT is projective (apperception of pictures); don't confuse with the Rorschach (inkblots).

Allport & Odbert (1936) — the lexical study

Question: how many trait words does the language hold? What: Gordon Allport & Henry Odbert combed an unabridged English dictionary and extracted about 18,000 person-describing words, sorting them into stable traits, temporary states, evaluative terms, etc. Found: the sheer size of the lexicon, taken as support for the lexical hypothesis — if an individual difference matters to human life, language will have coined a word for it. Matters: the starting point for the whole trait/lexical tradition that Cattell and then the Big Five mined. Trap: the ~18,000 figure and the lexical hypothesis = Allport & Odbert; but note Allport himself was idiographic and did not use factor analysis (Cattell did the reduction).

Harlow's monkeys — contact comfort & the roots of attachment

Question: do infants bond for food or for comfort? What: Harry Harlow raised infant rhesus monkeys with two surrogate "mothers": a bare wire mother that dispensed milk and a soft cloth mother that gave none. Found: the monkeys clung to the cloth mother for comfort and ran to her when frightened, going to the wire mother only to feed, decisively contradicting the drive-reduction/"cupboard-love" view that attachment is learned through feeding. Total isolation produced lasting social and emotional damage. Matters: "contact comfort" showed love/attachment is a primary need, not a by-product of feeding, undercutting both behaviourist and orthodox Freudian (oral-stage) accounts and supporting object-relations/attachment theory. Trap: the monkeys preferred the cloth (comfort) mother over the wire (feeding) mother — comfort, not food, drives bonding. (Maslow began his career studying monkeys with Harlow.)

Bowlby & Ainsworth — attachment and the Strange Situation

Question: what is the bond between infant and caregiver, and how is it measured? What: John Bowlby proposed attachment as an innate, evolved system: the infant seeks a secure base, builds an internal working model ("are others reliable? am I worthy?"), and is harmed by maternal deprivation. Mary Ainsworth operationalised it with the Strange Situation, a structured lab sequence of separations and reunions. Found: infants sort into secure (uses mother as a base, distressed at separation, comforted at reunion), insecure-avoidant (indifferent, avoids reunion), and insecure-resistant/ambivalent (clingy yet angry, hard to soothe); Main & Solomon later added disorganised. Matters: the bridge from object relations to empirical developmental psychology; attachment styles predict later relationships (the adult four-category model). Trap: Bowlby = the theory (secure base, internal working model, maternal deprivation); Ainsworth = the Strange Situation and the three styles; Main = disorganised. Don't credit the Strange Situation to Bowlby.

Maslow — the full hierarchy, B-values & the self-actualiser

Hierarchy (bottom → top): physiological → safety → belongingness/love → esteem → self-actualisation; later additions split the upper region into cognitive (know/understand) and aesthetic needs below self-actualisation, and self-transcendence above it. Lower four = deficiency (D-)needs; the top = being (B-)needs / growth needs.

Characteristics of self-actualisers: accurate perception of reality; acceptance of self/others/nature; spontaneity & naturalness; problem-centred (not self-centred); need for privacy & autonomy; freshness of appreciation; peak experiences; Gemeinschaftsgefühl (social interest); deep but few interpersonal relationships; democratic character; ethical discrimination of means/ends; philosophical humour; creativeness; resistance to enculturation. B-values (the "meta-needs" pursued at the top): truth, goodness, beauty, unity/wholeness, aliveness, uniqueness, perfection, justice, order, simplicity, richness, effortlessness, playfulness, self-sufficiency, meaningfulness. The Jonah complex = fear of one's own greatness; metapathology = the malaise from frustrated B-values; measured by the POI (Shostrom).

Rogers — the fully functioning person

Core machinery: the actualising tendency (and the organism's organismic valuing process); the self / self-concept; real self vs ideal self → congruence vs incongruence; conditions of worth imposed by conditional positive regard; the cure = unconditional positive regard (UPR); the therapist's three core conditions = empathy, congruence/genuineness, UPR.

The fully functioning person (Rogers' ideal of growth) shows: (1) openness to experience (no defensiveness), (2) existential living (fully in the present moment), (3) organismic trusting (trusting one's own judgement), (4) experiential freedom (sense of free choice and responsibility), and (5) creativity.

Kelly — the fundamental postulate & eleven corollaries

Fundamental postulate: "a person's processes are psychologically channelised by the ways in which they anticipate events." The eleven corollaries:

Self-test (active recall, by chapter)

Recall beats re-reading. Cover the answers, ask each question aloud, then check. If you miss one, jump back to its chapter. The answer follows in green.

Freud

  1. Name the three structures and their governing principles. Id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle), superego (morality, formed in the phallic stage).
  2. Which defence is the only conscious/mature one, and how does it differ from its twin? Suppression (deliberate set-aside, mature) vs repression (unconscious, neurotic).
  3. Whose patient was Anna O., and what did she illustrate? Breuer's; the "talking cure"/catharsis (Freud reported it with him).
  4. Order the psychosexual stages. Oral → anal → phallic → latency → genital.

Jung, Adler, Horney

  1. Collective unconscious belongs to whom, and what does it contain? Jung; archetypes (persona, shadow, anima/animus, self).
  2. Match the three "wills": pleasure, power, meaning. Freud (pleasure), Adler (power), Frankl (meaning).
  3. What is Adler's mark of mental health, and his term for actively shaping one's own personality? Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl); the creative power of the self.
  4. Horney's three neurotic trends? Moving toward / against / away from people.
  5. Inferiority complex vs basic anxiety, whose is each? Inferiority complex = Adler; basic anxiety = Horney.

Erikson

  1. How do Erikson's stages differ from Freud's in number and type? Eight (lifelong) and psychosocial, vs Freud's five (to puberty) and psychosexual.
  2. Give the adolescent crisis and its virtue. Identity vs role confusion → fidelity.
  3. Marcia's four identity statuses, and the tricky pair? Diffusion, foreclosure, moratorium, achievement; foreclosure (commit, no exploration) vs moratorium (explore, no commitment yet).

Allport & the trait approach

  1. Allport's three trait levels, and did he use factor analysis? Cardinal / central / secondary; no, he was idiographic.
  2. Match the trait numbers: Cattell, Eysenck, FFM, HEXACO. 16 (16PF), 3 (PEN), 5 (OCEAN), 6 (adds Honesty-Humility).
  3. Are introverts over- or under-aroused in Eysenck's theory? Over-aroused (high baseline cortical arousal), so they avoid extra stimulation.
  4. What does functional autonomy mean? A motive becomes independent of its childhood origin (Allport).

Maslow & Rogers

  1. D-needs vs B-needs? Deficiency needs (lower four, felt as lack) vs being/growth needs (self-actualisation, felt as pull).
  2. Rogers' three therapeutic core conditions? Empathy, congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard.
  3. What does the Q-sort measure and how? Self-ideal congruence, via the correlation between real-self and ideal-self card sorts (Rogers; Stephenson's tool).
  4. Which test operationalised self-actualisation? The Personal Orientation Inventory, POI (Shostrom).

Kelly, Skinner, Bandura

  1. Kelly's image of the person and his philosophy? Man-the-scientist; constructive alternativism.
  2. Which reinforcement schedule is most extinction-resistant? Variable-ratio (the slot machine).
  3. In the Bobo doll studies, what did the model's punishment affect? Performance, not learning (children still learned the acts).
  4. The four sources of self-efficacy in order of strength? Mastery (strongest) → vicarious → verbal persuasion → physiological/emotional state.
  5. Self-efficacy vs locus of control? Self-efficacy = Bandura ("can I do this task?"); locus of control = Rotter ("who controls outcomes?").

Facets & perspective

  1. Match the facet owners: locus of control, sensation seeking, learned helplessness, flow. Rotter, Zuckerman, Seligman, Csikszentmihalyi.
  2. Seligman's pessimistic explanatory style for bad events? Internal, stable, global.
  3. What settles the person–situation debate, and roughly how heritable are traits? Interactionism; ~40–60% (Bouchard's Minnesota twins).

Supplements (object relations, existential, type, Indian)

  1. Match: good-enough mother, separation-individuation, mirroring/self, borderline organisation/TFP, positions. Winnicott, Mahler, Kohut, Kernberg, Klein.
  2. Bowlby vs Ainsworth, who gave the theory and who the Strange Situation? Bowlby = theory (secure base, internal working model); Ainsworth = Strange Situation + the three styles (Main added disorganised).
  3. What did Harlow's monkeys prefer, and what does it prove? The cloth (comfort) over the wire (feeding) mother; attachment is about contact comfort, not food.
  4. Frankl, May, Yalom, own each by a phrase. Logotherapy/will to meaning; the daimonic/meaning of anxiety; four ultimate concerns (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness).
  5. Triguna's three gunas and the test that measures them? Sattva (balance), Rajas (activity), Tamas (inertia); the Vedic Personality Inventory (VPI).
  6. Sheldon vs Kretschmer? Sheldon = somatotypes (endo/meso/ecto); Kretschmer = body build linked to psychosis type.
