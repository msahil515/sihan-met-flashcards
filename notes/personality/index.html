0. First, what is a "personality theory" trying to do?

Personality is the characteristic, relatively stable pattern of how a particular person thinks, feels and behaves across situations and over time. It is what makes you recognisably you next week, and what lets us predict that your shy friend will probably hang back at a party again.

Every theory in this chapter is one answer to four questions: What is personality made of? Where does it come from? Why do people differ? And how does it change (or not)? The theories disagree violently, and that disagreement is the whole point. They cluster into a few big "camps":

  • Psychodynamic — personality is driven by unconscious conflict laid down in early childhood. (Freud and his successors.)
  • Trait / type — skip the hidden causes; just describe and measure the stable dispositions a person has. (Allport, Cattell, Eysenck, the Big Five.)
  • Humanistic — people are basically good and are pulled forward by a drive to grow and self-actualise. (Rogers, Maslow.)
  • Existential — we are defined by how we face meaning, freedom, isolation and death.
  • Behavioural / social-cognitive — personality is learned: it is the sum of your conditioning, your expectations and your sense of what you can control. (Skinner, Bandura, Rotter, Mischel.)
  • Indian / indigenous — personality as a balance of innate qualities (the three gunas).
The intuition that ties the chapter together: read each theory by asking "is this person looking backward (childhood causes), inward (stable structure), or forward (growth and choice)?" Freud looks back. Trait theorists look in. Humanists and existentialists look forward. That single axis already organises 80% of the names.

Two recurring distinctions worth fixing before we start, because the paper leans on them:

  • Nomothetic vs idiographic. Nomothetic = study general laws across many people (trait research). Idiographic = study the unique individual in depth (case studies, Allport's later work). Most trait psychology is nomothetic; psychodynamic and humanistic work is more idiographic.
  • Type vs trait. A type is a discrete category you either are or aren't (you ARE an introvert). A trait is a continuous dimension you have more or less of (you score 7/10 on introversion). Modern psychology overwhelmingly favours traits; types survive mostly in older, body-based theories.

1. Freud & the psychodynamic core

The problem Freud was solving. As a neurologist in 1890s Vienna he kept seeing patients with real symptoms (paralysis, blindness, fits) that had no physical cause. He concluded the cause was mental and hidden: a painful idea pushed out of awareness was leaking out as a bodily symptom. That single move, that the mind has a powerful unconscious that drives behaviour we can't explain, is the seed of everything below.

The topographic model: the iceberg

Freud's first map of the mind had three layers:

  • Conscious — what you are aware of right now (the tip of the iceberg).
  • Preconscious — not in awareness but easily retrievable (your phone number until you need it).
  • Unconscious — repressed material you cannot access at will, yet which steers your feelings, slips of the tongue, and dreams. The huge mass below the waterline.

Driving the whole system are two instinctual forces: Eros, the life instinct, whose energy is libido; and Thanatos, the death/aggression instinct. Behaviour is the compromise these drives strike with reality.

The structural model: id, ego, superego

Later Freud added a second, more famous map of three agencies that are constantly negotiating:

  • Id — present at birth, totally unconscious. Runs on the pleasure principle: it wants gratification now and doesn't care about consequences or logic (that illogical, timeless style is primary process thinking). The id is the toddler screaming for the sweet.
  • Ego — develops to deal with the real world. Runs on the reality principle: it finds realistic ways to satisfy the id, delaying gratification when needed (secondary process thinking, i.e. rational planning). The ego is the negotiator in the middle, and it is the ego that deploys the defence mechanisms. Mostly conscious/preconscious.
  • Superego — the internalised moral code, formed around age 5 once the Oedipus complex resolves. Two parts: the conscience (punishes wrongdoing with guilt) and the ego-ideal (rewards living up to standards with pride). The superego is the strict parent's voice in your head.
Picture it as a rider on a horse. The id is the horse's raw power and appetite; the superego is the rulebook of where it's allowed to go; the ego is the rider trying to get somewhere real without being thrown off or paralysed. Anxiety is the ego's alarm signal that it is losing the negotiation. Defence mechanisms are how the ego buys relief from that anxiety.

Psychosexual stages: how personality gets built in childhood

Freud argued libido settles on a different body zone at each age. How a child resolves the conflict at each stage shapes adult personality. Getting stuck, through over- or under-gratification, is called fixation, and it leaves a lasting mark.

StageAgeFocus / conflictFixation looks like in an adult
Oral0–1Mouth: sucking, biting; weaning is the conflictSmoking, overeating, nail-biting, dependency, sarcasm ("biting" remarks)
Anal1–3Bowel control; toilet training is the power struggleAnal-retentive (over-controlled: orderly, stingy, stubborn) vs anal-expulsive (under-controlled: messy, reckless)
Phallic3–6Oedipus / Electra complex; castration anxiety / penis envy; the superego forms hereVanity, recklessness, difficulties with sexual identity and authority

The Oedipus complex (phallic stage) is the centrepiece: the boy unconsciously desires his mother and sees his father as a rival, then fears punishment (castration anxiety), and resolves the conflict by identifying with the father, which is how he absorbs the father's morality (the superego). The female parallel Freud called the Electra complex, involving penis envy; this part of the theory is the most criticised and was rejected outright by Horney (see §2).

Memory hook for stage order: Old (oral) Age Pensioners (phallic) Love (latency) Guinness (genital). And: the superego forms in the phallic stage, not at birth.

Defence mechanisms: how the ego copes

When anxiety threatens to overwhelm the ego, it distorts reality unconsciously to feel safer. These are the defence mechanisms. Anna Freud (Freud's daughter) systematised and catalogued them. Learn them by the everyday example, not the definition.

TRAP — the three "redirect" defences they love to swap: Displacement = redirect to a substitute target (yell at the dog). Sublimation = redirect into a socially acceptable activity (aggression → surgery). Projection = put your impulse onto another person (you accuse them of what you feel). Read the stem for "target" vs "activity" vs "another person".
NIMHANS extra — Vaillant's hierarchy of defences. George Vaillant ranked defences by maturity, and NIMHANS asks which tier a defence sits in:
  • Psychotic (most primitive): denial of external reality, delusional projection, distortion.
  • Immature: projection, acting out, passive aggression, fantasy, splitting, somatisation.
  • Neurotic: repression, displacement, reaction formation, intellectualisation, rationalisation, undoing.
  • Mature: sublimation, humour, altruism, suppression (the only conscious one), anticipation.
Note the trap: repression is unconscious and neurotic; suppression is the deliberate, conscious choice to set a worry aside, and is mature.

Carl Jung's break (analytical psychology)

Jung was Freud's heir-apparent until they split over Freud's insistence that everything traces to sex. Jung kept the unconscious but enriched it. His big additions:

  • Personal unconscious — your own repressed/forgotten material (like Freud's), organised into emotionally charged complexes.
  • Collective unconscious — a deeper, inherited layer shared by all humans, containing universal patterns called archetypes: the persona (the mask you show the world), the shadow (your dark, denied side), the anima/animus (the inner feminine/masculine), and the self (the unifying centre). This is Jung's single most testable idea.
  • Introversion vs extraversion — Jung coined this attitude pair (energy turned inward vs outward), later borrowed by trait theory and the MBTI.
  • Individuation — the lifelong process of integrating all these parts into a whole self.
Recap §1. Freud: unconscious conflict → id/ego/superego → psychosexual stages → defences. Jung: added the collective unconscious and archetypes, coined introversion/extraversion. Anna Freud catalogued the defences.

2. The Neo-Freudians & ego psychology

The neo-Freudians accepted that an unconscious shapes us, but rejected Freud's heavy focus on sex and biology. Their shared move: personality is driven more by social and cultural relationships than by libido. Learn each one by the single phrase nobody else owns.

Alfred Adler — individual psychology

Adler said the central human motive is not pleasure but overcoming feelings of inferiority. Every child starts small, weak and dependent, and so feels inferior; healthy development means compensating for this by striving for superiority (really, striving for competence and mastery). When it goes wrong you get an inferiority complex (paralysing self-doubt) or an over-compensating superiority complex. Adler also stressed birth order (first-born, middle, youngest each develop differently), the unique style of life each person builds, and social interest (gemeinschaftsgefühl), the feeling of connection to humanity that marks mental health.

Carl Jung

(Covered in §1 because his theory is large; for the neo-Freudian lineup just hold collective unconscious + archetypes.)

Karen Horney

Horney directly challenged Freud's view of women. Where Freud said women suffer penis envy, Horney countered that men may feel "womb envy" (envy of women's ability to bear life), and that what looks like penis envy is really envy of men's social power. Her core concept is basic anxiety: the feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world, rooted in childhood. To cope, people adopt one of three neurotic trends: moving toward people (compliance, needing approval), moving against people (aggression, needing to dominate), or moving away from people (detachment, needing independence). She also described the "tyranny of the should", the tyranny of an idealised self we punish ourselves for not matching.

Erich Fromm

Fromm fused psychoanalysis with social philosophy. His famous idea is "escape from freedom": modern freedom is so isolating and burdensome that people flee from it into conformity, submission to authority, or destructiveness (his explanation for the rise of fascism). He described character orientations (receptive, exploitative, hoarding, marketing, and the healthy productive orientation).

Harry Stack Sullivan

Sullivan's interpersonal theory says personality only exists in relationships, never in isolation. The self-system is built out of anxiety-reducing interactions with others, organised into good-me (approved experiences), bad-me (disapproved, anxiety-laden), and not-me (so threatening it's dissociated).

Erik Erikson — ego psychology

Erikson extended Freud in two ways: he made development lifelong (8 stages, not 5, running birth to death) and psychosocial rather than psychosexual (each stage is a social crisis, e.g. identity vs role confusion in adolescence). He coined the term "identity crisis." The full stage table lives under Development; for personality just hold that he is the bridge from drives to the social ego.

Anna Freud & Heinz Hartmann

Anna Freud founded child psychoanalysis and catalogued the defence mechanisms. Heinz Hartmann is the "father of ego psychology", known for the "conflict-free ego sphere" (parts of the ego, like perception and memory, that develop independently of conflict) and the idea of adaptation.

TRAP — rotate these as distractors constantly: Collective unconscious = Jung. Inferiority complex / birth order = Adler. Basic anxiety / womb envy / three neurotic trends = Horney. Escape from freedom = Fromm. Interpersonal / self-system / good-me-bad-me = Sullivan. Conflict-free ego sphere = Hartmann.
Freud vs Adler vs Jung in one line each: Freud → sex/libido. Adler → power/overcoming inferiority. Jung → meaning/the collective unconscious. This trio plus their "wills" (will to pleasure / will to power / will to meaning, see §7) is the single most-tested cluster in personality.

3. Object relations & self psychology

NIMHANS-heavy. MET barely touched this; NIMHANS (clinical) asks these by name constantly. Slow down here.

The shift in one sentence: Freud thought the baby seeks pleasure; the object-relations school says the baby seeks relationship. An "object" is the psychoanalytic word for the internal mental image of a person, almost always the mother first. Personality, in this view, is built out of the internalised templates of our earliest relationships, which we then re-enact for life. This is also where borderline personality gets explained, which is why clinical exams love it.

Melanie Klein

The founder of object relations and of child play therapy. She argued the infant doesn't experience whole people at first, only part-objects (the "good breast" that feeds, the "bad breast" that's absent). The infant moves through two positions (not stages, because we can slip back into them all our lives):

  • Paranoid-schizoid position (~first 6 months): the world is split into all-good and all-bad; the infant uses projective identification (projecting feelings into the other and then treating them as if they hold those feelings).
  • Depressive position (later): the infant realises the good and bad mother are the same person, integrates them, and feels guilt and a wish to make reparation. Reaching this position is a developmental achievement.

Donald Winnicott

A paediatrician, so his ideas are warm and practical. The "good-enough mother": a baby doesn't need a perfect mother, just one who is reliably good enough, meeting needs well enough that the child can tolerate small failures and grow. The transitional object (the blanket or teddy) sits in the transitional space between "me" and "not-me", helping the child separate from the mother. And the true self vs false self: when a child must constantly comply with others' demands, it builds a compliant false self as a shell over the spontaneous true self.

W.R.D. Fairbairn

Took the school to its logical end: libido is fundamentally object-seeking, not pleasure-seeking. We want connection first; pleasure is just a route to it. This is the cleanest statement of the whole object-relations break from Freud.

Margaret Mahler

Mapped how the infant psychologically separates from the mother, called separation-individuation: normal autism → symbiosis (fused with mother) → differentiation → practising (exploring) → rapprochement (darting back to mother for reassurance) → object constancy (holding a stable inner image of mother even when she's absent).

Otto Kernberg

Integrated object relations with Freud's drive theory and built the modern understanding of borderline personality organisation, where splitting persists into adulthood. He developed Transference-Focused Psychotherapy (TFP) to treat it.

Heinz Kohut — self psychology

Kohut focused on narcissism and the development of a cohesive self. The child needs selfobjects, people who perform self-functions for it, above all empathic mirroring ("I see you, you're wonderful"). When mirroring fails, the grandiose self doesn't mature and you get pathological narcissism. Key terms: selfobject, mirroring, idealising vs grandiose self.

John Bowlby — attachment theory

Bowlby argued the infant has an innate, evolved need to attach to a caregiver as a secure base. From repeated interactions the child builds an internal working model, a template of "are others reliable, am I worthy of care?" that shapes relationships for life. Maternal deprivation damages it. Mary Ainsworth later operationalised attachment with the Strange Situation experiment (secure / anxious-ambivalent / avoidant; Main added disorganised).

TRAP — the pairings the paper swaps: Good-enough mother / transitional object / true-false self = Winnicott. Separation-individuation / rapprochement = Mahler. Self / selfobject / narcissism / mirroring = Kohut. Borderline organisation / TFP = Kernberg. Paranoid-schizoid & depressive POSITIONS = Klein (positions, not stages). Object-seeking libido = Fairbairn. Internal working model / secure base = Bowlby.
Recap §3. One internal-relationship template, six owners. Klein = positions + splitting. Winnicott = good-enough mother. Mahler = separation-individuation. Kernberg = borderline. Kohut = narcissism/self. Bowlby = attachment.

4. Trait theories

The big change of approach. Trait theorists got impatient with unprovable unconscious forces and asked a simpler question: what stable characteristics do people actually have, and can we measure them? A trait is a stable disposition to behave a certain way across situations (a "shy" person is shy at most parties). The method that powers most of this work is factor analysis: a statistical technique that takes thousands of trait words or test answers and finds the small number of underlying dimensions ("factors") that explain the correlations among them.

Gordon Allport

The pioneer. He counted ~18,000 trait words in the dictionary (the lexical hypothesis: if a trait matters, language will have a word for it) and organised individual traits into three levels:

  • Cardinal traits — so dominant they define a person's whole life (rare; e.g. someone so generous we call them "a Mother Teresa").
  • Central traits — the 5–10 core descriptors you'd use in a reference letter (honest, anxious, sociable).
  • Secondary traits — situational preferences that show up only sometimes (gets impatient in queues).

Two extra Allport terms: functional autonomy (a motive can become independent of its origin: you started running to lose weight but now run because you love it) and the proprium (his word for the self). Crucially, Allport favoured the idiographic study of the individual and did NOT use factor analysis, which is a common trap.

Raymond Cattell

The factor-analysis champion. He distinguished surface traits (clusters of behaviour you can see) from source traits (the deeper factors that cause them), and reduced personality to 16 source traits, which he turned into the 16PF questionnaire. (Fluid vs crystallised intelligence is also his.)

Hans Eysenck

Argued for a few biologically-based super-dimensions. His PEN model has three: Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism. He started with just E and N (the EPI), then added P (the EPQ). His biological theory is testable and elegant: extraverts have chronically low cortical arousal (their reticular activating system under-stimulates them), so they seek excitement to top it up; neuroticism reflects an over-reactive limbic/autonomic system that's easily upset.

Jeffrey Gray — Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory

Gray reframed Eysenck biologically. Personality, he said, comes from two brain systems: the BIS (Behavioural Inhibition System), sensitive to punishment and non-reward, which produces anxiety and makes you stop and check; and the BAS (Behavioural Activation System), sensitive to reward, which produces impulsivity and approach. (Plus a fight-flight-freeze system.) If a stem mentions sensitivity to reward vs punishment, it's Gray, not Eysenck.

TRAP — the numbers give it away: Allport = 3 trait levels (cardinal/central/secondary), idiographic, NO factor analysis. Cattell = 16 factors (16PF), via factor analysis. Eysenck = 3 dimensions (PEN). Watch the swap: Allport's "3" is levels, Eysenck's "3" is dimensions, and Cattell is the factor-analysis man, not Allport.
NIMHANS extra: BIS/BAS = Gray. Reward/punishment sensitivity, or a biological basis for anxiety vs impulsivity, points to Gray's RST, not Eysenck's arousal model.

5. The Big Five (OCEAN) & HEXACO

Why it won. For decades trait theorists argued over how many factors there are (Cattell's 16, Eysenck's 3). When researchers re-analysed the data again and again, five factors kept reappearing across languages and cultures. That convergence is why the Five-Factor Model (championed by Costa & McCrae, measured by the NEO-PI-R) is today's dominant model. It grew out of the lexical hypothesis (Galton → Allport & Odbert → Goldberg, who coined "Big Five"). Each factor is a continuum, not a box.

Mnemonic: OCEAN (or CANOE). Neuroticism's healthy pole is sometimes called emotional stability; same axis, flipped.
NIMHANS extra — HEXACO. A 6-factor model (Ashton & Lee) that adds H = Honesty-Humility (sincerity, fairness, modesty, greed-avoidance) to a reworked Big Five. The "dark triad" (narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy) maps onto low Honesty-Humility. Hold: HEXACO = 6 factors, H is the new one.
TRAP: The Big Five is descriptive and atheoretical (it says what traits exist, not why). Eysenck's PEN is causal/biological (it says why). Both contain Extraversion and Neuroticism, which is exactly why the paper pairs them to trip you.

6. Humanistic theories (the "third force")

Humanism arose in the 1950s–60s as a revolt against the two dominant "forces": psychoanalysis (which made us slaves to the unconscious) and behaviourism (which made us puppets of conditioning). The humanists insisted people have free will, are basically good, and are pulled forward by an innate drive toward growth and self-actualisation. Hence "third force."

Carl Rogers — person-centred theory

Rogers built everything around the self-concept: your picture of who you are. He contrasted the real self (who you actually are) with the ideal self (who you wish to be). When they roughly match, you have congruence and feel well-adjusted; a big gap is incongruence and breeds distress. Why does incongruence develop? Because others give us love conditionally, imposing conditions of worth ("I'll approve of you if you become a doctor"), so we betray our own organismic valuing process (our gut sense of what's right for us). The antidote is unconditional positive regard (UPR): being valued no matter what. The goal of growth is the fully functioning person. In therapy this becomes the three core conditions: empathy, congruence/genuineness, and UPR.

Abraham Maslow — self-actualisation

Maslow proposed that human needs stack in a hierarchy, and lower needs must be reasonably met before higher ones motivate us: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualisation (realising your full potential). The bottom four are deficiency needs (D-needs), felt as a lack; the top is a being need (B-need), felt as a pull toward growth. Late in life he added self-transcendence above self-actualisation. He studied unusually healthy, accomplished people and described their peak experiences (moments of intense joy and wholeness).

TRAP: Both are humanistic, so the paper pairs them. Rogers = self-concept, real/ideal self, congruence, UPR. Maslow = the hierarchy of needs, self-actualisation, peak experiences.

7. Existential theories

NIMHANS extra. Existentialism overlaps with humanism (both value freedom and meaning) but its mood is darker: it asks how we cope with the unavoidable "givens" of existence, death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. Asked by name at NIMHANS.

Viktor Frankl — logotherapy

A psychiatrist who survived Nazi concentration camps and concluded that the people who endured were those who held onto a meaning to live for. His logotherapy ("logos" = meaning) says the primary human motive is the "will to meaning". When that is frustrated you fall into an existential vacuum (emptiness, boredom). His book is Man's Search for Meaning; his techniques include paradoxical intention and dereflection.

Lock the three "wills": will to pleasure = Freud · will to power = Adler · will to meaning = Frankl. This is a guaranteed single-best-answer item.

Rollo May

Brought existential thought into American psychology. Known for The Meaning of Anxiety, the daimonic (any natural urge that can take over the whole person), and the tension between freedom and destiny.

Irvin Yalom

The major figure in existential psychotherapy. He named four "ultimate concerns" every person must face: death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness.

Ludwig Binswanger

Daseinsanalysis ("being-in-the-world"): applied Heidegger's philosophy to psychiatry.

8. Social-cognitive & learning approaches

This camp says personality is learned, but, unlike strict behaviourism, it puts thought back in the middle. We are shaped by conditioning plus our beliefs, expectations and sense of control. Pure behaviourism (Skinner) sits at one end; cognition-rich theories (Bandura, Rotter, Mischel, Kelly) build outward from there.

Albert Bandura

Two flagship ideas. Reciprocal determinism: behaviour, personal/cognitive factors, and the environment all influence each other in a three-way loop, rather than environment simply controlling you. And self-efficacy: your belief in your own ability to succeed at a specific task, which strongly predicts whether you'll even attempt it. His Bobo doll studies demonstrated observational learning (we learn by watching models, no reinforcement needed).

Julian Rotter

Best known for locus of control: do you believe outcomes flow from your own actions (internal locus) or from luck, fate and powerful others (external locus)? He framed behaviour as expectancy × reinforcement value (how likely you think a reward is, times how much you want it).

Walter Mischel

Lit the fuse of the person–situation debate: he showed behaviour is far less consistent across situations than trait theory assumes (you might be honest with friends but not on taxes). His resolution is the CAPS model (Cognitive-Affective Processing System): stable if-then signatures ("if criticised, then she withdraws"). He also ran the famous marshmallow delay-of-gratification studies.

George Kelly

Personal construct theory: each person is like a "scientist" who builds and tests personal constructs (bipolar dimensions like "kind–cruel") to predict their world. He measured them with the Rep Grid (Role Construct Repertory Test).

Henry Murray — personology

Murray coined "personology" and built a system of needs and press: internal needs (need for achievement, affiliation, power, etc.) interacting with environmental press (the pressures a situation applies). He built the TAT (Thematic Apperception Test) with Christiana Morgan to measure these needs.

Dollard & Miller

Translated Freud into learning theory, recasting psychodynamics as drive–cue–response–reinforcement. They are famous for the approach-avoidance conflict (being drawn to and repelled by the same goal) and the frustration-aggression hypothesis.

B. F. Skinner

The radical behaviourist: there is no inner "personality" to study; what we call personality is just a learned bundle of operant response patterns maintained by reinforcement history. Don't peek inside the black box.

TRAP: Self-efficacy = Bandura ("can I do this task?") vs locus of control = Rotter ("do I or outside forces control outcomes?"). Both sound like "belief about control"; the difference is task-ability vs outcome-source.
NIMHANS extra — the lesser names that appear as distractors: Needs & press + TAT = Murray. Personal constructs + Rep Grid = Kelly. Approach-avoidance conflict = Dollard & Miller.

9. Type theories (the older, body-based ones)

Before traits, people sorted personality into discrete types, often tied to the body. These are largely discredited but still examined because of who proposed them.

TheoristTypology
Hippocrates / GalenFour bodily humours → temperaments: sanguine (blood, cheerful), choleric (yellow bile, irritable), melancholic (black bile, sad), phlegmatic (phlegm, calm).
William SheldonSomatotypes: endomorph (round → viscerotonic: sociable, relaxed), mesomorph (muscular → somatotonic: assertive, energetic), ectomorph (thin → cerebrotonic: restrained, introverted).
Ernst KretschmerBody build → psychiatric tendency: pyknic (round → manic-depressive/cyclothymic), asthenic/leptosomic (thin → schizophrenic/schizothymic), athletic, dysplastic.
Friedman & RosenmanType A (competitive, hostile, time-urgent → heart disease) vs Type B (relaxed). Later Type C (cancer-prone, emotion-suppressing) and Type D (distressed: negative affect + social inhibition).
Jung → Myers & BriggsJung's introversion/extraversion + functions (thinking/feeling, sensing/intuiting) became the basis the MBTI was later built on.
TRAP — type vs trait: Types = discrete categories (you ARE an endomorph). Traits = continuous dimensions (you score 7/10 on extraversion). And keep the two body theorists apart: Sheldon = somatotypes (endo/meso/ecto); Kretschmer = body build linked to psychosis type.

10. Indian psychology — Triguna & allied concepts

NIMHANS-specific. Being India-rooted, NIMHANS periodically asks indigenous personality concepts that MET never touched. The Triguna model is the must-know.

Indian thought locates personality in the balance of three fundamental qualities (gunas) of nature (prakriti). Everyone contains all three; the dominant one defines temperament, and the balance can shift.

ConceptWhat it is
Triguna (Samkhya philosophy / Bhagavad Gita)Sattva (purity, clarity, balance, wisdom, calm), Rajas (activity, passion, restlessness, ambition, drive), Tamas (inertia, dullness, ignorance, lethargy). Measured by the Vedic Personality Inventory (VPI).
Ayurvedic TridoshaMind-body constitution: Vata (air/movement), Pitta (fire/metabolism), Kapha (earth-water/structure). A body-typology parallel to Sheldon.
PanchakoshaFive "sheaths" of the self (Taittiriya Upanishad): annamaya (food/body) → pranamaya (vital energy) → manomaya (mind) → vijnanamaya (intellect) → anandamaya (bliss). A layered model of the person.
Lock this line: Sattva = goodness/balance, Rajas = activity/passion, Tamas = inertia/darkness. Three "gunas" in a stem = Triguna, measured by the VPI.

11. How personality is measured

Two philosophies of testing, with a built-in trade-off. Projective tests show an ambiguous stimulus and assume you'll unconsciously project hidden material onto it; they're rich but score poorly on reliability and validity. Objective (self-report) tests use fixed items scored against norms; they're far more reliable but vulnerable to faking and social desirability (people answer how they'd like to look).

ProjectiveRorschach (inkblots, Exner scoring), TAT (Murray, ambiguous-picture stories), CAT (children), Sentence Completion (RISB), Draw-A-Person, Rosenzweig P-F, Word Association (Jung)Ambiguous stimulus → person projects unconscious content. Rich, hard to fake, but low reliability/validity.
Objective / self-reportMMPI-2 (clinical; validity scales L, F, K), 16PF (Cattell), NEO-PI-R (Big Five), EPQ (Eysenck), CPI, MCMI (Millon)Standardised, norm-referenced, objectively scored. High reliability, but fakeable / socially-desirable responding.

13. The MCQ traps, collected

  • Collective unconscious = Jung, NOT Freud (Freud = personal unconscious only).
  • Inferiority complex / birth order = Adler · basic anxiety / womb envy = Horney · escape from freedom = Fromm · self-system / interpersonal = Sullivan.
  • 16 factors = Cattell · 3 dimensions (PEN) = Eysenck · cardinal/central/secondary levels = Allport (no factor analysis) · BIS/BAS = Gray · 6 factors incl. Honesty-Humility = HEXACO.
  • Self-efficacy = Bandura · locus of control = Rotter · personal constructs / Rep Grid = Kelly · needs & press / TAT = Murray · approach-avoidance = Dollard & Miller.
  • Will to meaning = Frankl · will to power = Adler · will to pleasure = Freud.
  • Good-enough mother / transitional object = Winnicott · separation-individuation = Mahler · self / narcissism = Kohut · borderline organisation = Kernberg · positions (paranoid-schizoid / depressive) = Klein · object-seeking libido = Fairbairn · internal working model = Bowlby.
  • Sublimation = most mature defence · repression = master/neurotic, unconscious · suppression = the only conscious one (Vaillant, mature).
  • Superego forms in the phallic stage (after the Oedipus complex), not at birth.
  • Real vs ideal self / congruence / UPR = Rogers · hierarchy of needs / self-actualisation = Maslow.
  • Type = category; trait = continuum. Sheldon = somatotypes; Kretschmer = body build → psychosis.
  • Big Five describes (atheoretical); Eysenck's PEN explains via biology — both share E and N.
  • Sattva / Rajas / Tamas = Triguna (Indian psychology), measured by the VPI.
  • Projective tests (Rorschach, TAT) tap the unconscious but are low-reliability; objective inventories (MMPI, 16PF, NEO) are reliable but fakeable.
