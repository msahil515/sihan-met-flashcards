1. Biopsychology as a neuroscience (Pinel ch. 1)

The one idea behind the whole book. Biopsychology is the scientific study of the biology of behaviour: it asks how the nervous system produces what we think, feel and do. It is one branch of neuroscience (the larger study of the nervous system), the branch that keeps behaviour at the centre. Pinel's recurring lesson is that mind and brain are not two things: every thought is a physical event in tissue.

The six divisions of biopsychology

Pinel splits the field by what you study and how. Learn each by its defining method, because the paper tests "which division does X?".

DivisionWhat it studiesTypical method

PsychopharmacologyEffects of drugs on behaviour and the brainManipulate behaviour with drugs; develop therapeutic drugs
NeuropsychologyPsychological effects of brain damage in human patientsCase studies + standardised tests; the most applied division (NIMHANS leans here)

Cognitive neuroscienceNeural bases of cognition (thought, memory, attention)Mainly functional brain imaging (fMRI, PET) in humans

TRAP — psychophysiology vs physiological psychology. Both have "physiolog" in the name. Physiological psychology = invasive manipulation of the brain, usually in animals. Psychophysiology = non-invasive recording from the surface of healthy humans. And neuropsychology = the study of human brain-damaged patients.

Kinds of research, kinds of inference

  • Pure vs applied. Pure = curiosity-driven knowledge; applied = solving a practical problem. Most real work is a blend.
  • Experiments vs non-experiments. Only a true experiment (the researcher manipulates an independent variable and randomly assigns) can support a causal claim. Quasi-experiments and case studies cannot fully rule out confounds.
  • Converging operations. Because every single method is flawed, biopsychology trusts a conclusion only when several different methods point the same way. This is Pinel's signature methodological value.
  • The Morgan's Canon / law of parsimony spirit: prefer the simplest explanation that fits, and do not attribute a higher mental process when a lower one will do.
Lock it: human brain-damaged patients = neuropsychology; drugs = psychopharmacology; non-invasive measures in healthy humans = psychophysiology; imaging of cognition = cognitive neuroscience; animal brain manipulation = physiological psychology; across species = comparative.
Recap §1. Biopsychology = biology of behaviour, six divisions sorted by method. Causation needs a true experiment. Converging operations: trust the conclusion many methods agree on.

2. Evolution, genetics & experience (Pinel ch. 2)

The problem this chapter fixes. The old "nature versus nurture" question is a false dichotomy. Pinel's "nature-nurture done right" is that genes and experience are not rival causes adding up to 100%; they interact continuously. A trait is the product of a genetic program read out in an environment. You cannot say a behaviour is "60% genetic" for an individual, only how much variation in a population is associated with genetic variation (heritability).

Evolution of the brain

  • Natural selection (Darwin): heritable traits that improve fitness (reproductive success) spread. Behaviour evolves too.
  • Larger brains are not automatically "better"; what matters is brain size relative to body size and the expansion of specific regions, especially the cerebral cortex in primates.
  • Convergent evolution = similar traits arise independently (analogous structures). Homologous structures share a common ancestor. Do not read similarity as shared ancestry.
  • Spandrels & exaptations: not every trait is a direct adaptation; some are by-products later co-opted for new uses.

Mendelian genetics & the molecular layer

  • Gene = a stretch of DNA on a chromosome. We have 23 pairs; the 23rd is the sex pair (XX female, XY male).
  • Dominant vs recessive alleles; homozygous vs heterozygous; genotype (the genes) vs phenotype (the expressed trait).
  • Dizygotic (fraternal) twins share ~50% of genes (like ordinary siblings); monozygotic (identical) twins share ~100%. Comparing them is the classic heritability tool.
  • Central dogma: DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation) → protein.

Epigenetics: experience changes which genes are read

Why this matters. Epigenetics is the mechanism that finally dissolves nature-vs-nurture. The DNA sequence does not change, but experience can switch genes on or off via chemical tags, especially DNA methylation (silences genes) and histone modification. These marks can persist and even be inherited. So early experience literally edits gene expression: that is how environment becomes biology.

Ontogeny vs phylogeny

TermMeaningEveryday handle
OntogenyDevelopment of the individual across its own lifespanOne person growing up
PhylogenyEvolutionary history of the species/lineageThe family tree of life
TRAP: Ontogeny = individual development; phylogeny = species evolution. The mnemonic "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" (Haeckel) is largely discredited, but examiners still test which word means which. Also: heritability is a population statistic, not a personal percentage.
Recap §2. Genes × experience interact (epigenetics is the bridge: methylation silences genes). Natural selection acts on fitness. Ontogeny = the individual; phylogeny = the species. MZ twins ~100%, DZ ~50%.

3. Anatomy of the nervous system (Pinel ch. 3)

Before any function makes sense you need the map and the cells that build it. We start with the cells, then the divisions, then the protective wrappings, the directions, and finally the brain regions.

The neuron, part by part

  • Dendrites receive input; surface area is multiplied by spines.
  • Soma (cell body) integrates input; holds nucleus, ribosomes, mitochondria.
  • Axon hillock is the trigger zone; it decides whether threshold is reached.
  • Axon conducts the action potential; may be myelinated.
  • Axon terminals (buttons) release neurotransmitter at the synapse.

Neuron types by function

  • Sensory (afferent) — periphery → CNS. Often pseudounipolar.
  • Motor (efferent) — CNS → muscles/glands. Multipolar.
  • Interneurons — local processing inside the CNS; the most numerous.

Meninges, CSF and ventricles

The CNS is protected by three membranes, the meninges, from outside in: dura mater (tough outer), arachnoid mater (web-like middle), pia mater (delicate, hugs the brain). Between arachnoid and pia is the subarachnoid space, filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

  • CSF cushions the brain, removes waste, and lets it float (reducing effective weight). It is produced by the choroid plexus in the ventricles.
  • Ventricles: two lateral → third → cerebral aqueduct → fourth → central canal / subarachnoid space. Blockage causes hydrocephalus.

Subcortical structures

  • Right: spatial, music, faces, holistic, emotional prosody.

4. Neural conduction & synaptic transmission (Pinel ch. 4)

Full primer: action potential page. Here is the teaching version.

The resting potential: why a neuron sits charged

An unstimulated neuron holds its inside about −70 mV negative to the outside. Three forces set this up: the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (pushes 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, using ATP), leaky K⁺ channels (K⁺ drifts out down its gradient), and large negative proteins trapped inside. The membrane is a charged battery waiting to discharge.

The action potential, step by step

  • Threshold ~ −55 mV must be reached at the axon hillock.
  • Depolarisation: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ rushes in → spike to ~ +30 mV.
  • Repolarisation: Na⁺ channels inactivate; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open → K⁺ flows out.
  • Hyperpolarisation (undershoot): K⁺ channels close slowly → dips below −70 mV briefly.
All-or-none law: once threshold is reached the AP fires at constant amplitude. Stimulus intensity is coded by frequency of firing, never by spike size.

Refractory periods

  • Absolute refractory period — Na⁺ channels inactivated; no stimulus, however strong, can fire another AP. This sets the maximum firing rate and forces the AP to travel one way.
  • Relative refractory period — membrane is hyperpolarised; a stronger-than-usual stimulus can fire one.

Saltatory conduction

In myelinated axons the AP regenerates only at the Nodes of Ranvier and "jumps" between them. This is faster and far more energy-efficient than continuous conduction. Demyelination (as in MS) destroys this and slows or blocks conduction.

The synapse: step by step

  1. AP arrives at the terminal.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters.
  3. Vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane (SNARE proteins) and release NT.
  4. NT crosses the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.
  5. EPSP (depolarising) or IPSP (hyperpolarising); spatial + temporal summation at the axon hillock decides if a new AP fires.
  6. NT is cleared by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion.

Receptors come in two styles: ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels, fast, e.g. nicotinic ACh, GABA-A, AMPA/NMDA) and metabotropic (G-protein-coupled, slower, longer-lasting, e.g. muscarinic ACh, most dopamine receptors). Autoreceptors on the presynaptic terminal sense the cell's own NT and provide feedback.

5. Research methods of biopsychology (Pinel ch. 5)

Every claim about brain and behaviour rests on a method, and every method answers a different question. Pinel groups them into lesion, recording, imaging, and stimulation.

Lesion / ablation methods

  • Ablation / lesion: remove or destroy tissue and observe the behavioural deficit (the oldest approach). Aspiration (suction), radio-frequency / electrolytic lesions, knife cuts, and chemical excitotoxic lesions (kainic acid spares fibres of passage, killing only cell bodies).
  • Reversible lesions: cooling or local anaesthetic (lidocaine) temporarily knock out a region, then it recovers, a cleaner causal test.
  • Stereotaxic surgery: the stereotaxic atlas + instrument lets you reach a deep target by 3-D coordinates from the bregma landmark on the skull. This is how electrodes and cannulae are placed.

TRAP — structure vs function and resolution. CT/MRI = structure; fMRI/PET = function. EEG = great timing, poor location; fMRI = great location, poorer timing. Examiners pair these opposites. BOLD is the fMRI signal.

Stimulation methods

  • Electrical brain stimulation (ESB): drive a region and see the evoked behaviour (Penfield's cortical mapping; Olds & Milner's reward stimulation).
  • Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS): a magnetic pulse non-invasively excites or transiently disrupts a cortical area, a "virtual lesion" in healthy humans, and a treatment for depression.
  • Optogenetics (newer): light switches genetically modified neurons on/off with millisecond precision.

Psychophysiological measures (non-invasive, on the body)

  • Skin conductance (SCR/GSR) — sympathetic arousal via sweat; used in lie detection and emotion research.
  • ECG / heart rate, EMG (muscle), EOG (eye movement), pupillometry.
Lock the pairings: deep target by coordinates = stereotaxic; one neuron = single-unit; timing of cognition = ERP/EEG; non-invasive virtual lesion = TMS; receptor mapping = PET; structure = CT/MRI; function = fMRI/PET.
Recap §5. Lesion (incl. reversible) + stereotaxic placement; recording (single-unit, EEG, ERP); imaging (CT/MRI structure, fMRI/PET function); stimulation (ESB, TMS, optogenetics); psychophysiology (SCR, HR, EMG, EOG).

6. The visual system (Pinel ch. 6)

Vision is the model sensory system in Pinel because we know its wiring best. The job: turn patterns of light into a neural representation of the world.

The retina: where light becomes neural signal

  • Light passes through the retina's layers to reach the photoreceptors at the back, so the retina is "inside out".
  • Rods — peripheral, very light-sensitive, no colour, low acuity (night/scotopic vision). Cones — concentrated at the fovea, colour, high acuity (day/photopic vision).
  • Cones are densest at the fovea centralis. The optic disc (where the optic nerve leaves) has no photoreceptors = the blind spot.
  • Signal flow within the retina: photoreceptors → bipolar cells → retinal ganglion cells (whose axons form the optic nerve), with horizontal and amacrine cells doing lateral interaction. Lateral inhibition sharpens edges (Mach bands).

The retina-geniculate-striate pathway

Retina → optic nerve → optic chiasm (nasal fibres cross, so the left visual field of both eyes goes right) → optic tract → LGN of the thalamus → optic radiations → primary visual cortex (V1 / striate cortex) in the occipital lobe. The map is retinotopic (neighbouring retina → neighbouring cortex). Hubel & Wiesel found V1 cells are feature detectors (simple, complex, hypercomplex cells tuned to edges and orientation).

Two cortical streams: what vs where

StreamRouteJobLesion
Ventral ("what")V1 → temporal lobeObject and face recognitionVisual agnosia, prosopagnosia
Dorsal ("where / how")V1 → parietal lobeSpatial location, visually guided actionOptic ataxia, neglect

Theories of colour vision (these are complementary, not rivals)

  • Trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz) — three cone types (S/M/L, "blue/green/red"); colour = ratio of their activity. Operates at the receptor (retinal) level.
  • Opponent-process (Hering) — colour coded in opposing pairs: red-green, blue-yellow, black-white. Operates after the retina (ganglion cells, LGN). Explains negative afterimages and why we never see "reddish-green".
TRAP: the two colour theories are both right at different stages: trichromatic at the cones, opponent-process downstream. Negative afterimages = opponent-process evidence. Rods do not code colour. Dorsal = where/how (parietal), Ventral = what (temporal): "dorsal is up and points to where".
Recap §6. Rods (dim, no colour) vs cones (fovea, colour). Pathway: retina → chiasm → LGN → V1. Dorsal = where (parietal), ventral = what (temporal). Colour = trichromatic at cones + opponent-process after.

7. Mechanisms of perception: audition & the other senses (Pinel ch. 7)

Audition

  • Path: outer ear → middle ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) → inner ear cochlea.
  • Hair cells on the basilar membrane → CN VIII → cochlear nuclei → superior olive → inferior colliculus → MGN of thalamus → primary auditory cortex (Heschl's gyrus, temporal lobe).

How is pitch coded? Three theories

TheoryMechanismBest for
Place theory (von Békésy)Different frequencies peak at different places on the basilar membrane: high at the base, low at the apexHigh frequencies
Frequency (temporal) theoryThe whole membrane fires at the rate of the sound's frequencyLow frequencies (< ~500 Hz)
Volley principleGroups of neurons fire in rotating "volleys" to track frequencies above any single neuron's limitMid-range (bridges the two)
TRAP: Place = high frequency (base of cochlea), frequency theory = low, volley = middle. Loudness is coded by firing rate + number of hair cells, not by place.

Somatosensation: two ascending highways

Dorsal column–medial lemniscus (DCML)Anterolateral / spinothalamic
CarriesFine touch, vibration, proprioceptionPain, temperature, crude touch
DecussatesIn the medulla (high up)At the spinal level of entry (immediately)
Speed/fibresFast, large myelinatedSlower, small fibres
Why the decussation level matters clinically. Because the two systems cross at different levels, a one-sided spinal cord lesion (Brown-Séquard) loses fine touch/proprioception on the same side (DCML crosses later, up in the medulla) but pain/temperature on the opposite side (spinothalamic crossed immediately). That dissociation is a favourite exam item.

Pain & gate control (Melzack & Wall): non-painful input (rubbing) can "close a gate" in the dorsal horn and reduce pain; descending signals from the PAG (periaqueductal grey), via endorphins, also dampen pain.

Chemical senses

  • Taste (gustation): five basics, sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami. CN VII (anterior 2/3 tongue), IX (posterior 1/3), X (epiglottis). Insula = primary gustatory cortex.
  • Smell (olfaction): olfactory epithelium → olfactory bulb → piriform cortex, with no thalamic relay (the exception). Direct limbic ties explain odour-evoked emotional memory.

Attention

Perception is selective. Cocktail-party effect: you track one voice in a crowd, yet your own name still breaks through (so unattended channels are partly processed). The posterior parietal cortex and frontal eye fields shift spatial attention; damage to the right parietal causes hemispatial neglect (§19). Pinel distinguishes endogenous (goal-driven, top-down) from exogenous (stimulus-driven, bottom-up) attention.

Recap §7. Audition: ossicles → cochlea → basilar membrane → MGN → Heschl's gyrus; place=high, frequency=low, volley=mid. Touch: DCML (fine touch, crosses in medulla) vs spinothalamic (pain/temp, crosses at entry). Smell skips the thalamus. Attention: endogenous vs exogenous.

8. The sensorimotor system (Pinel ch. 8)

The organising idea: a hierarchy. Movement is planned top-down and executed bottom-up. High levels set goals; low levels handle the muscle detail; sensory feedback flows back up. The chain runs: association cortex (intention) → secondary motor cortex (SMA + premotor, the plan) → primary motor cortex M1 (the command) → brainstem & spinal circuits → muscle, with the basal ganglia and cerebellum tuning it from the side.

Pyramidal tract (corticospinal)

  • Origin: primary motor cortex (M1, Brodmann area 4) and premotor.
  • Path: internal capsule → cerebral peduncle → pons → medullary pyramids.
  • Decussates at the pyramids of the medulla (~85–90%) → lateral corticospinal tract (fine, distal, voluntary movement). The remaining 10–15% form the anterior corticospinal tract and decussate at the spinal segment.

Extrapyramidal pathways

  • Rubrospinal — from the red nucleus; flexor bias.
  • Vestibulospinal — postural extensors, balance.
  • Reticulospinal — locomotion, posture, gross movements.
  • Tectospinal — head/neck orienting to stimuli.

9. Development of the nervous system (Pinel ch. 9)

The question: how does a single fertilised cell become a brain with ~86 billion neurons wired into the right circuits? Pinel walks five phases, then myelination and pruning.

  1. Neural tube formation (neurulation): the ectoderm folds into the neural tube (~week 3–4); its hollow centre becomes the ventricles, the anterior end becomes the brain. Failure = spina bifida / anencephaly (folic acid protects).
  2. Proliferation: cells in the ventricular zone divide to produce neurons and glia.
  3. Migration: new neurons travel to their destinations, many guided along radial glia; the cortex is built inside-out (deep layers first).
  4. Differentiation & axon growth: neurons take on their type; the growth cone navigates by chemical cues (chemoaffinity, Sperry).
  5. Synaptogenesis: synapses form, in huge excess at first.

Then two sculpting processes refine the over-built brain:

  • Neural death (apoptosis) & synaptic pruning: ~50% of neurons die, and unused synapses are eliminated. Neurotrophins (e.g. NGF) keep useful connections alive: "use it or lose it".
  • Myelination: continues for years after birth (the prefrontal cortex finishes myelinating in the early–mid 20s, which is why mature judgement comes late).
Critical / sensitive periods. Some circuits must get the right experience within a time window or they wire abnormally for life. Hubel & Wiesel's monocular-deprivation kittens lost cortical responses to the closed eye (amblyopia). Language and binocular vision have sensitive periods. The lesson: experience is not a finishing touch, it is part of construction.

TRAP — order & direction. Sequence is proliferation → migration → differentiation → synaptogenesis → cell death/pruning → myelination. The cortex develops inside-out. Pruning reduces synapses (it does not add them). Do not confuse apoptosis (programmed, normal death) with necrosis (injury death).
Recap §9. Neural tube → proliferate → migrate (on radial glia, inside-out) → differentiate → synaptogenesis → prune + apoptosis → myelinate (into the 20s). Critical periods: experience wires the brain within windows.

Degeneration & regeneration

  • Anterograde degeneration = the cut axon segment away from the cell body dies. Retrograde = the segment toward the cell body (and sometimes the cell) degenerates.
  • Regeneration is much better in the PNS than the CNS, largely because Schwann cells (PNS) guide and support regrowth, whereas CNS oligodendrocytes/glial scarring inhibit it.

Plasticity & recovery of function

The brain rewires. "Plasticity" means the brain changes its structure and connections with experience and after injury. Mechanisms of recovery: cortical reorganisation (neighbouring areas take over), collateral sprouting, redundant circuits, and behaviourally driven relearning (the basis of rehabilitation). Adult neurogenesis occurs in the hippocampal dentate gyrus and the olfactory bulb/SVZ, but is limited.

11. Learning, memory & amnesia (Pinel ch. 11)

The landmark case that built the field. Patient H.M. had both medial temporal lobes (including the hippocampus) removed in 1953 to stop seizures. He could no longer form new long-term explicit memories (severe anterograde amnesia) yet kept old memories, normal IQ, and could still learn new motor skills (mirror drawing) without remembering having practised. This single dissociation proved (1) the hippocampus is needed to form new declarative memories but is not the storehouse, and (2) declarative and procedural memory are separate systems.

The taxonomy of memory

TypeSubtypeBrain region

Non-declarative (implicit)Procedural (skills, habits)Striatum / basal ganglia

  • Patient K.C. lost episodic memory (could not re-experience events) but kept semantic knowledge, dissociating the two.
  • Amygdala = emotional memory and fear conditioning; cerebellum = conditioned motor responses (eyeblink); striatum = habit/procedural learning.

Long-term potentiation (LTP): the synaptic mechanism

  • LTP = a long-lasting strengthening of a synapse after high-frequency stimulation, the leading cellular model of learning, first shown in the hippocampus.
  • It depends on the NMDA receptor, a coincidence detector: it opens only when glutamate binds and the postsynaptic cell is already depolarised (relieving its Mg²⁺ block). Ca²⁺ then floods in and drives AMPA receptor insertion, making the synapse stronger.
  • This is the molecular face of Hebb's rule: "neurons that fire together wire together." LTD (long-term depression) is the opposite, weakening unused synapses; both enable plasticity.

Amnesias

  • Anterograde = cannot form new memories after the event (H.M.). Retrograde = lost memories from before the event. Ribot's law: recent memories are more vulnerable than remote ones.
  • Korsakoff's syndrome = thiamine (B1) deficiency, usually from chronic alcoholism; damages the mammillary bodies + dorsomedial thalamus. Hallmark: dense anterograde amnesia + confabulation. The acute precursor is Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia), together Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome (see §19).
  • The engram = the physical memory trace; Lashley searched for it (mass action / equipotentiality), Richard Semon coined the term, and modern work locates engram cells in the hippocampus.
TRAP: H.M. = bilateral medial temporal/hippocampus → anterograde amnesia, intact procedural learning. Korsakoff = thiamine, mammillary bodies + thalamus, confabulation. Hippocampus forms declarative memory but is not the permanent store. LTP needs NMDA; AMPA carries the fast current.
Recap §11. H.M. → hippocampus forms new declarative memory; procedural is separate (striatum/cerebellum/amygdala). LTP via NMDA + Ca²⁺ → AMPA insertion = Hebbian learning. Korsakoff = thiamine + mammillary bodies + confabulation.

12. Hunger, eating & health (Pinel ch. 12)

The classic theory and its limits. The old dual-centre hypothalamic hypothesis said the lateral hypothalamus (LH) is the "hunger/go" centre and the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) is the "satiety/stop" centre. Lesion evidence: VMH lesion → hyperphagia + obesity (the rat overeats); LH lesion → aphagia (the rat stops eating). True but oversimplified, eating is regulated by a network and many peptides, not two switches.

The hormonal signals

Two competing frameworks for regulation

  • Set-point theory: the body defends a fixed weight/energy level like a thermostat; you eat to correct a deficit. Explains homeostasis but not why we eat when full or why obesity is rising.
  • Positive-incentive theory (Pinel favours this): we eat largely because of the anticipated pleasure of food (its incentive value), shaped by taste, learning, and availability, not just internal depletion. Explains overeating in a food-rich world.

Disorders of eating

  • Obesity: mismatch between an evolved drive to store energy and an environment of cheap palatable food; leptin resistance in many cases.
  • Anorexia nervosa (restriction, low weight, body-image disturbance) and bulimia nervosa (binge-purge); high mortality for anorexia.
TRAP: VMH = satiety (lesion → obese); LH = feeding (lesion → aphagia). Leptin from fat lowers appetite; ghrelin from the stomach raises it (do not swap). Pinel argues positive-incentive beats pure set-point.
Recap §12. VMH satiety / LH hunger. Leptin (fat) ↓ appetite via arcuate; ghrelin (stomach) ↑; insulin/CCK/PYY ↑ satiety. Set-point vs positive-incentive (Pinel prefers incentive).

14. Sleep, dreaming & circadian rhythms (Pinel ch. 14)

Full primer: sleep page.

The stages of sleep (read off the EEG)

  • NREM 1 — theta waves, easy to wake, hypnic jerks.
  • NREM 2 — sleep spindles + K-complexes; ~50% of total sleep.
  • NREM 3 — delta (slow-wave) sleep. Deep, restorative, growth hormone, sleepwalking/night terrors arise here.
  • REM — desynchronised low-voltage EEG resembling waking, rapid eye movements, muscle atonia, vivid dreams, genital arousal. This is the "paradoxical" stage. NOT delta-dominant.

The cycle: ~90 minutes; NREM 3 dominates the first third of the night, REM periods lengthen toward morning.

Circadian rhythms & the clock

  • The master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, entrained ("set") by light via the retinohypothalamic tract.
  • The SCN drives the pineal gland to release melatonin in darkness, signalling night. Circadian rhythms are free-running (~24.x h) without light cues.

Neural mechanisms of sleep/wake

  • Reticular activating system (ARAS) in the brainstem maintains wakefulness/arousal.
  • The VLPO (ventrolateral preoptic nucleus) promotes sleep (GABAergic "off switch"). Orexin/hypocretin neurons (lateral hypothalamus) stabilise wakefulness; their loss causes narcolepsy.
  • REM is generated by pontine circuits (REM-on/REM-off cells); the flip-flop switch model explains rapid state transitions.

TRAP: REM = mixed/low-voltage EEG (like waking), not delta; delta = NREM 3. Sleep spindles/K-complexes = NREM 2. SCN = the clock; melatonin from the pineal. Narcolepsy = orexin loss. Sleepwalking is a NREM (deep sleep) event, not a REM one.

15. Drug use, addiction & reward circuits (Pinel ch. 15)

Core concepts

  • Tolerance = needing more drug for the same effect (the dose-response curve shifts). Withdrawal = the opposite, aversive symptoms when the drug stops (often the mirror image of the drug's effects). Together they define physical dependence.
  • Sensitisation = the reverse of tolerance: increased response to a drug with repetition (often for its incentive/wanting component).
  • Conditioned tolerance / drug environment: tolerance is partly learned, cued by the setting, which is why a usual dose in a novel environment can become an overdose (Siegel).

The mesolimbic dopamine reward pathway

The final common path of reward. Nearly all addictive drugs converge on one circuit: dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) projecting to the nucleus accumbens (with the prefrontal cortex). Olds & Milner's rats pressed a lever endlessly for electrical stimulation here. Drugs hijack this "wanting" system. Incentive-sensitisation theory (Robinson & Berridge) splits "wanting" (dopamine, craving) from "liking" (opioid pleasure), explaining why addicts crave drugs they no longer enjoy.

16. Lateralisation, language & the split brain (Pinel ch. 16)

Two hemispheres, different jobs. Most functions are bilateral, but some are lateralised. In ~90% of people language sits in the left hemisphere; the right is biased toward spatial processing, faces, music and emotional prosody.

The split-brain studies (Sperry & Gazzaniga)

Cutting the corpus callosum (to stop severe epilepsy) leaves two hemispheres that cannot talk to each other. Because each visual field projects to the opposite hemisphere, an object flashed to the left visual field → right hemisphere cannot be named (language is on the left), yet the left hand (right hemisphere) can pick it out by touch. Sperry won the Nobel for this; it proved functional lateralisation directly.

Dichotic listening (Kimura): different sounds to each ear; the right-ear advantage for words reflects left-hemisphere language dominance. The Wada test (inject sodium amobarbital into one carotid to anaesthetise one hemisphere) clinically establishes the language-dominant hemisphere before surgery.

17. Emotion, stress & health (Pinel ch. 17)

The neural circuitry of emotion

  • Papez circuit (the early "emotion loop"): hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → anterior thalamus → cingulate → entorhinal → back to hippocampus. The limbic system (MacLean) extended this.
  • Amygdala = the hub of fear and threat detection; central to fear conditioning. Klüver-Bucy syndrome (bilateral amygdala/temporal lesion) = hyperorality, hypersexuality, placidity (tameness), visual agnosia.
  • LeDoux's two roads to fear: a fast "low road" (thalamus → amygdala, crude but quick) and a slower "high road" (thalamus → cortex → amygdala, accurate).

  • HPA axis: stress → hypothalamus releases CRH → anterior pituitary releases ACTH → adrenal cortex releases cortisol; negative feedback to the hypothalamus + pituitary. Chronically elevated in MDD, PTSD, Cushing's.
  • The fast arm is the SAM axis (sympathetic-adrenal-medullary): epinephrine/NE for immediate fight-or-flight.

18. Biopsychology of psychiatric disorders (Pinel ch. 18)

The chapter that ties neurotransmitters to clinical conditions and their treatments. Learn each disorder by its leading biological hypothesis plus the drug that follows from it.

Schizophrenia

  • Dopamine hypothesis: positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) arise from excess dopamine activity in the mesolimbic pathway; supported because antipsychotics are D2 antagonists and amphetamine (a DA agonist) can mimic psychosis.
  • Refinement: negative/cognitive symptoms link to low dopamine in the mesocortical (prefrontal) pathway, plus glutamate/NMDA hypofunction (PCP/ketamine, NMDA blockers, mimic the full syndrome).

Depression & bipolar

  • Monoamine hypothesis: depression reflects a deficit of serotonin and/or norepinephrine (the basis of SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs). Limitations: drugs raise monoamines in hours but lift mood in weeks, so neuroplasticity/BDNF and HPA dysregulation matter too.
  • Bipolar disorder: mood cycles between mania and depression; first-line mood stabiliser is lithium.

Anxiety disorders

Linked to GABA (benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A) and serotonin (SSRIs are first-line for chronic anxiety/OCD); the amygdala is overactive in fear circuits.

20. The MCQ traps, collected

  • Divisions: human brain-damage = neuropsychology; non-invasive healthy-human measures = psychophysiology; animal brain manipulation = physiological psychology; imaging of cognition = cognitive neuroscience; drugs = psychopharmacology.
  • Genetics: ontogeny = the individual, phylogeny = the species. Heritability is a population statistic. Epigenetics (methylation) silences genes. MZ ~100%, DZ ~50%.
  • Glia: CNS myelin = oligodendrocyte; PNS myelin = Schwann (and Schwann aids regeneration).
  • Action potential: all-or-none, frequency codes intensity. Absolute refractory = Na⁺ inactivated (no spike possible); relative = needs a stronger stimulus.
  • NT precursors: dopamine from tyrosine, serotonin from tryptophan. LTP needs NMDA (coincidence detector); AMPA carries the fast EPSP.
  • Methods: CT/MRI = structure, fMRI/PET = function. EEG = good timing/poor location; fMRI = good location/poorer timing. Stereotaxic = deep coordinates. TMS = non-invasive virtual lesion.
  • Vision: cones densest at fovea; pathway retina → chiasm → LGN → V1. Dorsal = where (parietal), ventral = what (temporal). Trichromatic (cones) + opponent-process (after retina, afterimages).
  • Audition: place theory = high frequency (base), frequency theory = low, volley = mid. Smell skips the thalamus.
  • Somatosensation: DCML (fine touch/proprioception, crosses in medulla) vs spinothalamic (pain/temp, crosses at entry) → Brown-Séquard dissociation.
  • Motor: pyramidal decussates at the medullary pyramids. SMA = internal, premotor = external. Basal ganglia = force/vigour (resting tremor); cerebellum = timing/coordination (intention tremor). UMN spastic / LMN flaccid.
  • Development: proliferate → migrate (inside-out) → differentiate → synaptogenesis → prune/apoptosis → myelinate. Critical periods; pruning reduces synapses.
  • Plasticity: PNS regenerates better than CNS; adult neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus. Alzheimer's = plaques + tangles; Parkinson's = SN dopamine + Lewy bodies; Huntington's = caudate/CAG/AD; MS = demyelination.
  • Memory: H.M. = hippocampus → anterograde amnesia, intact procedural. Korsakoff = thiamine + mammillary bodies + confabulation. LTP via NMDA + Ca²⁺ (Hebb).
  • Hunger: VMH satiety (lesion → obese), LH feeding (lesion → aphagia). Leptin (fat) ↓ appetite, ghrelin (stomach) ↑. Positive-incentive over set-point.
  • Hormones: posterior pituitary only stores ADH/oxytocin (made in hypothalamus). SRY → testes; default is female. Organizational = early/permanent, activational = later/reversible.
  • Sleep: REM = low-voltage EEG + atonia (NOT delta); delta = NREM 3; spindles/K = NREM 2. SCN clock, pineal melatonin. Narcolepsy = orexin loss. Sleepwalking = NREM.
  • Addiction: reward = VTA → nucleus accumbens (mesolimbic dopamine). Tolerance vs sensitisation = opposites. Wanting (DA) vs liking (opioid).
  • Lateralisation: language left in ~90%. Split-brain right hemisphere can't name. Broca non-fluent (comprehends), Wernicke fluent (no comprehension), conduction = bad repetition. Wada test localises language; dichotic = right-ear advantage.
  • Emotion/stress: James-Lange (body first) vs Cannon-Bard (simultaneous) vs Schachter-Singer (arousal+label) vs Lazarus (appraisal). HPA → cortisol; Selye GAS = alarm/resistance/exhaustion. Amygdala = fear; bilateral lesion = Klüver-Bucy.
  • Psychiatric: schizophrenia positive symptoms = ↑ mesolimbic dopamine (D2 antagonists); depression = ↓ monoamines; bipolar = lithium; anxiety = GABA/benzos.
  • Clinical syndromes: right parietal → left neglect + anosognosia; left angular gyrus → Gerstmann; fusiform → prosopagnosia; Capgras = "impostor"; three A's = agnosia (recognise)/apraxia (do)/aphasia (language). Batteries: Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska, NIMHANS battery, Bender-Gestalt.
  • Yerkes-Dodson: inverted U; optimal arousal is lower for complex tasks, higher for simple/well-learned ones.

21. The landmark studies / classic experiments, walked through

NIMHANS and MET stems love to describe a biopsych experiment and ask you to name it, its author, or its conclusion, and the favourite trick is the who-did-what swap (pairing the right finding with the wrong name). Here each landmark is walked through in the same shape: what they did, what they found, why it matters, and the trap. Learn the procedure, not just the headline, because the design ("a cat's visual cortex cell fired only to a bar at one angle" vs "a rat pressed a lever for brain stimulation") is the giveaway.

Hubel & Wiesel (1959 onward) — feature detectors in the visual cortex

What: recorded from single cells in the cat (and monkey) primary visual cortex (V1) while showing bars and edges of light at various orientations and positions. Found: V1 neurons are feature detectors tuned to specific stimulus properties: simple cells respond to an edge/bar of a particular orientation in a particular location; complex cells respond to that orientation anywhere in their field and often to a direction of movement; hypercomplex (end-stopped) cells add length/corner tuning. They also mapped ocular dominance columns and orientation columns (the columnar organisation of cortex). Monocular deprivation in kittens showed a critical period: an eye sutured shut early lost its cortical representation permanently (amblyopia), but the same deprivation in an adult did not. Matters: founded the modern understanding of cortical receptive fields and experience-dependent plasticity; Nobel Prize 1981 (with Sperry). Trap: simple → orientation + position; complex → orientation regardless of position (+ motion); hypercomplex → adds length. Critical-period plasticity (deprivation amblyopia) is Hubel & Wiesel, not Sperry. Do not confuse "feature detector" (Hubel & Wiesel) with "grandmother cell" (the caricature of one cell per object).

Olds & Milner (1954) — intracranial self-stimulation and the "pleasure centres"

What: implanted stimulating electrodes in rat brains and let the rat press a lever to deliver a brief electrical pulse to its own brain. Found: with electrodes in the septal area and the medial forebrain bundle / lateral hypothalamus, rats pressed the lever thousands of times an hour, choosing it over food, water, even crossing electrified grids, the discovery of brain reward / "pleasure" centres (electrical self-stimulation, ICSS). Matters: identified the substrate later mapped onto the mesolimbic dopamine reward pathway (VTA → nucleus accumbens) that all addictive drugs hijack (§15). Trap: the classic original site is the septal area (and medial forebrain bundle); the modern "reward pathway" name is VTA → nucleus accumbens. Olds & Milner = self-stimulation reward, not memory (that is the other Milner, Brenda, with H.M.).

Scoville & Milner (1957) — patient H.M., the medial temporal lobe and memory

What: surgeon William Scoville removed the medial temporal lobes bilaterally (including most of the hippocampus, plus amygdala and entorhinal cortex) to control intractable epilepsy in Henry Molaison (H.M.); Brenda Milner studied him for decades. Found: profound anterograde amnesia (could not form new long-term declarative memories) with relatively preserved remote memory, intact IQ, intact short-term/working memory, and crucially intact procedural learning, he improved at mirror-drawing across days while denying he had ever done the task. Matters: proved (1) the hippocampus/MTL is required to form new declarative memories but is not the permanent store, and (2) declarative and procedural memory are separate systems with separate substrates. The single most cited case in memory neuroscience. Trap: H.M. = bilateral medial temporal / hippocampus → anterograde amnesia, spared procedural (mirror-drawing). The neurosurgeon was Scoville; the neuropsychologist who tracked him was Brenda Milner (the mirror-drawing demonstration is hers). Do not confuse with Korsakoff (thiamine, mammillary bodies, confabulation).

Brenda Milner — mirror tracing & procedural memory

What: had H.M. trace a star while viewing only its mirror image (a skill that is awkward at first). Found: his tracing got faster and more accurate over successive days even though, each day, he had no memory of ever having done it before. Matters: the cleanest single demonstration that skill (procedural) learning is dissociable from conscious (declarative) memory, and that the hippocampus is not needed for the former. Trap: "improved at a motor skill but had no memory of practising" = Milner's mirror-tracing in H.M.; the motor/procedural system here is the striatum + cerebellum, not the hippocampus.

Sperry & Gazzaniga (1960s) — the split brain and lateralisation

What: studied patients whose corpus callosum had been severed (commissurotomy) to halt severe epilepsy. Stimuli were flashed to one visual field (hence one hemisphere) and responses tested by naming vs the hand. Found: an object shown to the left visual field → right hemisphere could not be named (speech is left-hemisphere) yet the left hand (right hemisphere) could select it by touch; the left hemisphere ("the interpreter," Gazzaniga) confabulated explanations for actions the right hemisphere initiated. Matters: direct proof of functional lateralisation and that the two hemispheres can operate independently; Sperry shared the 1981 Nobel. Trap: right hemisphere can't name but can point/select with the left hand; left = language, right = spatial. Sperry (senior, Nobel) and Gazzaniga (the "interpreter" idea) go together; do not swap with Wada (a clinical test, not the split-brain study) or with Kimura (dichotic listening).

Lashley (1929) — the search for the engram (equipotentiality & mass action)

What: Karl Lashley trained rats in mazes, then made cortical lesions of varying size and location, hunting for the seat of the memory trace (the engram). Found: the memory deficit depended on the amount of cortex removed, not its specific location, so he could not find a single engram site. Matters: yielded two principles, mass action (learning/memory impairment is proportional to the total mass of tissue destroyed) and equipotentiality (within an area, any part can substitute for any other in supporting the function). Now seen as overstated (memory is partly localised) but historically pivotal. Trap: Lashley = mass action + equipotentiality and the failed search for the engram. The term engram was coined by Richard Semon. Lashley's student Donald Hebb then proposed the opposite-spirited cell assembly.

Hebb (1949) — cell assemblies and "fire together, wire together"

What: Donald Hebb (Lashley's student) proposed a theory rather than a single experiment: repeatedly co-active neurons strengthen their connections, forming cell assemblies and phase sequences that are the physical basis of memory. Found / claimed: Hebb's rule, "when an axon of cell A repeatedly takes part in firing cell B, some growth process strengthens A's efficiency," popularised as "neurons that fire together wire together." Matters: the conceptual parent of long-term potentiation (LTP), discovered later (Bliss & Lømo, 1973, in the hippocampus), the cellular mechanism that vindicated Hebb. Trap: Hebb = the theory (cell assembly, fire-together-wire-together); LTP is the experimental mechanism that confirmed it, and it depends on the NMDA receptor as a coincidence detector. Lashley (engram search, mass action) vs Hebb (cell assemblies) is a classic contrast pair.

Penfield (1930s–50s) — cortical stimulation mapping and the homunculus

What: Wilder Penfield electrically stimulated the cortex of awake epilepsy patients during surgery (only local anaesthesia; the brain feels no pain) and recorded what they felt or did. Found: systematic stimulation of the precentral gyrus produced movements and of the postcentral gyrus produced sensations, mapped out as the motor and sensory homunculi (distorted little men with huge hands/lips). Temporal-lobe stimulation sometimes evoked vivid "experiential" memories. Matters: established somatotopic organisation of the cortex and the homuncular map. Trap: the homunculus and cortical stimulation mapping = Penfield; the disproportion reflects density of innervation (hands, lips, tongue are huge), not body size. Penfield = stimulation in humans; Hubel & Wiesel = recording in cats.

Phineas Gage (1848) — the frontal lobe and personality

What: a railway tamping iron blasted through Gage's left frontal lobe (orbitofrontal/ventromedial prefrontal region). He survived. Found: his intellect and memory were largely intact but his personality changed, he became impulsive, profane, socially disinhibited, unable to plan ("no longer Gage"). Matters: the first dramatic evidence that the frontal lobes govern personality, social conduct and executive control / inhibition. Trap: Gage = frontal (orbitofrontal/ventromedial) → personality change, disinhibition, intact basic cognition. It is a frontal case, not a memory case. Damasio's later reconstruction (Hanna Damasio, 1994) placed the damage in ventromedial PFC, the region tied to emotion-guided decision-making (somatic marker hypothesis).

Patient K.C. and Clive Wearing — episodic vs semantic memory

What: K.C. (studied by Tulving) had extensive medial temporal/hippocampal damage from a motorcycle accident; Clive Wearing, a musician, suffered herpes simplex encephalitis destroying hippocampi and temporal lobes. Found: K.C. lost episodic memory (could not mentally re-experience any personal event, past or imagined future) while retaining semantic knowledge (facts, vocabulary, how the world works). Clive Wearing had dense anterograde and retrograde amnesia with a ~7–30 second memory span, yet could still play piano and conduct (preserved procedural/musical memory) and recognised his wife. Matters: dissociate episodic (Tulving's "mental time travel," hippocampus-dependent) from semantic (temporal neocortex) and both from procedural. Trap: K.C. = lost episodic, kept semantic (Tulving's distinction). Clive Wearing = encephalitis, "the man with the 7-second memory," spared music/procedural. Both contrast with H.M., who showed the declarative/procedural split.

O'Keefe (1971) & the Morris water maze — place cells and the cognitive map

What: John O'Keefe recorded hippocampal neurons in freely moving rats; Richard Morris devised the Morris water maze (a hidden platform in opaque water that the rat must locate using room cues). Found: O'Keefe discovered place cells, hippocampal neurons that fire only when the animal is in a specific location, the neural substrate of a cognitive map (O'Keefe & Nadel). In the water maze, hippocampal lesions abolish the spatial (place) strategy. Later, the Mosers found grid cells in entorhinal cortex; O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the 2014 Nobel. Matters: ties the hippocampus to spatial memory and navigation, complementing its role in declarative memory. Trap: place cells = hippocampus = O'Keefe; grid cells = entorhinal cortex = the Mosers. The Morris water maze is the standard spatial-memory / hippocampal behavioural test (Morris); do not confuse with the radial-arm maze (Olton).

Kandel — Aplysia, habituation, sensitisation and the synapse

What: Eric Kandel used the sea slug Aplysia (large, few, identifiable neurons) and its gill-withdrawal reflex to study the cellular basis of learning. Found: habituation (a decrease in response to repeated harmless stimulation) reflects reduced neurotransmitter release at the sensory-motor synapse; sensitisation (an enhanced response after a noxious stimulus) reflects increased release, via serotonin → cyclic AMP → more transmitter. Long-term memory (unlike short-term) requires new protein synthesis and gene expression (CREB) and the growth of new synaptic connections. Matters: showed that learning is a physical change at the synapse and bridged short- vs long-term memory mechanisms; Nobel 2000. Trap: Kandel = Aplysia, habituation (less transmitter) vs sensitisation (more transmitter), long-term memory needs protein synthesis / CREB. Habituation is non-associative learning, not fatigue and not adaptation.

McGaugh & LeDoux — emotional memory and the amygdala

What: James McGaugh studied how emotional arousal strengthens memory consolidation; Joseph LeDoux mapped fear conditioning circuits. Found: emotionally arousing events are remembered better because stress hormones (epinephrine, cortisol) and the basolateral amygdala modulate consolidation in the hippocampus, β-blockers can blunt this. LeDoux showed fear conditioning runs through the amygdala via a fast subcortical "low road" (thalamus → amygdala) and a slower cortical "high road" (thalamus → cortex → amygdala). Matters: explains flashbulb memories and PTSD intrusions; the amygdala is the hub of emotional/fear memory (vs hippocampus for the declarative content). Trap: amygdala modulates emotional memory strength (McGaugh) and is the fear-conditioning hub (LeDoux's two roads); the hippocampus stores the facts of the event, the amygdala adds the emotional charge.

Cannon & Bard, and Papez — the emotion circuits

What / found: Walter Cannon (with Philip Bard) challenged James-Lange by showing that decorticate animals still display "sham rage," and argued the thalamus (later hypothalamus) triggers emotion and bodily arousal simultaneously (the Cannon-Bard thalamic theory, §17). James Papez (1937) proposed the Papez circuit (hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → anterior thalamus → cingulate → entorhinal → hippocampus) as the anatomical loop of emotion; Paul MacLean extended it into the limbic system concept. Matters: the historical backbone of emotion neuroanatomy. Trap: Cannon-Bard = simultaneous body + emotion (thalamic); James-Lange = body first. Papez = the circuit/loop; MacLean = the limbic system umbrella; Klüver-Bucy = bilateral amygdala/temporal lesion (hyperorality, hypersexuality, placidity).

Early lesion physiology — Magendie, Bell, Broca, Brown-Séquard

What / found: the Bell-Magendie law established that the dorsal (posterior) spinal roots are sensory and the ventral (anterior) roots are motor, the founding dissociation of sensory vs motor pathways. Paul Broca (1861) studied patient "Tan," who could understand but only say "tan," and on autopsy found damage to the left inferior frontal gyrus, Broca's area, the first solid evidence of localisation of language in the left hemisphere; Carl Wernicke later localised comprehension to the left superior temporal gyrus. Brown-Séquard syndrome (hemisection of the spinal cord) gives the classic dissociation: ipsilateral loss of fine touch/proprioception/motor (DCML and corticospinal cross higher up) and contralateral loss of pain/temperature (spinothalamic crosses at entry; §7). Matters: these are the bedrock localisation findings. Trap: dorsal root = sensory, ventral root = motor (Bell-Magendie). Broca = production/non-fluent (left frontal, patient "Tan"); Wernicke = comprehension/fluent (left temporal). Brown-Séquard = ipsilateral touch/motor loss + contralateral pain/temp loss.

Bouchard — the Minnesota twin study and heritability

What: Thomas Bouchard's Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart compared monozygotic twins raised separately with those raised together (and with dizygotic twins). Found: MZ twins reared apart were strikingly similar on IQ and many personality traits, yielding high heritability estimates (IQ heritability often quoted ~0.5–0.7 in adults). Matters: the landmark behaviour-genetics evidence that individual differences have a substantial genetic component, while still leaving room for environment. Trap: heritability is a population statistic (the proportion of variance in a population due to genetic variance), not a personal "% genetic." MZ ≈ 100% shared genes, DZ ≈ 50%. Twins-reared-apart = Bouchard (Minnesota).

NIMHANS angle. Pair each disorder with its hypothesis and its drug: schizophrenia = dopamine (D2 antagonists) + NMDA hypofunction; depression = monoamine + BDNF/neurotrophic; OCD/anxiety = serotonin + GABA; bipolar = lithium. Know the somatic treatments (ECT, rTMS, DBS) and the side-effect pairs (typicals → EPS/tardive dyskinesia, clozapine → agranulocytosis).

24. Self-test (active recall, by chapter)

Recall beats re-reading. Cover the answers, ask yourself each question aloud, then check. If you miss one, jump back to its section. The answer follows in green.

§1 Field & §2 evolution/genetics

  1. Which division studies human brain-damaged patients, and which studies non-invasive measures in healthy humans? Neuropsychology (patients) vs psychophysiology (non-invasive, healthy).
  2. Why is heritability not a personal percentage? It is the proportion of population variance due to genetic variance, not how "genetic" one individual's trait is.
  3. Ontogeny vs phylogeny? Development of the individual vs evolutionary history of the species.
  4. What is the epigenetic mechanism that silences genes? DNA methylation (also histone modification).

§3 Anatomy

  1. Which glia myelinate in the CNS vs PNS, and which aids regeneration? Oligodendrocyte (CNS) vs Schwann cell (PNS); Schwann aids regeneration.
  2. Name the meninges outer to inner. Dura → arachnoid → pia.
  3. Which sense bypasses the thalamus? Smell (olfaction).
  4. VMH vs LH; superior vs inferior colliculus? VMH satiety / LH feeding; superior = sight, inferior = hearing.

§4 Conduction & synapses

  1. What sets the resting potential, and what value? Na⁺/K⁺ pump + leaky K⁺ channels; about −70 mV.
  2. How is stimulus intensity coded if the AP is all-or-none? By the frequency of firing, never spike amplitude.
  3. What triggers neurotransmitter release at the terminal? Ca²⁺ influx through voltage-gated channels.
  4. Precursors of dopamine and serotonin? Tyrosine (dopamine) and tryptophan (serotonin).
  5. Which receptor is the coincidence detector behind LTP? The NMDA receptor (needs glutamate + depolarisation; AMPA carries the fast EPSP).

§5 Methods

  1. Structure vs function imaging? CT/MRI = structure; fMRI/PET = function (BOLD is the fMRI signal).
  2. Which method has great timing but poor location, and which the reverse? EEG = great timing/poor location; fMRI = great location/poorer timing.
  3. What is a TMS pulse used for experimentally? A non-invasive "virtual lesion" (and to treat depression).

§6–7 Vision, audition & senses

  1. Trace the retina-to-cortex pathway. Retina → optic nerve → chiasm → optic tract → LGN → V1 (striate cortex).
  2. Which colour theory operates at the cones and which after the retina? Trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz) at cones; opponent-process (Hering) after, explaining afterimages.
  3. Place vs frequency vs volley theory, best for which frequencies? Place = high, frequency = low, volley = mid.
  4. Which somatosensory tract crosses where, and what does Brown-Séquard show? DCML (fine touch) crosses in the medulla; spinothalamic (pain/temp) at entry → ipsilateral touch loss + contralateral pain loss.

§8 Sensorimotor

  1. SMA vs premotor cortex? SMA = internally generated/self-initiated; premotor = externally cued movement.
  2. Where does the pyramidal tract decussate? At the pyramids of the medulla (~85–90%).
  3. Resting vs intention tremor, which structure? Resting = basal ganglia (Parkinson's); intention = cerebellum.
  4. UMN vs LMN signs? UMN: spastic, hyperreflexic, Babinski up, no fasciculations. LMN: flaccid, areflexic, atrophy + fasciculations.

§9–10 Development & plasticity

  1. Give the developmental sequence. Proliferation → migration (inside-out, on radial glia) → differentiation → synaptogenesis → cell death/pruning → myelination.
  2. Where does adult neurogenesis mainly occur? Hippocampal dentate gyrus (and the SVZ → olfactory bulb).
  3. Match: Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Huntington's to their pathology. Amyloid plaques + tau tangles; substantia-nigra dopamine loss + Lewy bodies; caudate/putamen atrophy (CAG, dominant).

§11 Memory

  1. What did H.M.'s case prove, and what surgery caused it? Bilateral medial-temporal (hippocampal) removal → anterograde amnesia with spared procedural memory; hippocampus forms but doesn't store declarative memory.
  2. Korsakoff: cause, locus, hallmark sign? Thiamine (B1) deficiency; mammillary bodies + dorsomedial thalamus; confabulation.
  3. Lashley vs Hebb? Lashley = failed engram search → mass action + equipotentiality; Hebb = cell assemblies ("fire together wire together").
  4. What dissociation do K.C. and Clive Wearing illustrate? Episodic loss with spared semantic (K.C., Tulving); dense amnesia with spared music/procedural (Clive Wearing).

§12–13 Hunger & hormones

  1. Leptin vs ghrelin, source and effect? Leptin from fat lowers appetite; ghrelin from the stomach raises it.
  2. Which framework does Pinel favour for eating? Positive-incentive (pleasure of food) over pure set-point.
  3. Where are ADH and oxytocin made and released? Made in the hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary.
  4. Organisational vs activational hormone effects? Early + permanent wiring vs later + reversible switching; SRY → testes, default is female.

§14–15 Sleep & addiction

  1. What EEG features mark NREM 2 and NREM 3, and is REM delta? NREM 2 = spindles + K-complexes; NREM 3 = delta; REM is low-voltage, NOT delta.
  2. What is the master clock and what loss causes narcolepsy? The suprachiasmatic nucleus; loss of orexin/hypocretin causes narcolepsy.
  3. Name the reward pathway and the wanting/liking split. VTA → nucleus accumbens (mesolimbic dopamine); "wanting" = dopamine, "liking" = opioids.
  4. Tolerance vs sensitisation? Opposites: decreased vs increased response with repetition.

§16 Lateralisation & language

  1. In split-brain patients, why can't an object in the left visual field be named? It projects to the right hemisphere, which lacks speech; the left hand can still select it.
  2. Broca vs Wernicke vs conduction aphasia? Broca = non-fluent, comprehends; Wernicke = fluent, no comprehension; conduction = fluent + comprehends but poor repetition (arcuate fasciculus).
  3. What does the Wada test establish? The language-dominant hemisphere (carotid amobarbital).

§17 Emotion & stress

  1. Order the four emotion theories by their claim. James-Lange (body first) → Cannon-Bard (simultaneous) → Schachter-Singer (arousal + label) → Lazarus (appraisal first).
  2. Trace the HPA axis to its endpoint. Hypothalamus (CRH) → pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex (cortisol).
  3. Selye's three GAS stages? Alarm → resistance → exhaustion.
  4. Bilateral amygdala lesion produces what syndrome? Klüver-Bucy (hyperorality, hypersexuality, placidity, visual agnosia).

§18 Psychiatric disorders

  1. Dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia, and the drug class that supports it? Positive symptoms from excess mesolimbic dopamine; D2 antagonists (antipsychotics).
  2. Monoamine hypothesis of depression and its limitation? Deficit of serotonin/NE; but drugs raise monoamines in hours yet lift mood in weeks (neuroplasticity/BDNF matters).
  3. First-line treatment for bipolar disorder? Lithium.
  4. Match side effects: typical antipsychotics vs clozapine. Typicals → EPS/tardive dyskinesia; clozapine → agranulocytosis.

§19 Clinical neuropsychology

  1. Right parietal lesion produces what two signs? Left hemispatial neglect and anosognosia.
  2. Name Gerstmann's tetrad and its locus. Agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia, L-R disorientation; left angular gyrus.
  3. Distinguish the three "A"s. Agnosia = can't recognise; apraxia = can't perform a learned movement; aphasia = can't language.
  4. Capgras vs prosopagnosia? Capgras = "loved one replaced by impostor" (emotional disconnect); prosopagnosia = can't recognise the face at all (fusiform).
  5. Pair the batteries: Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska, NIMHANS, Bender-Gestalt, WCST. Fixed quantitative battery; standardised Luria qualitative; India-normed battery; visuomotor screen; frontal set-shifting.

§21 Landmark studies

  1. Who found visual-cortex feature detectors and the visual critical period? Hubel & Wiesel.
  2. Who discovered intracranial self-stimulation, and where was the classic electrode? Olds & Milner; septal area / medial forebrain bundle.
  3. Who discovered place cells and which test probes hippocampal spatial memory? O'Keefe (place cells); the Morris water maze.
  4. What did Kandel show in Aplysia? Habituation (less transmitter) vs sensitisation (more); long-term memory needs new protein synthesis (CREB).
  5. State the Bell-Magendie law. Dorsal spinal roots are sensory, ventral roots are motor.
