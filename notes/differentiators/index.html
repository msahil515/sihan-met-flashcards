Lookalikes — telling two close terms apart on the options

For when plain definitions aren't enough and the trap is a near-identical distractor. Each block is one confusable pair: what's the same (why both look right), what differs (line by line), and the one tell — the single thing in the stem that splits them. Read the tell, not the whole block, on exam day. Built 2026-05-25.

How to use it. The examiner picks two real, correct-sounding terms and bets you can't split them. Don't re-derive both definitions. Find the tell word in the stem (added vs removed? up vs down? before vs after? thought vs act?) and the option falls out. These are the pairs that actually bleed marks on MET/NIMHANS.

1. Learning & conditioning

Classical conditioning vs Operant conditioning

Same: both are associative learning; both show acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation and discrimination.

Classical (Pavlov)Operant (Skinner)
BehaviourInvoluntary, reflexive (salivation, fear)Voluntary, emitted (lever press)
AssociationStimulus → stimulus (CS–US)Behaviour → consequence (response–reinforcer)
LearnerPassive, responds to a cueActive, operates on the environment
Reinforcer timingComes before/with the responseComes after the response
The tell: Is the behaviour automatic and triggered by a cue that comes first (classical), or voluntary and shaped by what happens after (operant)? Any "reward / consequence / punishment" in the stem → operant.
Negative reinforcement vs Punishment

Same: both are operant procedures and both usually involve an aversive/unpleasant stimulus, so they read alike.

Negative reinforcementPunishment
Effect on behaviourIncreases itDecreases it
What happensRemoves an aversive stimulus (take painkiller → pain gone)Adds aversive (positive) or removes pleasant (negative)
"Negative" meansSubtraction, not "bad"—
The tell: Did the behaviour go up or down? Up = reinforcement (negative = the bad thing was taken away). Down = punishment. "Negative" is about removal, never about being unpleasant.
Positive punishment vs Negative punishment

Same: both decrease a behaviour.

Positive punishmentNegative punishment
OperationAdd something aversive (scolding, spanking)Remove something pleasant (take phone, time-out, fine)
Other name—Response cost / omission
The tell: Positive = something was added; negative = something was taken away. Both still reduce the behaviour, so judge by add vs subtract, not by good vs bad.
Primary reinforcer vs Secondary reinforcer

Same: both strengthen behaviour when they follow it.

PrimarySecondary (conditioned)
Why it reinforcesInnately satisfies a biological need (food, water, sex, warmth)Learned value through association
Learning needed?NoYes (money, tokens, praise, grades)
The tell: Does it satisfy a body need directly (primary) or only because you learned it stands for something else (secondary)? Money is the classic secondary reinforcer.
Shaping vs Chaining

Same: both build complex operant behaviour using reinforcement.

ShapingChaining
What's reinforcedSuccessive approximations to one target behaviourLinking a sequence of already-learned behaviours
Picture itRefining one act closer and closerStep 1 → step 2 → step 3 as a chain
The tell: One behaviour polished step-by-step (shaping) vs several separate behaviours joined in order (chaining).
Stimulus generalisation vs Stimulus discrimination

Same: both describe how a conditioned response transfers (or doesn't) across stimuli.

GeneralisationDiscrimination
Response to similar cuesYes — spreads to stimuli like the CSNo — only the exact CS triggers it
Little AlbertFeared all furry white thingsWould fear only the rat
The tell: Response spreads to lookalikes (generalisation) vs narrows to one exact cue (discrimination).

2. Developmental

Assimilation vs Accommodation (Piaget)

Same: the twin processes of adaptation; both are how a schema deals with new information.

AssimilationAccommodation
SchemaStays the same — new info forced into itChanges / a new one is created to fit reality
ExampleCalls a whale a "fish"Learns a whale is a mammal, makes a new category
The tell: Did the existing schema stay put (assimilation) or did it have to change (accommodation)? If the child updated their mental category, it's accommodation.
Piaget vs Vygotsky

Same: both constructivists; both say the child actively builds knowledge rather than passively absorbing it.

PiagetVygotsky
Engine of developmentThe individual child exploring; cognition leads languageSocial/cultural interaction; language leads cognition
Signature termsStages, schema, assimilation/accommodation, egocentrismZPD, scaffolding, More Knowledgeable Other, private speech
The tell: Is the driver the child alone (Piaget) or a guiding social other / ZPD (Vygotsky)? Any "scaffolding" or "zone of proximal development" → Vygotsky.

3. Sensation & perception

Sensation vs Perception

Same: both are stages of taking in stimulus information.

SensationPerception
What it isRaw detection by sense organs + transductionBrain's organisation & interpretation of it
DirectionBottom-up (data in)Top-down (meaning added)
The tell: Detecting the raw signal at the receptor (sensation) vs making sense of / recognising it (perception). Interpretation, meaning, recognition → perception.
Absolute threshold vs Difference threshold (JND)

Same: both are psychophysics thresholds (Weber/Fechner).

Absolute thresholdDifference threshold (JND)
DetectsMinimum intensity to detect a stimulus 50% of the timeSmallest detectable change between two stimuli
Law—Weber's law: JND is a constant proportion
The tell: Detecting that one stimulus is present (absolute) vs detecting a difference between two (difference / JND). "Just noticeable difference" → difference threshold.

4. Memory & cognition

Proactive interference vs Retroactive interference

Same: both are forgetting caused by interference between old and new learning.

ProactiveRetroactive
DirectionOld blocks new (forward)New blocks old (backward)
What you forgetThe new materialThe old material
ExampleOld password keeps coming upNew password wipes the old one
The tell: Which is forgotten? Forget the new → proactive ("pro = prior learning interferes forward"). Forget the old → retroactive.
Recall vs Recognition

Same: both are measures of retrieval from long-term memory.

RecallRecognition
TaskProduce the info with little/no cue (essay, free recall)Identify the correct item among options (MCQ)
DifficultyHarderEasier — the answer is in front of you
The tell: Generate it from scratch (recall) vs pick it out of choices (recognition). A multiple-choice exam tests recognition.
Anterograde amnesia vs Retrograde amnesia

Same: both are memory loss, usually after brain injury, defined relative to the moment of injury.

AnterogradeRetrograde
Lost memoriesCan't form new memories after the eventLost old memories from before the event
Time directionForward from injuryBackward from injury
The tell: "Antero = after" — no new memories form. Retrograde = the past is gone. (H.M. = classic anterograde.)
Explicit (declarative) vs Implicit (non-declarative) memory

Same: both are long-term memory systems.

Explicit / declarativeImplicit / non-declarative
AwarenessConscious — you can state itUnconscious — shows in performance
ContentsFacts (semantic) + events (episodic)Skills (procedural), priming, conditioning
The tell: Can you consciously declare it (explicit) or does it just show up as a skill/habit (implicit)? Riding a bike = implicit/procedural.

5. Stats & research methods

Type I error vs Type II error

Same: both are wrong decisions in null-hypothesis testing.

Type I (α)Type II (β)
MistakeReject a true null = false positiveFail to reject a false null = false negative
In wordsClaim an effect that isn't thereMiss a real effect
The tell: False alarm = Type I (α). Miss = Type II (β). "Type I = I see something that isn't there."
Reliability vs Validity

Same: both are desirable psychometric properties of a test or measure.

ReliabilityValidity
Question it answersIs it consistent / repeatable?Does it measure what it's supposed to?
RelationshipCan be reliable but not valid (consistently wrong)Validity requires reliability, not vice versa
The tell: Consistency = reliability; accuracy/measures-the-right-thing = validity. Same spot every time = reliable; the bullseye = valid.
Independent variable vs Dependent variable

Same: both are the variables defined in an experiment.

Independent (IV)Dependent (DV)
RoleManipulated — the presumed causeMeasured — the outcome (depends on the IV)
The tell: What the experimenter changes = IV; what they measure as the result = DV.

6. Abnormal / clinical

Delusion vs Hallucination

Same: both are psychotic symptoms; both appear in schizophrenia.

DelusionHallucination
Disturbance ofThought content — a false, fixed beliefPerception — a false sensory experience
Example"The FBI is tracking me"Hearing voices with no one there
The tell: A wrong belief (delusion) vs a wrong perception (hallucination). If there's a sensory experience, it's a hallucination.
Illusion vs Hallucination

Same: both are perceptual disturbances.

IllusionHallucination
External stimulus?Yes — a real stimulus is misperceivedNo — perception with no stimulus at all
ExampleA shadow looks like an intruderSeeing a person who isn't there
The tell: Is there a real object being misread (illusion) or nothing there to begin with (hallucination)?
Obsession vs Compulsion

Same: both are core to OCD; both are distressing and unwanted.

ObsessionCompulsion
TypeIntrusive thought, image or urge (mental)Repetitive act or ritual (behaviour)
FunctionCauses the anxietyDone to relieve the anxiety
The tell: The thought that intrudes (obsession) vs the act done to neutralise it (compulsion). Hand-washing = compulsion; the fear of germs = obsession.
Affect vs Mood

Same: both describe emotional state in a mental status exam.

AffectMood
TimescaleMomentary, observable expressionSustained, pervasive feeling
AnalogyThe weatherThe climate
The tell: Short-term, outwardly observed = affect; sustained, subjectively reported = mood.
Delirium vs Dementia

Same: both cause cognitive impairment and confusion, often in older patients.

DeliriumDementia
OnsetAcute, fluctuating (hours–days)Gradual, progressive (months–years)
ConsciousnessClouded, attention impairedClear early; memory leads
Reversible?Usually yesUsually no
The tell: Sudden onset + fluctuating + altered consciousness = delirium. Slow, progressive, alert = dementia.
Neurosis vs Psychosis

Same: both are older umbrella categories of mental disorder.

NeurosisPsychosis
Reality contactIntact — insight presentLost — no insight
ExamplesAnxiety, phobia, OCDSchizophrenia, delusional disorder
The tell: Is reality testing intact with insight (neurosis) or broken (psychosis)? Delusions/hallucinations → psychosis.
Positive vs Negative symptoms (schizophrenia)

Same: both are symptom clusters of schizophrenia.

PositiveNegative
NatureAdded to normal experienceLoss / absence of normal function
ExamplesHallucinations, delusions, disorganised speechFlat affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia
The tell: Excess / something added = positive; deficit / something missing = negative.

7. Defense mechanisms

Repression vs Suppression

Same: both push distressing material out of awareness.

RepressionSuppression
AwarenessUnconscious, involuntaryConscious, deliberate
The tell: Done unknowingly (repression) vs on purpose (suppression). "Decided not to think about it" = suppression.
Displacement vs Sublimation

Same: both redirect an unacceptable impulse onto a new target.

DisplacementSublimation
New targetA safer but still similar targetA socially valued, productive outlet
ExampleYell at the kids instead of the bossChannel aggression into sport or surgery
The tell: Just a safer target (displacement) vs a constructive, socially-approved channel (sublimation). Sublimation is the mature one.
Projection vs Reaction formation

Same: both defend against one's own unacceptable feelings.

ProjectionReaction formation
StrategyAttribute your impulse to someone elseAct the opposite of your impulse
Example"He hates me" (when you hate him)Hating someone → being overly nice to them
The tell: Pin the feeling on someone else (projection) vs flip to the opposite behaviour (reaction formation).

8. Therapy

Transference vs Countertransference

Same: both are displaced feelings inside the psychodynamic therapeutic relationship.

TransferenceCountertransference
Whose feelingsThe client's, redirected onto the therapistThe therapist's, redirected onto the client
The tell: Client → therapist = transference. Therapist → client = countertransference.

9. Social

Conformity vs Compliance vs Obedience

Same: all three are behaviour change due to social influence.

ConformityComplianceObedience
Source of pressureImplicit group normA direct requestAn order from authority
StudyAsch (lines)Foot-in-the-doorMilgram (shocks)
The tell: Group norm with no one asking (conformity) vs someone requests (compliance) vs an authority commands (obedience).
Stereotype vs Prejudice vs Discrimination

Same: all three are components of intergroup bias and constantly swapped in options.

StereotypePrejudiceDiscrimination
Component (ABC)Cognition — a beliefAffect — a negative attitudeBehaviour — an action
The tell: Thought = stereotype, feeling = prejudice, action = discrimination. Map it to Affect / Behaviour / Cognition.

10. Intelligence

Fluid intelligence vs Crystallised intelligence (Cattell)

Same: both are Cattell's components of general intelligence (Gf–Gc).

Fluid (Gf)Crystallised (Gc)
What it isReasoning with novel problems, independent of knowledgeAccumulated knowledge, vocabulary, learned skills
With ageDeclines earlierHolds up / keeps rising
The tell: On-the-spot novel reasoning (fluid) vs stored, learned knowledge (crystallised). If age makes it better, it's crystallised.
