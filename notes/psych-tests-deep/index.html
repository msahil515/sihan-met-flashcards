Personality & Projective Tests, Deep Reference

Built 2026-05-19 after you flagged that the test entries on the assessment cheatsheet were too thin. This page does one job: for every personality + projective + Beck/Bell inventory in the MET/NIMHANS pool, give you item count, scales/subscales, scoring, author + year, and use case in one screen. Memorize the numbers in bold, the rest is texture. Cross-link with the read-first primer for reliability/validity and the revision pack for the one-page table.

How MCQ-writers attack this section. Almost every question is one of four moves: (1) swap the item count between two tests, (2) swap the author or year, (3) ask which scale belongs to which test, (4) ask which test is projective vs objective. So your job here is to lock the quadruple (test → items → scales → author/year) cold. Get those four right and you bank the marks.

1. MMPI Family (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory)

MMPI (original, 1943)

  • Authors: Starke R. Hathaway (psychologist) & J. Charnley McKinley (psychiatrist), University of Minnesota.
  • Year: 1943.
  • Items: 566 true/false statements (often reported as 550 unique + 16 repeats; the canonical number you quote is 566).
  • Method of construction: empirical criterion-keyed. Items were retained only if they discriminated a diagnostic group from normals — not based on face validity. This was the breakthrough method versus older content-based inventories.
  • Original norming sample: "Minnesota normals" (724 visitors to the Univ. of Minnesota Hospitals) — later criticised as a narrow, mostly white rural Midwest base, which drove the MMPI-2 restandardisation.

MMPI-2 (the version exam-writers mean by default)

  • Year: 1989 (restandardisation), revised manual 2001.
  • Items: 567 true/false items. Memorize this exact number.
  • Age range: 18+ years.
  • Reading level: 6th grade.
  • Administration time: 60–90 minutes.
  • Scoring: scores converted to T-scores (mean 50, SD 10). Clinically significant elevation: T ≥ 65 on MMPI-2 (was T ≥ 70 on original MMPI).

10 clinical scales (memorize the order, scale 5 and 0 are the supplementary ones):

#CodeNameWhat it picks up
1HsHypochondriasisBodily preoccupation, somatic complaints
2DDepressionSadness, hopelessness, low morale
3HyHysteriaConversion features, somatic + denial
4PdPsychopathic DeviateAuthority conflict, antisocial features
5MfMasculinity-FemininityGender-role interest pattern
6PaParanoiaSuspiciousness, ideas of reference
7PtPsychastheniaAnxiety, obsessional rumination, doubt

Validity scales (the L/F/K triad is the high-yield bit):

  • ? (Cannot Say): number of unanswered items. > 30 omissions invalidates profile.
  • L (Lie): 15 items detecting naive "faking good" — claiming impossibly virtuous behaviour ("I have never told a lie"). High L = unsophisticated impression management.
  • F (Infrequency): 60 items rarely endorsed by normals. High F = random responding, malingering, or genuine severe pathology. Cannot distinguish those without further evidence.
  • K (Correction): 30 items measuring subtle defensiveness. Used as a correction factor added to 5 clinical scales (1, 4, 7, 8, 9) to compensate for guardedness.
  • VRIN (Variable Response Inconsistency): inconsistent answers to similar items → random responding.
  • TRIN (True Response Inconsistency): all-true or all-false bias.
  • FB (Back F): infrequency on later items, catches fatigue or "give-up" responding.
  • Fp (F-Psychopathology): infrequency among psychiatric inpatients; helps separate malingering from genuine pathology.

Interpretation logic. Profile is plotted with all scales on one graph in T-score units. Clinicians read code types (e.g., "2-7/7-2" = elevations on Depression + Psychasthenia → classic anxious-depressed picture; "4-9" = antisocial acting-out). Single-scale interpretation is discouraged.

MMPI-2-RF (Restructured Form, 2008)

  • Items: 338.
  • Scales: 9 Restructured Clinical (RC) scales + a demoralization scale (RCd) + higher-order scales. The "common variance" of demoralization is pulled out so each RC scale measures something more specific.
  • Use: shorter, psychometrically tighter version. Increasingly the research default but MET still tests the classic MMPI-2 numbers.

MMPI-3 (2020)

  • Items: 335. New norms.
  • Scales: 52 total — H-O scales, RC scales, Specific Problem (SP) scales, PSY-5 scales.

MMPI-A (Adolescent)

  • Items: 478. Ages 14–18. Released 1992. There is also an MMPI-A-RF (2016, 241 items).

2. 16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire)

  • Author: Raymond B. Cattell (with Tatsuoka & Eber on later editions).
  • Year: first edition 1949. Current edition is the 16PF Fifth Edition (1993).
  • Items: 185 items (5th edition). Each item is 3-choice: agree / ? / disagree, except the reasoning subtest which is forced-choice.
  • Age range: 16+ years.
  • Administration time: 35–50 minutes.
  • Method of construction: factor analysis of the lexicon. Cattell started with Allport & Odbert's list of ~4500 trait words, reduced to ~160 surface traits, then factor-analysed to 16 source traits.
  • Scoring: raw scores → Sten scores (Standard Ten: mean 5.5, SD 2, range 1–10). Sten 1–3 = low pole, 4–7 = average, 8–10 = high pole.

5 global factors (second-order, conceptually close to the Big Five): Extraversion, Anxiety, Tough-Mindedness, Independence, Self-Control.

Validity indices: Impression Management (IM), Acquiescence (ACQ), Infrequency (INF).

Use case: normal-population personality assessment for selection (industrial/organisational), career counselling, couples/family counselling. Not a clinical screening tool.

3. NEO Personality Inventory (Five-Factor Model)

  • Authors: Paul T. Costa Jr. & Robert R. McCrae (NIH/National Institute on Aging).
  • Year: NEO-PI 1985 → NEO-PI-R 1992 → NEO-PI-3 2010.
  • Items: 240 items (NEO-PI-R and NEO-PI-3). 5-point Likert (Strongly disagree → Strongly agree). 8 items per facet × 30 facets.
  • NEO-FFI / NEO-FFI-3: short form, 60 items, gives the 5 domain scores only (no facets). Useful when time is tight.
  • Age range: 17+ (NEO-PI-3 extended down to age 12). Forms S (self-report) and R (observer rating).

5 domains × 6 facets (memorize the mnemonic OCEAN, plus at least two facets per domain):

DomainFacets
O — Openness to ExperienceFantasy, Aesthetics, Feelings, Actions, Ideas, Values
C — ConscientiousnessCompetence, Order, Dutifulness, Achievement Striving, Self-Discipline, Deliberation
E — ExtraversionWarmth, Gregariousness, Assertiveness, Activity, Excitement-Seeking, Positive Emotions
A — AgreeablenessTrust, Straightforwardness, Altruism, Compliance, Modesty, Tender-Mindedness
N — NeuroticismAnxiety, Angry Hostility, Depression, Self-Consciousness, Impulsiveness, Vulnerability

Scoring: raw → T-scores. No validity scales in the classical sense (NEO assumes honest responding), though item-position consistency checks exist.

Use case: non-clinical research, occupational selection, characterising personality structure for therapy planning. The dominant trait model in academic psychology since the 1990s.

4. EPQ / EPQ-R (Eysenck Personality Questionnaire)

  • Authors: Hans J. Eysenck & Sybil B. G. Eysenck.
  • Lineage: Maudsley Medical Questionnaire (1952) → Maudsley Personality Inventory (MPI, 1959) → Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI, 1964) → EPQ (1975) → EPQ-R (1985) → EPQ-R Short (1992).
  • Items: EPQ 90 items; EPQ-R 100 items; EPQ-R Short 48 items. Yes/No format.
  • Age range: adult. Junior EPQ for ages 7–15.

Three (later four) PEN dimensions:

  • P — Psychoticism: tough-mindedness, aggression, low empathy. Added in 1975 (the move from EPI to EPQ).
  • E — Extraversion: sociability, impulsivity, sensation-seeking.
  • N — Neuroticism: emotional reactivity, anxiety.
  • L — Lie scale: social desirability.

Theoretical anchor: Eysenck linked each dimension to biology — Extraversion to cortical arousal (introverts have higher baseline arousal), Neuroticism to limbic/ANS reactivity, Psychoticism to dopaminergic function. This is what makes EPQ a "biologically grounded" trait theory and is a common MCQ pairing (Eysenck = biology of personality).

Use case: personality research, especially anything testing the arousal theory of extraversion. Clinical screening is secondary.

5. MBTI (Myers-Briggs Type Indicator)

  • Authors: Katharine Cook Briggs & her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers. Built on Carl Jung's Psychological Types (1921).
  • Year: first version 1944; commercial release 1962 (Educational Testing Service); current MBTI Form M (1998).
  • Items: Form M = 93 forced-choice items, Form Q = 144 items (adds 20 facets). Form G (older) had 126 items.
  • Age range: 14+.

Four dichotomies → 16 types:

  • E vs I — Extraversion / Introversion (energy direction).
  • S vs N — Sensing / iNtuition (information style).
  • T vs F — Thinking / Feeling (decision style).
  • J vs P — Judging / Perceiving (lifestyle / outer-world orientation).

Scoring: forced-choice → preference score on each dichotomy → categorical type (e.g., INTJ, ESFP). Critically, scores are not dimensional; the test claims discrete types. This is the main psychometric criticism of MBTI: traits are normally distributed, dichotomisation throws away information, and test-retest reliability for type assignment is mediocre (~50% of takers get a different type on retest within weeks).

Use case: career counselling, team building, coaching. Not recommended for clinical diagnosis or selection. Often invoked in MET as a contrast case: "Which test claims discrete types instead of continuous traits?" → MBTI.

6. CPI (California Psychological Inventory)

  • Author: Harrison Gough.
  • Year: 1957 → CPI-434 (1996) → CPI-260 short form.
  • Items: 434 (full) / 260 (short). True-false.
  • Scales: 20 "folk-concept" scales (Dominance, Sociability, Empathy, Independence, etc.) grouped under 4 broad themes: Interpersonal, Self-management, Achievement, Personal Style. Also 3 vector scales.
  • Use case: normal populations — explicitly the non-clinical complement to MMPI. Used in selection, counselling, leadership development.

7. TAT (Thematic Apperception Test)

  • Authors: Henry A. Murray & Christiana D. Morgan, Harvard Psychological Clinic.
  • Year: 1935. Manual published 1943.
  • Stimuli: 31 picture cards total — 30 black-and-white ambiguous scenes + 1 blank card. 20 cards are administered per subject, selected by age (adult / child) and sex (M / F). Cards are labelled with these codes (e.g., 3BM = card 3 for Boys + Men).
  • Procedure: subject is asked to make up a story for each picture — what led up to the scene, what is happening now, what the characters are thinking and feeling, and how it will end. Stories are recorded verbatim.
  • Theoretical basis: projective hypothesis — when stimulus is ambiguous, the subject "projects" their dominant needs (achievement, affiliation, power, etc.) and the environmental press they perceive. Built directly on Murray's needs-press theory of personality (Explorations in Personality, 1938).
  • Scoring systems:
    • Murray's original system: code needs and press in each story.
    • SCORS (Social Cognition and Object Relations Scale): Westen, 1985 — 8 dimensions of object relations.
    • Defense Mechanism Manual (DMM): Cramer.
    • McClelland's nAch scoring: used in achievement-motivation research.
  • Indian adaptation: Indian TAT by Uma Choudhury (1960) — culture-adapted scenes with Indian figures.
  • Use case: uncovering unconscious motives, conflicts, defenses, and interpersonal schemata. Common in clinical assessment, achievement-motivation research, and forensic work.

8. Rorschach Inkblot Test

  • Author: Hermann Rorschach (Swiss psychiatrist).
  • Year: published Psychodiagnostik in 1921. Rorschach died in 1922 at 37, before he could refine the system.
  • Stimuli: 10 inkblot cards. 5 are achromatic (black + grey: cards I, IV, V, VI, VII), 2 are black + red (cards II, III), and 3 are fully chromatic (cards VIII, IX, X). Often summarised as "5 black/grey, 2 red-and-black, 3 colour" or simply "5 chromatic + 5 achromatic" in shorthand.
  • Administration: two phases. (1) Free Association / Response phase: "What might this be?" (2) Inquiry phase: clinician asks where on the blot the subject saw it and what features (form, colour, shading, movement) made it look that way.

Scoring — Exner Comprehensive System (1974), the dominant system till 2011:

  • Location: W (whole blot), D (common detail), Dd (unusual detail), S (white space).
  • Determinants — what made it look like that:
    • F = form / shape.
    • M = human movement; FM = animal movement; m = inanimate movement.
    • C = pure colour; FC = form-dominated colour; CF = colour-dominated form. (Form-colour ratio is a classic Rorschach inference about emotional control.)
    • C' = achromatic colour. T = texture (shading). V = vista (shading-depth). Y = diffuse shading.
    • FD = form-dimension. (2) = pair. r = reflection.
  • Content: H (human), Hd (human detail), A (animal), Ad (animal detail), An (anatomy), Bl (blood), Sx (sex), Xy (X-ray), etc.
  • Popularity: P = popular / common response (e.g., bat or butterfly on card V).
  • Form quality: +, o (ordinary), u (unusual), - (minus — distorted).

Key indices Exner derived:

  • Erlebnistypus (EB) = M : sum-C. Introversive (M-dominated) vs Extratensive (C-dominated) vs Ambitent.
  • Lambda (L): ratio of pure-form responses to all others. High L = constricted, defensive responding.
  • Egocentricity Index: (reflections + pairs) / R.
  • Schizophrenia Index (SCZI), Depression Index (DEPI), Coping Deficit Index (CDI), Suicide Constellation (S-CON).

R-PAS (Rorschach Performance Assessment System), 2011: Meyer, Viglione, Mihura, Erard, & Erdberg. Updates Exner's norms, drops weakly-validated indices, standardises optimal response count (R-Optimised). Increasingly the research standard, but MET still tests Exner.

Use case: personality structure, thought disorder screening, object relations, defensive style. Use heavily debated in evidence-based practice — Mihura et al.'s 2013 meta-analysis supported a subset of variables, criticised others.

9. Bell's Adjustment Inventory

  • Author: Hugh M. Bell.
  • Year: Student form 1934; Adult form 1938; Indian adaptations by R. K. Ojha (Hindi) and others.
  • Items: 140 items (Student form). Adult form has 160 items. Yes/No/? format.
  • Age range: Student form for high school + college students; Adult form for general adult population.

Areas of adjustment scored (Student form measures 4; Adult form adds Occupational):

  • Home adjustment — relationships with family.
  • Health adjustment — somatic preoccupation, physical wellbeing.
  • Social adjustment — submissiveness vs aggressiveness in social situations.
  • Emotional adjustment — stability vs instability of mood.
  • Occupational adjustment (Adult form only) — satisfaction and adjustment to work.

Scoring: higher score = poorer adjustment in that area. Total adjustment score is the sum across areas. Score brackets are labelled excellent / good / average / unsatisfactory / very unsatisfactory.

Use case: screening for adjustment problems in school counselling, college counselling centres, and Indian educational/research settings (very commonly used in Indian masters dissertations — that is why it shows up on MET despite being old).

10. Beck Inventories

All authored or co-authored by Aaron T. Beck (often with colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania Centre for Cognitive Therapy). Memorize each one's item count and 0–3 Likert scoring; they all share the same response-scale skeleton.

BDI / BDI-II — Beck Depression Inventory

  • BDI: 1961 (Beck, Ward, Mendelson, Mock, Erbaugh). 21 items, 0–3 each.
  • BDI-II: 1996. 21 items, self-report, each rated 0–3. Total 0–63. Items revised to match DSM-IV depressive criteria (added agitation, worthlessness, concentration difficulty; removed body image, work difficulty, weight loss, somatic preoccupation).
  • Time frame: past 2 weeks.
  • Cutoffs: 0–13 minimal, 14–19 mild, 20–28 moderate, 29–63 severe.
  • Reading level: 5th–6th grade.
  • Use: screening, severity tracking. Not diagnostic on its own.

BAI — Beck Anxiety Inventory

  • Year: 1988 (Beck, Epstein, Brown, Steer).
  • Items: 21 items, 0–3, total 0–63. Time frame: past week.
  • Cutoffs: 0–7 minimal, 8–15 mild, 16–25 moderate, 26–63 severe.
  • Design intent: measure anxiety symptoms with minimal overlap with depression. BAI deliberately loads on somatic anxiety items (sweating, dizziness, racing heart) rather than mood items.

BHS — Beck Hopelessness Scale

  • Year: 1974.
  • Items: 20 true/false items.
  • Use: measures pessimism about the future. Strongly predictive of eventual suicide (more predictive than depression severity alone). Cutoff ≥ 9 flags elevated risk.

BSS / BSI — Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation

  • Year: 1979 (clinician version), 1991 (self-report version).
  • Items: 21 items, 0–2 each. First 5 screen for desire to live/die and active/passive ideation; if any are positive, complete the remaining 14 detail items.
  • Use: assessment of suicidal ideation, intent, and planning over the past week.

BYI — Beck Youth Inventories

  • Year: 2001, second edition 2005. Ages 7–18.
  • Items: 5 scales × 20 items each = 100 items: Depression, Anxiety, Anger, Disruptive Behavior, Self-Concept.

Other Beck instruments worth recognising

  • BSSI (Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation) — see above.
  • Clark-Beck Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory (CBOCI): 25 items.
  • Beck Self-Concept Test.

14. Top MCQ Traps (do not lose these marks)

  1. MMPI-2 = 567 items, 10 clinical scales, 3+ validity scales (L, F, K). Don't confuse with 16PF's 16 factors.
  2. MMPI-2 uses T-scores (mean 50, SD 10). Clinical cutoff T ≥ 65 (was 70 on original MMPI). Sten is 16PF, not MMPI.
  3. L scale = naive faking-good; F scale = exaggeration / random / severe pathology; K scale = subtle defensiveness. Don't swap them.
  4. 16PF = 185 items, 16 primary factors, 5 globals, Sten scores, Cattell. Big Five (OCEAN) ≠ 16PF — Big Five is Costa & McCrae.
  5. NEO-PI-R / NEO-PI-3 = 240 items. NEO-FFI = 60. Don't confuse the long and short forms.
  6. NEO has 5 domains × 6 facets = 30 facets. 16PF has 16 factors × 0 facets but 5 globals.
  7. EPQ-R = 100 items. PEN model: Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism + Lie. Psychoticism was the addition that made EPI → EPQ in 1975.
  8. MBTI = forced-choice, type-based (16 types), 4 dichotomies (E/I, S/N, T/F, J/P). Jung-derived. Common criticism: dichotomisation, poor retest reliability.
  9. TAT = 31 cards total, 20 administered. Murray & Morgan, 1935. Theory: needs-press.
  10. Rorschach = 10 inkblots. Hermann Rorschach, 1921. Not 20, not 12. Scored by Exner system → R-PAS (2011).
  11. CAT = Bellak, 1949, 10 cards. Animal version for kids; human version exists too.
  12. Bell's Adjustment Inventory: Student form = 140 items, 4 areas (Home, Health, Social, Emotional). Adult form = 160 items, adds Occupational.
  13. BDI-II = 21 items, 0–3, total 0–63, past 2 weeks. BAI = 21 items, past week. BHS = 20 T/F. BSS = 21 items, 0–2.
  14. BHS predicts suicide better than BDI severity alone — hopelessness is the active risk factor.
  15. Exner determinants letters: M = human movement, FM = animal movement, m = inanimate movement, F = form, C = colour. Common trick: FC vs CF (which one is form-dominated → FC).
  16. Rosenzweig P-F scores aggression direction (extra/intro/im-punitive) × type — not needs-press (that's TAT).
  17. Holtzman Inkblot Technique = 45 cards, 1 response each, designed to fix Rorschach's reliability problems.
  18. CPI is for normal populations (Gough's "folk concepts"). MMPI is for clinical populations. Easy to swap.
  19. Indian TAT is by Uma Choudhury (1960). Indian WAIS/WISC adaptation is MISIC (Malin). Bhatia Battery is by C. M. Bhatia (1955). Don't confuse the Indian adaptations.
  20. Allport & Odbert → ~4500 trait words is the starting point Cattell factor-analysed down to 16PF. Common stem.
