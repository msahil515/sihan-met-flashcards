How social psych is tested. (1) Describe a scene → name the effect/technique ("the more people watched, the less anyone helped" = bystander). (2) Name a theory → who proposed it (dissonance = Festinger). (3) Tell the rival theories apart (FAE vs actor-observer, foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face). Learn each in its contrast pair, that's where the distractor lives.

1. Social cognition (how we think about the social world)

TermWhoWhat it is
Heuristics (availability, representativeness, anchoring)Tversky & KahnemanMental shortcuts: judge by ease of recall / by resemblance to a prototype / by clinging to a first value. Fuller list on the Phenomena page.
SchemaBartlett (social use: Fiske & Taylor)Mental framework that guides what we notice, encode and recall about people/situations.
Priming—A recently activated schema shapes the next interpretation, even unconsciously.
Self-fulfilling prophecyRobert MertonAn expectation makes itself come true by changing behaviour (basis of the Pygmalion effect).
Counterfactual thinkingKahneman & TverskyImagining "what might have been"; near-misses sting most (the bronze medallist is happier than the silver).
Optimistic / unrealistic-optimism biasNeil WeinsteinWe think bad things are less likely to happen to us than to others.
Negativity bias—Negative info weighs more heavily than equally strong positive info.
Planning fallacyKahneman & TverskyWe chronically underestimate how long our own tasks will take.
Magical thinking—Assumptions that defy causality (law of contagion / similarity; won't eat fudge shaped like dog faeces).

2. Social perception & attribution

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Attribution theoryFritz HeiderWe explain behaviour as internal (disposition) or external (situation); "naïve psychologist."
Correspondent inference theoryJones & DavisWe infer a trait from acts that are freely chosen, unexpected and low in social desirability.
Covariation modelHarold KelleyAttribution uses consensus, consistency & distinctiveness (high all three → situation).
Fundamental attribution error (correspondence bias)coined by Lee RossOver-blaming others' character, under-weighting their situation.
Actor–observer effectJones & NisbettMy acts = situation; your acts = your character.
Self-serving bias—My success = me; my failure = bad luck/situation.
Just-world hypothesisMelvin LernerBelief that people get what they deserve → blaming the victim.
Facial-feedback hypothesisTomkins; EkmanYour facial expression feeds back to intensify/trigger the felt emotion.
Thin slicesAmbady & RosenthalSurprisingly accurate judgements from very brief (seconds) exposure to behaviour.
The attribution swap: FAE = judging others (blame the person). Actor–observer = the gap between how you explain your own vs others' acts. Self-serving = how you explain your own outcomes (take credit, dodge blame). All three look alike on a stem — read whose behaviour and whose outcome it's about.

3. The self

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Social comparison theoryLeon FestingerWe evaluate ourselves by comparison to others (upward vs downward comparison).
Self-perception theoryDaryl BemWhen attitudes are weak, we infer them by watching our own behaviour.
Self-discrepancy theoryE. Tory HigginsActual vs ideal self → dejection (sadness); actual vs ought self → agitation (anxiety).
Self-monitoringMark SnyderHigh self-monitors tailor behaviour to the situation; low ones act from inner attitudes.
Self-efficacyAlbert BanduraBelief in your capacity to carry out the actions a task needs.
Self-handicappingBerglas & JonesSetting up an excuse in advance (not studying) to protect self-esteem from failure.
Self-verificationWilliam SwannWe seek feedback that confirms our existing self-view, even if negative.
Self-affirmation theoryClaude SteeleAffirming a valued part of the self buffers threats to self-integrity.
Spotlight effectThomas GilovichOverestimating how much others notice/judge us.
BIRGing (basking in reflected glory)Robert Cialdini"We won" — claiming a winning group to boost self-image (CORFing = distancing from losers).
Terror management theoryGreenberg, Pyszczynski & SolomonAwareness of mortality drives us to defend our cultural worldview and self-esteem.

4. Attitudes & persuasion

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Cognitive dissonanceLeon FestingerTension from clashing cognitions drives attitude change to reduce it.
Insufficient justification / induced complianceFestinger & CarlsmithThe $1 group changed their attitude more than the $20 group (less reward = more dissonance).
Effort justificationAronson & MillsThe harder the initiation, the more we value the group we joined.
Balance theoryFritz HeiderWe seek consistency among our liking relationships (P-O-X triangles).
Elaboration Likelihood ModelPetty & CacioppoPersuasion via the central route (thoughtful, when motivated/able) vs peripheral route (cues).
Heuristic-Systematic ModelShelly ChaikenSame dual-route idea: systematic (effortful) vs heuristic (rules-of-thumb) processing.
Theory of reasoned action / planned behaviourFishbein & AjzenIntention (from attitudes + norms + perceived control) predicts behaviour.
Yale attitude-change approachCarl HovlandWho says what to whom: source, message, audience factors in persuasion.
Inoculation theoryWilliam McGuireA weak counter-argument first "vaccinates" an attitude against later strong attacks.
Psychological reactanceJack BrehmThreaten a freedom → people want the forbidden thing more (boomerang).
LaPiere studyRichard LaPiereThe classic attitude–behaviour gap: stated prejudice didn't match actual service to a Chinese couple.
Dissonance vs self-perception: both predict attitude follows behaviour. Festinger (dissonance) = there's arousal/discomfort when the attitude is strong. Bem (self-perception) = no arousal, you just infer the attitude — applies when the attitude was weak/unclear.

5. Stereotyping, prejudice & discrimination

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Social identity theoryTajfel & TurnerGroup membership feeds self-esteem → in-group favouritism, out-group bias.
Minimal group paradigmHenri TajfelEven arbitrary, meaningless group splits produce in-group favouritism.
Realistic conflict theoryMuzafer SherifCompetition over scarce resources breeds intergroup hostility (Robbers Cave); superordinate goals reduce it.
Authoritarian personalityTheodor AdornoA rigid, submissive-to-authority personality (F-scale) prone to prejudice.
Scapegoat theory(frustration–aggression roots)Frustration is displaced onto a weaker out-group blamed for problems.
Contact hypothesisGordon AllportContact reduces prejudice if there's equal status, common goals, cooperation & institutional support.
Jigsaw classroomElliot AronsonCooperative learning where each pupil holds one piece → forces interdependence, cuts prejudice.
Stereotype threatSteele & AronsonFear of confirming a negative stereotype about your group depresses your performance.
Illusory correlationHamilton & GiffordSeeing a link between minority membership and rare (negative) behaviour that isn't really there.
Out-group homogeneity effect—"They're all alike"; we see less variability in out-groups than our own.
Stereotype content modelSusan FiskeGroups judged on warmth × competence → four emotions (pride, envy, pity, contempt).

6. Interpersonal attraction, relationships & love

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Propinquity (proximity) effectFestinger, Schachter & BackThe closer people live/sit, the more likely they form friendships (Westgate housing study).
Mere-exposure effectRobert ZajoncRepeated exposure alone increases liking.
Similarity–attractionDonn ByrneWe like those with similar attitudes (not "opposites attract"); the law of attraction.
Matching hypothesisWalster & BerscheidWe pair with partners of roughly our own level of physical attractiveness.
"What is beautiful is good"Dion, Berscheid & WalsterPhysical-attractiveness stereotype: we assume good-looking people have better traits.
Triangular theory of loveRobert SternbergIntimacy + passion + commitment combine into 7 love types (consummate = all three).
Passionate vs companionate loveHatfield & BerscheidIntense arousal/longing vs deep affection & trust that lasts.
Two-factor theory of love (misattribution of arousal)Dutton & AronArousal from a scary suspension bridge gets relabelled as attraction.
Adult attachment stylesHazan & ShaverSecure / anxious-ambivalent / avoidant carried from infancy into romantic love.
Social exchange theoryThibaut & KelleyRelationships weighed as rewards minus costs vs a comparison level (and CL-alt).
Equity theoryWalster / AdamsWe're happiest when our ratio of inputs to outcomes matches the partner's.
Investment modelCaryl RusbultCommitment = satisfaction + investment size − quality of alternatives.
The "who studied liking" cluster: Byrne = similarity, Zajonc = mere exposure, Festinger = propinquity, Sternberg = triangular love, Rusbult = investment model, Dutton & Aron = misattributed arousal (bridge). These names rotate as each other's distractors.

7. Social influence (conformity, compliance, obedience)

TermWhoWhat it is
ConformitySolomon AschGoing along with a unanimous group's wrong answer (line-judging task).
Norm formationMuzafer SherifUsed the autokinetic illusion to show groups converge on a shared norm.
Informational vs normative influenceDeutsch & GerardConform to be right (info) vs to be liked/accepted (normative).
Obedience to authorityStanley Milgram65% delivered "lethal" shocks when an authority ordered it.
Six principles of influenceRobert CialdiniReciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity.
Foot-in-the-doorFreedman & FraserSmall request first → bigger one later gets agreed to (consistency).
Door-in-the-faceRobert CialdiniBig request refused first → the smaller real one looks reasonable (reciprocal concession).
LowballingRobert CialdiniGet a commitment, then raise the cost; people stick to the deal.
That's-not-all techniqueJerry BurgerAdd a bonus/drop the price before the person decides → boosts compliance.
Norm of reciprocityAlvin GouldnerWe feel obliged to return favours (engine behind door-in-the-face).
Minority influenceSerge MoscoviciA consistent minority can shift the majority (via genuine conversion, not just compliance).
Foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face: FITD = small → big (Freedman & Fraser, consistency). DITF = big → small (Cialdini, reciprocal concession). Lowballing = change the deal after you've agreed. Examiners love swapping the direction.

8. Prosocial behaviour (helping)

TermWhoWhat it is
Bystander effect & 5-step decision modelLatané & DarleyMore witnesses → less help. Steps: notice → interpret as emergency → take responsibility → know how → decide to act. (After Kitty Genovese.)
Diffusion of responsibilityDarley & Latané"Someone else will do it" — responsibility spreads thin in a crowd.
Pluralistic ignorance—Everyone stays calm because no one else looks worried, so no one reads it as an emergency.
Empathy–altruism hypothesisC. Daniel BatsonFeeling empathy produces genuinely selfless helping (not just self-relief).
Negative-state relief modelRobert CialdiniWe help to relieve our own bad mood — helping is egoistic.
Arousal: cost–reward modelPiliavin et al.A bystander's unpleasant arousal motivates help when costs of helping are low.
Kin selection / reciprocal altruismHamilton; TriversEvolutionary: help relatives (shared genes) or those likely to return the favour.
Norm of social responsibility—We should help those who depend on us, regardless of return.
Why we help — the egoism vs altruism split: Batson = true altruism (empathy). Cialdini (negative-state relief) and Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward) = we ultimately help to fix our own discomfort. That contrast is the question.

9. Aggression

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Frustration–aggression hypothesisDollard, Doob, Miller et al.Frustration (blocked goal) always breeds aggression; aggression always traces to frustration.
Reformulated frustration–aggressionLeonard BerkowitzFrustration creates a readiness; aggression needs aggressive cues + negative affect.
Weapons effectBerkowitz & LePageMere presence of a weapon increases aggression (an aggressive cue).
Social learning of aggressionAlbert BanduraAggression is learned by observing & imitating models (Bobo doll).
General Aggression Model (GAM)Anderson & BushmanPerson + situation inputs → arousal/affect/cognition → appraisal → aggressive act.
Excitation-transfer theoryDolf ZillmannLeft-over arousal from one source intensifies aggression in a later, unrelated situation.
Cognitive-neoassociationLeonard BerkowitzAversive events trigger negative affect that primes a network of anger/escape responses.
Catharsis hypothesis(Freud / Lorenz roots)The idea that venting reduces aggression — largely unsupported; venting often raises it.

10. Groups & individuals

TermWhoWhat it is
Social facilitationTriplett; ZajoncAn audience boosts performance on easy/well-learned tasks, hurts hard ones (Zajonc: arousal strengthens the dominant response).
Evaluation apprehensionNickolas CottrellIt's the fear of being judged, not mere presence, that drives social facilitation.
Social loafingLatané, Williams & HarkinsPeople exert less effort in a group when their output isn't individually identifiable (Ringelmann roots).
DeindividuationFestinger; ZimbardoAnonymity in a crowd → loss of self-awareness and restraint (Stanford prison).
Group polarisationMoscovici & ZavalloniDiscussion pushes a group to a more extreme version of its initial leaning.
Risky shiftJames StonerEarly name for polarisation: groups often decide riskier than individuals.
GroupthinkIrving JanisA cohesive group suppresses dissent for consensus → poor decisions (Bay of Pigs).
Tragedy of the commons / social dilemmaGarrett HardinIndividually rational self-interest depletes a shared resource everyone needs.
GRITCharles OsgoodGraduated Reciprocated Initiatives in Tension-reduction: small unilateral concessions de-escalate conflict.
Facilitation vs loafing: Social facilitation = others present make you try harder (you're being watched/evaluated individually). Social loafing = others present make you try less (your effort is pooled and hidden). The deciding variable is whether your individual output is identifiable.
