0. Social psychology as a science

What the field actually claims. Social psychology is the scientific study of how the thoughts, feelings and behaviour of individuals are influenced by the actual, imagined or implied presence of others (Allport's classic definition). The key word is scientific: it does not settle questions by intuition or anecdote, it runs studies. And the key insight, repeated throughout Baron, is the power of the situation: ordinary people do extraordinary things (help, conform, obey, aggress) far more because of where they are and who is around than because of fixed traits. That theme, situation over disposition, returns in obedience (Milgram), bystander apathy (Latané & Darley) and the attribution chapter (the FAE is precisely our failure to see it).

The role of theory. A theory is an integrated set of principles that explains and predicts observed events. Good social psychology runs a loop: a theory generates a specific, falsifiable hypothesis; a study tests it; results refine or refute the theory. A theory is never "proven", only supported or disconfirmed, and it earns its keep by making predictions that could have failed but did not.

The three core research methods

Baron's methods chapter contrasts three ways to gather evidence, each with a trade-off between control and realism.

MethodWhat you doStrength / limit
ExperimentalManipulate one factor (the independent variable, IV), hold everything else constant, measure the effect on the dependent variable, DV. Use random assignment to conditions.The only method that establishes cause and effect. Limit: artificiality, low external validity if over-controlled.
CorrelationalMeasure two or more variables as they naturally occur and see if they covary (correlation coefficient, −1 to +1).Good for real-world variables you cannot ethically manipulate. Limit: correlation ≠ causation (direction problem + third-variable problem).
SurveyAsk a representative sample questions to describe a population's attitudes/behaviour.Cheap, broad. Limit: sampling bias, wording effects, social-desirability responding.
The IV/DV intuition. The IV is the cause you dial (the dose), the DV is the effect you read off (the symptom). Picture the experimenter's hand on a knob (IV) and an eye on a dial (DV). Random assignment is what lets you claim the knob, not some pre-existing difference between people, moved the dial. Correlational work has no knob, you only watch two dials move together, which is why it cannot tell you which one is driving.

Mediators, moderators and meta-analysis

A mediator is the mechanism, the variable that comes between cause and effect and explains why the IV affects the DV (frustration → anger → aggression: anger mediates). A moderator is a variable that changes the strength or direction of the relationship, telling you when or for whom it holds (heat increases aggression more for provoked people). A meta-analysis statistically combines many studies to estimate the true overall effect size, the gold standard for asking "does this effect really hold?"

Deception, ethics and replication

Because telling participants the true purpose would change their behaviour, social psychology often uses deception (a cover story). This is allowed only under strict ethical safeguards: informed consent, the right to withdraw, minimising harm, and a thorough debriefing afterwards that reveals the true purpose and removes any deception's after-effects. An Institutional Review Board / ethics committee must approve the study first. The discipline has also taken its replication crisis seriously: a finding is trusted only when independent labs replicate it, which is why classic single studies (Milgram, the marshmallow studies) are now discussed alongside their replications and failures.

NIMHANS angle. NIMHANS, being a clinical and research institute, asks methods crisply: define IV vs DV, name the only method that proves causation (experimental), state why correlation cannot (third-variable + directionality), and distinguish mediator (the why, the mechanism in between) from moderator (the when/for whom, changes the strength). Also expect: the purpose of debriefing after deception, and what an ethics board protects.

A fourth method, and the four lenses Baron uses

Alongside the big three there is systematic / naturalistic observation (watching and recording behaviour as it occurs, without intervening, e.g. counting jaywalkers). Baron's opening chapter also frames the whole book through four perspectives that recur in every later chapter, and NIMHANS likes to name them:

PerspectiveThe core claim
CognitiveTo explain social behaviour, study how people think: notice, store, recall, interpret. The dominant modern lens (schemas, heuristics, attribution all live here).
EvolutionarySome social tendencies (mate choice, helping kin, fear of strangers) were shaped by natural selection because they aided survival/reproduction.
Social neuroscienceLinks social behaviour to brain activity and biology (fMRI, hormones), e.g. the amygdala in out-group fear, oxytocin in trust.
Cultural / multiculturalMany "findings" hold differently across cultures; social psych must test whether an effect is universal or culture-bound (individualist vs collectivist).
The hidden bias to name. Baron stresses that the social psychologist is also a thinker prone to the very biases the field studies, so methods (random assignment, double-blind procedures, pre-registration, replication) exist precisely to keep the researcher's expectations from manufacturing the result. This is why "expectancy effects / experimenter bias" is itself a methods-chapter term.
Lock these lines. Allport's definition = "actual, imagined or implied presence of others." Cause and effect = experiment only. IV = manipulated, DV = measured. Mediator = mechanism (why), moderator = condition (when/for whom). A theory is supported or disconfirmed, never proven. Four perspectives: cognitive · evolutionary · social neuroscience · cultural.
Recap §0. Social psych studies how others' real/imagined presence shapes the individual, scientifically. Methods: experimental (causation, via IV manipulation + random assignment), correlational (covariation only), survey (description), systematic observation. Four perspectives: cognitive, evolutionary, social neuroscience, cultural. Mediator = why, moderator = when. Deception is balanced by consent, debriefing and ethics review; findings must replicate, and methods exist partly to fence out the researcher's own expectancy bias.

1. Social cognition (how we think about the social world)

The problem. The social world throws more information at us than we could ever fully process. So the mind takes shortcuts. Social cognition is the study of how we notice, store, recall and use information about people and situations, and especially of the predictable errors those shortcuts produce. The governing idea is that we are "cognitive misers": we spend mental effort sparingly, defaulting to fast, cheap strategies that are usually good enough but sometimes badly wrong.

Dual-process thinking: automatic vs controlled

Underlying everything here is the dual-process view (popularised as System 1 vs System 2). Automatic processing is fast, effortless, unconscious and emotional; controlled processing is slow, effortful, deliberate and logical. Most social judgement runs on autopilot (System 1), and the biases below are largely automatic processing's blind spots. This same automatic/controlled split reappears in persuasion (peripheral vs central route, §4) and prejudice (implicit vs explicit attitudes, §5).

Heuristics: the mental shortcuts

A heuristic is a rule of thumb that gives a quick answer without full analysis. Three dominate the syllabus.

TermWhoWhat it is
Heuristics (availability, representativeness, anchoring)Tversky & KahnemanMental shortcuts: judge by ease of recall (availability) / by resemblance to a prototype (representativeness) / by clinging to a first value then adjusting too little (anchoring & adjustment). Fuller list on the Phenomena page.
SchemaBartlett (social use: Fiske & Taylor)Mental framework that guides what we notice, encode and recall about people/situations.
Priming—A recently activated schema shapes the next interpretation, even unconsciously.
Feel each heuristic. Availability: you fear plane crashes more than car crashes because crashes make the news and spring to mind easily, even though driving is deadlier. Representativeness: told someone is quiet and loves poetry, you guess "librarian" over "salesperson" because they fit the prototype, ignoring that salespeople vastly outnumber librarians (base-rate neglect). Anchoring: the first price you hear drags your estimate toward it. A schema is the bigger structure these run on, a packaged expectation ("restaurant" schema tells you to wait for a menu); priming is switching a schema on so it colours what comes next.

The biases of social thought

When heuristics misfire systematically, we get biases, errors that lean reliably in one direction. Baron groups several together.

TermWhoWhat it is
Self-fulfilling prophecyRobert MertonAn expectation makes itself come true by changing behaviour (basis of the Pygmalion effect).
Counterfactual thinkingKahneman & TverskyImagining "what might have been"; near-misses sting most (the bronze medallist is happier than the silver).
Optimistic / unrealistic-optimism biasNeil WeinsteinWe think bad things are less likely to happen to us than to others.

Planning fallacyKahneman & TverskyWe chronically underestimate how long our own tasks will take.

What schemas actually do, and why they stick

Baron is precise about a schema's three effects, a favourite "schemas influence ___" item: they shape attention (what info we notice), encoding (what gets stored, usually schema-consistent info, though wildly inconsistent info also grabs attention), and retrieval (what we later recall, biased toward the schema). Two further effects matter:

TermWhat it is
Perseverance effectBeliefs and schemas survive even after the evidence that created them is totally discredited. First impressions are sticky.
Self-fulfilling effect of schemasA schema can make the social world confirm it (we treat people per the schema, they respond in kind), tying back to Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy.

Two more "errors of social thought"

TermWhoWhat it is
Thought suppression & the rebound effectDaniel WegnerTrying not to think of something (the "white bear") makes it intrude more once you relax control; the monitoring process keeps the unwanted thought primed.
Overconfidence barrier—We have more confidence in our judgements and predictions than their accuracy warrants; a recurring obstacle to accurate social thought.
NIMHANS angle. Wegner's rebound effect is the cognitive model behind why "just stop thinking about it" fails in OCD, PTSD intrusions and insomnia, and why acceptance-based therapies (ACT, mindfulness) outperform suppression. Pair it with the perseverance effect, which explains why a discredited self-belief ("I'm a failure") persists in depression.

Affect and cognition cut both ways. Mood shapes thought (positive mood broadens thinking and increases heuristic use; we recall mood-congruent material), and thought shapes mood (how you appraise an event determines how you feel about it). This two-way street is why a good mood makes you a lazier, more shortcut-prone thinker.

Two near-twins: availability = judged by how easily examples come to mind (recent, vivid, emotional). Representativeness = judged by how well it matches a prototype (and so neglects base rates). If the stem stresses "easy to recall / vivid", it's availability; if it stresses "fits the stereotype/category", it's representativeness.
NIMHANS angle. These biases map straight onto clinical cognition. Negativity bias + counterfactual thinking underlie depressive rumination ("if only I had..."); the opposite of optimistic bias, depressive realism / pessimistic explanatory style, links to depression; magical thinking is a named symptom in OCD and schizotypal personality. Cognitive therapy is essentially retraining these automatic biases.
Recap §1. We are cognitive misers running mostly automatic (System 1) thinking. Heuristics (availability, representativeness, anchoring = Tversky & Kahneman) and schemas/priming speed judgement but bias it. Schemas steer attention, encoding and retrieval, and persevere after being disproved (perseverance effect). Errors: self-fulfilling prophecy (Merton), counterfactuals, optimistic bias (Weinstein), negativity bias, planning fallacy, magical thinking, thought-suppression rebound (Wegner), overconfidence. Affect and cognition feed each other.

2. Social perception & attribution

The problem. To navigate other people we must answer two questions about them: what are they feeling right now (read from nonverbal cues) and why did they do that (attribution). Social perception is how we form impressions and infer causes from limited information.

Nonverbal communication

Before a word is spoken we read nonverbal channels: facial expressions (Ekman's six basic emotions are largely universal), eye contact (gaze), body language and posture, touch, and paralanguage (tone, pace, pauses). These leak information the person may not mean to send, which is why we often trust the nonverbal over the verbal. Baron adds the cues to deception: microexpressions (fleeting true feelings that flash before they are masked), interchannel discrepancies (the face says one thing, the hands or voice another), and changes in eye contact and linguistic style. Despite all this, people detect lies barely above chance, because liars control the easy channels and we over-trust the face.

Attribution: explaining behaviour

Attribution theory asks how we decide whether an action springs from the person (an internal/dispositional cause) or the circumstance (an external/situational cause). Three classic models built the field.

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Attribution theoryFritz HeiderWe explain behaviour as internal (disposition) or external (situation); the person as "naïve psychologist."
Correspondent inference theoryJones & DavisWe infer a trait from acts that are freely chosen, unexpected and low in social desirability.
Covariation modelHarold KelleyAttribution uses consensus, consistency & distinctiveness (high all three → situation/stimulus; low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency → the person).
Walk Kelley's three Cs. Your friend laughs at a comedian. Consensus: does everyone laugh? Distinctiveness: does she laugh only at this comedian? Consistency: does she laugh every time? High on all three (everyone laughs, only at him, every time) → it's the comedian (external). Low consensus + low distinctiveness (no one else laughs, she laughs at everything) + high consistency → it's her (internal). Heider gave the basic internal/external split; Jones & Davis asked when an act reveals a true trait; Kelley added the data-driven rule.

The attributional biases

Impression formation & management

Solomon Asch showed first impressions form fast and that some traits are central (swapping "warm" for "cold" in a trait list transforms the whole impression, while "polite/blunt" barely moves it). The primacy effect means early information weighs most. Baron contrasts Asch's configural / gestalt view (traits fuse into a whole) with Norman Anderson's information-integration / averaging model (we mentally average the weighted values of each trait to form an overall impression). We also lean on an implicit personality theory, a lay assumption about which traits go together ("warm people are also generous"), which is how one central trait drags the rest. On the flip side, impression management (self-presentation) is our effort to control the impression others form of us, a theme Goffman framed dramaturgically (life as theatre, front-stage vs back-stage); its tactics include self-promotion (look competent), ingratiation (flattery, agreement, to look likeable) and self-deprecation.

Asch vs Anderson, and Kelley's two extra rules. Asch (configural): an impression is more than the sum of traits, a central trait reorganises the whole. Anderson (averaging): we weight and average traits like a mental calculator, so adding a mildly positive trait to a strongly positive set can lower the average. Separately, when judging why someone acted, Kelley added two single-observation rules: the discounting principle (downweight a cause if other plausible causes are present, e.g. praise from a salesman) and the augmenting principle (upweight a cause that produced an act despite obstacles, e.g. succeeding while sick).
The attribution swap: FAE = judging others (blame the person, ignore their situation). Actor–observer = the gap between how you explain your own acts (situation) vs others' acts (character). Self-serving = how you explain your own outcomes (take credit for success, dodge blame for failure). All three look alike on a stem, read whose behaviour and whose outcome it is about.
NIMHANS angle. Attribution style is core to clinical models. Depression's negative (pessimistic) attributional style (internal, stable, global for failures) is the mirror image of the self-serving bias, which depressed people lose. The just-world belief explains victim-blaming in trauma work. And the FAE is exactly why we pathologise individuals while underrating their situations, the bias clinical formulation tries to correct.
Recap §2. We read others nonverbally (channels + deception cues: microexpressions, interchannel discrepancies), then attribute causes. Heider (naïve psychologist) → Jones & Davis (correspondent inference) → Kelley (covariation three Cs, plus discounting & augmenting principles). Biases: FAE (Ross), actor–observer (Jones & Nisbett), self-serving, just-world (Lerner). Impressions form fast: Asch (configural, central traits, primacy) vs Anderson (averaging model), via an implicit personality theory; and we manage our own (self-presentation: self-promotion, ingratiation).

3. The self

The problem. The self is both the thinker and the thing thought about. Social psychology asks: how do we build a self-concept (the content, "who am I"), how do we set our self-esteem (the evaluation, "how do I feel about who I am"), and what lengths do we go to to protect that esteem? The recurring finding is that the self is fundamentally social: we know ourselves largely by comparison to, and reflection in, other people.

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Social comparison theoryLeon FestingerWe evaluate ourselves by comparison to others (upward comparison to better-off = motivating but deflating; downward to worse-off = self-enhancing).
Self-perception theoryDaryl BemWhen attitudes are weak or ambiguous, we infer them by observing our own behaviour ("I'm eating salad, I guess I like salad").
Self-discrepancy theoryE. Tory HigginsActual vs ideal self → dejection (sadness, disappointment); actual vs ought self → agitation (anxiety, guilt).
Self-monitoringMark SnyderHigh self-monitors tailor behaviour to the situation; low ones act from inner attitudes.
Self-efficacyAlbert BanduraBelief in your capacity to carry out the actions a specific task needs.
Self-handicappingBerglas & JonesSetting up an excuse in advance (not studying, drinking before a test) to protect self-esteem from possible failure.
Self-verificationWilliam SwannWe seek feedback that confirms our existing self-view, even when it is negative.
Self-affirmation theoryClaude SteeleAffirming a valued part of the self (in a different domain) buffers threats to self-integrity.
Spotlight effectThomas GilovichOverestimating how much others notice and judge us (the stain on your shirt).
BIRGing (basking in reflected glory)Robert Cialdini"We won" (claiming a winning group to boost self-image); CORFing = cutting off reflected failure, distancing from losers.
Terror management theoryGreenberg, Pyszczynski & SolomonAwareness of our own mortality drives us to defend our cultural worldview and bolster self-esteem.

Building the self-concept (the content side Baron opens with)

Before the esteem defences, Baron lays out how the self-concept is built and stored:

TermWhoWhat it is
Self-reference effectRogers et al.Information processed in relation to the self is remembered better; the self is a powerful memory organiser.
Possible selvesMarkus & NuriusImages of what we might become (hoped-for, feared, expected selves) that motivate and guide behaviour.
Self-complexityPatricia LinvilleHaving many distinct self-aspects buffers mood swings; a simple self lets one bad domain flood the whole self.
Sociometer theoryMark LearySelf-esteem is an internal gauge of social acceptance/belonging; it drops when we feel rejected.

The self across cultures (high-yield NIMHANS contrast)

Hazel Markus & Shinobu Kitayama drew the field's biggest cultural distinction: the independent self-construal (typical of individualist/Western cultures, the self defined by internal traits, separate, self-promoting) versus the interdependent self-construal (typical of collectivist cultures including India, the self defined by relationships, roles and group harmony). This single distinction explains cultural differences in the FAE (weaker in collectivist cultures), self-serving bias, self-enhancement (lower in East Asian samples) and conformity. India is the textbook collectivist/interdependent case.

The thread. Notice how many of these are defences of self-esteem, not neutral descriptions: self-handicapping pre-loads an excuse, self-affirmation patches a wound in one domain by celebrating another, BIRGing borrows glory, terror management props up the whole worldview against death anxiety. The self is not a calm observer, it is a press secretary working to keep esteem intact. The exceptions are the cooler cognitive ones: social comparison (Festinger, how we measure ourselves) and self-perception (Bem, how we infer attitudes from behaviour).
Two self-discrepancy directions (Higgins): falling short of your ideal self (hopes, what you want to be) → dejection (sad, depressed). Falling short of your ought self (duties, what you should be) → agitation (anxious, guilty). Ideal→sad, ought→anxious.
NIMHANS angle. Self-efficacy (Bandura) and self-esteem are workhorses of clinical assessment and CBT. Self-discrepancy theory (Higgins) directly models depression (ideal-self gap) vs anxiety (ought-self gap). Self-verification (Swann) explains why people with low self-esteem resist positive feedback and stay in confirming (even abusive) relationships, a real treatment obstacle. Terror management connects to existential anxiety.
Recap §3. The self is social. Content side: self-reference effect (Rogers), possible selves (Markus & Nurius), self-complexity (Linville), sociometer (Leary); and the independent vs interdependent self-construal (Markus & Kitayama, India = interdependent). We measure it by comparison (Festinger) and infer attitudes from behaviour (Bem). Higgins splits ideal-self gaps (sad) from ought-self gaps (anxious). Snyder = self-monitoring, Bandura = self-efficacy, Steele = self-affirmation, Swann = self-verification, Gilovich = spotlight, Cialdini = BIRGing, TMT = mortality defends the worldview. Most are esteem-protecting manoeuvres.

4. Attitudes & persuasion

The problem. An attitude is an evaluation of an object (person, issue, thing) along a like–dislike dimension. The classic ABC structure has three components: Affective (feelings), Behavioural (action tendencies) and Cognitive (beliefs). Attitudes serve functions (knowledge, ego-defence, value-expression, utilitarian). The two big questions in this chapter are: do attitudes actually predict behaviour? and how do you change them (persuasion)?

Where attitudes come from, and the implicit/explicit split

Baron lists the routes by which attitudes form, mostly low-level learning: classical conditioning (an object repeatedly paired with positive/negative stimuli, including subliminal conditioning and mere exposure), instrumental conditioning (attitudes that earn approval are reinforced), and observational learning / social comparison (we adopt the attitudes of models and reference groups). Twin studies show attitudes even have a partly heritable component. Crucially, Baron splits:

TermWhat it is
Explicit attitudesConscious, reportable, controllable evaluations.
Implicit attitudesAutomatic, often unconscious evaluations, measured by the Implicit Association Test (IAT) via reaction times; can diverge from what we say, and predict spontaneous behaviour.

The attitude–behaviour link

For decades the embarrassing answer was "barely". The famous demonstration is the LaPiere study: a researcher toured 1930s America with a Chinese couple and was served almost everywhere, yet when later asked by mail, most of those same establishments said they would refuse Chinese guests. Stated attitude did not match actual behaviour. The resolution came from Fishbein & Ajzen: attitudes predict behaviour best through intentions, and intentions are shaped by attitudes plus subjective norms plus (in the planned-behaviour update) perceived behavioural control. Attitudes also predict better when they are strong, specific, accessible, and based on direct experience.

Persuasion: how attitudes change

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Yale attitude-change approachCarl HovlandWho says what to whom: source (credibility, attractiveness), message, and audience factors in persuasion.
Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)Petty & CacioppoPersuasion via the central route (thoughtful scrutiny of arguments, when motivated & able) vs the peripheral route (surface cues: attractiveness, length, expert badge).
Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM)Shelly ChaikenSame dual-route idea: systematic (effortful argument analysis) vs heuristic (rules of thumb, "experts are right").
Theory of reasoned action / planned behaviourFishbein & AjzenIntention (from attitudes + subjective norms + perceived behavioural control) predicts behaviour.
Inoculation theoryWilliam McGuireA weak counter-argument first "vaccinates" an attitude against later strong attacks.
Psychological reactanceJack BrehmThreaten a freedom → people want the forbidden thing more (boomerang effect).
LaPiere studyRichard LaPiereThe classic attitude–behaviour gap: stated prejudice did not match actual service to a Chinese couple.
The dual route, intuitively. When you care and can concentrate (buying a house), you take the central route, weighing real arguments, and the resulting attitude is durable. When you do not care or are distracted (a soft-drink jingle), you take the peripheral route, swayed by who is pretty or how many points were listed, and the attitude is flimsy. ELM (Petty & Cacioppo) and HSM (Chaiken) are the same insight under different labels: central = systematic, peripheral = heuristic. This is §1's automatic/controlled split applied to persuasion.

Cognitive dissonance & consistency theories

The most-tested idea in the whole chapter. When two cognitions clash, or behaviour contradicts an attitude, we feel an unpleasant tension (dissonance) and are driven to reduce it, usually by changing the attitude to fit the behaviour.

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Cognitive dissonanceLeon FestingerTension from clashing cognitions drives attitude change to reduce it.
Insufficient justification / induced complianceFestinger & CarlsmithThe $1 group changed their attitude more than the $20 group (less external reward = more dissonance = more internal attitude change).
Effort justificationAronson & MillsThe harder/more embarrassing the initiation, the more we value the group we joined.
Balance theoryFritz HeiderWe seek consistency among our liking relationships (P-O-X triangles; an imbalanced triangle pressures change).
Hypocrisy paradigmAronson et al.Make people publicly advocate a behaviour (e.g. condom use), then remind them of their own past failures to do it → dissonance drives them to actually change behaviour.
Free-choice dissonanceJack BrehmAfter a hard choice between close options, we boost the chosen and derogate the rejected option ("spreading of alternatives") to quiet post-decision dissonance.
Why $1 beat $20. Participants did a boring task then were paid to tell the next person it was fun. Those paid $20 had a big external reason to lie ("I did it for the money"), so no dissonance, attitude unchanged. Those paid $1 had no good external reason, so the only way to resolve "I said it was fun but I had no reason to lie" was to decide it actually was fun. Less reward, more attitude change. Same logic in effort justification: "I suffered to get in, so this must be worth it."
Dissonance vs self-perception: both predict attitude follows behaviour. Festinger (dissonance) = there is arousal/discomfort, and it applies when the attitude was strong/clear and the behaviour contradicts it. Bem (self-perception, §3) = no arousal, you simply infer the attitude by watching yourself, and it applies when the attitude was weak or unclear. The deciding clue is whether the stem mentions tension/discomfort (Festinger) or a calm inference (Bem).
NIMHANS angle. Dissonance and reactance have direct clinical use. Effort justification is one reason expensive/effortful therapy can be valued more (and underlies hazing/cult commitment). Reactance (Brehm) explains why heavy-handed advice backfires and is the rationale for motivational interviewing, which deliberately avoids triggering it. Dissonance is harnessed in attitude-change health interventions.
Recap §4. Attitudes (ABC, with functions) form via classical/instrumental conditioning, mere exposure, observational learning and partly heredity; split into explicit vs implicit (IAT). They predict behaviour weakly except via intentions (Fishbein & Ajzen); LaPiere showed the gap. Persuasion runs two routes, central/systematic vs peripheral/heuristic (ELM = Petty & Cacioppo, HSM = Chaiken; Yale = Hovland). McGuire = inoculation, Brehm = reactance. Dissonance (Festinger) drives attitude-to-behaviour alignment; the $1>$20 result = insufficient justification (Festinger & Carlsmith); effort justification = Aronson & Mills; balance = Heider; hypocrisy paradigm = Aronson; post-decision spreading of alternatives = Brehm.

5. Stereotyping, prejudice & discrimination

The problem, and the three words kept straight. A stereotype is the cognitive component (a belief about a group's traits). Prejudice is the affective component (a negative feeling toward a group). Discrimination is the behavioural component (unequal treatment). They are the ABC of intergroup bias. The chapter asks where this bias comes from (identity, competition, personality, cognition) and what reduces it (contact).

Sources of prejudice

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Social identity theoryTajfel & TurnerPart of our self-esteem comes from group membership → we favour the in-group and derogate the out-group to feel good.
Minimal group paradigmHenri TajfelEven arbitrary, meaningless group splits (coin toss, dot-estimation) produce in-group favouritism.
Realistic conflict theoryMuzafer SherifCompetition over scarce resources breeds intergroup hostility (Robbers Cave study); superordinate (shared) goals reduce it.

Scapegoat theory(frustration–aggression roots)Frustration is displaced onto a weaker out-group blamed for problems.
Identity vs competition vs personality. Three different engines. Social identity (Tajfel & Turner): bias arises just from categorising people into us/them, no real conflict needed, the minimal group studies prove arbitrary groups suffice. Realistic conflict (Sherif): bias arises from real competition over resources, and Robbers Cave showed shared goals (a broken-down truck both camps must pull) dissolve it. Authoritarian personality (Adorno): bias sits inside a particular personality type. NIMHANS loves to swap these three.

The modern, subtle forms (and the cost to targets)

Baron stresses that open bigotry has gone underground into subtler forms, which NIMHANS can name:

Benevolent = subjectively positive ("cherish and protect them") but patronising and still keeps women in restricted roles. Both are prejudice; the "nice"-sounding one is benevolent.

6. Interpersonal attraction, relationships & love

The problem. Why do we like and love some people and not others, and what holds relationships together? Baron moves from the cold, almost mechanical predictors of initial attraction (who do you even end up near and noticing) to theories of love and finally to the economic models of why relationships last or end.

Predictors of initial attraction

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Propinquity (proximity) effectFestinger, Schachter & BackThe closer people live or sit, the more likely they form friendships (the Westgate housing study).
Mere-exposure effectRobert ZajoncRepeated exposure to a stimulus, by itself, increases liking for it.
Similarity–attractionDonn ByrneWe like those with similar attitudes (not "opposites attract"); the "law of attraction".
Matching hypothesisWalster & BerscheidWe tend to pair with partners of roughly our own level of physical attractiveness.

Evolutionary mate preferencesDavid BussAcross cultures, men weight youth and physical attractiveness (fertility cues), women weight status and resources (provisioning); explained as evolved reproductive strategies (parental investment).
Reciprocity of liking—We like those who like us; learning someone likes you boosts your liking for them.
Critique to keep. The evolutionary mate-preference account (Buss) is heavily tested but also heavily criticised: social-role theory (Eagly & Wood) argues the same sex differences fall out of women's restricted access to resources and power, not genes. Know both sides, NIMHANS likes "which explanation."
Why proximity and exposure come first. You cannot like someone you never encounter, so propinquity (Festinger's housing study: friendships clustered by who lived nearest the stairs and mailboxes) sets the pool. Mere exposure (Zajonc) then does its quiet work, familiarity breeds liking, not contempt. Only then do similarity (Byrne, the real rule, not "opposites attract") and looks filter further.

Why relationships last or end

The dark side Baron covers too. Beyond attraction, the chapter treats loneliness (the painful gap between desired and actual relationships, a health risk), jealousy (sex differences: evolutionary view says men fear sexual infidelity, women emotional infidelity), and social allergens (a partner's small repeated annoyances that accumulate into disgust). These are easy "name the term" items.
The two-factor love trick. Dutton & Aron's bridge study is misattribution of arousal in action: men who crossed a high, swaying bridge (physiological arousal from fear) were more attracted to the woman who met them there than men on a safe low bridge. They felt arousal, looked around for a cause, and pinned it on her. This is Schachter–Singer's two-factor theory of emotion applied to love: arousal + a label = the felt emotion.
The "who studied liking" cluster: Byrne = similarity, Zajonc = mere exposure, Festinger = propinquity, Sternberg = triangular love, Rusbult = investment model, Dutton & Aron = misattributed arousal (the bridge). These names rotate as each other's distractors, fix one hook per name.
NIMHANS angle. Adult attachment (Hazan & Shaver) bridges straight to Bowlby/Ainsworth in the developmental and object-relations syllabus, and insecure attachment is heavily used in couple and trauma therapy. Investment model (Rusbult) is the standard frame for why people stay in unsatisfying or abusive relationships (high investment + low alternatives = high commitment despite low satisfaction), a key clinical insight.
Recap §6. Attraction order: propinquity (Festinger et al.) → mere exposure (Zajonc) → similarity (Byrne) → matching & beauty stereotype. Love: Sternberg's triangle, Hatfield & Berscheid (passionate vs companionate), Dutton & Aron (misattributed arousal), Hazan & Shaver (attachment). Evolutionary mate preferences = Buss (vs Eagly & Wood's social-role critique). Endurance: social exchange (Thibaut & Kelley), equity (Walster/Adams), investment model (Rusbult), self-disclosure / social penetration (Altman & Taylor). Dissolution phases = Duck. Plus loneliness, jealousy, social allergens.

7. Social influence (conformity, compliance, obedience)

The problem, and the three levels. Social influence is changing because of others, and it comes in three intensities. Conformity = changing to match a group with no direct request (you just go along). Compliance = changing in response to a direct request (the sales techniques). Obedience = changing in response to a direct order from an authority. The levels rise from implicit group pressure to explicit command.

Conformity & obedience

TermWhoWhat it is
ConformitySolomon AschGoing along with a unanimous group's obviously wrong answer (the line-judging task); ~33% conformity rate.
Norm formationMuzafer SherifUsed the autokinetic illusion (a still dot of light appears to move) to show groups converge on a shared norm.
Informational vs normative influenceDeutsch & GerardWe conform to be right (informational, especially when uncertain) vs to be liked/accepted (normative).
Obedience to authorityStanley Milgram65% of ordinary people delivered "lethal" 450V shocks when an authority calmly ordered them to continue.
Minority influenceSerge MoscoviciA consistent minority can shift the majority, via genuine private conversion rather than mere public compliance.

What raises or lowers Asch conformity, and the kinds of norms. Conformity rises with group size (up to ~3–4, then plateaus) and unanimity, and collapses the moment one ally / dissenter breaks ranks. Baron also separates two kinds of social norm, a Cialdini distinction NIMHANS likes:

TermWhoWhat it is
Descriptive normsCialdini et al.What most people actually do ("everyone litters here"). Tell people what is, and they copy it.
Injunctive normsCialdini et al.What most people approve/disapprove of ("littering is wrong"). More durable for changing behaviour.
Mindlessness / the "because" effectEllen LangerA request with any reason ("...because I have to make copies") gains compliance even when the reason is empty, we respond to the form of a justification automatically.
Symbolic social influence—We are influenced by others even when they are merely imagined or represented (a mental image of a parent's reaction shapes behaviour).
Sherif vs Asch, the two faces of conformity. Sherif used an ambiguous task (how far did the dot move? nobody knows), so people genuinely looked to others for the right answer, this is informational influence and it produces real, internalised norm change. Asch used an unambiguous task (which line is longer? obvious), so people who caved did so to avoid standing out, this is normative influence and it is often just public compliance. Deutsch & Gerard named the two motives.

Compliance techniques

TermWhoWhat it is

Foot-in-the-doorFreedman & FraserGet agreement to a small request first → a bigger one later gets agreed to (works via commitment/consistency).
Door-in-the-faceRobert CialdiniMake a big request that is refused first → the smaller real request now looks reasonable (reciprocal concession).

That's-not-all techniqueJerry BurgerAdd a bonus or drop the price before the person decides → boosts compliance.

Foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face: FITD = small → big (Freedman & Fraser, via consistency). DITF = big → small (Cialdini, via reciprocal concession). Lowballing = get the yes, then change the deal. That's-not-all = sweeten the offer before the yes. Examiners love swapping the direction (small-then-big vs big-then-small).
NIMHANS angle. Milgram and Asch are perennial favourites: lock the headline numbers (Milgram ~65% to maximum voltage; Asch ~33% conformity) and the situational factors that reduce obedience (authority less close/legitimate, peers who rebel, victim more present). Minority influence (Moscovici) via consistency is the standard contrast to majority conformity. Clinically, these explain compliance pressures in institutions and the value of even one dissenting ally.
Recap §7. Conformity (Asch, line task; Sherif, autokinetic norm) runs on informational vs normative motives (Deutsch & Gerard). Obedience = Milgram (~65%). Minority influence via consistency = Moscovici. Asch conformity rises with size & unanimity, breaks with one ally. Norms: descriptive (what people do) vs injunctive (what they approve) = Cialdini; mindlessness/"because" = Langer; symbolic influence = imagined others. Compliance: Cialdini's six principles, FITD (Freedman & Fraser, small→big), DITF/lowballing/that's-not-all, reciprocity norm (Gouldner).

8. Prosocial behaviour (helping)

The problem. Why do we help, when do we fail to, and is helping ever truly selfless? The chapter splits into situational work (the bystander effect: why help fails) and motivational work (the egoism vs altruism debate: why we help at all).

When help fails: the bystander effect

TermWhoWhat it is
Bystander effect & 5-step decision modelLatané & DarleyMore witnesses → less help. The five steps: notice the event → interpret it as an emergency → take responsibility → know how to help → decide to act. (Sparked by the Kitty Genovese case.)
Diffusion of responsibilityDarley & Latané"Someone else will do it"; responsibility spreads thin in a crowd (fails step 3).
Pluralistic ignorance—Everyone stays calm because no one else looks worried, so no one reads it as an emergency (fails step 2).
Why a crowd freezes you. Latané & Darley's five-step model is a chain, and the crowd snaps it at two links. At step 2 (interpret), you look around, see other calm faces, and conclude it must not be an emergency, that is pluralistic ignorance. At step 3 (take responsibility), with many present, each person assumes someone else will act, that is diffusion of responsibility. More witnesses make both failures more likely, so paradoxically you are less likely to be helped in a big crowd.

Why we help: egoism vs altruism

TermWhoWhat it is
Empathy–altruism hypothesisC. Daniel BatsonFeeling empathy for the victim produces genuinely selfless helping, aimed at their welfare, not our own relief.
Negative-state relief modelRobert CialdiniWe help to relieve our own bad mood; helping is ultimately egoistic.
Arousal: cost–reward modelPiliavin et al.A bystander's unpleasant arousal at seeing distress motivates help, and we help when the costs of helping are low and costs of not helping are high.
Kin selection / reciprocal altruismHamilton; TriversEvolutionary: help relatives (shared genes, kin selection) or those likely to return the favour (reciprocal altruism).
Norm of social responsibility—A social norm that we should help those who depend on us, regardless of any return.

What makes helping more or less likely

Beyond the crowd, Baron lists the factors that boost helping: similarity to the victim, seeing a helpful model, feeling gratitude, being in a good mood (the "feel-good, do-good" effect), and the victim being not responsible for their plight. Helping drops with a hurry (the Good Samaritan study, Darley & Batson: seminary students late for a talk stepped over a slumped man), diffuse responsibility, and ambiguity. Two more named ideas:

TermWhoWhat it is
Empathic joy hypothesisSmith et al.We help partly to feel the pleasure of having a positive impact; we want feedback that our help worked.
Threat-to-self-esteem / reactions to receiving helpNadler & FisherReceiving aid can feel demeaning and imply inferiority, so recipients sometimes refuse help or resent the helper, especially from a similar other.
Why we help, the egoism vs altruism split: Batson (empathy–altruism) = there is true altruism, born of empathy and aimed at the other's welfare. Cialdini (negative-state relief) and Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward) = we ultimately help to fix our own discomfort, so helping is egoistic. That contrast (selfless vs self-serving motive) is the question.
NIMHANS angle. The bystander 5-step model and its two crowd failures (pluralistic ignorance at "interpret", diffusion of responsibility at "take responsibility") are textbook MCQ material, expect a scene and a "which step failed" stem. The empathy–altruism vs egoism debate is a favourite "which theorist argued helping is/ isn't truly selfless" item.
Recap §8. Bystander effect & 5-step model = Latané & Darley; crowds break it via pluralistic ignorance (interpret) and diffusion of responsibility (take responsibility). Motive debate: Batson (true altruism from empathy) vs Cialdini (negative-state relief) and Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward), both egoistic; plus evolutionary kin selection (Hamilton) / reciprocal altruism (Trivers). Helping rises with similarity, models, gratitude, good mood ("feel-good do-good"); falls with hurry (Good Samaritan study, Darley & Batson). Empathic joy = Smith; receiving help can threaten self-esteem (Nadler & Fisher).

9. Aggression

The problem, and the first distinction. Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another who does not want to be harmed. Baron first splits its types: hostile aggression (driven by anger, the goal is to hurt, often impulsive) vs instrumental aggression (a cold means to some other end, money, status), with a related relational aggression (harming through gossip, exclusion, reputation). The chapter then traces a historical arc: from a simple drive theory (frustration always causes aggression), through its cognitive reformulation (frustration only readies aggression, cues pull the trigger), to social learning (aggression is imitated) and integrative models.

Theory / termWhoWhat it is
Frustration–aggression hypothesisDollard, Doob, Miller et al.Original strong form: frustration (a blocked goal) always breeds aggression, and aggression always traces back to frustration.
Reformulated frustration–aggressionLeonard BerkowitzFrustration creates only a readiness (negative affect); aggression follows when aggressive cues are present.
Weapons effectBerkowitz & LePageThe mere presence of a weapon increases aggression, because the weapon is an aggressive cue.
Social learning of aggressionAlbert BanduraAggression is learned by observing and imitating models (the Bobo doll studies).
General Aggression Model (GAM)Anderson & BushmanIntegrative: person + situation inputs → present internal state (arousal, affect, cognition) → appraisal → aggressive (or not) act.
Excitation-transfer theoryDolf ZillmannLeft-over physiological arousal from one source (exercise, a prior fright) intensifies aggression in a later, unrelated provoking situation.
Cognitive-neoassociationLeonard BerkowitzAversive events (heat, pain, frustration) trigger negative affect that automatically primes a network of anger and fight/flight responses.
Catharsis hypothesis(Freud / Lorenz roots)The idea that venting "drains" aggression. Largely unsupported; venting (hitting a pillow, aggressive sport) tends to raise later aggression.
The arc in one breath. Dollard et al. said frustration → aggression, full stop, too rigid. Berkowitz rescued it: frustration only builds a charge of negative affect (readiness); whether it discharges as aggression depends on aggressive cues in the environment, hence the weapons effect (a gun on the table makes you more aggressive). Bandura's Bobo doll added that we also just copy aggressive models. And the popular belief in catharsis (let it out and you'll feel calmer) is a myth: research shows venting fuels rather than drains aggression, a high-yield "which of these is false" item.
Two Berkowitz ideas, kept apart: the reformulated frustration–aggression (frustration → readiness, cue → act) and cognitive-neoassociation (aversive events prime an anger network) are both his. And do not confuse excitation-transfer (Zillmann), where arousal carries over from a prior unrelated event, with the weapons effect, where an external cue triggers aggression. Also remember the catharsis hypothesis is largely disproven, examiners frame it as the false option.

Social & cultural causes, and how to reduce it

TermWhoWhat it is

Culture of honourNisbett & CohenIn cultures (e.g. the US South) where reputation must be defended, insults trigger far more aggression; shows aggression is culturally shaped, not just biological.

Reducing aggression (Baron's toolkit). Catharsis does not work. What does: punishment only under strict conditions (prompt, certain, proportionate, or it backfires); incompatible responses (humour, empathy, mild non-aggressive feelings that can't co-exist with anger); apology and mitigating information; cognitive interventions and exposure to non-aggressive models; and self-regulation training.

NIMHANS angle. Determinants of aggression are testable as a list: biological (low serotonin, high testosterone, amygdala/prefrontal function, the role of alcohol disinhibition) and situational/social (heat, provocation, crowding, frustration, media/violent models, culture of honour). The catharsis myth and the weapons effect are classic single-best-answer traps. Dodge's hostile attribution bias is core to the clinical model of conduct disorder and is itself a clean attribution-error item. Bandura's Bobo doll connects to the social-learning theory you know from the personality and learning chapters.
Recap §9. Frustration–aggression (Dollard et al., strong original) → reformulated (Berkowitz: frustration = readiness, cues fire it; weapons effect = Berkowitz & LePage) → social learning/Bobo (Bandura) → GAM (Anderson & Bushman). Excitation-transfer = Zillmann; cognitive-neoassociation = Berkowitz; catharsis = a largely unsupported myth. Types: hostile vs instrumental vs relational. Causes: media violence/desensitisation (Anderson & Bushman), culture of honour (Nisbett & Cohen), hostile attribution bias (Dodge), bullying (Olweus). 10. Groups & individuals

The problem. What does the mere presence of, or membership in, a group do to the individual, to effort, to self-restraint, and to the quality of decisions? The chapter pairs opposing effects (presence can boost or sap effort; groups can decide better or worse) and the deciding variable is usually whether you are individually identifiable.

What actually makes a "group"

Baron opens by defining a group's structure: roles (expected behaviours for a position), status (rank within the group), norms (shared rules), and cohesiveness (the forces binding members together). These are the four ingredients that turn a mere collection of people into a group, and they set up everything that follows.

Presence of others: facilitation vs loafing

TermWhoWhat it is
Social facilitationTriplett; ZajoncAn audience or co-actor boosts performance on easy/well-learned tasks but hurts hard/novel ones (Zajonc: arousal strengthens the dominant response).
Evaluation apprehensionNickolas CottrellIt is the fear of being judged, not mere physical presence, that creates the facilitation arousal.
Social loafingLatané, Williams & HarkinsPeople exert less effort in a group when their individual output cannot be identified (the Ringelmann rope-pull roots).
DeindividuationFestinger; ZimbardoAnonymity and arousal in a crowd → loss of self-awareness and personal restraint (the Stanford prison study).
The first historical experiment. Triplett (1898) noticed cyclists rode faster racing others than alone, arguably social psychology's first experiment. Zajonc explained it: an audience raises arousal, and arousal strengthens whatever response is dominant, the right one on an easy task (so you do better) but the wrong one on a hard task (so you do worse). Cottrell refined it: the arousal comes from evaluation apprehension, fear of being judged, not bare presence.

TermWhoWhat it is

Leadership

TermWhoWhat it is
Great-person / trait theory—Leaders are born with distinguishing traits; weakly supported on its own.

Risky shift was the clue to polarisation. Stoner first found groups decide riskier than individuals. Later work showed that was only half the story: groups can also shift more cautious if members started cautious. The general law is group polarisation (Moscovici & Zavalloni), discussion amplifies the pre-existing tilt in either direction. Groupthink (Janis) is the pathological extreme of cohesion, where the drive for agreement crushes realistic appraisal, with classic symptoms (illusion of invulnerability, self-censorship, mindguards, illusion of unanimity).
Facilitation vs loafing: social facilitation = others present make you try harder (you are watched and evaluated individually). Social loafing = others present make you try less (your effort is pooled and your individual output is hidden). The deciding variable is whether your individual output is identifiable. Bonus: risky shift is the special case, group polarisation is the general rule.
NIMHANS angle. Watch the deindividuation attribution: Festinger coined the term, but the dramatic demonstration (and the one usually cited) is Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment. Groupthink (Janis) with its named symptoms is a reliable MCQ. Social dilemmas / tragedy of the commons (Hardin) and GRIT (Osgood) connect social psych to public-health and conflict-resolution applications relevant to an Indian clinical-research context.
Recap §10. Presence effects: social facilitation (Triplett/Zajonc, arousal strengthens dominant response; Cottrell = evaluation apprehension) vs social loafing (Latané/Ringelmann, hidden individual effort); deindividuation (Festinger coined, Zimbardo demonstrated). Decisions: group polarisation (Moscovici & Zavalloni) generalises risky shift (Stoner); groupthink (Janis). A group = roles + status + norms + cohesiveness. Conflict: social dilemma (Hardin), GRIT (Osgood); fairness = distributive vs procedural justice (procedure can matter more than outcome). Leadership: trait/great-person vs transactional vs transformational (Bass).

11. The landmark studies, walked through

Baron teaches social psychology through its classic experiments, and NIMHANS/MET stems often describe a study and ask you to name it, its author, or its conclusion. Here each landmark is walked through in the same shape: the question, what they did, what they found, why it matters, and the trap. Learn the design, not just the headline number, because examiners test the procedure ("participants judged the length of lines" vs "a dot of light that appeared to move") as the giveaway.

Triplett (1898) — the first social-psychology experiment

Question: does the presence of others change performance? What: Norman Triplett noticed cyclists posted faster times racing against others than against the clock alone, then had children wind fishing reels alone vs alongside a co-actor. Found: children wound faster in the presence of a co-actor. Matters: widely cited as the first published social-psychology experiment and the seed of social facilitation. Trap: Triplett observed facilitation; Zajonc later explained it (arousal strengthens the dominant response); Cottrell refined the cause to evaluation apprehension.

Sherif (1936) — autokinetic norm formation

Question: how do group norms form when reality is ambiguous? What: in a dark room a stationary pinpoint of light appears to move (the autokinetic effect, an optical illusion). Alone, people gave wildly different estimates of how far it "moved"; in groups, their estimates converged on a shared figure, and each person kept using that group figure even when later tested alone. Found: a private, internalised norm forms and persists. Matters: the model case of informational social influence (we use others as evidence when uncertain). Trap: Sherif = ambiguous stimulus, real internal conversion (informational); Asch = unambiguous stimulus, public compliance (normative). The "dot of light" is the tell for Sherif.

Asch (1951) — conformity on the line task

Question: will people conform to a group that is clearly wrong? What: a single real participant judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard, seated among confederates who unanimously gave the same wrong answer on critical trials. The correct answer was obvious. Found: about 75% of participants conformed on at least one trial; the conformity rate per critical trial was about 33–37%. Conformity rose with group size (up to 3–4, then plateaued) and unanimity, and collapsed the moment a single ally also dissented (it dropped to roughly a quarter of its level). Matters: normative pressure can override the plain evidence of one's senses. Trap: lock the two numbers separately (≈75% conformed at least once; ≈33% per trial) and remember one dissenting ally is what frees the participant, not the size of the majority.

Milgram (1963) — obedience to authority

Question: how far will ordinary people go in harming another when ordered by an authority? What: the participant ("teacher") was told to deliver escalating shocks (15V up to 450V, labelled "XXX") to a "learner" (a confederate, who was never actually shocked) for wrong answers, while a calm experimenter in a lab coat issued four scripted prods ("the experiment requires that you continue"). Found: 65% went all the way to 450V; almost all went past the point the learner screamed and fell silent. Matters: situational pressure, not sadism, produced the obedience, ordinary people did it. The factors that REDUCED obedience (a favourite list): the authority was physically absent or gave orders by phone; the authority's legitimacy was undercut (ordinary office vs Yale); the victim was more present (same room, then touch-proximity); and crucially peers who rebelled (disobedient models) cut obedience sharply. Obedience rose when responsibility could be diffused (the teacher only read words while another "pressed the switch"). Trap: the headline is ~65%; know at least two factors that lower it (proximity of victim, rebellious peers, reduced authority legitimacy). Milgram's concept of the agentic state (seeing oneself as the authority's instrument, not the author of the act) is the explanatory mechanism.

Zimbardo (1971) — the Stanford Prison Experiment

Question: do situations and roles, not dispositions, drive cruelty? What: college men were randomly assigned to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock basement prison; the study was meant to run two weeks. Found: guards rapidly became abusive and prisoners passive/distressed; it was halted after six days. Matters: headline demonstration of deindividuation and the power of assigned roles over individual character. Trap: Festinger coined "deindividuation"; Zimbardo gave the dramatic demonstration. Modern critique (demand characteristics, guards coached, low replicability) is now part of the discussion, so a stem may ask what the study is criticised for.

Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) — $1 vs $20

Question: when does doing something for too little reward change your attitude? What: participants did an excruciatingly boring task, then were paid either $1 or $20 to tell a waiting participant it was enjoyable, then privately rated how much they had actually enjoyed it. Found: the $1 group rated the task as more enjoyable. Matters: classic cognitive dissonance via insufficient justification, with too little external reason to lie, the only way to resolve the tension is to believe the lie. Trap: the counter-intuitive direction (less reward → more attitude change) is the whole point; $20 gave an external justification, so no dissonance.

Latané & Darley (1968–70) — the bystander effect

Question: why do witnesses fail to help? What: two famous variants. In the smoke-filled room study, participants filling out a questionnaire saw smoke seep in; alone, most reported it quickly, but in a group of passive confederates most sat in the haze and said nothing (pluralistic ignorance). In the seizure study, the more bystanders a participant believed were on an intercom, the slower and less likely they were to help a person apparently having a fit (diffusion of responsibility). Found: more witnesses → less helping. Matters: formalised as the five-step decision model (notice → interpret as emergency → assume responsibility → know how → decide/act). Trap: match the failure to the step, pluralistic ignorance breaks step 2 (interpret), diffusion of responsibility breaks step 3 (assume responsibility). The Kitty Genovese case was the journalistic spark, though its "38 witnesses" detail was later shown to be exaggerated.

Darley & Batson (1973) — the Good Samaritan study

Question: does being in a hurry suppress helping? What: seminary students walked to give a talk (some on the parable of the Good Samaritan) and passed a man slumped, coughing, in a doorway; the experimenters varied how rushed the student was. Found: being in a hurry was the strongest predictor of not helping; the talk topic barely mattered. Matters: shows the power of the situation over personal values/personality even in the most prosocially primed people. Trap: the lesson is "hurry > piety," situational time pressure beats disposition.

Bandura (1961–63) — the Bobo doll

Question: is aggression learned by imitation? What: children watched an adult model either attack or ignore an inflatable Bobo doll, then were mildly frustrated and left alone with it. Found: children who saw aggression imitated it (including novel acts and verbal aggression); seeing the model rewarded increased imitation, seeing the model punished decreased performance but not learning (they could still reproduce the acts when later incentivised). Matters: founded social learning theory and the learning/performance distinction; a bridge to the personality and learning chapters. Trap: children learned the aggression by observation regardless of the model's consequences; consequences only changed whether they performed it.

Tajfel (1971) — the minimal group paradigm

Question: how little does it take to produce in-group bias? What: strangers were split into groups on a trivial, meaningless basis (e.g. supposed preference for Klee vs Kandinsky paintings, or a coin toss), then allocated points/money to anonymous in-group and out-group members. Found: people favoured their own group even though groups were arbitrary, there was no prior contact, no self-interest, and no conflict, and they would even sacrifice absolute in-group gain to maximise the difference over the out-group. Matters: the empirical bedrock of social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner), categorisation alone breeds favouritism. Trap: minimal group = bias with no real competition; contrast with Sherif's realistic conflict, where bias needs competition over resources.

Sherif (1954) — the Robbers Cave study

Question: what creates and what cures intergroup hostility? What: at a boys' summer camp, two groups (Eagles, Rattlers) were formed, then put into competition for prizes, breeding real hostility (name-calling, raids). The experimenters then introduced superordinate goals, problems both groups had to solve together (a broken water supply, a stuck truck needing every boy to pull). Found: competition manufactured prejudice; shared goals requiring cooperation dissolved it, mere contact alone did not. Matters: the field demonstration of realistic conflict theory and the reason the contact hypothesis requires cooperation toward common goals, not just proximity. Trap: it was the superordinate (shared) goal, not contact by itself, that reduced hostility.

Dutton & Aron (1974) — the shaky-bridge study

Question: can arousal from one source be mislabelled as attraction? What: men crossed either a high, swaying suspension bridge (fear arousal) or a low, stable bridge, where an attractive female interviewer gave them her number. Found: men from the scary bridge were far more likely to call her and rated her as more attractive. Matters: misattribution of arousal, an application of Schachter & Singer's two-factor theory of emotion (arousal + a cognitive label = the felt emotion) to romantic attraction. Trap: the arousal was really fear; participants relabelled it as attraction.

LaPiere (1934) — attitudes vs behaviour

Question: do stated attitudes predict actual behaviour? What: LaPiere toured the US with a young Chinese couple and recorded their treatment at ~250 hotels/restaurants; they were refused only once. Months later he wrote to the same establishments asking whether they would serve Chinese guests; over 90% said no. Found: a glaring gap between expressed prejudice and real behaviour. Matters: set off decades of work resolved by Fishbein & Ajzen (attitudes predict behaviour through intentions, and best when specific, strong, accessible). Trap: LaPiere = the attitude–behaviour gap; the resolution (intentions, specificity) is the theory-of-reasoned-action/planned-behaviour line.

13. The MCQ traps, collected

  • Method core: experiment = the only design proving causation (manipulate IV, measure DV, random assignment). Correlation ≠ causation (third-variable + directionality). Mediator = the why (mechanism in between); moderator = the when/for whom (changes strength).
  • Availability = ease of recall · representativeness = fit to a prototype (neglects base rates) · anchoring = stuck on a first value. All three = Tversky & Kahneman.
  • The attribution swap: FAE (Ross) = over-blame others' character · actor–observer (Jones & Nisbett) = my acts = situation, your acts = character · self-serving = my success = me, my failure = the situation.
  • Attribution models in order: Heider (naïve psychologist, internal/external) → Jones & Davis (correspondent inference) → Kelley (covariation: consensus, consistency, distinctiveness).
  • Self-discrepancy (Higgins): ideal-self gap → dejection (sad); ought-self gap → agitation (anxious).
  • Self cluster: social comparison = Festinger · self-perception = Bem · self-monitoring = Snyder · self-efficacy = Bandura · self-handicapping = Berglas & Jones · self-verification = Swann · self-affirmation = Steele · spotlight = Gilovich · BIRGing = Cialdini · TMT = Greenberg, Pyszczynski & Solomon.
  • Dissonance vs self-perception: Festinger = arousal/discomfort, applies when the attitude was strong; Bem = no arousal, just inference, applies when the attitude was weak.
  • $1 > $20 (insufficient justification, Festinger & Carlsmith): less reward = more attitude change. Effort justification = Aronson & Mills. Balance theory = Heider.
  • Persuasion routes: central/systematic vs peripheral/heuristic. ELM = Petty & Cacioppo; HSM = Chaiken; Yale "who-what-whom" = Hovland; inoculation = McGuire; reactance = Brehm; attitude–behaviour gap = LaPiere; intention model = Fishbein & Ajzen.
  • Prejudice sources: social identity / minimal group = Tajfel (& Turner) · realistic conflict / Robbers Cave = Sherif · authoritarian personality (F-scale) = Adorno. Reduction: contact hypothesis (four conditions) = Allport; jigsaw classroom = Aronson.
  • Stereotype cognition: illusory correlation = Hamilton & Gifford · out-group homogeneity · stereotype threat = Steele & Aronson · stereotype content model (warmth × competence) = Fiske. Note stereotype threat ≠ self-fulfilling prophecy (target's own fear vs perceiver's expectation).
  • The "who studied liking" cluster: Byrne = similarity · Zajonc = mere exposure · Festinger = propinquity · Sternberg = triangular love · Rusbult = investment model · Dutton & Aron = misattributed arousal (bridge).
  • Conformity: Asch = unambiguous line task (normative) · Sherif = autokinetic, ambiguous (informational) · Deutsch & Gerard named informational vs normative · Milgram = obedience (~65%) · Moscovici = minority influence via consistency.
  • Foot-in-the-door vs door-in-the-face: FITD = small→big (Freedman & Fraser, consistency); DITF = big→small (Cialdini, reciprocal concession); lowballing = change the deal after the yes; that's-not-all = sweeten before the yes; reciprocity norm = Gouldner.
  • Helping: bystander effect & 5-step model = Latané & Darley; crowd failures = pluralistic ignorance (interpret) + diffusion of responsibility (take responsibility). Egoism vs altruism: Batson = true altruism (empathy); Cialdini (negative-state relief) and Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward) = egoistic. Evolutionary: kin selection (Hamilton), reciprocal altruism (Trivers).
  • Aggression: frustration–aggression = Dollard et al.; reformulation + weapons effect = Berkowitz (& LePage); social learning/Bobo = Bandura; GAM = Anderson & Bushman; excitation-transfer = Zillmann; cognitive-neoassociation = Berkowitz; catharsis is a myth (venting raises aggression).
  • Groups: facilitation (Triplett/Zajonc, dominant-response) vs loafing (Latané/Ringelmann, hidden effort); evaluation apprehension = Cottrell; deindividuation = Festinger coined / Zimbardo demonstrated; group polarisation (Moscovici & Zavalloni) generalises risky shift (Stoner); groupthink = Janis; tragedy of the commons = Hardin; GRIT = Osgood.
  • Perspectives (methods chapter): cognitive · evolutionary · social neuroscience · cultural. Schemas act on attention/encoding/retrieval and show the perseverance effect; thought-suppression rebound = Wegner.
  • Impression formation: Asch (configural, central traits) vs Anderson (averaging model); plus Kelley's discounting (other causes present → downweight) and augmenting (acted despite obstacles → upweight).
  • Self & culture: independent vs interdependent self-construal = Markus & Kitayama (India = interdependent/collectivist); possible selves = Markus & Nurius; sociometer = Leary.
  • Attitudes: explicit vs implicit (IAT); formed via conditioning/mere exposure/observational learning. Hypocrisy paradigm = Aronson; post-decision spreading of alternatives = Brehm.
  • Modern prejudice: aversive/subtle racism; hostile vs benevolent sexism = Glick & Fiske (benevolent = the "nice"-sounding one); recategorisation / common in-group identity = Gaertner & Dovidio.
  • Relationships: evolutionary mate preferences = Buss (vs Eagly & Wood social-role critique); self-disclosure/social penetration = Altman & Taylor; dissolution phases = Duck.
  • Influence norms: descriptive (what people do) vs injunctive (what they approve) = Cialdini; mindless "because" compliance = Langer.
  • Aggression types: hostile (anger, harm is the goal) vs instrumental (means to an end) vs relational; hostile attribution bias = Dodge; culture of honour = Nisbett & Cohen; catharsis is a myth.
  • Group justice: distributive (fair outcome) vs procedural (fair process, can matter more); leadership transactional (reward exchange) vs transformational (inspire/remake) = Bass.

Recall beats re-reading. Cover the answers, ask yourself each question aloud, then check. If you miss one, jump back to its section. The answer follows in green.

§0 Science & methods

  1. Which is the only method that establishes cause and effect, and what two features make it work? Experimental; manipulating the IV + random assignment.
  2. Distinguish a mediator from a moderator. Mediator = the mechanism in between (the why); moderator = changes the strength/direction (the when/for whom).
  3. Name Baron's four perspectives. Cognitive, evolutionary, social neuroscience, cultural.

§1 Social cognition

  1. Availability vs representativeness, what is each judged by? Availability = ease of recall; representativeness = fit to a prototype (neglects base rates).
  2. What three processes do schemas bias? Attention, encoding, retrieval, plus they persevere after disconfirmation.
  3. Who showed thought suppression backfires, and what is it called? Wegner; the rebound (white-bear) effect.

§2 Perception & attribution

  1. Put the three attribution theorists in order with their contribution. Heider (internal/external, naïve psychologist) → Jones & Davis (correspondent inference) → Kelley (covariation: consensus, consistency, distinctiveness).
  2. FAE vs actor–observer vs self-serving, whose behaviour/outcome does each concern? FAE = others' acts (blame character); actor–observer = my acts (situation) vs yours (character); self-serving = my outcomes (credit success, dodge failure).
  3. Asch's configural view vs Anderson's model? Asch = central traits reorganise the whole; Anderson = we average weighted trait values.

§3 The self

  1. Higgins: ideal-self gap leads to ___, ought-self gap leads to ___. Dejection (sad); agitation (anxious).
  2. Independent vs interdependent self-construal, and where does India sit? Markus & Kitayama; India = interdependent/collectivist.
  3. Match: Bem, Festinger, Snyder, Swann, Steele. Self-perception; social comparison; self-monitoring; self-verification; self-affirmation.

§4 Attitudes & persuasion

  1. What did LaPiere demonstrate, and who resolved it? The attitude–behaviour gap; Fishbein & Ajzen (via intentions).
  2. Why did the $1 group change attitudes more than the $20 group? Insufficient justification, no external reason to lie, so they had to believe it (Festinger & Carlsmith).
  3. Dissonance vs self-perception, what is the deciding clue? Arousal/discomfort + strong prior attitude (Festinger) vs no arousal, weak attitude, mere inference (Bem).

§5 Prejudice

  1. Social identity vs realistic conflict vs authoritarian personality, the engine of each? Categorisation alone (Tajfel & Turner) vs resource competition (Sherif) vs a personality type (Adorno).
  2. Allport's four conditions for contact to reduce prejudice? Equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation, institutional support.
  3. Hostile vs benevolent sexism, which sounds "nice"? Benevolent (cherish/protect), still subordinating (Glick & Fiske).

§6 Attraction & love

  1. Order the early predictors of attraction. Propinquity (Festinger et al.) → mere exposure (Zajonc) → similarity (Byrne) → matching/beauty stereotype.
  2. What does the investment model add to social exchange, and its clinical use? Commitment = satisfaction + investment − alternatives (Rusbult); explains staying in unsatisfying/abusive relationships.
  3. Name Duck's break-up phases. Intrapsychic → dyadic → social → grave-dressing.

§7 Social influence

  1. Sherif vs Asch, ambiguous or unambiguous, informational or normative? Sherif = ambiguous, informational, internalised; Asch = unambiguous, normative, public compliance.
  2. Milgram's headline figure and two factors that reduce obedience? ~65% to 450V; victim more present / rebellious peers / less legitimate or absent authority.
  3. FITD vs DITF direction and mechanism? FITD = small→big (consistency); DITF = big→small (reciprocal concession).

§8 Prosocial

  1. The five steps of the bystander model, and which step each crowd-failure breaks? Notice → interpret → take responsibility → know how → act; pluralistic ignorance breaks step 2, diffusion of responsibility breaks step 3.
  2. Batson vs Cialdini/Piliavin on why we help? Batson = true altruism from empathy; Cialdini (negative-state relief) & Piliavin (arousal:cost-reward) = egoistic.

§9 Aggression

  1. How did Berkowitz reformulate frustration–aggression, and what's the weapons effect? Frustration creates only readiness (negative affect); aggressive cues fire it; a weapon's mere presence is such a cue (Berkowitz & LePage).
  2. True or false: catharsis reduces aggression. False, venting tends to raise later aggression.
  3. Low or inflated self-esteem predicts aggression? Inflated, unstable (narcissism), when ego is threatened (Baumeister & Bushman).

§10 Groups

  1. Facilitation vs loafing, what is the deciding variable? Whether your individual output is identifiable (identifiable → try harder; hidden → loaf).
  2. Who coined deindividuation vs who gave the famous demonstration? Festinger coined it; Zimbardo (Stanford prison) demonstrated it.
  3. Distributive vs procedural justice, and which can matter more? Fair outcome vs fair process; people accept bad outcomes if the process felt fair.
