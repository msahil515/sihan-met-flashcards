MCQ traps to lock in:

  • REM EEG looks like awake (low-voltage, fast, mixed) — the "paradox" of paradoxical sleep. Question often phrased as "REM is characterised by all EXCEPT delta-wave EEG."
  • REM has atonia (skeletal muscle paralysis) + REM atonia loss = REM Sleep Behaviour Disorder (RBD), often a prodrome for Parkinson's / Lewy body dementia.
  • Sleep cycle = ~90 minutes, 4–6 cycles per night. Slow-wave sleep dominates the first half of the night, REM dominates the second half.
  • Growth hormone is released in N3, not REM.
  • Memory consolidation: declarative in slow-wave sleep, procedural + emotional in REM.
  • Newborns spend ~50% of sleep in REM (called "active sleep"); adults ~20–25%. Drops with age.
  • Sleepwalking, night terrors, bed-wetting = N3 (slow-wave) parasomnias. Nightmares = REM parasomnia.

Theory of mind

Theory of mind (ToM) = the capacity to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge — to oneself and to others, and to understand that others can hold mental states different from one's own. Term coined by Premack & Woodruff (1978) in a chimpanzee study. Critical milestone in social cognitive development.

How it's tested

  • Sally-Anne task (false-belief) — Wimmer & Perner (1983), popularised by Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith (1985). Sally puts marble in basket, leaves. Anne moves marble to box. Sally returns. "Where will Sally look for her marble?" Correct answer = basket (Sally's false belief). Passed reliably at ~4 years.
  • Smarties / Maxi task — child is shown a Smarties tube containing pencils. "What will another child think is in here?" Pre-ToM: "pencils" (can't separate own knowledge from other's). Post-ToM: "Smarties."
  • Second-order false belief — "What does Mary think John thinks?" Passed ~6–7 years. Tests recursive ToM.
  • Reading the Mind in the Eyes — adult ToM test (Baron-Cohen), identify emotion from eye region only. Used in autism research.

Developmental timeline

  • ~18 months — joint attention, pointing to share, pretend play (precursors).
  • ~2–3 years — understands desires drive behaviour (desire psychology).
  • ~4 years — first-order false belief, Sally-Anne passed.
  • ~6–7 years — second-order false belief, recursive mentalising.
  • ~9–11 years — faux pas detection, irony, sarcasm, white lies.

Clinical relevance

  • Autism Spectrum Disorder — classic ToM deficit, especially on false-belief tasks. Baron-Cohen's "mindblindness" hypothesis (1995). Note this is one theory, not the whole story; ASD also involves weak central coherence and executive dysfunction.
  • Schizophrenia — ToM impairment linked to delusions and social withdrawal (Frith).
  • Frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) — selective ToM loss with preserved IQ early on.
Trap to avoid. Sally-Anne tests first-order false belief (what Sally thinks). It is NOT a test of empathy, perspective-taking in general, or appearance-reality distinction (that's the Smarties task variant).

p-value refresher

You got Q6 right but flagged "I forgot this concept. Knew it well before but need a detailed revision." Here's the clean version, plus the things examiners weaponise.

The definition (one sentence, memorise it)

p-value = the probability of observing the obtained data (or more extreme), assuming the null hypothesis is true.

What it does NOT mean

  • p is NOT the probability that H₀ is true.
  • p is NOT the probability that H₁ is true.
  • p is NOT the probability you're wrong.
  • p is NOT the effect size — a small p with a tiny effect on a huge sample is statistically significant but clinically meaningless.
  • p < 0.05 does NOT prove H₁; it just lets you reject H₀ at α = 0.05.

Decision rule

  • p < α (e.g., 0.03 < 0.05) → reject H₀. Result is "statistically significant."
  • p ≥ α → fail to reject H₀. (Never "accept H₀" — absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence.)
  • α is set before the study, not chosen after seeing the data.

The four-cell decision matrix (the foundation)

H₀ actually trueH₀ actually false
Reject H₀Type I error (α) — false positive, you think there's an effect when there isn'tCorrect rejection (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀Correct retention (1 − α)Type II error (β) — false negative, you miss a real effect

Common asks on MET / NIMHANS

  • α (alpha) = pre-set threshold, usually 0.05. Probability of Type I error.
  • β (beta) = probability of Type II error. Usually 0.20.
  • Power = 1 − β = probability of detecting a real effect. Aim for ≥ 0.80. Power depends on sample size, effect size, α, and variability.
  • Effect size = magnitude of the effect, independent of n. Cohen's d (means), r (correlation), η² (ANOVA), OR/RR (epidemiology).
  • Confidence interval at 95% complements p < 0.05: if the 95% CI for a mean difference excludes 0, p < 0.05.
  • One-tailed vs two-tailed — directional vs non-directional H₁. One-tailed makes it easier to reject H₀ but you must pre-specify the direction.
  • Bonferroni correction — adjust α when running multiple comparisons. α_new = α / k.
  • p-hacking / multiple comparisons — running many tests inflates Type I error. Not a "scientific finding," it's a statistics failure.
One-liner that always works in MCQ: "If p < α, reject H₀." If asked for the meaning of p, the safest answer is always "probability of the observed result (or more extreme) under the assumption that H₀ is true." Anything stronger than this is a distractor.

EMDR + trauma therapists

From Q13. Francine Shapiro developed EMDR in 1987. You asked who Shapiro and Foa were — here's the trauma-therapy lineage.

EMDR — Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing

  • Founder: Francine Shapiro, 1987.
  • Mechanism (theoretical): bilateral stimulation (eye movements, taps, tones) while recalling a traumatic memory facilitates reprocessing, hypothesised to mimic REM sleep memory consolidation.
  • 8 phases: (1) history-taking, (2) preparation, (3) assessment, (4) desensitisation, (5) installation of positive cognition, (6) body scan, (7) closure, (8) re-evaluation.
  • Indication: PTSD primary. Endorsed by APA (2017) and WHO (2013) as a first-line PTSD treatment alongside trauma-focused CBT.
  • Controversy: whether the eye movements themselves are essential or just the exposure component. Component-dismantling studies are mixed.

T-scores have mean = 50, SD = 10. T = 60 → +1 SD. T = 65 → conventional "elevated" cut-off on MMPI-2. T = 70 → +2 SD → clinically significant elevation. T = 80 → +3 SD, rare and extreme.

Other standardised score systems to keep separate:

  • Z-score: M = 0, SD = 1.
  • Standard score / IQ: M = 100, SD = 15 (Wechsler) or 16 (Stanford-Binet 4th ed, older).
  • Subtest scaled scores (WAIS/WISC): M = 10, SD = 3. So scaled 7 = −1 SD, scaled 13 = +1 SD.
  • Stanine: M = 5, SD ~ 2. Range 1–9.
  • Percentile: rank 1–99, not a standardised score, NOT normally distributed.

[5] WAIS IQ ranges — "what is dependent on edition"

The IQ range labels shifted between WAIS-III and WAIS-IV. Memorise the WAIS-IV (current) version:

  • 130+ — Very Superior / Extremely High
  • 120–129 — Superior
  • 110–119 — High Average
  • 90–109 — Average
  • 80–89 — Low Average
  • 70–79 — Borderline
  • <70 — Extremely Low (DSM-5 requires concurrent adaptive deficits to call it intellectual disability)

Older texts (WAIS-III, Wechsler-1955) sometimes used "Borderline" for 70–79 AND "Dull Normal" for 80–89. The WAIS-IV uses "Low Average / Borderline" depending on cut-point. Trap: if a question says "70–84 falls under…" with both "Low Average / Borderline (depending on edition)" and just "Borderline" as options, the more inclusive answer (edition-dependent) is safer.

[9] Ellis ABC, "I was confused between A and C"

REBT's ABC is:

  • A — Activating event (not just "Antecedent" — that's Skinnerian).
  • B — Beliefs (irrational, in REBT).
  • C — Consequences (emotional + behavioural).
  • D — Disputing (the irrational belief). Therapist's job.
  • E — Effective new belief / Effect.

The trap option in Q9 (option A: "Activating event, Behaviour, Consequence") looks right because it sounds like the operant ABC. But Ellis is cognitive — the middle term is Belief, not Behaviour. Skinner's three-term contingency is the look-alike: Antecedent → Behaviour → Consequence.

[10] Basal ganglia, "I thought it was subthalamic first"

Basal ganglia = caudate, putamen, globus pallidus (internal + external), subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra. Sometimes lumped as "striatum" (caudate + putamen) and "lentiform nucleus" (putamen + globus pallidus). Red nucleus is NOT basal ganglia — it's midbrain tegmentum, gives the rubrospinal tract. Subthalamic IS in the basal ganglia (lesion = hemiballismus, contralateral wild flinging movements). Red nucleus lesion = rubral tremor + ataxia.

[11] Place theory + Bekesy

Georg von Békésy, Nobel 1961. Place theory: pitch is encoded by the location of maximum basilar membrane displacement. High frequencies vibrate the basal (narrow, stiff) end of the cochlea; low frequencies vibrate the apical (wide, floppy) end. Best for high frequencies (above ~1 kHz). Frequency / Volley theory (Wever) handles low frequencies: groups of auditory neurons fire in volleys phase-locked to the sound wave. Above ~5 kHz, place theory does all the work.

[12] LTP and NMDA, "completely forgot"

Long-term potentiation = persistent strengthening of synapses with repeated stimulation. Bliss & Lomo, 1973, hippocampus. NMDA receptor is the coincidence detector: needs both glutamate binding AND postsynaptic depolarisation (to expel the Mg²⁺ block). Once both happen, Ca²⁺ floods in → triggers cascade → more AMPA receptors inserted into the membrane → synapse permanently stronger. AMPA is the workhorse for fast excitatory transmission; NMDA is the gate-keeper for plasticity. LTP is the cellular substrate of learning/memory. Hippocampus + NMDA + Ca²⁺ + LTP = memorise as a chain.
