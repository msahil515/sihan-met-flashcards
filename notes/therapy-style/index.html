1 · Your approach / modality, as the sessions actually show it

You are not a single-school therapist, and that's a strength, not a hedge: the sessions show a deliberate, repeatable logic for when you use what. The spine is cognitive-behavioural (you name automatic thoughts, loops, triggers, and you set small behavioural homework), the relational stance is person-centred (validate first, collaborative, never directive), and you reach for ACT/compassion/solution-focused moves when they fit the moment.

The defining feature: you're state-driven, not modality-driven. You don't run "CBT clients" and "person-centred clients." You read the client's level of overwhelm and switch:
• Overwhelmed / flooded → containment + supportive holding
• Stuck in a thought → gentle CBT / ACT reframing
• Ambivalent about change → motivational interviewing (roll with resistance)
• Functionally stuck → behavioural activation + problem-solving
• Self-critical → compassion-focused ("what would you tell a friend?")

You've actually codified this. Your own session framework is built on two named gears:

A) DEFAULT MODE (use when client is stable enough to talk) … B) CONTAINMENT MODE (override default when client is overwhelmed) … Goal: Stabilize the nervous system, not solve the problem. your YD session framework, written out for yourself

You reuse the moves of several schools, but you sequence and simplify them around the client's capacity in the moment. That is textbook integrative, humanistic-CBT practice, and you can say so with examples.

How you formulate a case

Your working model is consistently a loop, and you locate the intervention at the earliest link. You also separate the automatic/biological driver from the psychological one and sequence treatment accordingly (break the automatic pattern first, then go after deeper triggers).

Your recurring formulation: Trigger → Thought → Urge → Action → Relief → Guilt → repeat. You teach the client to step in "right when we start having the first few thoughts," and you reframe the relief as the thing that maintains the problem: "the relief you feel after testing is actually what keeps the anxiety alive and not solving it."

You set agendas collaboratively rather than imposing them:

You have mentioned … studies … Another would be … managing the urges. What would you like to focus on in todays session?to Vaisakh — collaborative agenda-setting

And you reformulate when the referral label is wrong — e.g. you rejected a platform's "gaming disorder" tag and re-read it as post-bereavement anxiety and executive dysfunction after the client lost his father and sister. That's diagnostic flexibility worth naming in an interview.

Your documentation house-style

After each session you produce a fixed format (good to mention — it shows structure):

5 · Strengths & growth edges (be honest, it reads as insight)

Strengths — well evidenced

• Validation-first, genuinely warm, non-judgmental, especially on shame-loaded topics (porn/urges, relapse, cheating). "It takes courage to do this and I appreciate you for it."
• Disciplined one-question pacing + ellipsis holding — keeps text sessions therapeutic, not interrogative.
• Strong reframes around shame and loss ("the relapse is evidence you can change the pattern").
• Harm-reduction maturity — alternative coping before abstinence.
• Good risk awareness — confidentiality framing every session, warm SI screening, safety planning, psychiatric referral, knows scope.
• Conscientious continuity — formal formulation + next-session plan every time; carries personal threads forward.
• Diagnostic humility — handles self-diagnosis well, reformulates wrong referral labels, rules cases out of psychology when they're medical.

Growth edges — own these as "what I'm working on"

• Over-talking / chasing every thread when a client needs containment. Your honest self-critique on one tough session: you "chased every thread instead of anchoring … he needed containment, not commentary." The fix you already know: anchor one target, talk less.
• Over-psychoeducating — your longest turns can tip from collaborative into lecturing. You catch it yourself ("this sounds very preachy. talk in my style").
• Stock phrasing — "I understand..", "That makes sense.." can pile up before any intervention; some clients want something concrete sooner.
• Pacing toward solutions vs. exploration under a 45-min cap — efficient, but can short-change depth.
• Formal outcome tracking is light — progress is narrative; a standardised measure (even a quick scale) would strengthen it.
Interview tip: a growth edge you can name and show a fix for is worth more than a fake weakness. "Early on I'd chase every thread when a client was overwhelmed; I've learned to anchor one target and contain first" is a strong, true answer.

6 · Interview answers, built from your own work

Say these in your own voice; the substance underneath each is real.

"What is your therapeutic approach?" "I'd describe myself as integrative but state-driven. My base is person-centred — I validate and build safety before anything else — and my main toolkit is cognitive-behavioural: I work with automatic thoughts, the loops that maintain a problem, and small behavioural steps. But I don't apply one school to every client. I read where the person is: if they're overwhelmed I contain and stabilise first, if they're stuck in a thought I do gentle cognitive work, if they're ambivalent I use motivational interviewing. My rule is regulate before you explore, validate before you intervene."
"Give an example of how you handle a client in distress." "I have a two-gear approach. By default I validate, ask one focused question, then offer one small step. But the moment a client says things like 'I don't know,' 'nothing helps,' or starts repeating themselves, I switch into containment — I stop exploring and stop problem-solving. I'll say something like 'You've shared a lot, it's okay to not say more, we can just sit with what's here.' The goal in that moment is to settle the nervous system, not to solve the problem. Once they're regulated, we can think again."
"How do you build the therapeutic relationship?" "Consistency and permission. I open every session the same way — who I am, the time we have, that it's confidential unless there's risk of harm — so the frame is safe and predictable. Then I let the client lead: 'feel free to start wherever you'd like.' I name strengths back to them, I normalise how hard sharing is, and I never rush. I'd rather the person feel understood than get through my agenda."
"How do you handle risk?" "I screen warmly but directly. If someone signals it, I ask plainly — 'right now, do you get thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life?' — I don't dance around it. Then I stabilise and safety-plan: who can be with you, are you safe right now. I know my scope: I'm a psychologist, I don't prescribe, and I refer to psychiatry when it's indicated."
"Tell me about a difficult case / a mistake you learned from." "I had a session with a client in real distress where I over-talked — I chased every thread he raised instead of anchoring one, and he left without anything concrete. It taught me that when someone's flooded they need containment, not commentary. Now I consciously anchor one target per session and stabilise before I explore. It made me a much more focused clinician."
"How do you work with habits or compulsive behaviour?" "Harm-reduction, not white-knuckling. I don't ask someone to stop a behaviour abruptly — that usually spikes anxiety and shame. I map the loop with them — trigger, thought, urge, action, relief, guilt — and we intervene at the earliest link: notice the thought, delay the action, build an alternative coping skill first. I frame it as reducing the behaviour's grip on their life, not eliminating it overnight."
"How do you handle a client who self-diagnoses from the internet?" "I stay neutral on the label and move to lived experience. If someone says 'I have ADHD and hypomania,' I don't confirm or argue — I ask what actually happens for them, when, and for how long. I explore the phenomenology before the pathology. It keeps it clinical without turning into a diagnostic debate."
