Learning & Conditioning — Full Terms Page

Built 2026-05-16 because MET / NIMHANS love the operant-conditioning vocabulary as 1-mark definition traps. Every term has the same 3-row block: Definition · Classic example · MCQ trap. Skim once, then drill by closing the example/trap columns and self-quizzing on the term name.

0. Big picture — where each term sits

FamilyTerms inside
Classical (Pavlovian) phenomenaExtinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination, habituation, sensitization
Operant (Skinnerian) shaping toolsShaping, chaining, fading, prompting
Operant schedulesFR, VR, FI, VI
Cognitive / social learningLatent learning (Tolman), insight learning (Köhler), observational learning (Bandura)
Implicit / cognitive primingPriming (semantic, perceptual, affective)

Read this once: all of classical conditioning is about stimulus pairings, all of operant is about consequences shaping voluntary behaviour, priming is about one stimulus making a later stimulus easier to process, and habituation/sensitization are non-associative (single stimulus, no pairing). If a question mixes the families, the answer is almost always the term whose family matches the stem.

1. Priming

DefinitionExposure to one stimulus (the prime) unconsciously influences the response to a later stimulus (the target), by activating related representations in memory. A form of implicit memory.
Classic exampleYou read the word NURSE. A moment later you identify DOCTOR faster than an unrelated word like BREAD. The semantic network around "medical" was pre-activated.
MCQ trapPriming is not classical conditioning — there is no UCS, no reflex, no pairing. It is also not recall — the person doesn't consciously remember the prime. Tested on the implicit-vs-explicit memory split. Subtypes: semantic (NURSE → DOCTOR), perceptual (briefly flashed word → faster identification), affective (smiling face → positive judgement).

2. Shaping

DefinitionReinforcing successive approximations of a target behaviour the organism doesn't yet emit. Operant, Skinner.
Classic exampleTraining a rat to press a lever: first reinforce facing the lever, then approaching it, then touching it, then pressing it. Each step gets closer to the final behaviour.
MCQ trapShaping is for behaviours the organism cannot yet do. Don't confuse with chaining (linking behaviours the organism already does into a sequence) or prompting (cueing an existing behaviour). The reinforcement criterion changes across trials — that is the defining feature.

3. Chaining

DefinitionLinking a sequence of already-learned behaviours into a longer chain, where each behaviour serves as the discriminative stimulus (SD) for the next, and only the final link is reinforced.
Classic exampleBrushing teeth: pick up brush → wet it → apply paste → brush → rinse → put brush away. Each step cues the next. Used heavily in ABA / autism skill training.
MCQ trapTwo variants — forward chaining teaches step 1 first; backward chaining teaches the last step first (so the learner always experiences the reinforcer at the natural endpoint). Backward chaining is often preferred clinically because the final reinforcer is reached immediately. Do not confuse with shaping — chaining links existing behaviours; shaping builds a new one.

4. Fading

DefinitionGradually removing a prompt while transferring stimulus control to the natural cue, so the behaviour eventually occurs without the prompt.
Classic exampleTeaching a child to say "ball" — start with full verbal model ("say ball"), fade to partial ("say buh…"), fade to just holding up the ball. The picture/object becomes the controlling stimulus.
MCQ trapFading is about removing the prompt, not the reinforcer. Removing the reinforcer is extinction. Fading transfers stimulus control; extinction eliminates the behaviour. Both can run side-by-side but they target different things.

5. Prompting

DefinitionProviding an additional stimulus (verbal, gestural, physical, modelled, visual) that helps the learner emit the correct response in the presence of the natural cue.
Classic exampleTherapist says "wave bye-bye" and physically guides the child's hand (physical prompt). Over trials, prompts are faded until the verbal cue alone works.
MCQ trapKnow the prompt hierarchy from most to least intrusive: physical → modelling → gestural → verbal → visual/positional. "Least-to-most" vs "most-to-least" prompting are common clinical-vignette distractors. Prompts are antecedent manipulations; reinforcement is a consequence — that distinction has been tested.

6. Extinction

DefinitionReduction and eventual disappearance of a conditioned response when reinforcement (operant) or the UCS (classical) is no longer presented.
Classic exampleClassical: Pavlov's dog stops salivating to the bell once the bell is repeatedly presented without food. Operant: a child stops tantrumming after parents consistently ignore tantrums that previously got attention.
MCQ trapWatch for the extinction burst — a temporary increase in the behaviour's frequency and intensity right after reinforcement is withdrawn, before it declines. Extinction does not erase the learning; the response can return as spontaneous recovery. Behaviours on variable schedules are most resistant to extinction (partial-reinforcement extinction effect).

7. Spontaneous Recovery

DefinitionReappearance of a previously extinguished conditioned response after a rest period, without any new conditioning trials.
Classic examplePavlov extinguished salivation to a bell. He let the dog rest for several hours, presented the bell again, and the salivation came back (weaker than original, but real).
MCQ trapSpontaneous recovery proves extinction is not unlearning — the original association is suppressed, not erased. The recovered response is typically weaker than the original. Re-extinction is also faster the second time (savings effect). Don't confuse with reinstatement (re-emergence after UCS-alone presentation) or renewal (re-emergence in a new context).

8. Generalization vs Discrimination

GeneralizationDiscrimination
DefinitionResponding similarly to stimuli that resemble the original CS / SD.Responding differently to stimuli that resemble the CS / SD but are not it — only the specific stimulus elicits the response.
Classic exampleLittle Albert (Watson) was conditioned to fear a white rat. He then also feared a white rabbit, cotton wool, a Santa beard — fear generalized to similar stimuli.A dog is reinforced for salivating to a 1000 Hz tone but not to a 1200 Hz tone. After training, only 1000 Hz produces salivation — the dog discriminates.
MCQ trapGeneralization gradient — response strength decreases with distance from the original CS. Stimulus generalization (similar CS) ≠ response generalization (different responses to same CS).Discrimination is taught by differential reinforcement: reinforce in presence of SD, withhold in presence of SΔ. Sharper gradient = better discrimination. Failure to discriminate is the mechanism behind many phobias.

One-liner to memorize: generalization is the default, discrimination is trained.

9. The Four Reinforcement Schedules

ScheduleDefinitionResponse patternClassic exampleMCQ trap
Fixed Ratio (FR)Reinforcement after a fixed number of responses.High rate, brief post-reinforcement pause, then burst. Step-like cumulative record.Factory piece-work: paid per 10 units assembled. FR-10.The pause comes after the reinforcer, not before. Higher ratios = longer pauses. Ratio strain (work stops) if ratio is raised too fast.
Variable Ratio (VR)Reinforcement after an average number of responses (unpredictable trial-to-trial).Highest, steadiest response rate of any schedule. Almost no pause.Slot machines, fishing, sales cold-calls. The schedule behind gambling addiction.Most resistant to extinction. Produces the most persistent behaviour. If a stem says "extremely hard to stop" or "addictive" → VR.
Fixed Interval (FI)Reinforcement for the first response after a fixed time has elapsed.Scallop pattern — slow responding right after reinforcement, accelerating as the interval ends.Checking the mail when you know it arrives at 11 am. Studying right before a scheduled weekly quiz.FI gives the lowest overall rate among the four. Don't confuse with FR — FI is time-based, FR is response-count based.
Variable Interval (VI)Reinforcement for the first response after a variable, unpredictable time interval.Steady, moderate rate. No scallop. Resistant to extinction.Checking phone for a text. Refreshing email. Pop quizzes.Produces the most steady response rate. VR produces the most vigorous rate — distinguish the two by "steady vs vigorous."

Memory hook: Ratio schedules = paid per response → high rates. Interval schedules = paid per time → moderate rates. Variable = unpredictable → most resistant to extinction. Fixed = predictable → pause / scallop pattern after reinforcement.

Rank order of resistance to extinction: VR > VI > FR > FI > continuous (CRF). Continuous reinforcement learns fastest but extinguishes fastest.

10. Latent Learning

DefinitionLearning that occurs without reinforcement and without immediate behavioural expression; it shows up later when reinforcement is introduced. Demonstrates that learning ≠ performance.
Classic exampleTolman & Honzik (1930) rat-in-maze experiment. Three groups: (a) reinforced every trial, (b) never reinforced, (c) unreinforced for 10 days, then reinforced from day 11. Group (c) immediately outperformed group (a) on day 12 — proving they had been learning the maze all along ("cognitive map") but not showing it.
MCQ trapThe buzzword is cognitive map (Tolman, 1948 "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men"). Latent learning was the first big challenge to strict behaviourism — it showed mental representations form without external reinforcement. Don't confuse with insight learning (sudden problem-solving) or observational learning (via a model).

11. Insight Learning

DefinitionSudden understanding of a problem's solution, typically after a period of contemplation, without trial-and-error. Associated with the Gestalt school.
Classic exampleKöhler's chimpanzee Sultan (1925). Sultan was placed in a cage with a banana out of reach and two short sticks that could be joined. After a period of inactivity, Sultan suddenly joined the sticks and retrieved the banana — the "aha!" moment.
MCQ trapLinked to Wolfgang Köhler and the Gestalt psychologists (perceptual reorganization). It is cognitive, not behaviourist. Contrast with Thorndike's trial-and-error learning (law of effect, puzzle box cats). If the stem mentions "aha!", "sudden," or "no gradual improvement" → insight.

12. Observational Learning

DefinitionLearning by watching others (the model) and imitating their behaviour. No direct reinforcement of the learner is required — vicarious reinforcement of the model is enough.
Classic exampleBandura's Bobo doll experiment (1961). Children who watched an adult aggressively beat a Bobo doll later beat it themselves; controls did not. Aggression was learned by observation alone.
MCQ trapBandura's four processes are commonly tested: Attention → Retention → Reproduction → Motivation (ARRM). Vicarious reinforcement (seeing the model rewarded) and vicarious punishment (seeing the model punished) modulate motivation. Bandura's broader framework is Social Learning Theory (1977), later renamed Social Cognitive Theory (1986) — also home of self-efficacy and reciprocal determinism.

13. Habituation vs Sensitization

HabituationSensitization
DefinitionDecrease in response to a repeated, harmless stimulus. Non-associative learning.Increase in response to a stimulus after exposure to an intense or noxious stimulus. Also non-associative.
Classic exampleYou stop noticing the ticking of a wall clock after a few minutes. Aplysia (sea slug, Kandel) reduces gill-withdrawal reflex with repeated mild touch.After a sudden loud bang, you flinch harder at every subsequent door creak. Aplysia gill-withdrawal becomes stronger after a tail shock — even to mild touch.
MCQ trapHabituation is stimulus-specific (a new stimulus restores the response → dishabituation). Do not confuse with sensory adaptation (receptor fatigue, peripheral) or with extinction (which requires a prior pairing). Habituation needs only one stimulus, no learning history.
Mechanism (Kandel)Reduced neurotransmitter release at the sensory-motor synapse.Heterosynaptic facilitation via serotonergic interneurons → increased NT release.

One-liner: habituation says "this is safe, ignore it"; sensitization says "the world is dangerous, react more." Both are the simplest forms of learning and were Kandel's 2000 Nobel Prize work.

14. Confusable-pairs cheat-table (read last)

PairDistinguishing feature
Shaping vs ChainingShaping builds a new behaviour. Chaining links existing behaviours.
Fading vs ExtinctionFading removes the prompt. Extinction removes the reinforcer / UCS.
Extinction vs Spontaneous recoveryExtinction suppresses; spontaneous recovery shows the memory is still there.
Generalization vs DiscriminationGeneralization is the default; discrimination is trained by differential reinforcement.
FR vs FIFR = fixed responses. FI = fixed time. Both pause after reinforcement, but FI shows a scallop.
VR vs VIVR = highest, most vigorous rate (gambling). VI = steady, moderate rate (checking phone).
Latent vs Insight learningLatent = hidden until reinforced (Tolman, rats). Insight = sudden "aha" (Köhler, chimps).
Observational learning vs ImitationImitation is one component; observational learning is the whole 4-stage process (Bandura's ARRM).
Habituation vs Sensory adaptationHabituation is in the CNS (learning). Sensory adaptation is in the receptor (fatigue).
Habituation vs ExtinctionHabituation needs only one stimulus, no prior pairing. Extinction requires a previously conditioned association.
Priming vs Classical conditioningPriming = implicit memory facilitation, no UCS. Classical = reflex paired with neutral stimulus.

15. Quick self-test (close the answers, write your own)

  1. A pigeon stops pecking when food is no longer delivered, then pecks again after a 24-hour rest. What is the second peck called?
  2. You praise a child for any sound that sounds like "ma" — over weeks she progresses to "ma-ma," then to "mama." Which procedure?
  3. Slot machines maintain behaviour on which schedule, and why is that schedule the hardest to extinguish?
  4. Tolman's rats had learned the maze even though they showed no improvement until food was introduced. What is this phenomenon called, and what concept did Tolman propose to explain it?
  5. A therapist holds a child's hand to help her wave bye-bye. Over sessions she removes the hand prompt and only the verbal cue remains. Name the two procedures involved.
  6. You stop hearing the air conditioner after 5 minutes. The cat suddenly jumps on the table and you immediately hear the AC again. Which two non-associative processes did you just experience?
Show answers
  1. Spontaneous recovery.
  2. Shaping (successive approximations).
  3. Variable Ratio (VR). Highly resistant to extinction because the next response could always be the reinforced one.
  4. Latent learning. Tolman proposed the cognitive map.
  5. Prompting (the hand) being faded; the verbal cue takes over stimulus control.
  6. Habituation (stopped hearing AC) and dishabituation (cat jumping restored the response).
