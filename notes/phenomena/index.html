1. Perceptual illusions

IllusionWhoWhat you see
Müller-LyerFranz Müller-LyerTwo equal lines look unequal depending on arrow-head direction (inward vs outward fins).
PonzoMario PonzoTwo equal lines on converging "railway" lines look unequal (depth misread).
Ebbinghaus (Titchener) circlesEbbinghausA circle ringed by big circles looks smaller than the same circle ringed by small ones.
ZöllnerZöllnerParallel lines look slanted because of crossing hatch marks.
Kanizsa triangleGaetano KanizsaYou "see" a white triangle edge that isn't drawn (illusory/subjective contours).
Necker cubeLouis NeckerA wire cube flips between two 3-D orientations (bistable perception).
Rubin's vaseEdgar RubinFaces-or-vase: figure–ground reversal.
Ames roomAdelbert AmesA distorted room makes equal-size people look giant vs tiny.
Moon illusion—The moon looks larger near the horizon than overhead.
Hermann grid—Ghostly grey blobs appear at the white intersections of a black grid.
Café wallGregory & HeardStraight mortar lines look wedge-shaped between offset tiles.
Phi phenomenonMax WertheimerLights flashing in sequence look like one moving light (the founding Gestalt demo of apparent motion).
Autokinetic phenomenonused by SherifA still point of light in the dark seems to drift; Sherif used it to study norm formation.
Two "size of equal lines" illusions: Müller-Lyer = arrow fins; Ponzo = converging rails. Ebbinghaus = circles surrounded by other circles. Examiners mix these three.

2. Gestalt grouping principles

Wertheimer, Koffka & Köhler. "The whole is other than the sum of its parts." Master law = Prägnanz (we see the simplest, most stable form).

PrincipleWhat groups together
ProximityThings close together are seen as a group.
SimilarityThings that look alike are grouped.
Continuity (good continuation)We follow smooth, continuous lines over abrupt changes.
ClosureWe fill gaps to see a complete figure.
Figure–groundWe split a scene into an object and its background.
Common fateThings moving together are grouped.
Prägnanz (good form)The overarching law: perceive the simplest, most regular organisation.

3. Attention & perception phenomena

PhenomenonWhoWhat it is
Inattentional blindnessMack & Rock; Simons & Chabris (gorilla)You miss an obvious unexpected object while attending elsewhere.
Change blindnessSimons & LevinYou fail to notice a large change across a visual interruption.
Attentional blinkRaymond, Shapiro & ArnellBrief inability to detect a 2nd target ~200–500 ms after the 1st.
Cocktail-party phenomenonColin CherryTracking one voice in noise, yet still catching your own name.
Weber's law / JNDErnst WeberThe just-noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the stimulus.
Fechner's lawGustav FechnerSensation grows with the log of stimulus intensity (founded psychophysics).
Sensory adaptation—Constant stimulation → declining response (you stop feeling your clothes).
Pareidolia—Seeing meaningful patterns (faces) in random stimuli (clouds, toast).
Synaesthesia—One sense automatically triggers another (hearing colours, tasting words).
BlindsightWeiskrantzResponding to visual stimuli without conscious sight (V1 damage).
Perceptual constancy—Objects seen as stable in size/shape/colour despite changing retinal image.

4. Memory phenomena

PhenomenonWhoWhat it is
Tip-of-the-tongue (TOT)Brown & McNeillKnowing a word is there but not retrieving it (you often get its first letter/length).
Flashbulb memoryBrown & KulikVivid, confident memory of where you were during a shocking event (often inaccurate).
Déjà vu / jamais vu—Novel scene feels familiar / familiar scene feels alien.
Reminiscence bump—Older adults recall disproportionately many memories from ages ~10–30.
Childhood (infantile) amnesia—Few/no episodic memories before ~age 3–4.
Proactive interference—Old learning blocks new (old password keeps coming up).
Retroactive interference—New learning blocks old (new password erases the old one).
Forgetting curveHermann EbbinghausRetention drops sharply then levels off; relearning is faster (savings).
Misattribution / source monitoring errorSchacterRight memory, wrong source (cryptomnesia: feeling you invented something you read).

5. Group & social phenomena

PhenomenonWhoWhat it is
Social facilitationTriplett; ZajoncPresence of others boosts easy tasks, harms hard ones.
Social loafingLatané; Ringelmann rootsIndividuals slack in groups when effort isn't identifiable.
Diffusion of responsibilityDarley & LatanéThe mechanism behind the bystander effect: "someone else will."
Group polarisationMoscovici; Stoner (risky shift)Group discussion pushes members to a more extreme version of their leaning.
GroupthinkIrving JanisCohesive group suppresses dissent for consensus → bad decisions.
DeindividuationFestinger; ZimbardoLoss of self-awareness/restraint in a crowd or anonymity.
ConformitySolomon AschGoing along with a group's wrong judgement (line task).
ObedienceStanley MilgramFollowing authority's orders against your own conscience (shock study).
Bystander apathyLatané & DarleyThe umbrella phenomenon: less help as the crowd grows.
Risky shiftStonerGroups make riskier decisions than individuals (early name for polarisation).

6. Cognitive biases & heuristics

Heuristics = mental shortcuts (Tversky & Kahneman). Biases = the systematic errors they cause.

Bias / heuristicWhoWhat it is
Availability heuristicTversky & KahnemanJudge frequency by how easily examples come to mind.
Representativeness heuristicTversky & KahnemanJudge by resemblance to a prototype (ignores base rates → conjunction fallacy).
Anchoring & adjustmentTversky & KahnemanCling to an initial value when estimating.
Confirmation biasWasonSeeking/weighting evidence that fits your belief.
Hindsight biasFischhoff"I knew it all along" after the outcome is known.
Fundamental attribution errorcoined by Lee RossOver-attributing others' behaviour to character, under-weighting situation.
Self-serving bias—Credit success to self, blame failure on circumstance.
Sunk-cost fallacy—Continuing because of past investment that can't be recovered.
Gambler's fallacy—Believing past random outcomes change future odds.
Loss aversion / framingKahneman & Tversky (prospect theory)Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good.
Tells: "judged by ease of recall" = availability; "judged by stereotype/prototype" = representativeness; "blamed the person not the situation" = FAE (Ross). Don't swap the two heuristics.

7. Neuropsych & clinical phenomena

Phenomenon / signWhoWhat it is
Phantom limbMitchell; RamachandranSensation/pain in an amputated limb (cortical remapping).
Capgras delusionJoseph CapgrasBelief that a loved one has been replaced by an identical impostor.
Fregoli delusion—Belief that different people are one person in disguise.
Cotard delusionJules CotardBelief that one is dead, doesn't exist, or is rotting (nihilistic delusion).
Folie à deuxLasègue & FalretA shared delusion transmitted between two close people.
AnosognosiaBabinskiUnawareness/denial of one's own deficit (e.g. paralysis after stroke).
Confabulation—Filling memory gaps with fabricated events without intent to lie (Korsakoff).
Hemispatial neglect—Ignoring one side of space after (usually right) parietal damage.
Sundowning—Worsening confusion/agitation in the late afternoon/evening (dementia).
Pseudobulbar affect—Uncontrollable laughing/crying out of proportion to feeling.
La belle indifférence—Surprising lack of concern about a disabling symptom (conversion disorder).
Delusional misidentification trio: Capgras = loved one is an impostor; Fregoli = strangers are one disguised person; Cotard = "I am dead." These three are a classic NIMHANS distractor set.
