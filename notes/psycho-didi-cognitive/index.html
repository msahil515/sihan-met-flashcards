1 · Sensation & Perception

The perceptual sequence runs stimulation → transduction → sensation → attention → perception. Perception can be driven two ways:

  • Top-down — expectation driven (knowledge and context shape what we perceive).
  • Bottom-up — stimulus / data driven (the raw sensory input drives perception).
Top-down vs bottom-up. Top-down = expectation/knowledge driven (constructivist, e.g. Gregory). Bottom-up = stimulus/data driven (direct/ecological, e.g. Gibson). Endogenous attention is top-down; exogenous attention is bottom-up.

Gestalt psychology

Core dictum: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. Key ideas include the Phi phenomenon and the law of Prägnanz — every stimulus pattern is seen so that the resulting structure is as simple as possible.

Max Wertheimer — founder of Gestalt perception; described the Phi phenomenon, a type of stroboscopic motion, best seen at a time interval of 30 to 200 milliseconds.

Perceptual Constancy

  • Colour / brightness constancy — perceiving colour or brightness as constant despite changes in lighting.
  • Size constancy — perceiving an object as the same size regardless of its distance from the observer.
  • Shape constancy — perceiving an object as maintaining its shape despite changes in orientation.

Apparent (Illusory) Motion

Perception of motion when there actually is none.

  • Autokinetic effect — a stationary point of light appears to move in a dark room. Found by Muzafer Sherif (1935).
  • Induced motion — motion of one object causes a nearby stationary object to appear to move (e.g. clouds moving past the moon make the moon appear to race through the clouds).
  • Phi phenomenon — an optical illusion in which stationary objects shown in rapid succession, past the threshold at which they can be perceived separately, appear to move. Temporal threshold: 30–200 ms.
  • Beta phenomenon — similar to the phi phenomenon but usually circular motion.
  • Critical Flicker Fusion Frequency — the frequency at which a flickering light appears steady; the threshold where separate/discontinuous images seem to fuse.
  • Motion parallax — as we move, nearby objects appear to glide rapidly past us while more distant objects move more slowly (extremely far objects such as the moon seem to move away from you).
  • Stroboscopic effect — a rapid succession of still images or lights creates the illusion of motion.

Binocular cues

  • Binocular convergence — depth cue based on the inward movement of the eyes as they focus on nearby objects.
  • Retinal disparity — the slight difference in the images seen by each eye, used by the brain to perceive depth.
    • Vertical disparity — due to the image falling on a different place in the retina.
    • Horizontal disparity — due to the difference in angles at which the eyes are placed.
  • Horopter — the imaginary line at which an object placed there produces no disparity.
Eleanor Gibson & Richard Walk — the Visual Cliff depth-perception experiment on 3–6 month-old babies; concluded that depth perception is innate.

Perceptual Set

Refers to the influence of values on perception.

Jerome Bruner & Goodman (1947) — coins were perceived as larger than same-size cardboard discs, and more so by poor children than rich children. Because coin size correlates with value, valuable coins "loomed larger" in perception even when the cardboard was the same size.

Top-Down Theories of Perception

  • Constructivist theory (Processing theory) — proponent Richard Gregory (1970). Perception is a hypothesis based on prior knowledge; we actively construct our perception of reality from the environment and stored information. Higher-order thinking is involved.
  • Template Matching theory — a template is an internal construct that, when matched by sensory stimuli, leads to recognition of an object. "Lock and key" idea of pattern recognition; the object must match the template exactly.
  • Prototype theory — an alternative to template matching. A prototype for each object is stored in memory; a pattern is checked against the prototype and recognised if resemblance is found. Not as rigid as templates. Shortcoming: does not account for exceptions.

Bottom-Up Theories of Perception

J. J. Gibson — Direct (Ecological) Perception: the information in the stimulus itself is the important element; learning and cognition are unnecessary because there is enough information in the environment to make an interpretation.
  • Constraints — a barrier in the environment that helps perception.
  • Affordance — a mediator in the environment that helps perception.
  • Light feedback — from the reflection of light we decide whether to move forward or backward.

Feature Detection theory — object recognition is high-order processing preceded by a step in which complex incoming stimuli are identified according to simpler features.

Irving Biederman (1987) — Geon theory (aka recognition by components): the human information-processing system has a limited number of simple geometric shapes (geons, "geometric ions") that combine to form meaningful objects. Identifying an object from its geons = the principle of componential recovery; each geon's unique set of Non-Accidental Properties gives geons the property of discriminability.
Oliver Selfridge — Pandemonium Model ("pandemonium" = a very noisy, chaotic place). Four kinds of demons: image demons (sensation) → feature demons (feature separation) → cognitive demons (compare with memory) → decision demons (perception).

Properties of colour

  • Visible spectrum of light: 380 to 760 nm.
  • Hue — the actual colour seen.
  • Saturation — concentration of colour.
  • Brightness — intensity of light.
Young–Helmholtz — Trichromatic theory of colour: the retina has three types of colour receptor, each especially sensitive to red, green or blue; stimulating combinations produces all other colours.
Ewald Hering — Opponent-Process theory: three sets of opponent retinal processes enable colour vision — red–green, yellow–blue, white–black. If one colour of a pair is perceived, the other cannot be. Explains afterimages.

Audition

Properties of sound

  • Audible frequency: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
  • Amplitude — height of the sound wave; determines loudness.
  • Frequency — waves per second; determines pitch.
  • Timbre — quality of sound.
Hermann von Helmholtz — Place theory: we hear different pitches because different sound waves trigger activity at different places along the cochlea's basilar membrane. Best explains high pitches.
Rinne & Rutherford (1880) — Frequency theory: the brain reads pitch by monitoring the frequency of neural impulses travelling up the auditory nerve. Does not explain sound sensing above 1000 Hz. Best explains low pitches.
Key — Volley principle. Supports frequency theory: neurons fire in alternating rapid succession so that, combined, they achieve a frequency above 1000 waves per second.

Auditory pathway: Pinna → Auditory canal → Tympanic membrane → Hammer (Malleus) → Anvil (Incus) → Stirrup (Stapes) → Cochlea → Auditory nerve → Cochlear nerve → Superior olivary complex → Lateral lemniscus → Inferior colliculus → Medial geniculate body → Auditory cortex.

Other senses

  • Kinesthetic / Proprioception — sense of body position and movements.
  • Vestibular — sense of head positioning (parietal lobe); semi-circular canals and cerebellum involved.

3 · Theories of Attention

Attention is the concentration of mental activity that lets you take in a limited portion of the vast stream of information available from both your sensory world and your memory. Sequence: sensation → attention → perception.

Types of attention (source)

  • Exogenous — controlled by the stimulus; bottom-up.
  • Endogenous — the brain internally decides what is important; top-down.
Sohlberg & Mateer (1987, 1989) — Hierarchical model of attention: focused → sustained → selective → alternating → divided.
  • Focused attention — ability to focus attention on a stimulus.
  • Sustained attention (vigilance) — ability to attend to a stimulus/activity over a long period. Determinants: sensory factor, temporal certainty, spatial certainty, clarity of stimuli.
  • Selective attention — ability to attend to a specific stimulus in the presence of distracting stimuli.
  • Alternating attention — ability to shift focus between two or more stimuli.
  • Divided attention (multitasking) — ability to attend to different stimuli at the same time.

Signal Detection Theory (SDT)

Wesley W. Peterson & Theodore G. Birdsall — Signal Detection Theory: detection of a signal depends on sensitivity and response bias. Sensitivity (d′) = number of hits − number of false alarms.
Signal presentSignal absent
Responds "yes"Hit (correct)False alarm (incorrect)
Responds "no"Miss (incorrect)Correct rejection (correct)

For a liberal participant the receiver-operating (ROC) curve peaks towards the right; for a conservative participant it peaks towards the left.

Theories of Selective Attention

Selective attention is tested with a shadowing / dichotic listening task.

Colin Cherry (1953) — early selection model: no overlap in dichotic listening; we do not understand a stimulus before we perceive it.
Donald Broadbent (1958) — Filter model (a bottleneck model): we filter information right after we notice it, at the sensory level.
Anne Treisman (1980) — Attenuation model: explains the cocktail-party phenomenon. Instead of blocking stimuli out, the filter merely weakens the strength of stimuli other than the target.
Deutsch & Deutsch (1963) — Late Selection model: stimuli are filtered out only after being analysed for both their physical properties and their meaning.
Johnston & Heinz (1978) — Multimode attention theory: attention is a flexible system that selects a stimulus at three stages — (1) sensory representations created, (2) semantic representations created, (3) both sensory and semantic representations enter consciousness. More processing requires more mental effort.
Lavie & Tsal (1994) — Perceptual Load theory: perception is a limited-capacity process that proceeds automatically until that capacity is filled.
Anne Treisman & Garry Gelade (1980) — Feature Integration Theory (FIT): explains how an individual combines pieces of observable information about an object to form a complete perception. Two kinds of visual search: feature search (fast, pre-attentive, primitive features) and conjunction search (slower, effortful; targets defined by a conjunction of features).
Eriksen & St James (1986); Posner et al. — Spotlight theory of attention: people can attend to only one region of space at a time. Inspired by William James, attention has a focus (central area extracting high-resolution information where attention is directed), a fringe (extracts information more crudely, extending over a specified area), and a margin (the cut-off between them).

Shortcomings of selective attention

  • Inattentional blindness — people fail to see things that are actually there.
  • Change blindness — inability to detect changes in objects or scenes being viewed.

4 · Classical & Operant Conditioning

Key — definition. Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour as a result of experience.

Two basic kinds of learning

  • Non-associative learning (short term) — learning about a single stimulus:
    • Habituation — a decreased behavioural response to an innocuous stimulus.
    • Sensitization — an increase in a behavioural response to an intense stimulus.
  • Associative learning (long term) — learning relationships among events: classical conditioning and instrumental (operant) conditioning.

Classical Conditioning

Ivan Pavlov (1897) — first researched classical conditioning: a previously neutral stimulus becomes associated with another stimulus through repeated pairing.

Based on three broad principles:

  • Contiguity — nearness in space and time.
  • Contingency — prediction power.
  • Blocking — a CS blocks conditioning with another neutral stimulus.

Principles of conditioning

Second-order (higher-order) conditioning — once a CS elicits a CR, that CS can briefly be used as a US to condition a response to a new stimulus.

Contiguity and Contingency

Robert Rescorla — conditioning involves cognitive components. The CS must be a reliable predictor of the US (contingency); mere temporal contiguity is not enough.
  • Positive contingency — CS predicts the presence of the UCS → excitatory conditioning.
  • Zero contingency — no pattern, so no learning takes place.
  • Negative contingency — CS predicts the absence of the UCS → inhibitory conditioning.

Classical conditioning & biological preparedness

Animals and humans are biologically prepared to make certain connections more easily (e.g. learned taste aversions can be learnt in a single trial).

John Garcia & Robert Koelling (1966) — rats more readily learned certain associations: food more likely to become CS for nausea; noise more likely to become CS for shock.
Key — classical conditioning at a glance. Totally based on reflexes; response is elicited by the stimulus (also called respondent conditioning); passive learning; reflexes can be conditioned or unconditioned; magnitude of response matters; it is an S–S type of conditioning. The amygdala (lateral nucleus) organizes emotional responses provoked by aversive stimuli.

Operant Conditioning

Edward Thorndike — learning theory leading to operant conditioning; used a cat in a puzzle box (trial-and-error learning). His theory is called associationism / connectionism. Three laws: law of readiness, law of practice/exercise, and the law of effect (behaviour followed by pleasant consequences is repeated; behaviour followed by unpleasant consequences is stopped).
B. F. Skinner (1937) — coined the term operant conditioning. The Skinner box delivers food via a lever/disk; the food is a reinforcer and giving it is reinforcement. Operant/instrumental conditioning = learning the relationship between responses and outcomes (an S–R association).

Principles of operant conditioning

  • Acquisition — the rat learns to press the bar for food.
  • Extinction — the rat unlearns the bar–food connection and stops pressing.
  • Spontaneous recovery — after extinction and rest, the rat presses the bar again.
  • Generalization — the rat presses other objects that look like the bar.
  • Discrimination — the rat learns to press only a particular bar.

Reinforcement & Punishment

Positive (add)Negative (remove)
Reinforcement (increases behaviour)Deliver a pleasant stimulus after the response (add pleasant).Remove an unpleasant/aversive stimulus after the response (remove unpleasant).
Punishment (reduces behaviour)Deliver an unpleasant/aversive stimulus after the response (add unpleasant).Remove a pleasant stimulus after the response (remove pleasant).
Negative reinforcement ≠ punishment. Negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant to increase a behaviour; punishment reduces a behaviour. Don't confuse "negative" (remove) with "punishment" (weaken).

Shaping and Chaining

  • Shaping — reinforces the steps used to reach the desired behaviour.
  • Chaining — animals are taught to perform a number of responses successively to get a reward.

Reinforcement schedules

Two basic types: Continuous (rewarded every time; better for learning new behaviour) and Partial (better once behaviour is learnt; more resistant to extinction).

ScheduleReinforcement provided...
Fixed ratioAfter a fixed number of responses.
Variable ratioAfter a number of responses that varies unpredictably.
Fixed intervalAfter a fixed amount of time since the last reinforcement.
Variable intervalAfter an amount of time that varies unpredictably since the last reinforcement.

Learning and maintaining fear (two-factor)

  1. Stage 1 — classical conditioning / escape learning: through repeated pairing of the warning light (CS) and shock (UCS), the animal learns the light predicts the shock and shows a conditioned fear response (CR) to the light alone.
  2. Stage 2 — instrumental conditioning / avoidance learning: successful reduction of the aversive stimulus reinforces and strengthens the avoidance behaviour. It gives temporary relief, but the long-run consequences of avoidance are detrimental.

5 · Cognitive & Social Learning

Learned Helplessness

Martin Seligman — cognitive approach to learning: control over the outcome of behaviour is as important for learning as contiguity and contingency.

Experiment — Maier & Seligman (1976):

  • Stage 1: some dogs learn that whether they receive a shock depends on their own behaviour; other dogs learn they have no control over the shock.
  • Stage 2: both groups placed in a shuttle box; a tone signals an imminent shock, and the dog must jump the barrier to avoid it.
  • Found: control dogs learn the response rapidly; yoked dogs do not. They had learned that shocks were uncontrollable, and this non-control made avoidance learning impossible → learned helplessness.
Robert Woodworth — S–O–R approach: to predict the response we must know not only the stimulus but also the organism stimulated; human motivations and conscious decisions play an important part in behaviour.
Wolfgang Köhler (1925) — Insight learning, studied on chimpanzees: insight occurs when one suddenly realises how to solve a problem. Learning happens in a sudden way due to insight, not gradual strengthening of the S–R connection.

Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura — Social Learning Theory (aka observational learning): learning is achieved by mentally rehearsing and then imitating the observed actions of models. An S–O–R theory.

Process of learning (sequence): attention → retention → motor reproduction → motivation.

Bobo doll experiment — conditions: model hits doll and is reinforced (vicarious reinforcement), model hits doll and is not reinforced, model hits doll and is punished (vicarious punishment).

Key. Learning occurs in all three conditions irrespective of reinforcement; however, reproduction of behaviour was dependent on reinforcement.

Tolman's Purposive Behaviourism

Edward Tolman — Purposive behaviourism (aka latent learning, expectancy, place learning, sign-stimulus theory). Experiment: Tolman & Honzik (1930).
  • Group A — reinforced on each trial; Group B — never reinforced; Group C — reinforced only on day 11.
  • Found: Group C had actually learnt the maze without reinforcement (latent learning) but showed it once reinforcement appeared. The rats developed a cognitive map — a mental representation of the maze.

Components of learning: the significate (desired outcome/goal), the sign (stimulus prompting action), and means–end relations (the learner's internal thought process).

Field Theory

Kurt Lewin (1930) — Field Theory, based on Gestalt psychology, an S–O–R approach: a person's psychological state affects their social field and vice versa. "Field" = the totality of a person's experiences, needs and topography; field forces (intrinsic or extrinsic) drive behaviour.

Types of conflict

6 · Theories of Memory

Key — definition. Memory is our capacity to register, store and recover information over time — the persistence of learning over time. It is a complex mental system involving multiple stages of information processing.

Early models

In the mid-1960s researchers proposed two structures of memory:

  • Primary memory — proposed by William James; holds temporary information currently in use.
  • Secondary memory — proposed by Waugh & Norman (1965); holds information permanently or for a very long time.

Sperling & iconic memory

George Sperling — iconic memory: a type of visual memory lasting only a fraction of a second. Sensory memory capacity is much larger than believed. In Sperling's test, partial report was more successful than whole report; iconic memory capacity is 9–12× that of STM.

Multi-Store Model

Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968) — Multi-Store (modal) Model: information passes from one storage system to the next in a fixed order — Sensory memory → STM → LTM.

Flow: Sensory memory → (selective attention → encoding) → STM → (maintenance rehearsal keeps it in STM; elaborative rehearsal moves it) → LTM.

George Miller (1956) — the magic number 7 ± 2: the average number of items held in short-term memory. Emphasized recoding / chunking of information.

Working Memory Model

Alan Baddeley & Graham Hitch (1974) — Working Memory Model: temporarily holds and manipulates information during cognitive tasks. Memory span is determined by the speed with which we rehearse information.

STM vs Working memory. STM (Atkinson–Shiffrin) is a passive store (~7±2 items, 18–30 sec). Working memory (Baddeley–Hitch) is an active system that holds and manipulates information via a central executive plus slave subsystems.

Fergus Craik & Robert Lockhart (1972) — Levels of Processing model: information is more likely remembered when processed at a deep level. There is only one memory system.

Short-term memory testing

Brown–Peterson task — a test of STM using a trigram; the trigram was forgotten within 15–18 to 30 seconds when rehearsal was prevented.

Other memory types

  • Autobiographical memory — LTM related to the self (semantic + episodic).
  • Eidetic memory — photographic memory; children have it better.
  • Flashbulb memory — vivid memory of emotionally arousing, surprising events; a strong autobiographical subtype.

7 · Forgetting & Memory Distortion

Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) — systematically tested his own memory of lists of nonsense syllables. Found a huge drop in retention soon after learning that eventually levelled off (the method of savings); emphasized overlearning / revision.

Retrieval types

RetrievalNote
RecallWithout a cue — the hardest.
Cued recallEasier than free recall.
RecognitionEasiest.
RelearningThe most sensitive measure of learning.

Neural basis

  • Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) — persistent strengthening of a synaptic connection lasting minutes to hours; based on the Hebbian principle ("neurons that fire together, wire together"). NMDA glutamate receptors are necessary for memory formation; AMPA receptors — dendrites double their contact surface by splitting in two; terminal buttons release more neurotransmitter after potentiation; nitric oxide acts as a catalyst. In the lab, a high-frequency electric charge is applied to the pre-synaptic neuron.
  • Long-Term Depression (LTD) — long-term weakening of a synaptic connection; prunes unused synapses, trimming unimportant connections and leaving only the salient connections strengthened by LTP.

Theories of forgetting

  • Decay theory — forgetting is an automatic process; the engram for the information disappears.
  • Interference theory — supported by the serial-position curve.

Serial Position Curve

Represents the probability of recall of a word given its serial position in a list.

  • Primacy effect — superior recall of words at/near the beginning of a list (into LTM); disrupted by proactive interference.
  • Recency effect — superior recall of words at/near the end of a list (still in STM); disrupted by retroactive interference.
John Bergström (1892) — Interference theory of forgetting.
Proactive vs retroactive interference. Proactive — old information learned before disrupts recall of the new item (forward-acting). Retroactive — new information disrupts retrieval of previously learned information (backward-acting). Factors: learning new information unlearns previous; response competition between similar materials.

Motivated forgetting

Sigmund Freud — motivated forgetting via repression: we unconsciously prevent traumatic events from entering awareness to avoid anxiety or blows to self-concept. The repressed material is not lost, but hidden from consciousness.

Encoding specificity

Endel Tulving — encoding specificity. Two related types:
  • State-dependent memory — information is better retrieved in similar emotional and physical states to those at encoding.
  • Context-dependent memory — information is easier to retrieve in the place/environment where it was originally learnt.

The Zeigarnik effect

Bluma Zeigarnik — the Zeigarnik effect: unfinished, incomplete and unpleasant tasks are not forgotten as easily as completed ones.

The Seven Sins of Memory

Daniel Schacter (2001) — the Seven Sins of Memory.
  1. Transience
  2. Absent-mindedness
  3. Blocking — blocked by sensory information; e.g. the tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon.
  4. Suggestibility / misinformation — occurs due to a reality-monitoring error.
  5. Bias — five types: consistency, change, hindsight, egocentric, stereotypical.
  6. Misattribution — occurs due to a source-monitoring error.
  7. Persistence

(Also noted as memory failings: encoding failure.)

Distorted / manufactured memories

Three common factors that contribute to faulty reconstructions: source misattributions, exposure to misinformation, and effects of imagination.

Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues (1978) — the misinformation effect: participants watched a film of a traffic accident. Group 1 was asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" into each other (implying a severe accident); Group 2 heard "hit" (milder). Those who heard "smash" remembered higher speeds and were more likely to (falsely) report broken glass — there was none in the film.
Elizabeth Loftus, James Coan & Jacqueline Pickrell — the Lost in the Mall study: showed how easy it is to implant a fictitious memory of having been lost in a shopping mall as a child.

Mnemonic devices

Helpful for consolidation:

  • Categorical clustering — organizing a list into a set of categories.
  • Interactive images — creating images that link the isolated words in a list.
  • Pegword system — associating each new word with a word on a previously memorized list via an interactive image.
  • Method of loci — visualizing a walk around a familiar area with distinctive landmarks, linking landmarks to items.
  • Acronym — a word/expression whose letters each stand for another word or concept.
  • Acrostic — forming a sentence (rather than a single word) to help remember new words.
  • Keyword system — an interactive image linking the sound and meaning of a foreign word with a familiar word.

8 · Theories of Intelligence

Key — definition. Wechsler (1944): intelligence is the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with his environment.

The first theory of intelligence was Binet's unifactor theory; the second was Spearman's two-factor theory.

Spearman's Two-Factor Theory

Charles Spearman (1904) — Two-factor theory; laid the groundwork of factor analysis. Intelligence has a general 'g' factor (an overriding intelligence, almost exclusively inherited) and specific 's' factors (competence in specific abilities such as mathematics, language, music).

Spearman used the method of tetrad difference: the tetrad difference should be zero but was not, due to a common g factor. Tetrad equation: r12 × r34 = r23 × r14.

Cattell — Fluid & Crystallized Intelligence

Raymond Cattell — general intelligence = fluid (Gf) + crystallized (Gc) intelligence (aka the investment theory); used factor analysis and second-order factors.
Fluid intelligence (Gf)Crystallized intelligence (Gc)
NatureBasic capacity for learning and problem solving, independent of education/experience.Result of the interaction of fluid intelligence and culture; learned knowledge and skills.
DevelopmentGrows rapidly in adolescence to the 20s, then declines (does not disappear).Keeps increasing until death; created by the investment of fluid intelligence into the environment.
EnvironmentCannot be affected by environment.Strong environmental influence.
John Horn — expanded the Gf–Gc model with additional abilities: visual processing (Gv), short-term acquisition & retrieval (SAR / Gsm), long-term storage & retrieval (TSR / Glr), processing speed (Gs), auditory processing (Ga), reaction/decision speed (Gt), quantitative ability (Gq), and broad reading–writing (Grw).

Jensen's view of intelligence

Arthur Jensen (1969) — divides intelligence into two sets of abilities (method of study: regression).
  • Level 1 abilities — memory functions and simple associative learning; equally distributed among the races.
  • Level 2 abilities — abstract reasoning and conceptual thought; White and Asian students showed advantages on Level 2 tests.

Carroll's Three-Stratum Theory (1993)

A hierarchy: Stratum 3 = general intelligence (g); Stratum 2 = broad abilities; Stratum 1 = ~70 narrow abilities. Broad abilities include: Fluid intelligence (Gf), Crystallized intelligence (Gc), General Memory and Learning (Gy), Broad visual perception (Gv), Broad auditory perception (Gu), Broad retrieval ability (Gr), Broad cognitive speediness (Gs), and Processing speed / RT decision speed (Gt).

Vernon's Hierarchical Theory

Philip Vernon — hierarchical theory.
  • Level 1: general ability (g).
  • Level 2 (major group): verbal-educational (v:ed) and spatial-mechanical (k:m).
  • Level 3 (minor group): creative abilities, verbal fluency, numerical factors, spatial factors, psychomotor factors, mechanical-information factors.
  • Level 4: specific abilities (s).

Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities (1938)

L. L. Thurstone — Primary Mental Abilities (group-factor theory); each primary ability is distinct, rejected the g factor.

The seven factors (in order): Verbal comprehension, Verbal fluency, Numerical ability, Memory, Perceptual speed, Inductive reasoning, Spatial visualization.

J. P. Guilford — Structure of Intellect: every intellectual task is classified by Operation × Content × Product = 5 × 5 × 6 = 150 factors (per the model dimensions below).

Thorndike's theory of intelligence (1920)

Edward Thorndike — classified intelligence into concrete / mechanical, abstract, and social intelligence. Does not believe in a g factor; no factor analysis.

Four attributes of intelligence: level (difficulty of a solvable task), range (number of tasks at a given difficulty), area (total situations at each level = summation of all ranges), and speed (rapidity of response). Factors: 6 × 6 × 5 = 180 (third-order factors).

Gardner's Multiple Intelligences

Howard Gardner — Theory of Multiple Intelligences, in his book Frames of Mind; qualitative method, no factor analysis.

Types: Linguistic, Logical/Mathematical, Musical, Spatial, Bodily/Kinesthetic, Interpersonal, Intrapersonal, Naturalistic, Existentialist.

Sternberg's Triarchic Theory

Robert Sternberg — Triarchic theory; no factor analysis, rejected g; disagrees with Spearman, agrees with Gardner.

PASS Theory

Das, Naglieri & Kirby (1994) — PASS theory of intelligence, based on Alexander R. Luria's research; did not accept the g factor. Components: potential abilities, environmental context, internal motivation.

Heritability & the Flynn effect

Francis Galton — began the study of heritability of intelligence, inspired by Darwin's On the Origin of Species. In Hereditary Genius he argued children inherit their intelligence from their parents.

Flynn effect — the observed rise in average IQ scores over generations.

9 · Intelligence & Creativity

Creativity is a group of mental abilities covered by divergent thinking. Components include fluency (swift current of ideas and tendency to alter direction), flexibility (readiness to change), originality (newness), and elaboration (detail).

Graham Wallas' theory of creativity (1926)

Graham Wallas (1926) — the first theory of creativity; five steps.
  1. Preparation
  2. Incubation — unconscious.
  3. Orientation
  4. Illumination — maximum creative thinking (insight).
  5. Verification — evaluation and revision.

4 Ps model of creativity

Mel Rhodes (1961) — the 4 Ps of creativity.

The 4 C model

James C. Kaufman & Ron Beghetto (2009) — the 4 C model.
  • Mini-C — only "I" appreciate it.
  • Little-C — everyday creativity; others also appreciate.
  • Pro-C — professional creativity.
  • Big-C — eminent creativity; universally appreciated.

Investment (Confluence) theory of creativity

Sternberg & Lubart (1995, 1996) — Investment / Confluence theory: creativity requires the confluence of Intelligence, Knowledge, Thinking styles, Personality, Motivation (and environment). Creativity is a superset of intelligence; captured by "buying low, selling high".

Torrance Test of Creative Thinking (TTCT)

An open-ended test with verbal and figural components.

Figural testScored on

Verbal tests include: Asking, Guessing causes, Guessing consequences, Product improvement, Unusual uses, Unusual questions, Just suppose — scored on fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration (flexibility optional in some).

Creativity–intelligence relationship

Guilford — Threshold hypothesis: students with lower intelligence scored significantly lower in creativity, but those with higher intelligence were neither significantly higher nor lower. Thus intelligence is necessary but not sufficient for creative thinking.
Getzels & Jackson (1962) — found positive but low correlations between creativity and intelligence; concluded that the difference between the high-creative and high-IQ child stems from the family environment.

10 · Thinking & Concepts

Key — definition. Thinking involves the mental manipulation and processing of information — generating and/or evaluating ideas, arguments, beliefs and statements. Its two major components are mental images and concepts.

Concepts

  • Natural concept — a mental representation of an object/event drawn from personal experience, used to categorize other objects/events.
  • Artificial concept — a concept defined by a specific set of characteristics.
  • Simple concept — has a single common feature.
  • Complex concept:
    • Conjunctive — simultaneous presence of two or more common characteristics.
    • Disjunctive — presence of one common characteristic or another.

Learning concepts (concept formation)

Bruner, Goodnow & Austin (1956) — the hypothesis-testing model of concept formation. Strategies participants may use:
StrategyDescription
Simultaneous scanningStart with all possible hypotheses and eliminate the untenable ones.
Successive scanningBegin with a single hypothesis, keep it if successful; if not, change it based on all previous experience.
Conservative focusingFormulate a hypothesis, pick a positive instance as focus, then reformulate changing only one feature at a time, noting each outcome.
Focus gamblingChange more than one feature at a time, gambling to determine the concept more quickly.

How concepts are organized

  • A schema is a mental construct consisting of a cluster of related concepts.
  • Concepts are organized in semantic networks — a complex web of associations.

Reasoning

  • Inductive reasoning — from specific observations to a general conclusion.
  • Deductive reasoning — from general premises to a specific conclusion.

Wertheimer's types of thinking

Max Wertheimer — productive vs reproductive thinking.
  • Productive thinking — thinking with insights that go beyond existing associations.
  • Reproductive thinking — thinking based on existing associations / what is already known.

Types of problem solving (Wertheimer): Type A — productive thinking; Type B — partially productive and partially unproductive; Type Y — blind trial and error.

Other modes of thinking

  • Autistic thinking — mental activity directed inward and subjective; inner thoughts and individual reality; narcissistic, egocentric processes such as fantasizing and daydreaming with little relation to reality.
  • Dereistic thinking (given by Freud) — thinking not bound by logic or reality, directed away from reality, not following ordinary rules of logic; often seen in children and creative individuals; excessive daydreaming leading to negative consequences.

11 · Problem Solving, Reasoning & Decision-Making

Formal vs informal problem-solving methods

Formal methods:

  • Trial and error — try a number of different solutions until the correct one is reached.
  • Algorithm — sequences of operations that, in theory, guarantee a solution.

Heuristics — general rules of thumb that help achieve a solution while decreasing effort by letting us examine fewer cues. Informal heuristics:

HeuristicDescription
SatisficingConsider options one by one and select the first that is satisfactory / good enough to meet a minimum level of acceptability.
Elimination by aspectsEliminate alternatives by focusing on aspects of each, one at a time.
Selection by aspectsSelect from alternatives by focusing on aspects of each, one at a time.
Lexicographic heuristicChoose the option best on a single criterion, ignoring other factors.
AnalogyUse an old solution to solve a new problem.
Means–end analysisCompare current position with the goal and take steps to reduce the difference. Example: Tower of Hanoi.
Repeated state avoidanceA formal heuristic: avoiding repetition leads to solution in the minimum number of steps.
Hill climbingA formal heuristic: combine two or more steps into a single step to reach the goal.

Judgment & decision-making heuristics

HeuristicDescription
AvailabilityJudge frequency/likelihood by the ease with which relevant instances come to mind.
RepresentativenessJudge frequency/likelihood by how much the event resembles the typical case.
Anchoring & adjustmentJudge by starting from an anchor and adjusting up or down.
SimulationJudge by the ease with which you can imagine or mentally simulate an event.
Peak–endJudge past experiences almost entirely on their peak and their end.
Base rateDecide based on probability (base rates).
ContagionDecide based on the idea that things once in contact continue to influence each other.

Halo effect — being influenced by a person's initial impression or external features (favourable). Horn effect — allowing an unfavourable first impression to influence overall judgment.

Obstacles to problem solving

  • Fallacies — a failure in reasoning that renders an argument invalid.
  • Fixed mindset — belief that you have a fixed amount of intelligence/skill and no effort can improve performance.
  • Mental set — tendency to keep trying a previously used solution even when an easier method exists; represents overactive top-down processing.
  • Functional fixedness — the uses we assign to an object tend to remain fixed/stable.

Metacognition in problem solving

Metacognition — cognition about cognitive phenomena; thinking about thinking. Components:

  • Metacognitive knowledge — declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge, conditional knowledge.
  • Metacognitive regulation — planning (prior to a problem), monitoring (during learning), and checking outcomes.

Cognitive biases

All cognitive biases can lead to cognitive distortions.

Bias / fallacyDescription
Confirmation biasAttending to information that confirms one's beliefs while ignoring the rest. (Related: Barnum effect — accepting vague, general personality descriptions as uniquely applicable to oneself, e.g. astrology.)
Hindsight biasBelieving, in retrospect, that we easily saw all the signs leading to an outcome.
Illusory correlationSeeing events/attributes as going together even when they do not.

Gambler's fallacyBelief that if an event occurred more than normal in the past, it is less likely in the future.

12 · Theories of Language Development

Key. Sensitive period of language development: 0 years to 5–7 years.

When does language develop?

AgeMilestone
2–4 monthsPre-vocal learning; cooing.
6 monthsBabbling; phonemes — meaningless units of speech.
1 yearLearns morphemes — the smallest unit of speech that carries meaning (content morphemes; function morphemes = tense / singular–plural). Uses a holophrase — one word conveying a full sentence.
2 yearsLearns syntax — the rules of grammar.
3 yearsTelegraphic speech — 2–3 word phrases; makes errors of growth from overuse of syntax.
4–5 yearsLearns pragmatics (not necessary for language development).

Holophrase errors: over-extension (child calls every dog "Timmy") and under-extension (mummy is only my mummy). Errors of growth: overregularization and over-generalisation.

Components of language

  • Phonology — sound system of a language.
  • Morphology — units of meaning involved in word formation.
  • Syntax — rules for combining words into phrases/sentences.
  • Semantics — meaning of words and sentences.
  • Pragmatics — appropriate use of language in different contexts.

Serial order of learning: Phonemes → Morphemes → Semantics (Holophrase) → Syntax → Pragmatics.

Sapir–Whorf Hypothesis

Sapir–Whorf Hypothesis (1956) — linguistic relativity: speakers of different languages have differing cognitive systems that influence how they think about the world; we cannot think about things not experienced in our culture.

Behaviorist approach

B. F. Skinner — identified six elementary verbal operants plus one secondary operant.
Verbal operantDescription
MandA demand.
TactSpeaker names things/actions in the environment; the "naming explosion".
EchoicRepeating what has been heard.
IntraverbalSpeaker responds to another speaker's verbal behaviour.
TextualReading without any implication that the reader understands what is read.
TranscriptionWriting/spelling words that are spoken to the individual (Skinner also called this "taking dictation").
Autoclitic (secondary)Softens or modifies the effect of subsequent statements (e.g. "I don't mean to criticize, but...").

Vygotsky's theory of language

Lev Vygotsky — stages of language development.
  1. Primitive stage — infant experimenting with sound production; cooing / "ga-ga".
  2. Naive stage — baby speaks words without grasping their purpose and meaning.
  3. External (egocentric) stage — child uses objects to signify meaning and words; rhyming is used in this phase.
  4. Ingrowth stage — inner speech shortens (called predication).

Nativist theory — Chomsky

Noam Chomsky — nativist theory: children have an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD). Language is modular — a set of specific linguistic abilities that do not follow the principles of other cognitive processes.
  • Deep structure — the underlying syntactic structure linking phrase structures through transformation rules.
  • Surface structure — the phrase structures resulting from applying transformational rules.
  • Transformational grammar — two sentences may have very different surface structures but similar deep structures.
  • Ambiguous sentences — two sentences may have identical surface structures but very different deep structures.

Bruner — LASS

Jerome Bruner — Language Acquisition Support System (LASS): parents, teachers and elders act as a support system that helps in the acquisition of language.
