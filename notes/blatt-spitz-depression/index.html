Blatt & Spitz — Anaclitic vs Introjective Depression

Abnormal · Mood disorders · Exam-grade · Built 2026-06-07

Why this page exists: Both Blatt and Spitz showed up on the NIMHANS paper. That makes them high-yield, not edge cases. This is the focused one-pager; the same content folds into the Abnormal book's mood-disorders chapter. The single biggest trap is below: there are two different "anaclitic depressions" (one infant, one adult) and they get conflated constantly.
THE TRAP, up front: "Anaclitic depression" means two different things depending on who said it.
  • Spitz's anaclitic depression = an infant syndrome caused by separation from the mother (clinical/developmental observation, 1946).
  • Blatt's anaclitic depression = an adult depression subtype / personality configuration centred on dependency (theory of depression, 1974).
Both come from the same root word "anaclitic" = leaning on / depending on (Greek anaklinein, to lean upon). Same word, different age group, different theorist. If a stem mentions infants/separation → Spitz. If it mentions an adult dependency-vs-self-criticism split → Blatt.

1. The Word: "Anaclitic"

  • Anaclitic = leaning on / dependent on another for support. From Greek anaklinein ("to lean upon").
  • Freudian root: anaclitic object-choice = choosing a love object on the model of the person who fed/protected you (mother), i.e. love based on dependency, vs narcissistic object-choice (loving someone like yourself).
  • So "anaclitic depression" in both Spitz and Blatt = a depression organised around dependency and loss of the caregiving/loved other.

2. Blatt — Two-Polarities (Two-Configurations) Model

Sidney J. Blatt (Yale) argued that personality develops along two fundamental, lifelong coordinates / developmental lines:

LineAlso calledWhat it is about
AnacliticRelatedness (interpersonal)Establishing and maintaining close, satisfying relationships — intimacy, trust, mutuality.
IntrojectiveSelf-definition (identity)Establishing a stable, coherent, realistic, positive sense of self — autonomy, identity, self-worth.
Core claim: Normal personality development is a dialectic — the two lines develop in reciprocal, synergistic interaction. You need relatedness to define a self, and a self to relate. Maturity = a balance of both.
  • Blatt, 1974 (and refined through 1992, 2004/2008 Polarities of Experience).
  • Psychopathology = a one-sided, exaggerated over-development of one line at the expense of the other. You compensate on one axis because the other is distorted.
  • Two corresponding configurations of depression fall out of this (next section).

3. Blatt's Two Types of Depression

TRAP: Don't map "anaclitic = sad" and "introjective = guilty" too crudely. The cleaner split: anaclitic depression is about the relationship (losing the other); introjective depression is about the self (failing the self/standards). Dependency vs self-criticism.

3.1 How Blatt's split lines up with other two-factor depression models

Examiners love asking which models are "the same idea under different names." Blatt's anaclitic/introjective parallels:

One-liner: Blatt = Beck's sociotropy/autonomy from a psychoanalytic angle. Anaclitic ≈ sociotropic; introjective ≈ autonomous.

3.2 The measure

  • Depressive Experiences Questionnaire (DEQ) — Blatt, D'Afflitti & Quinlan (1976).
  • Yields the factors Dependency (anaclitic) and Self-Criticism (introjective) — plus an Efficacy factor.
  • If a question names the DEQ → it's Blatt's anaclitic/introjective framework.

3.3 Therapeutic change — the two types improve through different mechanisms

Blatt did not just describe the two types, he tied them to how people get better in therapy (his reanalysis of the Menninger Psychotherapy Research Project, and later work on therapeutic action):

Anaclitic (dependent)Introjective (self-critical)
What drives improvementThe therapeutic relationship itself — feeling held, cared for, securely attached to the therapistInsight & interpretation — understanding, mastery, cognitive/interpretive work
More responsive toSupportive, relational elements of treatmentInterpretive, insight-oriented elements
Changes shown most inQuality of interpersonal relationshipsSelf-concept, cognitive functioning, reduced self-criticism
Exam line: Blatt's two types improve through different mechanisms — anaclitic patients respond to the relationship, introjective patients respond to insight. (This is why the model matters for treatment matching, not just diagnosis.)

3.4 Perfectionism and suicide — the dangerous pole

  • Blatt's later signature work was on perfectionism, especially self-critical perfectionism (the introjective pole taken to the extreme).
  • Key paper: Blatt (1995), "The Destructiveness of Perfectionism" (American Psychologist) — he linked harsh, self-critical perfectionism to severe depression, poor treatment response, and suicide.
  • He drew on high-achieving people who died by suicide despite outward success: the relentless inner standard, never "good enough," is lethal.
TRAP / high-yield: Between the two types it is the introjective / self-critical one that carries the higher suicide risk — the self-punishing, perfectionistic route. Anaclitic = clings and seeks care; introjective = turns the attack inward.

4. Spitz — Anaclitic Depression (the infant syndrome)

René Spitz (1887–1974), Austrian-American psychoanalyst, studied infants in institutions and foundling homes.

  • Anaclitic depression (Spitz, 1946): a syndrome in infants roughly 6–18 months old who, after forming a good, normal attachment to the mother, are separated from her for a prolonged period (about 3 months) with no adequate substitute caregiver.
  • Course: at first the infant protests/cries and clings; then withdraws, becomes weepy, apprehensive, sad, refuses contact; loses appetite and weight, develops insomnia; motor/cognitive development slows and regresses; becomes susceptible to infection; a frozen, dejected expression.
  • Reversibility: if the mother (or an adequate substitute) is returned within ~3–5 months, the infant typically recovers. The key is that the infant had a good relationship and then lost it — it is a depression of loss, not of never-having-had.
  • "Anaclitic" because the depression follows the loss of the love object on whom the infant was anaclitically (dependently) leaning.
Spitz anaclitic depression in one line: good attachment → prolonged separation (~3 months) in mid-infancy → withdrawal, weight loss, developmental arrest → reversible if the mother comes back in time.

4.1 The bridge to attachment theory — protest → despair → detachment

  • Spitz's observations of the infant's reaction to separation (first protest/crying and clinging, then the withdrawn, weepy, despairing anaclitic-depression phase) prefigure Bowlby's three-stage separation sequence:
StageBowlby (separation response)Spitz's parallel
1Protest — crying, searching, clinging, angerInitial protest/clinging at separation
2Despair — withdrawal, sadness, hopelessnessThe anaclitic-depression phase: weepy, withdrawn, dejected
3Detachment — apparent indifference, defensive shutdownSlides toward the deeper deterioration if loss persists
The chain examiners love: emotional bond > physical provision; separation produces protest → despair → detachment; early deprivation leaves lasting effects. Spitz is one of the founding maternal-deprivation studies, feeding straight into Bowlby (separation/loss), Harlow (the cloth vs wire monkeys — contact comfort over food), and Ainsworth (Strange Situation / attachment types).

5. Spitz — Hospitalism (the severe, often irreversible deprivation)

  • Hospitalism (Spitz, 1945): the extreme, prolonged deterioration — physical, cognitive, emotional — of infants raised in institutions (foundling homes) under severe emotional / maternal deprivation, even when physical and nutritional needs are met and hygiene is good.
  • Spitz's classic comparison:
    • Foundling Home — infants cared for by overworked nurses (about one nurse to seven infants), almost no individual mothering. Despite clean, hygienic, well-fed conditions, infants showed catastrophic decline: severe developmental delay, marasmus (wasting), high susceptibility to infection, and a very high mortality rate.
    • Nursery (a women's prison) — infants cared for by their own mothers. These infants thrived and developed normally.
  • Conclusion: it is maternal/emotional contact, not just food and hygiene, that infants need. Emotional deprivation alone produces devastating, often irreversible harm.
  • Linked downstream to marasmus (failure to thrive / wasting) and to Bowlby's maternal deprivation work and Harlow's monkeys.

6. Spitz: Anaclitic Depression vs Hospitalism

TRAP: Both are Spitz, both are infants, both are emotional deprivation. The dividing line is partial & reversible (anaclitic depression) vs total & often irreversible (hospitalism). Hospitalism is the worse, institutional one with the high death rate.

8. High-Yield MCQ Lines

  1. Anaclitic = leaning on / dependent (Greek, to lean upon).
  2. Spitz, 1946: anaclitic depression = infant separated from an attached mother for ~3 months → withdrawal, weight loss, developmental arrest; reversible if she returns in time.
  3. Spitz, 1945: hospitalism = institution-reared infants, severe emotional deprivation despite good physical care → marasmus, high mortality, often irreversible.
  4. Spitz's foundling-home vs nursery study showed emotional/maternal contact matters more than food & hygiene.
  5. Blatt's two-polarities model: personality develops along relatedness (anaclitic) and self-definition (introjective) lines, in a dialectic.
  6. Blatt's anaclitic depression = dependency, helplessness, fear of abandonment, longing to be cared for.
  7. Blatt's introjective depression = self-criticism, guilt, worthlessness, harsh superego, failure to meet standards.
  8. Blatt ≈ Beck: anaclitic ≈ sociotropy, introjective ≈ autonomy. Also Arieti's dominant-other vs dominant-goal; Bakan's communion vs agency.
  9. Measure of Blatt's types = Depressive Experiences Questionnaire (DEQ) (1976): Dependency, Self-Criticism, Efficacy.
  10. Therapeutic change differs by type: anaclitic patients improve via the relationship, introjective patients via insight/interpretation (Blatt, Menninger reanalysis).
  11. Higher suicide risk = introjective / self-critical perfectionist. Blatt (1995), "The Destructiveness of Perfectionism."
  12. Spitz prefigures Bowlby: protest → despair → detachment; founding maternal-deprivation study (with Harlow, Ainsworth, Bowlby).
  13. Do not confuse Spitz's infant anaclitic depression with Blatt's adult anaclitic depression subtype.

9. 30-Second Recall Script

"Anaclitic" = leaning/dependent. Two theorists, two age groups. Spitz (infants): anaclitic depression (1946) is an infant who had a good mother, lost her ~3 months in mid-infancy, withdrew, lost weight, stopped developing — reversible if she comes back; hospitalism (1945) is institution-reared infants with total emotional deprivation despite food & hygiene — marasmus, high death rate, often irreversible (foundling home vs prison nursery study). Blatt (adults): personality grows along two lines — relatedness (anaclitic) and self-definition (introjective) — in a dialectic; pathology = lopsided over-development of one. Anaclitic depression = dependency, abandonment fear, wanting care; introjective depression = self-criticism, guilt, harsh superego, failing one's standards. Maps onto Beck's sociotropy/autonomy. Measured by the DEQ. The two types even improve differently in therapy — anaclitic via the relationship, introjective via insight; and the introjective/self-critical perfectionist carries the higher suicide risk (Blatt, "Destructiveness of Perfectionism," 1995). Spitz's separation reaction prefigures Bowlby's protest → despair → detachment.
