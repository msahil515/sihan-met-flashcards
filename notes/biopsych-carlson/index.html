Biopsychology — Carlson depth pass

Source: Carlson & Birkett, Physiology of Behavior (the book Sihan named on 2026-05-28). This page is the delta on top of the Pinel chapter: it walks the systems Carlson goes deeper on than Pinel does, in Carlson's order, and locks the parts NIMHANS / MET love to test. 1. How Carlson is organised (and why that matters)

Carlson is a mechanism-first physiology book, not a "tour of the field" book. Pinel writes around case studies and divisions; Carlson writes around systems: neurotransmitter system → behavior it produces → drug that hits that system → disorder when it fails. If a question asks "which transmitter / which circuit / which receptor / which drug", Carlson is the book it was written from.

His chapters (in his order, condensed): structure of the nervous system → neurons & conduction → synaptic transmission → drugs & receptors → methods → vision → audition & somatosenses → control of movement → sleep → reproductive behavior → emotion → ingestive behavior → learning & memory → communication → neurological disorders → schizophrenia, mood disorders, anxiety → autism, ADHD, stress, addiction.

Lock it: when an MCQ stem names a receptor subtype (D2, 5-HT2A, NMDA, GABA-A α1, μ-opioid), a circuit (mesolimbic, nigrostriatal, mesocortical, tuberoinfundibular, locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe), or a drug class with its mechanism (NSRI, MAO-B inhibitor, atypical antipsychotic), reach for Carlson, not Pinel.

2. Neurotransmitter systems, system by system

Carlson's signature move: don't memorise transmitters in a list, learn each one as a system (cell-body cluster → projection pathway → receptors → behavior → drug). NIMHANS leans on these.

Dopamine — three pathways, one transmitter

PathwayFrom → ToFunctionWhat happens when it fails
NigrostriatalSubstantia nigra (pars compacta) → dorsal striatum (caudate + putamen)Motor initiation, habit learningLoss = Parkinson's disease
MesolimbicVentral tegmental area (VTA) → nucleus accumbens (NAc, ventral striatum), amygdala, hippocampusReward, motivation, "wanting"Hyperactivity = positive symptoms of schizophrenia; addiction hijacks it
MesocorticalVTA → prefrontal cortexWorking memory, executive control, motivationHypoactivity = negative symptoms of schizophrenia
TuberoinfundibularArcuate nucleus (hypothalamus) → median eminenceInhibits prolactin releaseD2-blocking antipsychotics → hyperprolactinaemia → amenorrhoea, gynaecomastia

Receptors: 5 subtypes, two families. D1-like (D1, D5) are Gs-coupled → ↑ cAMP. D2-like (D2, D3, D4) are Gi-coupled → ↓ cAMP. Classical antipsychotics block D2. Atypicals add 5-HT2A blockade.

TRAP — DA pathway naming. "Mesolimbic" and "mesocortical" share the VTA origin; the difference is the target. Mesolimbic → NAc (reward); mesocortical → PFC (cognition). "Nigrostriatal" is a separate origin (substantia nigra, not VTA).

Norepinephrine — one nucleus, the whole brain

Cell bodies live in the locus coeruleus (pons). One small cluster projects diffusely across cortex, thalamus, hypothalamus, cerebellum, spinal cord. Function: arousal, vigilance, attention to novelty. NE firing rises during alertness, falls during sleep, peaks during stress. α1, α2, β1, β2, β3 receptors. β-blockers cross the blood-brain barrier (propranolol) → reduce somatic anxiety.

Serotonin (5-HT) — raphe nuclei, fourteen receptors

Cell bodies: raphe nuclei (midline brainstem). Dorsal raphe → forebrain, midbrain; median raphe → limbic system; caudal raphe → spinal cord. ≥14 receptor subtypes; the exam-relevant ones:

  • 5-HT1A — autoreceptor on raphe neurons; postsynaptic in hippocampus. Buspirone is a 5-HT1A partial agonist (anxiolytic).
  • 5-HT2A — psychedelic site (LSD, psilocybin agonists); atypical antipsychotics antagonise it.
  • 5-HT3 — the only ionotropic 5-HT receptor; ondansetron antagonist (antiemetic).

SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram) raise synaptic 5-HT by blocking the reuptake transporter SERT.

Acetylcholine — two systems

  • Basal forebrain (nucleus basalis of Meynert + medial septum) → cortex + hippocampus. Drives attention & learning. Lost first in Alzheimer's — basis for donepezil / rivastigmine / galantamine.
  • Pontine cholinergic (PPT + LDT) → thalamus + basal forebrain. Drives cortical arousal in waking and REM sleep.

Receptors: nicotinic (ionotropic, fast, at NMJ + brain) and muscarinic M1–M5 (metabotropic). Anticholinergic drugs (atropine, benztropine, scopolamine) cause confusion + memory loss + dry mouth + mydriasis — classic in old-age psychiatry.

GABA & glutamate — the workhorses

Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter (≈ 80% of cortical synapses). Receptors:

  • AMPA — fast Na+ ionotropic; "the everyday excitation".
  • NMDA — Na+/Ca2+ ionotropic, voltage-and-glutamate-gated, blocked at rest by Mg2+. Carrier of LTP. Blocked by ketamine, PCP, MK-801 → schizophrenia-like state.
  • mGluR — metabotropic, modulatory.

GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter. GABA-A is an ionotropic Cl⁻ channel; benzodiazepines + barbiturates + alcohol + neurosteroids all bind allosterically to it. GABA-B is metabotropic (Gi) → K⁺ efflux; baclofen agonist.

TRAP — benzo vs barbiturate site. Both are GABA-A positive allosteric modulators but at different sites. Benzos increase the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening; barbiturates increase the duration. That is why barbiturates kill at overdose (open the channel all the way) and benzos do not, on their own.

Neuropeptides — the slow channel

Peptides act on G-protein receptors with slow, modulatory effects. The exam-rated set: endorphin / enkephalin / dynorphin (opioids — μ, δ, κ receptors), substance P (pain), oxytocin / vasopressin (bonding, fluid balance), NPY / AgRP (hunger), orexin / hypocretin (wakefulness; loss = narcolepsy).

Recap §2. Each NT system has a cell-body cluster, a projection, a receptor family, a behavior, and a drug. Lock the table: DA = VTA/SN, NE = locus coeruleus, 5-HT = raphe, ACh = basal forebrain + pontine, Glu/GABA = everywhere. Pathway naming + receptor subtypes are the MCQ engine.

3. Reward circuit & mesolimbic dopamine

This is Carlson's signature chapter. Pinel covers it; Carlson lives in it.

The core circuit, end-to-end

  1. VTA (ventral tegmental area, midbrain) is the origin of mesolimbic DA neurons.
  2. They project up the medial forebrain bundle (MFB) — the very bundle Olds & Milner (1954) stuck electrodes into and got self-stimulation.
  3. The main target is the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in the ventral striatum.
  4. From NAc, output goes to the ventral pallidum, then back via mediodorsal thalamus → prefrontal cortex.
  5. PFC sends glutamatergic feedback to NAc + VTA; amygdala & hippocampus also feed NAc with affective + contextual signal.

What dopamine actually signals

Not "pleasure" — Carlson is firm on this. Dopamine signals reward prediction error (Schultz's work, after Wolfram Schultz): when an outcome is better than expected, DA fires; when expected, DA doesn't change; when worse than expected, DA dips below baseline. This is the substrate Carlson uses to explain learning, craving and addiction.

Why it matters. "Wanting" (incentive salience, mesolimbic DA) is dissociable from "liking" (the hedonic hotspot, opioid + cannabinoid signalling in NAc shell + ventral pallidum). Addiction is mostly wanting without liking — the addict still craves long after the drug stops being fun. Berridge & Robinson's incentive-sensitisation theory is the model Carlson hangs the chapter on.

Self-stimulation, place preference, self-administration — the three behavioural assays

Lock it: reward = VTA → MFB → NAc; Olds & Milner = the experiment that founded the field; DA codes reward prediction error, not pleasure; "wanting" ≠ "liking".

4. Drugs of abuse — mechanism by class

The unifying claim Carlson makes: every drug of abuse, by some route, raises dopamine in NAc. Different drugs do it at different points in the circuit; that is what makes their effects (and treatments) differ.

TRAP — cocaine vs amphetamine. Both raise synaptic DA but by different mechanisms. Cocaine blocks the reuptake transporter (DAT). Amphetamine reverses the transporter, pumping DA out of the terminal. That is why amphetamine's effect outlasts the drug being in the cleft and why it depletes vesicles more.
TRAP — what alcohol "does". Alcohol is not a single-receptor drug. Pick the answer that says "GABA-A positive modulator + NMDA antagonist + endogenous opioid release", not "GABA agonist" alone. The opioid component is why naltrexone cuts alcohol craving.
NIMHANS angle. Substance-use disorders sit in clinical psychology + psychiatry. Likely stems: (a) which receptor mediates the "high" of opioids? μ. (b) Disulfiram mechanism? blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase → acetaldehyde build-up. (c) NMDA-stabilising anti-craving drug for alcohol? acamprosate. (d) Partial agonist at α4β2 nicotinic receptor? varenicline.

5. Sleep neurochemistry & the flip-flop switch

Pinel walks through the stages and the EEG. Carlson explains the switch: how the brain stays in one state and not another.

The wake-promoting system

  • Pontine cholinergic neurons (PPT, LDT) → thalamus → cortical desynchronisation.
  • Locus coeruleus (NE) → cortex.
  • Dorsal raphe (5-HT) → cortex.
  • Tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine) → cortex. (This is why old-generation H1 antihistamines sedate.)
  • Lateral hypothalamus orexin/hypocretin neurons → all the above. Orexin is the "supervisor" that holds the wake system on. Lose it → narcolepsy with cataplexy.

The sleep-promoting system

  • Ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) of the anterior hypothalamus → GABA + galanin → inhibits every wake-promoter above.

The flip-flop switch

VLPO inhibits the wake nuclei; the wake nuclei inhibit VLPO. Mutual inhibition makes the system bistable: one side wins fast, transitions are sharp, intermediate states are unstable. Orexin from the lateral hypothalamus biases the switch toward "wake" by exciting the wake nuclei. Lose orexin → switch flips erratically → sleep attacks in daytime, fragmented sleep at night = narcolepsy.

REM atonia

During REM, descending glycine + GABA inhibition from the sublaterodorsal nucleus (subLDT) → medulla (gigantocellular nucleus) → spinal motor neurons paralyses skeletal muscle (sparing diaphragm + eye muscles). Lesion this in cats (Jouvet's classic experiment) → "REM without atonia" → cats act out their dreams. The human analogue is REM sleep behaviour disorder, which strongly predicts Parkinson's / Lewy body dementia within 10–15 years.

Lock it: sleep = VLPO; wake = ARAS (cholinergic + NE + 5-HT + histamine) supervised by orexin; REM atonia = subLDT → medulla → spinal cord. Loss of orexin = narcolepsy. REM-without-atonia in humans = REM sleep behaviour disorder = prodromal Parkinson's.
TRAP — "which transmitter is highest in REM?" ACh. NE + 5-HT both drop in REM (the "aminergic-cholinergic" model — REM is when ACh wins). Histamine drops too.
NIMHANS angle. "Cataplexy on emotional triggers + sleep paralysis + hypnagogic hallucinations + sleep attacks" = narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy) = loss of orexin neurons. CSF orexin low. HLA-DQB1*06:02 association.

6. Hunger — arcuate POMC/AgRP loop

Pinel teaches the old dual-centre hypothesis (LH = hunger, VMH = satiety). Carlson teaches the modern arcuate-nucleus model, which the syllabus increasingly tests.

The two arcuate populations

NeuronsCo-releaseWhat they do
POMC / CART neuronsα-MSH (a POMC product) + CARTPromote satiety — project to paraventricular nucleus, activate MC4R receptor → ↓ food intake
NPY / AgRP neuronsNPY + AgRP + GABAPromote hunger — same target, AgRP blocks MC4R, NPY excites feeding directly. Inhibits POMC neurons too.

The peripheral signals that drive the loop

  • Leptin — from adipose tissue, proportional to fat stores. Excites POMC, inhibits NPY/AgRP → satiety. Leptin deficiency or receptor mutation → obesity. Most obese humans have high leptin = leptin resistance.
  • Insulin — from pancreas (β-cells) post-meal. Same direction as leptin (satiety).
  • Ghrelin — from empty stomach (X/A cells). Excites NPY/AgRP → hunger. Rises before meals, falls after.
  • PYY 3-36 — from gut L-cells, post-meal. Inhibits NPY → satiety.
  • CCK — from gut I-cells, fat/protein meal. Acts via vagus → NTS → satiety, meal termination.

The downstream integrator

Arcuate POMC + NPY/AgRP project to the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus, and the lateral hypothalamus (LH). LH neurons fire orexin → both promotes feeding and arousal (an animal must wake up to eat). This is why orexin sits at the centre of both chapters.

Lock it: leptin from fat + insulin from pancreas + PYY/CCK from gut = satiety (act on POMC). Ghrelin from stomach = hunger (acts on NPY/AgRP). LH orexin links hunger to arousal. MC4R mutations are the commonest monogenic cause of obesity in humans.
TRAP — leptin and obesity. Leptin levels are high in obese people, not low. The pathology is leptin resistance, not leptin deficiency (rare congenital exception aside).

7. HPA axis, glucocorticoids & the hippocampus

This is where Carlson, Sapolsky and clinical neuropsychology converge. The depth pass here is the receptor-level feedback story, because every MCQ on cortisol & the hippocampus rides on it.

The axis, end-to-end

  1. Stressor sensed by amygdala (threat) + prefrontal/hippocampus (contextual appraisal).
  2. Paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus releases CRH + vasopressin into the hypophyseal portal vein.
  3. Anterior pituitary corticotropes release ACTH into systemic blood.
  4. Adrenal cortex (zona fasciculata) releases cortisol.
  5. Cortisol acts everywhere (gluconeogenesis, immunosuppression, sodium retention) and feeds back to shut off CRH + ACTH.

Two receptors, two feedback loops

ReceptorAffinityWhereRole
MR (mineralocorticoid)HighHippocampus & some PVNTonic, basal cortisol regulation; saturated at low cortisol levels
GR (glucocorticoid)LowHippocampus, PFC, amygdala, hypothalamus, pituitary, everywhereStress-level feedback shutoff; recruited when cortisol is high

The hippocampus is the brain's main inhibitor of the HPA axis via GR. That is the load-bearing fact.

What chronic stress does to the hippocampus

Sustained high cortisol → hippocampal neurons in CA3 + dentate gyrus suffer dendritic atrophy, suppression of neurogenesis in the dentate, eventual volume loss. Less hippocampus → less feedback shutoff → more cortisol → more damage. This is the loop Sapolsky describes; it appears in PTSD, major depression, Cushing's disease.

Lock it: PVN → CRH → ACTH → cortisol → MR (low) + GR (high) → hippocampus shuts the axis off. Chronic stress shrinks hippocampus → feedback fails → cortisol stays high.
NIMHANS angle. Smaller hippocampal volume on MRI is reported in PTSD, MDD with melancholia, Cushing's, and chronic schizophrenia. Dexamethasone suppression test (a synthetic GR agonist) → non-suppression of morning cortisol = an old biological marker for melancholic depression; sensitivity is low but it is the marker NIMHANS still asks about.

8. Sex hormones — organising vs activating

The cleanest two-word concept in the book, and a perennial MCQ.

  • Organising effects — during a critical period (perinatal in humans, in utero in rodents), gonadal hormones permanently build sexually dimorphic brain circuits. Effects are permanent and structural. Example: prenatal testosterone in males masculinises the sexually dimorphic nucleus (SDN) of the medial preoptic area (mPOA).
  • Activating effects — after puberty, the same hormones switch on the behavior in the already-built circuit. Effects are reversible. Castrate an adult male rat → mounting drops; replace testosterone → it comes back.

The circuits, by sex

BehaviorCritical regionHormone
Male sexual behavior (mounting, intromission)Medial preoptic area (mPOA)Testosterone → aromatised to estradiol → ERα binding (the "aromatisation hypothesis")
Female sexual behavior (lordosis in rodents)Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH)Estradiol → priming → progesterone receptor expression
Maternal behaviormPOA + bed nucleus of stria terminalisEstradiol + prolactin + oxytocin
Pair-bonding (prairie vole work)Ventral pallidum, NAcVasopressin (males), oxytocin (females)
TRAP — the aromatisation hypothesis. In rodents, testosterone masculinises the male brain by being converted to estradiol within the brain (aromatase enzyme). The same chemical (estradiol) masculinises male brains and feminises female brains, depending on when and where it acts. Female fetuses are protected from circulating maternal estradiol by alpha-fetoprotein.
Lock it: organising = perinatal, permanent, structural. Activating = adult, reversible, behavioural. Male = mPOA (testosterone → aromatised). Female = VMH (estradiol → progesterone). Maternal behavior + pair bonds = oxytocin/vasopressin.

9. LTP — the molecular cascade

If the exam asks "molecular basis of learning", they want this story.

  1. Baseline: glutamate is released onto the postsynaptic dendritic spine. AMPA receptors open → Na+ in → depolarises the spine. NMDA receptors are also bound by glutamate, but at resting potential Mg2+ plugs the channel. Nothing flows.
  2. Strong / paired stimulation: the postsynaptic membrane depolarises enough that the Mg2+ plug is electrostatically expelled.
  3. NMDA channel is now open with glutamate and voltage. Ca2+ flows in.
  4. Ca2+ activates CaMKII (calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II). CaMKII phosphorylates AMPA receptors (more conductance) and inserts new AMPA receptors into the spine membrane.
  5. Same spine, next glutamate release → bigger AMPA response. The synapse is potentiated. With sustained activity, gene transcription (CREB) + protein synthesis (Arc, BDNF) makes the change last hours to days → late-phase LTP.
Why it matters. NMDA = the coincidence detector (needs glutamate AND depolarisation simultaneously). That is Hebb's "cells that fire together wire together" in molecules. Block NMDA (APV, ketamine) → no LTP → no spatial learning (Morris water maze, Morris & Frey).
Lock it: NMDA = coincidence detector (Mg2+ block, voltage-and-ligand-gated). LTP carrier = Ca2+ → CaMKII → AMPA insertion. Late phase = CREB → protein synthesis. APV / ketamine block LTP.

10. Neurobiology of the major psychiatric disorders

Carlson collapses these into one chapter. The MCQ trick is to map each disorder to a one-line neurobiological hypothesis + its drug logic.

Schizophrenia

  • Dopamine hypothesis (original): too much DA. Evidence: D2-blocking drugs work; amphetamine (raises DA) produces a paranoid psychosis indistinguishable from schizophrenia.
  • Updated: mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia → positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions); mesocortical hypodopaminergia → negative symptoms (avolition, flat affect) + cognitive symptoms.
  • Glutamate hypothesis: NMDA hypofunction. Evidence: PCP / ketamine produce the full spectrum (positive + negative + cognitive), classical DA-only drugs produce only positive symptoms.
  • Structural: ventricular enlargement, reduced cortical grey matter (esp. PFC + temporal), reduced hippocampal volume.
  • Drug logic: typicals (haloperidol, chlorpromazine) = pure D2 blockade → EPS. Atypicals (clozapine, risperidone, olanzapine) = D2 + 5-HT2A blockade → fewer EPS, more metabolic side effects. Clozapine = only one shown to help treatment-resistant + reduce suicide; needs WBC monitoring (agranulocytosis).

Depression

  • Monoamine hypothesis: deficit of 5-HT & NE. Evidence: tricyclics raise both; SSRIs raise 5-HT; MAOIs raise everything. But: monoamine rises in hours, antidepressant effect takes weeks — so the simple deficit story is incomplete.
  • Neuroplasticity / BDNF hypothesis: depression involves loss of hippocampal volume, low BDNF. Effective antidepressants raise BDNF + promote hippocampal neurogenesis over weeks — fits the time course.
  • HPA hypothesis: hypercortisolism + DST non-suppression in melancholic depression (see §7).
  • Drug logic: SSRI (first line), SNRI, atypical (bupropion = NDRI; mirtazapine = α2 + 5-HT2/3 antagonist), TCA, MAOI. Ketamine (NMDA antagonist) → rapid antidepressant effect — re-frames the whole field as glutamate-dependent.

Bipolar

  • Strong genetic loading, polygenic.
  • Lithium = mood stabiliser of choice; mechanism partly via inositol monophosphatase + GSK-3β inhibition; also reduces suicide in bipolar specifically.
  • Antidepressant monotherapy can flip a bipolar patient into mania — classic exam trap.

Anxiety

  • Amygdala-centred fear circuit (see §12). Increased reactivity, weaker prefrontal regulation.
  • GABA-A modulation (benzos) → fast relief.
  • 5-HT (SSRI / SNRI) → first-line maintenance for panic, GAD, OCD, PTSD, social anxiety.
  • OCD specifically responds to high-dose SSRI + cortico-striatal-thalamic loop involvement (caudate hyperactivity on PET, normalises with treatment).

Addiction

  • Hijack of mesolimbic DA (see §3–4).
  • Repeated drug exposure → tolerance (receptor downregulation) + sensitisation (incentive-salience strengthening) + withdrawal (opponent process: NAc DA below baseline, amygdala stress system activated).
  • Long-term cue-induced craving driven by amygdala + PFC + hippocampus, not VTA-NAc alone.
Recap §10. Schiz = DA imbalance + NMDA hypofunction. Depression = monoamines + plasticity + cortisol. Bipolar = mood stabilisers, watch the antidepressant switch. Anxiety = amygdala + GABA + 5-HT. Addiction = mesolimbic hijack + opponent withdrawal.

11. Sensorimotor — what Carlson adds over Pinel

The basics (motor cortex map, basal ganglia, cerebellum) are on the Pinel page. Carlson's two extra emphases:

  • Direct vs indirect basal ganglia loops. The direct pathway (cortex → striatum → GPi/SNr → thalamus → cortex) facilitates movement; the indirect pathway (striatum → GPe → STN → GPi) inhibits movement. D1 receptors on direct-pathway neurons (go), D2 receptors on indirect-pathway neurons (no-go). DA from substantia nigra biases the system toward "go" — lose it and you get Parkinson's bradykinesia.
  • Mirror neurons (di Pellegrino, Rizzolatti, area F5 of macaque premotor cortex) — neurons that fire both when the monkey executes a goal-directed action and when it watches another do it. Hypothesised role in imitation, action understanding, theory of mind; human homologues in inferior frontal gyrus + inferior parietal lobule.
TRAP — direct vs indirect pathway receptor. Direct = D1 (facilitating). Indirect = D2 (inhibiting). DA excites direct and inhibits indirect, so it pushes both ways toward "more movement". L-DOPA in Parkinson's restores both.

12. Emotion — amygdala in detail

The Papez circuit / James-Lange / Cannon-Bard story is on the Pinel page. Carlson focuses on the amygdala as the emotion hub.

Amygdala nuclei

  • Lateral nucleus — sensory input from thalamus + cortex. The "input gate".
  • Basal + accessory basal nuclei — integrate input with cortical (PFC, hippocampus) context.
  • Central nucleus (CeA) — output to hypothalamus (autonomic), PAG (freezing, analgesia), brainstem (startle), basal forebrain (vigilance). The "output gate" of fear.

Fear conditioning circuit (LeDoux)

Two roads to the amygdala: the low road = thalamus → lateral amygdala, fast & crude (the snake-shaped stick); the high road = thalamus → cortex → lateral amygdala, slower & precise (it's a stick). Lateral amygdala stores the CS-US association via LTP at its glutamate synapses. CeA drives the output (freeze + autonomic burst).

Klüver-Bucy

Bilateral anterior temporal lobectomy (originally in monkeys, then in humans with HSV encephalitis or post-surgical) → hypersexuality, hyperorality (mouthing objects), placidity, visual agnosia, hypermetamorphosis. Damage to amygdala + adjacent temporal cortex.

Lock it: amygdala = fear / threat detection. Lateral = input, central = output. LeDoux's two roads = thalamus-amygdala (low) and thalamus-cortex-amygdala (high). Klüver-Bucy = bilateral temporal damage.

15. MCQ traps Carlson sets that Pinel does not

  1. Cocaine = DAT blocker. Amphetamine = DAT reverser + VMAT2 emptier. Not the same.
  2. Benzos = ↑ GABA-A frequency. Barbiturates = ↑ GABA-A duration. (Same channel, different sites.)
  3. Direct pathway = D1 = go. Indirect pathway = D2 = no-go. (DA pushes both toward "go".)
  4. Mesolimbic = positive symptoms (hyper). Mesocortical = negative + cognitive symptoms (hypo). Antipsychotics work on the first; they make the second worse.
  5. NMDA receptor is voltage-AND-ligand-gated; Mg2+ is the voltage-dependent block. AMPA is ligand-only.
  6. Leptin is elevated in human obesity (leptin resistance), not deficient.
  7. Ghrelin = stomach, pre-meal, hunger. Don't confuse with ghrelin/leptin direction.
  8. MR (mineralocorticoid) is the high-affinity cortisol receptor; GR is the low-affinity one recruited in stress. Hippocampus has both.
  9. Aromatisation: testosterone is converted to estradiol in the male brain, which is what masculinises it.
  10. Orexin loss → narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy). Orexin neurons live in the lateral hypothalamus.
  11. Clozapine is the only antipsychotic shown to lower suicide in schizophrenia. Side effect: agranulocytosis.
  12. Lithium reduces suicide in bipolar. Mechanism: GSK-3β + inositol monophosphatase inhibition.
  13. Acamprosate stabilises NMDA glutamate signalling in alcohol use disorder. Disulfiram blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase.
  14. Varenicline is a partial agonist at α4β2 nicotinic ACh receptor (smoking cessation).
  15. The transmitter that peaks in REM is ACh; NE + 5-HT + histamine all drop.
