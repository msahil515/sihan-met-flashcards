The major approaches to personality — psychoanalytic/psychodynamic (Freud), the neo-Freudians and analytical psychology (Jung, Horney, Adler, Fromm), humanistic (Carl Rogers), existential and transpersonal approaches (Heidegger, Rollo May, Wilber), and Murray & Morgan's personology — followed by the theories of motivation (instinct, arousal, drive-reduction, and the need theories of Abraham Maslow, Alderfer, David McClelland, Edward Deci & Richard Ryan, and Zuckerman).

1 · Psychoanalytic & Psychodynamic (Freud)

Theory → Theorist. Psychosexual / psychodynamic personality theory → Sigmund Freud.

Freud — the psychodynamic model

Sigmund Freud used a topographical model of the mind and held that behaviour springs from innate instincts and unconscious conflict.

The iceberg: topographical model (three levels of awareness)

Freud set out the topographical model in The Interpretation of Dreams (1900). It divides the mind by how aware we are of its contents, drawn as an iceberg:

LevelWhere on the icebergWhat it holdsExample
ConsciousThe tip above the waterWhatever you are aware of right now.Reading this sentence.
PreconsciousThe band at the waterlineNot in awareness now, but easily recalled when you turn attention to it.Your phone number or what you ate yesterday.
UnconsciousThe huge mass under the waterWishes, impulses and memories kept out of awareness (often by repression); they show up indirectly in dreams, slips of the tongue and symptoms.A forgotten childhood conflict driving adult anxiety.

The drawing also places the structural model (id, ego, superego; The Ego and the Id, 1923) on the same iceberg: the id lies wholly below the water (entirely unconscious), while the ego and superego stretch across all three levels, so each has conscious, preconscious and unconscious parts.

Trap. Topographical model (1900) = levels of awareness (conscious, preconscious, unconscious). Structural model (1923) = id, ego, superego. Examiners swap the two names. Also, "ego = conscious" is wrong: much of the ego works unconsciously (for example, defence mechanisms are unconscious ego operations). Only the id is purely unconscious.

Life and death instincts — both are innate:

  • Life instinct (eros) — libidinal energy that pushes us to function; survival-related instincts.
  • Death instinct (thanatos) — instincts to hurt; destructive energy.

The Id, Ego and Superego

StructurePrincipleDevelops in
IdPleasure principle — seeks immediate gratificationOral phase
EgoReality principleAnal phase
SuperegoMorality principlePhallic stage

The superego consists of two parts:

  • Ego ideal — rewards ideal behaviour.
  • Conscience — punishes with guilt.

Three types of anxiety (Freud)

AnxietyNatureDescription
Realistic anxietyRealistic fearThe fear of an actual item.
Moral anxietySocial fearFear of punishment; represented by shame and guilt.
Neurotic anxietyNervous fearThe fear of being overwhelmed; feeling that you are about to lose it; fear of the id taking over.

Defence mechanisms

  • Repression — keeping unpleasant memories or thoughts buried in the unconscious. Primal: innately part of the id. Proper: unacceptable derivatives of original id impulses.
  • Denial — refusing to recognize an unpleasant reality.
  • Rationalization — creating a socially acceptable excuse to justify unacceptable behaviour.
  • Reaction formation — not acknowledging unacceptable impulses and over-emphasizing their opposite.
  • Projection — transferring one's unacceptable qualities or impulses to others.
  • Displacement — diverting one's impulses to a more acceptable target.
  • Sublimation — channelling socially unacceptable impulses into acceptable activities.
  • Regression — reverting to immature ways of responding.
  • Identification — enhancing self-esteem by imagining or forming alliances with others.
  • Intellectualization — ignoring troubling emotional aspects by focusing on abstract ideas or thoughts.
  • Suppression — actively choosing to ignore something to do what is better and more important at the moment.
Key. Id = pleasure principle (oral); Ego = reality principle (anal); Superego = morality principle (phallic), made of the ego ideal (rewards) and conscience (guilt). Repression is the "primal" defence; suppression is conscious, repression is unconscious.

2 · Neo-Freudian & Analytical Psychology

Jung — Analytical Psychology

Theory → Theorist. Analytical psychology → Carl Jung.
Point of differenceSigmund FreudJung
Role of libidoSexual energyBroad psychic energy
Forces that influence personalityPresent in the past onlyPresent in the past as well as the future
The unconsciousPersonal unconsciousPersonal and collective unconscious

Structures of the psyche:

  • Ego — is in control of itself; the conscious mind. Its main role is to be the centre of consciousness: it is made of our conscious perceptions, thoughts, feelings and memories, acts as the "gatekeeper" that decides what is allowed into awareness, and gives us a sense of identity and continuity.
  • Personal unconscious — accessible once in a while; personal memories that have lost their intensity. Contains complexes: emotionally charged clusters of thoughts, feelings and memories organized around one theme (for example a mother complex or a power complex). Jung found them through his word association test, where a delayed or disturbed reaction to a stimulus word signals that the word has touched a complex. A strong complex can take over the personality for a time. Each complex is built around an archetypal core from the collective unconscious, which is why the original notes link the two.
  • Collective unconscious — shared memories (patterns of behaviour), motifs, primordial patterns, inherited by all humans. Its contents are the archetypes.
Trap. Personal unconscious → complexes (from your own life). Collective unconscious → archetypes (inherited, shared by all humans). Also, Jung's ego is the centre of consciousness only, while the Self is the archetype of wholeness and the centre of the whole psyche (conscious plus unconscious). Do not mix up ego and Self.

Energy principles:

  • The principle of opposites — every wish or feeling has its opposite.
  • The principle of equivalence — there is a constant amount of energy in our psyche.
  • The principle of entropy — redistribution of energy within our psyche.

Four functions: thinking, feeling, sensing, intuiting.

Psychological types — attitudes: introversion / extraversion. Rational / irrational is not an attitude; it is how Jung grouped the four functions.

In Psychological Types (1921) Jung described two attitudes (the direction psychic energy flows) and four functions (ways of taking in and handling experience). The functions fall into two pairs:

FunctionGroupWhat it does
ThinkingRational (judging)Judges by logic: tells you what a thing is.
FeelingRational (judging)Judges by value: tells you whether a thing is good or bad, agreeable or not. It is evaluation, not emotion.
SensingIrrational (perceiving)Perceives through the senses: tells you that something exists, here and now.
IntuitingIrrational (perceiving)Perceives via hunches and the unconscious: tells you where a thing came from and where it is going (possibilities).

The two attitudes are introversion (energy flows inward, to the inner world) and extraversion (energy flows outward, to people and things). Combining 2 attitudes with 4 functions gives Jung's eight psychological types, for example the introverted thinking type or the extraverted feeling type. The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (Katharine Cook Briggs and Isabel Briggs Myers) is built on this scheme.

Trap. Attitudes = introversion and extraversion only. Rational (judging) = thinking and feeling. Irrational (perceiving) = sensing and intuition. "Irrational" does not mean illogical or abnormal; it means the function simply perceives without judging.

Archetypes and the anima/animus:

  • Anima — the female archetype in the male psyche.
  • Animus — the male archetype in the female psyche.
  • Syzygy — anima and animus (Carl Jung) together.
  • Self — finding a balance between the different aspects of ourselves.
  • Persona — a mask, a facade to meet society's demands; a compromise between your needs and society's demands.
  • Shadow — deepest, darkest instincts; comparable to the id.
  • Other major archetypes: Joker, Wise Old Man, Hero, Witch, Villain.

Horney

Karen Horney gave the concept of womb envy. When children are born they have basic anxiety; if they are met with love and care this anxiety subsides and a healthy personality develops. In the neurotic personality there is a huge difference between the ideal and the real self, because of a rigid ideal self.

Neurotic needs — three orientations:

OrientationNormalNeuroticNeeds
Towards peopleFriendlyCompliantSeeks acceptance and help all the time; need for affection and approval; need for a partner who will control their life.
Against peopleCompetitiveAggressiveNeed for power, prestige, personal achievement; need to exploit other people; need to compete all the time.
Away from peopleAutonomousDetachedNeed to move away from people; independence; self-reliance; perfection; narrow limits.

Adler — Individual Psychology

Theory → Theorist. Individual psychology → Alfred Adler.

Adler's approach is future-oriented, non-deterministic and idiographic.

  • Fictional finalism — dreams that may not come true and are fictional, but motivate the child to do well in life.
  • Masculine protest — when female children feel male children within the family are being treated better, they behave more like boys as a protest.
  • Organ inferiority — experienced by each child when they are young.

Types of inferiority:

TypeDescription
Primary inferiorityThe "original and normal feeling" of inferiority maintained by an infant. This feeling is productive, as it provides motivation for the child to develop.
Secondary inferiorityThe inferiority feeling in the adult that results when the child develops an exaggerated feeling of inferiority. These feelings in the adult are what are harmful, and they comprise the inferiority complex.

Adler's interpretation of birth order:

PositionTypical characteristics
First bornPerfectionist, achiever, leader, bossy, responsible, motivated, conscientious, controlling, cautious, reliable.
Middle bornAdaptable, independent, go-between, people pleaser, can be rebellious, feels left out, peacemaker, social.
Last bornSocial, charming, outgoing, uncomplicated, manipulative, seeks attention, self-centred, fun, confident.
The only childConscientious, responsible, perfectionist, centre of attention, mature for their age, seek approval, sensitive, leader.

Three types of problems: problems of occupation; problems of love; problems involving our behaviour towards others.

Four styles of life: the dominant type; the getting type; the avoiding type; the socially useful type.

Erich Fromm

Erich Fromm was a Neo-Freudian. He identified two fundamental needs: freedom and belonging.

Character orientations:

TypeDescription
ProductiveNurturing, loving, meaningful; a good spouse and friend; the only healthy approach.
ExploitativeDeceives; manipulative; disregard for others' well-being.
MarketingRelationship is considered a transactional exchange; shallow; opportunistic.
ReceptiveCompliant; dependent.
HoardingPossession.

Needs:

  • Relatedness — connection.
  • Transcendence — meaning.
  • Rootedness — belonging to a safe space; if too much, causes inflexibility.
Don't confuse — Freud vs Carl Jung vs Alfred Adler. Freud: libido = sexual energy, personality shaped by the past, only a personal unconscious. Jung: libido = broad psychic energy, past and future, personal and collective unconscious (archetypes). Adler: future-oriented, striving from inferiority, birth order and social interest, non-deterministic and idiographic. Karen Horney = womb envy + three interpersonal orientations; Erich Fromm = freedom vs belonging + five character types.

3 · Humanistic Approach (Rogers)

Theory → Theorist. Person-centred / self theory → Carl Rogers.

Humanists hold that humans want to reach their highest potential. Actualization is the process of becoming a fully functioning individual.

Rogers

Phenomenology argues that the only reality of which we can be sure is our own subjective world of experience — our inner perception of reality. The phenomenal field is the accumulation of a person's experiences.

Research technique — Q-sort (mixed method):

  • Qualitative: ideal self, through preference arrangement.
  • Quantitative: factor analysis.

The Fully Functioning Person: open to experience; existential living; trusts feelings; creativity and spontaneity; a fulfilled life; freedom of choice; a continual need to grow.

Congruence vs incongruence — the degree of overlap between the self-concept and experience: congruence = they match; incongruence = a gap between them.

Conditional vs unconditional positive regard:

ConditionalUnconditional
Having conditions that need to be met in order to receive love.Love without any condition.
An effort to manipulate the actions of individuals.No effort to manipulate or control an individual.
Child develops conditions of worth.Congruent personality; child develops the organismic valuing process; does not need therapy.
Key. Unconditional positive regard → organismic valuing process → congruent, healthy personality. Conditional regard → conditions of worth → incongruence.

4 · Existential & Transpersonal Approaches

Existential approach

Proponents: Rollo May, Viktor Frankl, Martin Heidegger. Meaning in life is what keeps you grounded. Dasein = "to be in the world".

Heidegger

Martin Heidegger's modes of being-in-the-world:

ModeMeaning
UmweltOne's relationship with the natural world or environment.
MitweltOur relationship with other people and the meanings given to those relationships.
EigenweltOne's relationship with oneself and how we interpret things based on self.
UberweltSpirituality.

Existential anxiety — the fear that we won't be alive. It can motivate us to achieve our best potential (proportionate), or demotivate us so that we wish to give up, thinking nothing really matters (neurotic).

Rollo May

Types of anxiety: Normal; Neurotic (disproportionate to the situation).

Four stages of development:

  • Innocence — physical and psychological dependence.
  • Rebellion — people define themselves in opposition to or defiance of another.
  • Ordinary — the normal adult ego; a healthy, mature personality.
  • Creative — transcending our usual limited viewpoint and catching a glimpse of ultimate reality.

Transpersonal psychology

Founded by Abraham Maslow and Stanislav Grof; it also has roots in earlier work by psychologists such as William James and Carl Jung.

Methods: psychedelic; transpersonal therapy; dreamwork; mindfulness.

Altered state of consciousness: connectedness beyond ego boundaries; altruism, creativity, intuitive wisdom; a journey into self-exploration.

Groups: Pre-personal (when you don't understand self fully); Personal (concerns of the ego); Transpersonal (concerns beyond the ego).

These three groups are the broad realms of Ken Wilber's model of development. Pre-personal (pre-egoic) covers infancy and early childhood, before a stable, separate sense of self has formed. Personal (egoic) is the ordinary adult self that thinks rationally and has a clear identity. Transpersonal (trans-egoic) lies beyond the ego: states in which the sense of self widens past the individual. The stages below sit inside these groups: vision logic is the highest personal stage, and psychic, subtle, causal and the final (nondual) stage are transpersonal. So pre-personal is a group, not one of the stages.

Trap: Wilber's pre/trans fallacy. Because both pre-personal and transpersonal states are "non-rational", people confuse them: either treating a mystical (trans) state as a regression to infancy (Wilber's criticism of Freud), or glorifying an infantile (pre) state as spiritual. Pre-rational is not the same as trans-rational.

Ken Wilber's transpersonal stages of development:

  • Vision logic — integration of mind and body.
  • Psychic — empathetic understanding.
  • Subtle — across archetypal forms.
  • Causal — non-dual awareness.
  • Final — freely transverse between all stages.

5 · Personology (Murray & Morgan)

Theory → Theorist. Personology → Henry Murray & Christiana Morgan (the notes spell her name "Christina"; she is Christiana Morgan, who also co-created the Thematic Apperception Test with Murray).

Principles

  1. Personality is rooted in the brain.
  2. The process of tension reduction is satisfying, but a tension-free state is a source of distress.
  3. An individual's personality develops over time.
  4. Personality is not fixed or static.
  5. Each person is unique.

Structures: Id — innate impulses that society considers unacceptable; Ego: the central organizer of behaviour; Superego — internalization of cultural values.

Henry Murray (Explorations in Personality, 1938) kept Freud's three divisions but changed their weight. The id holds all innate impulses: the unacceptable ones listed above, but also, unlike Freud's id, socially acceptable ones such as empathy. The ego is much stronger than Freud's: it is the central organizer of behaviour, which consciously decides and plans what to do and actively promotes the satisfaction of needs, rather than only refereeing between id and superego. The superego is the culture's values, learned mainly from parents, which tell the ego which needs may be expressed, when and how.

Trap. In a Freud vs Murray question, the answer is that Murray's ego is an active planner and organizer of behaviour, not a weak mediator.

Needs

CategoryDescription
PrimarySurvival-related.
SecondaryEmotional or psychological.
ProactiveSpontaneous; achievement-related.
ReactiveResponse to object; harm-avoidance.

Types of needs: materialistic, affiliation, information, power, achievement, sensual.

Press, thema and complexes

  • Press — the influence of environment and past events on the current activation of a need.
  • Thema — need × press + how they affect personality = unity thema. Subsidation: one need will activate another, and the second need will fulfil the first need.

Complexes:

Claustral (womb)The desire to reinstate the conditions experienced in the mother's womb.

6 · Theories of Motivation & Emotion

Instinct theory

Behaviour is motivated by instincts that are inborn and that are activated by environmental stimuli.

  • Sigmund Freud's object relations theory — basic instincts from eros motivate us; explains motivation in terms of primary needs.
  • Homeostatic approach — disturbance of homeostasis creates drive.

Instincts have four basic characteristics (S-A-I-O):

  • Source — source of deficit (internal).
  • Aim — what we want to do.
  • Impetus — the drive that propels us to act.
  • Object — that through which the instinct achieves its aim.
Heads up: the notes (and diagram) label this "Freud's object relations theory". Object relations theory is usually credited to later analysts such as Melanie Klein, Ronald Fairbairn and Donald Winnicott. What is listed here is Freud's own instinct (drive) theory: the four characteristics, source, aim, impetus (pressure) and object, come from his paper "Instincts and Their Vicissitudes" (1915).

Arousal theory

Behaviour is motivated by the need to achieve optimum levels of arousal.

Yerkes-Dodson law — arousal increases performance up to a point, after which additional arousal impedes performance.

The law comes from Robert Yerkes and John Dodson (1908). The graph plots performance against arousal as an inverted U:

  • Low arousal (left end): boredom or apathy, so performance is poor.
  • Moderate arousal (the peak): the optimal level, where performance is best.
  • High arousal (right end): high anxiety, so performance falls again.

The two curves show that the best level depends on the task. Difficult tasks (blue curve) peak at a lower level of arousal; easy or well-practised tasks (red curve) peak at a higher level. Example: solving a tricky exam paper goes best when you are only mildly keyed up, while a simple, well-learned task like a sprint can benefit from being highly pumped up.

Trap. Higher arousal helps easy tasks and hurts difficult ones sooner. Do not reverse this. Also, arousal theory is the answer when a question asks why people seek stimulation (for example thrill seeking or curiosity), which drive-reduction theory cannot explain.

Alderfer's ERG theory

One does not necessarily start from the lowest level.

  • Moving from a lower level to an upper level of the hierarchy is called satisfaction-progression.
  • Moving from a higher level to a lower level is called frustration regression.
Heads up: the original notes write "succession progression"; the standard term is satisfaction-progression (a satisfied need lets you move up to the next one).

Clayton Alderfer (1969) squeezed Maslow's five levels into three groups of needs, E-R-G:

LetterNeedMeaningMaslow's levels it covers
EExistenceMaterial and physical needs for staying alive and safe.Physiological + safety
RRelatednessNeeds for meaningful relationships with family, friends and co-workers.Belonging/love + the external part of esteem (recognition and respect from others)
GGrowthNeeds to develop yourself and use your abilities fully.The internal part of esteem (self-esteem) + self-actualization

How ERG differs from Maslow: there are 3 needs, not 5; more than one need can be active at the same time; there is no fixed order; and if a higher need is frustrated a person can slide back and put more effort into a lower one (frustration-regression). Example: an employee blocked from promotion (growth) may start demanding more pay or spending more time socialising at work.

Trap. Esteem is split in ERG: esteem from others goes under Relatedness, self-esteem goes under Growth. And frustration-regression belongs to Alderfer, not Maslow (Maslow's model moves only upward).

Maslow's need hierarchy

Theory → Theorist. Hierarchy of needs → Abraham Maslow.
  • Lower needs come from deficiencies; higher needs come from satisfaction.
  • Measured with the Personal Orientation Inventory (Everett Shostrom, 1964), a test of self-actualization.
  • Progression is only upward, but not automatic.
  • Jonah complex — fear of your own best potential.

Abraham Maslow proposed the hierarchy in 1943 ("A Theory of Human Motivation") with five levels. In his later writings (up to about 1970) he added cognitive, aesthetic and transcendence needs, giving the eight-level pyramid in the diagram. Read from the bottom up:

#LevelWhat it meansExampleType
1PhysiologicalBodily survival needs.Food, water, sleep, warmth.Deficiency needs (D-needs): felt when lacking; once met, they stop motivating.
2SafetySecurity and stability.A safe home, a steady job, health.
3Belonging and loveAffection and acceptance.Friends, family, a partner, a group.
4EsteemRespect from others and self-respect.Recognition, status, confidence.
5CognitiveTo know and understand.Curiosity, learning, exploring.Growth needs (being or B-needs): they grow stronger as they are fed.
6AestheticBeauty, order, symmetry.Enjoying art, music, nature.
7Self-actualizationBecoming everything one is capable of becoming.Fulfilling your potential; peak experiences.
8TranscendenceGoing beyond the self.Helping others reach their potential; spiritual or mystical experience.
Trap. Check whether a question means the original five levels (1943) or the expanded eight. In the five-level version, self-actualization is the top and only growth need; in the eight-level version, transcendence is the top. Esteem is always a deficiency need, not a growth need.

Self-determination theory

Three basic needs:

  • Autonomy — the need to control the course of our lives.
  • Competence — the need to be effective in dealing with the environment.
  • Relatedness — the need to have close, affectionate relationships with others.

Causality orientations:

OrientationNeeds satisfiedMotivation
AutonomousAutonomy, competence, relatednessIntrinsic motivation

Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull)

Theory → Theorist. Drive-reduction theory → Clark Hull.

A homeostatic approach. A bodily need (e.g. hydration) creates a drive; the organism is motivated to satisfy the drive through goal-directed behaviour, restoring homeostasis (drive reduced, need satisfied).

Equation: SER = V × D × J × K × SHR − SIR − IR − SOR − SLR

TermMeaning
SERExcitatory / reaction potential
VStimulus-intensity dynamism: how strong the stimulus is (the notes and diagram gloss V as "need")
DDrive
JDelay
KSize of incentive
SHRHabit strength
SIRConditioned inhibition
IRFatigue (reactive inhibition)
SORRandom error (behavioural oscillation)
SLRReaction threshold: the minimum reaction potential needed before a response occurs (the notes put it as minimum reinforcement required for learning to occur)

Reading the cycle in the diagram (Clark Hull, Principles of Behavior, 1943, and his later revisions). Each stage carries the term of the equation that acts there:

Stage in the cycleTermWhat happens (thirst example)
Bodily needVThe body is short of water. The diagram puts V here; strictly, V is how intense the stimulus is (for example a strongly felt dry mouth).
DriveDThe need produces drive, an unpleasant state of tension (thirst). More hours without water means more D.
Organism motivated to satisfy the driveJ, KHow motivated you are also depends on the incentive (K: a cold drink pulls harder than a sip of warm water) and on how long you must wait for it (J: delay).
Goal-directed behaviourSHRYou do what has worked before, such as walking to the water cooler. Habit strength (SHR) grows each time that response was reinforced.
Drive reduced, need satisfiedDrinking reduces the drive. Hull held that this drive reduction is the reinforcement that strengthens the habit.
HomeostasisThe body is back in balance until the next need arises, and the cycle repeats.

The terms after the minus signs pull reaction potential down: tiredness from repeating a response (IR), learned inhibition (SIR), random moment-to-moment variation (SOR), and the threshold the result must exceed for any response to appear (SLR). Note that D and SHR are multiplied: if either is zero, there is no response, however strong the other is.

Heads up: the diagram and the notes call V "need". In Hull's system V is stimulus-intensity dynamism (stimulus strength); the need itself shows up as drive (D). Likewise SLR is the reaction threshold. Trap: drive-reduction theory cannot explain behaviour that raises tension, such as curiosity or thrill seeking; arousal theory was offered to cover those cases.

McClelland's theory of needs

Theory → Theorist. Need for achievement / acquired-needs theory → David McClelland.

All three needs are learned: Power (nPow), Achievement (nAch), Affiliation (nAff).

Zuckerman's sensation-seeking theory

Theory → Theorist. Sensation-seeking theory → Marvin Zuckerman.

Four types:

  • Thrill and adventure seeking
  • Experience seeking
  • Disinhibition
  • Boredom susceptibility
Key. Need theories to keep straight: Abraham Maslow (hierarchy, deficiency vs growth needs), Alderfer (ERG — satisfaction-progression vs frustration regression), David McClelland (learned nAch/nPow/nAff), Edward Deci & Richard Ryan (self-determination — autonomy, competence, relatedness). Clark Hull's drive-reduction is the homeostatic model; Yerkes-Dodson governs the optimum-arousal model.
Don't confuse — the need theorists. Abraham Maslow = a fixed upward hierarchy driven by deficiency then growth; Alderfer's ERG = flexible, allows frustration regression; David McClelland = three learned needs measured by projective tests, no hierarchy. And in McClelland's model, high-nAch people take internal credit for success while low-nAch people show an external locus for success and an internal one for failure.
