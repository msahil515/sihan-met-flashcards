How counselling theory is tested. (1) Name the theory from its claim ("we dispute irrational beliefs" = REBT). (2) Pin the theory to its founder (choice theory = Glasser). (3) Pin the named technique to its theory ("miracle question" = solution-focused / de Shazer; "empty chair" = Gestalt / Perls). The frame that organises the whole book. Sharf describes every theory on the same recurring template, and you can predict almost any "which approach" answer from where the theory sits on five axes: (1) view of human nature (determined by the past vs free / growth-oriented) · (2) role of the past (childhood causes vs the here-and-now / future) · (3) directiveness (therapist as expert/teacher vs as a non-directive companion) · (4) the relationship (a tool, the cure itself, or a collaboration) · (5) insight vs action (does change come from understanding or from doing). Part One · What theory is for

Ch 1. Theory, and the role of theory in counselling

Sharf opens by asking what a theory of psychotherapy is and why a counsellor needs one. A theory is a coherent set of assumptions that explains how problems arise and how change happens; without one, a counsellor is improvising. A theory gives the clinician three things: a map of personality (what a person is made of and how distress develops), a set of goals, and a set of techniques that follow logically from the goals.

A good theory, Sharf says, is precise / clear, comprehensive (covers a wide range of behaviour), parsimonious (no needless complexity), useful / applicable (generates testable predictions and practical methods), and verifiable / testable by research. He also sets up the recurring chapter frame used throughout the book: history of the originator, theory of personality, theory of psychotherapy (goals, the therapeutic relationship, assessment, techniques), application to a brief case, treatment of specific disorders, brief / short-term versions, current trends, use with diverse populations and gender issues, group counselling applications, a summary, and research / critique.

Brief history. Counselling grew from several roots: Freud's psychoanalysis (the depth tradition), the vocational-guidance movement (Frank Parsons, early 1900s), psychometrics and the testing movement, and later the humanistic reaction (Rogers) and the behavioural / cognitive revolutions. The book then groups the theories into broad families: psychodynamic (Freud, Jung, Adler), humanistic / experiential (existential, person-centred, Gestalt), action / cognitive-behavioural (behaviour therapy, REBT, cognitive therapy, reality therapy), constructivist / postmodern (solution-focused, narrative), feminist, and family systems.
Recap Ch 1. A theory = assumptions about personality + goals + techniques. A good theory is clear, comprehensive, parsimonious, useful, and testable. Every chapter follows the same frame: originator → personality → goals → relationship → techniques → applications → critique.

Part Two · The psychodynamic approaches

Ch 2. Psychoanalysis — Sigmund Freud

Originator. Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), Viennese neurologist, founder of psychoanalysis. Working with hysteria (and Breuer's patient Anna O., source of the "talking cure"), he concluded symptoms have hidden mental causes in the unconscious. The first systematic theory of psychotherapy; the tradition every later theory reacts to.

Theory of personality / how problems develop. Behaviour is driven by instincts (life/Eros with its energy libido, and death/Thanatos), and by an unconscious we cannot access directly. The mind has a topographic layout (conscious / preconscious / unconscious) and a structural one: the id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle, mediator), and superego (internalised morality). Personality forms through five psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital); over- or under-gratification produces fixation. Problems arise from unconscious conflict and unresolved early-childhood experience; the ego manages anxiety with defence mechanisms (repression the master defence, plus projection, displacement, reaction formation, sublimation, denial, regression, rationalisation, etc., catalogued by Anna Freud).

Goals of therapy. Make the unconscious conscious; bring repressed material into awareness so the ego can deal with it; strengthen the ego; resolve childhood conflicts that are re-enacted in the present. Insight is the engine of change.

Techniques. Free association (say whatever comes, censorship off), dream analysis (the "royal road to the unconscious"; separate manifest from latent content), analysis and interpretation of resistance and of the transference, attention to parapraxes (Freudian slips), and working through (repeatedly re-examining a conflict until it loosens its grip).

The therapeutic relationship. Centres on transference: the client unconsciously transfers feelings about early figures onto the analyst, and analysing that transference is the work. The analyst stays relatively neutral / blank-screen; countertransference (the analyst's own reactions) must be monitored. Classically intensive (several sessions a week, the couch).

Used for / evidence. Originally for neurosis / hysteria; now for personality and relational problems. Built on case studies (rich, but unrepeatable and hard to verify). Modern brief psychodynamic therapy and outcome research give it more empirical footing.

Criticisms. Long, expensive, weakly testable / largely unfalsifiable; overweighted on sex and on the deterministic past; built on a biased sample; little support for the psychosexual stages.

Free association & dream analysis & transference = Freud / psychoanalysis. The single defining mechanism of psychoanalytic therapy is working with the transference; the single defining technique is free association.
Recap Ch 2. Unconscious conflict + early childhood → defences → symptoms. Goal: make the unconscious conscious via free association, dream analysis, interpretation of resistance and transference. Relationship = transference. Insight-based, past-focused, deterministic.

Ch 3. Jungian analysis — analytical psychology

Originator. Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961), Swiss psychiatrist, Freud's "crown prince" until their 1913 break over the sexual nature of libido. Founded analytical psychology.

Theory of personality. Jung kept the unconscious but enlarged it and de-sexualised libido into general psychic energy. The psyche = ego (consciousness) + personal unconscious (your own forgotten/repressed material, organised into emotionally charged complexes) + collective unconscious (an inherited layer shared by all humanity, containing the archetypes: persona, shadow, anima/animus, the self). Jung coined introversion / extraversion and added four functions (thinking, feeling, sensing, intuiting) → psychological types. Problems often reflect a one-sided development and a failure of individuation, especially at midlife.

Goals. Individuation: integrate the conscious and unconscious, the persona and shadow, into a balanced self. Restore the dialogue with the unconscious.

Techniques. Dream analysis read in series, with amplification (linking dream symbols to myth and culture) rather than Freud's free association; active imagination (dialoguing with inner images); the word-association test (reveals complexes); analysis of symbol and myth. Therapy has four stages (confession, elucidation, education, transformation).

Relationship. A collaborative, mutual encounter; the analyst's own psyche is engaged (Jung stressed that both parties are changed by the work).

Used for / evidence. Existential/meaning crises, midlife issues. Largely interpretive; light controlled evidence; the MBTI descends from his types but has weak psychometrics.

Criticisms. Mystical, obscure, hard to test; the collective unconscious and archetypes resist verification.

Collective unconscious + archetypes + individuation = Jung, NOT Freud (Freud = personal unconscious only). Amplification (Jung) vs free association (Freud) for dreams.
Recap Ch 3. Jung: collective unconscious + archetypes (persona, shadow, anima/animus, self); goal = individuation; dreams via amplification, active imagination, word-association test. De-sexualised libido; midlife focus.

Ch 4. Adlerian therapy — individual psychology

Originator. Alfred Adler (1870–1937), Viennese, broke from Freud in 1911 over the sexual emphasis, founding individual psychology. A major forerunner of humanistic and cognitive therapy.

Theory of personality. The one dynamic force is striving for superiority (really, striving for competence and completion). Everyone starts small and feels inferiority; healthy growth means compensating for it, while derailment yields an inferiority complex or an over-compensating superiority complex. Each person builds a unique style of life by about age 5; we are pulled by future fictional goals (fictional finalism), we are not passive (the creative power of the self), and the mark of mental health is social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) — a feeling of kinship with humanity. Birth order shapes the style of life. Problems = a mistaken style of life and discouragement / low social interest.

Goals. Foster social interest, correct the mistaken style of life and faulty basic mistakes, replace discouragement with encouragement, and move the client toward useful goals.

Techniques. Lifestyle assessment, early recollections (the earliest memory reveals the style of life), birth-order analysis, exploring family constellation, encouragement, "the question" ("how would your life be different if you were well?"), acting as if, catching oneself, spitting in the client's soup (making a payoff unappealing), and paradoxical intention.

Relationship. Egalitarian, collaborative, respectful; therapist as an encouraging, teaching partner.

Used for / evidence. Parent education, school counselling, group work; modest research base. Hugely influential on later theories.

Criticisms. Loosely defined constructs; birth-order findings are mixed; more a practical philosophy than a tested theory.

Inferiority/superiority complex · style of life · social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) · birth order · early recollections · encouragement = Adler. Will to power = Adler; will to pleasure = Freud; will to meaning = Frankl.
Recap Ch 4. Adler: inferiority → striving for superiority → unique style of life by ~5; health = social interest. Goal: encouragement + correcting the lifestyle. Tools: early recollections, birth order, "the question," acting as if. Egalitarian relationship.

Part Three · The humanistic / experiential approaches

Ch 5. Existential therapy — May, Yalom, Frankl

Originators. Not one founder but a movement: Rollo May (brought existentialism to US psychology), Irvin Yalom (the four ultimate concerns), and Viktor Frankl (logotherapy, forged in the concentration camps). Philosophical roots in Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, Binswanger, Boss.

Theory of personality / human nature. There is no fixed human nature; we are free and responsible, and we create ourselves through choices (existence precedes essence). Distress comes not mainly from the past but from facing, or fleeing, the ultimate concerns / givens of existence. Yalom's four givens: death (and its anxiety), freedom (and the responsibility / groundlessness it brings), isolation (existential aloneness), and meaninglessness. Avoiding these through inauthenticity / "bad faith" produces existential anxiety and guilt. The three modes of being-in-the-world: Umwelt (physical), Mitwelt (social), Eigenwelt (self).

Goals. Help the client live authentically, accept freedom and responsibility, confront the givens, and find or create meaning. Not symptom removal but a fuller, more honest way of living.

Techniques. Existential therapy is deliberately light on technique; the relationship is the method. Frankl's logotherapy is the main exception, with named methods: paradoxical intention (deliberately wish for the feared thing, to break anticipatory anxiety), dereflection (shift attention away from the self/symptom), and Socratic dialogue toward meaning. The therapist may also confront avoidance and explore choices.

Relationship. A real, authentic, I–Thou (Buber) human encounter; presence matters more than technique.

Used for / evidence. Grief, terminal illness, life transitions, "existential vacuum," meaning crises. Hard to manualise; evidence is more conceptual than controlled.

Criticisms. Vague, hard to operationalise and test; few specific techniques; demands an articulate, reflective client.

Logotherapy · will to meaning · existential vacuum · paradoxical intention · dereflection = Frankl. The four givens (death/freedom/isolation/meaninglessness) = Yalom. Rollo May = brought existentialism to American therapy. (Paradoxical intention appears in both Frankl and Adlerian/strategic work, but as a named logotherapy technique it's Frankl.)
Recap Ch 5. Existential: free, responsible, self-creating; distress = fleeing the four givens (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness — Yalom). Goal = authentic living + meaning. Relationship-driven; logotherapy (Frankl) adds paradoxical intention + dereflection.

Ch 6. Person-centred therapy — Carl Rogers

Originator. Carl Rogers (1902–1987), American; created the humanistic "client-centred" (later "person-centred") approach and pioneered recorded-session psychotherapy research.

Theory of personality / human nature. Deeply optimistic: every organism has an innate actualising tendency toward growth. The self develops from experience; trouble starts when the child, needing love, takes on conditions of worth ("I'm valued only if…") and so distorts or denies experience that doesn't fit the self-concept. The gap between the organism's experience and the self-concept is incongruence, the source of anxiety and maladjustment.

Goals. Reduce incongruence; help the client drop conditions of worth, reconnect with their own organismic valuing, and move toward becoming a fully functioning person (open to experience, present-living, self-trusting, free, creative).

Techniques / how therapy is done. Famously non-directive: no diagnosis, no advice, no technique in the usual sense. Change is produced by the relationship itself. Rogers specified six necessary and sufficient conditions for change; the three the therapist provides are the core triad: congruence / genuineness (the therapist is real, not a façade), unconditional positive regard (UPR, non-possessive warmth and acceptance), and empathic understanding (accurately sensing the client's inner world and communicating it). The other three conditions: two persons in psychological contact, the client is incongruent / anxious, and the client perceives the therapist's empathy and regard. Skills: reflection of feeling, active listening.

Relationship. The relationship is the therapy. The core conditions are held to be both necessary and sufficient for change.

Used for / evidence. Wide range; strong influence on counselling training. Rogers' own Q-sort and recorded-session research gave humanistic therapy an empirical base. The "common factors" tradition descends partly from here.

Criticisms. May be too passive for clients who want direction or for severe disorders; "necessary and sufficient" is disputed; underplays technique and the social context.

The core triad — congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard, empathy — is Rogers (person-centred). "Six necessary and sufficient conditions" and "fully functioning person" and "actualising tendency" = Rogers. Don't attribute UPR or empathy-as-the-cure to any other theorist.
Lock the count: six conditions total, of which the therapist supplies three (the core triad). Necessary and sufficient.
Recap Ch 6. Rogers: actualising tendency vs conditions of worth → incongruence. Goal = the fully functioning person. Method = the relationship, via the core triad (congruence, UPR, empathy) within six necessary-and-sufficient conditions. Non-directive.

Ch 7. Gestalt therapy — Fritz Perls

Originator. Fritz (Frederick) Perls (1893–1970), with Laura Perls and Paul Goodman. Roots in Gestalt perceptual psychology (figure/ground), psychoanalysis, and existentialism.

Theory of personality / human nature. A person is a whole in constant contact with the environment, organising experience into figure (what's salient now) and ground. Health = staying aware in the here-and-now and completing the cycle of need → contact → satisfaction → withdrawal. Trouble = unfinished business (unexpressed feelings from the past intruding on the present) and breaks in awareness produced by contact-boundary disturbances: introjection (swallowing others' values whole), projection, retroflection (turning back on the self what you'd do to others), confluence (no boundary), and deflection. Perls described five layers of neurosis (phony, phobic, impasse, implosive, explosive).

Goals. Restore awareness in the present; help the client re-own disowned parts and feelings; finish unfinished business; move from environmental support to self-support.

Techniques / experiments. All aimed at present awareness: the empty-chair technique (dialogue with an absent person or a part of the self), the two-chair / top-dog vs under-dog dialogue (the "shoulds" tyrant vs the resisting victim within), staying with feeling, exaggeration, the "I take responsibility" exercise, dream work (every dream element = a projected part of the self, acted out rather than interpreted), playing the projection, attention to body language and language ("it" → "I").

Relationship. Active, present-centred, an I–Thou dialogic meeting; the therapist is engaged and may be confrontational, frustrating avoidance to push awareness.

Used for / evidence. Awareness work, blocked emotion, experiential growth; the empty-chair has decent evidence (Greenberg's emotion-focused therapy descends from it).

Criticisms. Anti-intellectual, technique can become gimmicky / theatrical; the classic confrontational Perls style risks the client.

Empty chair · top-dog vs under-dog · here-and-now awareness · unfinished business · figure/ground · layers of neurosis = Gestalt / Perls. Top-dog (the moralising "should") vs under-dog (the evasive resister) is the internal split played out in the two-chair dialogue. Don't confuse the empty chair (Gestalt) with the "miracle question" (solution-focused).
Recap Ch 7. Perls/Gestalt: whole person, figure/ground, here-and-now awareness; trouble = unfinished business + boundary disturbances (introjection, projection, retroflection, confluence). Goal = awareness + self-support. Tools: empty chair, top-dog/under-dog, dream work as enactment.

Part Four · The action: behavioural & cognitive approaches

Ch 8. Behaviour therapy

Originators. No single founder. Roots in classical conditioning (Pavlov; Watson & Rayner's Little Albert), operant conditioning (Skinner), and the clinical pioneers Joseph Wolpe (systematic desensitisation), Hans Eysenck, and Arnold Lazarus (later, multimodal therapy).

Theory of personality / how problems develop. No deep "personality" structure; behaviour (including symptoms) is learned and maintained by its antecedents and consequences. Problems are maladaptive learned responses: a phobia is a conditioned fear, a habit is a reinforced operant. Change the learning, change the behaviour. Strong focus on observable behaviour, the present, and measurement.

Goals. Concrete, specific, measurable behavioural change agreed with the client; remove maladaptive behaviour and build adaptive behaviour.

Techniques. Built on the conditioning principles: systematic desensitisation (Wolpe — pair a graded fear hierarchy with relaxation, using reciprocal inhibition, so relaxation displaces anxiety); exposure therapies including flooding (intense, prolonged exposure to the feared stimulus with no graded hierarchy and no relaxation, until anxiety extinguishes) and in-vivo / imaginal exposure; token economy (operant — earn tokens for target behaviour, exchange for reinforcers, classically on wards); aversion therapy (pair the unwanted behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus); modeling (Bandura — learn by watching); shaping, contingency contracting, extinction, relaxation training, assertiveness / social-skills training. All begins with a behavioural assessment / functional analysis (A–B–C: antecedent, behaviour, consequence).

Relationship. Important but not curative in itself; the therapist is an active teacher / trainer / consultant who forms a working alliance and reinforces progress.

Used for / evidence. Phobias, anxiety, OCD, habit disorders, skills deficits, institutional and developmental settings. The strongest empirical evidence base of the classical theories.

Criticisms. Can ignore feelings, insight, the relationship and the underlying cause; risk of symptom focus; older "behaviour controls everything" stance is reductionist.

Systematic desensitisation (Wolpe) vs flooding: desensitisation = graded hierarchy + relaxation (reciprocal inhibition), gentle, gradual. Flooding = maximal exposure, no hierarchy, no relaxation, anxiety left to extinguish. Same idea (exposure), opposite intensity. Systematic desensitisation = Wolpe. Token economy = operant/Skinnerian. Modeling = Bandura.
Recap Ch 8. Behaviour therapy: symptoms = learned responses; goal = measurable behaviour change. Tools: systematic desensitisation (Wolpe, graded + relaxation), flooding (intense, no hierarchy), token economy, aversion, modeling, behavioural assessment (A-B-C). Strongest evidence base; therapist as trainer.

Ch 9. Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) — Albert Ellis

Originator. Albert Ellis (1913–2007), American; created REBT in the mid-1950s, the first of the cognitive-behavioural therapies (predates Beck).

Theory of personality / how problems develop. It is not events that disturb us but our beliefs about them. Humans have an innate tendency to think both rationally and irrationally; emotional disturbance comes from irrational beliefs — rigid, absolutist demands expressed as musts, shoulds, oughts (musturbation), and their derivatives: awfulising / catastrophising, low frustration tolerance ("I-can't-stand-it-itis"), and global self/other-rating / damnation.

Goals. Identify and dispute irrational beliefs and replace them with rational, flexible preferences; reach a deep philosophical change and unconditional self-acceptance.

The model — A-B-C-D-E. A = activating event; B = belief about it (rational or irrational); C = emotional/behavioural consequence. The insight: it's B, not A, that causes C. Therapy adds D = disputing the irrational belief (logical, empirical, pragmatic disputation), leading to E = a new effective philosophy / emotion. (Sometimes written A-B-C-D-E-F, F = new feeling.)

Techniques. Active-directive disputing, cognitive (challenging beliefs, cost-benefit analysis), emotive (rational-emotive imagery, shame-attacking exercises, humour, forceful self-statements), and behavioural (homework, risk-taking, in-vivo desensitisation, reinforcement). Heavy use of homework and psychoeducation.

Relationship. Warm but secondary; not necessary in the Rogerian sense. The therapist is a forceful, active, sometimes blunt teacher / debater who models unconditional acceptance of the client while attacking the beliefs.

Used for / evidence. Anxiety, depression, anger, low frustration tolerance; broad applicability; reasonable evidence.

Criticisms. Confrontational style can clash with some clients/cultures; the line between "rational" and "irrational" can be the therapist's value judgement; underplays the relationship and emotion.

REBT (Ellis) vs Cognitive Therapy (Beck) — the most-tested pair. Ellis = A-B-C(DE), irrational beliefs, musturbation, disputing; directive, confrontational, philosophical, therapist argues the belief is illogical. Beck = automatic thoughts, cognitive distortions, schemas, collaborative empiricism; Socratic, gentler, the client tests the thought as a hypothesis with evidence. "Dispute the irrational belief" = Ellis; "test the automatic thought against the evidence" = Beck.
Recap Ch 9. Ellis/REBT: not the event (A) but the irrational belief (B) causes the consequence (C); therapy disputes B (D) → effective new philosophy (E). Irrational beliefs = musts/shoulds (musturbation), awfulising, low frustration tolerance. Directive, confrontational; homework-heavy.

Ch 10. Cognitive therapy — Aaron Beck

Originator. Aaron T. Beck (1921–2021), American psychiatrist; developed cognitive therapy in the 1960s out of his research on depression. (Daughter Judith Beck carried it forward.)

Theory of personality / how problems develop. Emotion and behaviour are shaped by how we interpret events. Early experience lays down schemas (core beliefs); these generate, in the moment, automatic thoughts (rapid, plausible-feeling appraisals just below awareness). When schemas are dysfunctional, perception is filtered through cognitive distortions — systematic errors such as all-or-nothing / dichotomous thinking, overgeneralisation, magnification / minimisation (catastrophising), selective abstraction / mental filter, arbitrary inference, personalisation, labelling, "should" statements, mind-reading / fortune-telling. Depression is marked by the cognitive triad: negative view of the self, the world, and the future.

Goals. Identify, test and correct distorted thinking; change dysfunctional automatic thoughts and, deeper, the underlying schemas; teach the client to become their own therapist.

Techniques — cognitive restructuring. Socratic questioning / guided discovery, identifying and testing automatic thoughts (thought records, the dysfunctional-thought log), collaborative empiricism (client + therapist treat beliefs as hypotheses and gather evidence), behavioural experiments, activity scheduling and mastery-and-pleasure ratings (for depression), graded task assignment, decatastrophising, examining evidence for/against, downward-arrow technique to reach core schemas, homework.

Relationship. A genuine, warm, collaborative partnership (Rogers' conditions are necessary but not sufficient) — the hallmark is collaborative empiricism: not arguing, but jointly investigating.

Used for / evidence. The strongest evidence base of any single approach for depression and anxiety; foundation of modern CBT; extended to many disorders.

Criticisms. May neglect emotion, the past and the relationship; can be too technique-focused; the structure may not suit every client.

Cognitive triad (self / world / future) = Beck, for depression. Automatic thoughts · cognitive distortions · schemas · collaborative empiricism = Beck. See the REBT-vs-Beck trap above: Beck is collaborative and Socratic (client tests the thought against evidence); Ellis is directive and disputational (therapist argues the belief is irrational).
Recap Ch 10. Beck: schemas → automatic thoughts → cognitive distortions → emotion; depression = the cognitive triad. Goal = cognitive restructuring. Method = collaborative empiricism (test thoughts as hypotheses) via Socratic questioning, thought records, behavioural experiments. Foundation of CBT.

Ch 11. Reality therapy / Choice theory — William Glasser

Originator. William Glasser (1925–2013), American psychiatrist; developed reality therapy in the 1960s, later grounding it in choice theory (originally "control theory").

Theory of personality / how problems develop. Behaviour is our best attempt to meet five innate basic needs: survival, love/belonging, power/achievement, freedom, and fun. We act to close the gap between what we want (our "quality world") and what we perceive we're getting. Choice theory: we choose nearly all our behaviour, and we are responsible for it. Total behavior has four inseparable components — acting, thinking, feeling, physiology — and we have the most direct control over acting and thinking (Glasser often uses verbs: "depressing," "anxietying," to stress that even these are chosen). Problems = irresponsible choices and unsatisfying relationships, not "mental illness."

Goals. Help the client make more effective, responsible choices to meet their needs and rebuild relationships; take ownership rather than blaming the past or others.

Techniques — the WDEP system (Robert Wubbolding's formalisation): W = explore Wants (and needs / quality world); D = examine Doing / current behaviour and direction; E = Evaluation (the client self-evaluates: "is what you're doing getting you what you want?"); P = make a Plan (specific, achievable, committed-to). Plans should be SAMIC (simple, attainable, measurable, immediate, controlled by the client). No excuses, no punishment, no giving up.

Relationship. Warm, involved, friendly; the therapist builds involvement first, then focuses on present behaviour and choices. Firm but caring; refuses to accept excuses.

Used for / evidence. Schools, corrections, addictions, behaviour management; popular in education; modest controlled evidence.

Criticisms. Underplays the past, the unconscious, emotion and biology; "you choose your misery" can feel blaming and dismissive of mental illness; limited research.

WDEP · choice theory · total behavior · the five needs (survival, love/belonging, power, freedom, fun) = Glasser (WDEP formalised by Wubbolding). Don't confuse Glasser's five needs with Maslow's hierarchy. "Reality therapy" focuses on present behaviour and responsibility, NOT the past.
Recap Ch 11. Glasser: we choose our behaviour to meet five needs; total behavior = acting/thinking/feeling/physiology (control the first two). Goal = responsible choices + better relationships. Method = WDEP (Wants, Doing, Evaluation, Plan). Present-focused, no excuses.

Part Five · Constructivist & postmodern approaches

Ch 12. Solution-Focused Brief Therapy & Narrative therapy

These constructivist / postmodern approaches share a premise: reality is socially constructed through language and story, so therapy works on meaning, not on a fixed underlying pathology. They are brief, collaborative, and resolutely non-pathologising; the client is the expert on their own life.

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)

Originators. Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg at the Brief Family Therapy Center, Milwaukee (building on the MRI brief-therapy tradition and Milton Erickson).

Core idea. Don't analyse the problem, build the solution. Focus on what the client wants and on times the problem is already absent. The future is created and negotiable.

Goals. Co-construct a clear, concrete preferred future and amplify what already works; small change leads to bigger change.

Signature techniques. The miracle question ("Suppose tonight while you sleep a miracle happens and the problem is solved — what would you notice tomorrow that tells you?"), exception questions ("when is the problem not happening, or less?"), scaling questions ("on 0–10, where are you, and what would +1 look like?"), the formula first-session task, coping questions, and compliments. Rule of thumb: if it works do more of it; if it doesn't, do something different.

Relationship. Collaborative, respectful, client-as-expert; therapist asks, doesn't prescribe.

Used for / evidence. Brief, broad applicability; growing evidence; popular in schools and agencies.

Criticisms. May be too quick, can gloss over real pathology and emotion, may not suit complex/chronic cases.

Narrative therapy

Originators. Michael White (Australia) and David Epston (New Zealand).

Core idea. We live by stories we tell about ourselves; a problem-saturated "dominant story" can trap a person. The problem is the problem; the person is not the problem.

Goals. Separate the person from the problem and help them re-author a richer, preferred story rooted in their own values and competence.

Signature techniques. Externalising the problem (talk about "the Anger" or "the Depression" as a separate entity acting on the person, often by naming it), mapping the problem's influence, finding unique outcomes / sparkling moments (times the person resisted the problem), re-authoring the story around those exceptions, therapeutic documents / letters, and recruiting an audience / definitional ceremony ("outsider witnesses").

Relationship. Collaborative, curious, non-expert, de-centred-but-influential; the therapist is an investigative co-author.

Used for / evidence. Family work, identity and trauma narratives, oppression-linked problems; evidence largely qualitative.

Criticisms. Hard to evaluate empirically; intellectually demanding; can underweight biological/severe illness.

Miracle question · exception · scaling questions = SFBT (de Shazer & Berg). Externalising the problem · re-authoring · unique outcomes = Narrative (White & Epston). Both are postmodern/constructivist and "client-as-expert," but SFBT builds the solution/future while narrative re-writes the story. "Miracle question" is the most-tested single item — it's de Shazer, NOT Gestalt's empty chair.
Recap Ch 12. SFBT (de Shazer & Berg): build the solution, not analyse the problem; miracle/exception/scaling questions. Narrative (White & Epston): externalise the problem, find unique outcomes, re-author the story. Both brief, non-pathologising, client-as-expert.

Ch 13. Feminist therapy

Originators. No single founder; a movement arising from the 1960s–70s women's movement (figures include Jean Baker Miller, Carolyn Enns, Laura Brown). Often integrated with other approaches.

Theory of personality / how problems develop. Psychological distress cannot be separated from the social and political context, especially gender-role socialisation and the unequal distribution of power. Much "pathology" in women is a reasonable response to oppression, sexism and trauma. The guiding slogan: "the personal is political."

Goals. Symptom relief plus broader change: raise awareness of how gender-role and power dynamics shaped the client's problems; foster empowerment, self-definition, and (often) social action; value the client's own experience.

Techniques. Gender-role analysis (examine how gendered expectations affected the client) and gender-role intervention; power analysis (examine power differences and how to gain personal power); assertiveness training; reframing and relabelling (move blame from the individual to the social cause); bibliotherapy; self-disclosure; demystifying therapy.

Relationship. Deliberately egalitarian — minimise the power differential, share information, treat the client as an equal collaborator.

Used for / evidence. Trauma, abuse survivors, identity, gender and multicultural issues; emphasises social justice. Mostly principle-driven rather than RCT-driven.

Criticisms. Risk of imposing the therapist's politics/values; debated boundaries (self-disclosure); critiqued for sometimes centring on particular groups of women.

"The personal is political" · gender-role analysis · power analysis · egalitarian relationship = feminist therapy. The egalitarian, power-flattening relationship is its signature stance.
Recap Ch 13. Feminist therapy: distress is rooted in gender-role socialisation and power inequality ("the personal is political"). Goal = empowerment + awareness + social action. Tools: gender-role analysis, power analysis, assertiveness, reframing. Egalitarian relationship.

Part Six · Family systems & other approaches

Ch 14. Family systems therapies

The unit of treatment shifts from the individual to the system. Drawing on general systems theory and cybernetics, these models hold that a symptom in one member (the "identified patient") expresses a dysfunction in the whole family's patterns; you change the person by changing the system. Key shared concepts: homeostasis (the system resists change), circular causality (not A→B but a mutual loop), feedback loops, boundaries, and subsystems. Sharf covers four major schools.

Bowen — multigenerational / transgenerational family therapy

Originator. Murray Bowen.

Core constructs: differentiation of self (the capacity to separate intellect from emotion and self from family fusion — the central goal), triangulation (a two-person tension pulls in a third to stabilise), the nuclear family emotional system, the family projection process, the multigenerational transmission process, emotional cutoff, and sibling position. Signature tool: the genogram (a multigenerational family map/diagram). Goal: raise differentiation and detriangulate; the therapist stays calm and de-triangled (a non-anxious presence). Insight-leaning, slower, multigenerational.

Structural family therapy

Originator. Salvador Minuchin.

Focus on the family's structure: its boundaries (which range from rigid → disengaged families, to diffuse → enmeshed families, with clear boundaries in between), its subsystems (spousal, parental, sibling), hierarchy, alignments and coalitions. Pathology = a faulty structure. Techniques: joining and accommodating, mapping the structure, enactment (have the family play out the problem interaction in session so the therapist can see and reshape it), boundary-making, unbalancing, and reframing. Active, present-focused; the therapist restructures the system.

Strategic family therapy

Originators. Jay Haley and Cloé Madanes; the MRI group (Watzlawick, Weakland) and the influence of Milton Erickson; the Milan group nearby.

Focus on solving the presenting problem via planned interventions; the therapist takes responsibility for designing a strategy. Symptoms serve a function and are maintained by the family's attempted "solutions." Techniques: directives (tasks assigned between sessions), paradoxical interventions (e.g. prescribing the symptom — instructing the family to keep doing it, so it loses its grip), reframing, ordeals, pretend techniques. Brief, directive, problem-focused; little emphasis on insight.

Satir — experiential / communications (human validation)

Originator. Virginia Satir.

Warm, growth- and communication-focused; emphasises self-esteem, congruent communication and emotional experience. Her famous communication / coping stances under stress: placater, blamer, super-reasonable (computer), irrelevant (distractor), and the healthy congruent / leveller. Techniques: family sculpting, family reconstruction, role play, touch and warmth, modelling congruent communication. Goals: raise self-esteem and honest, congruent communication.

Family-theorist swaps (a top NIMHANS trap): Bowen = differentiation, triangulation, genogram, multigenerational (insight). Minuchin = structural: boundaries (enmeshed vs disengaged), subsystems, enactment, joining. Haley = strategic: directives, paradox / prescribing the symptom, problem-focused. Satir = experiential/communication: the four stress stances + congruent leveller, family sculpting. "Genogram" → Bowen; "enactment / boundaries" → Minuchin; "directive / paradox" → Haley; "sculpting / placater-blamer" → Satir.
Recap Ch 14. Family systems: treat the system, not the person (identified patient, homeostasis, circular causality, boundaries). Bowen = differentiation + genogram; Minuchin = structure/boundaries + enactment; Haley = strategic directives + paradox; Satir = communication stances + sculpting.

Ch 15. Transactional Analysis & a note on integrative / eclectic approaches

Transactional Analysis (TA)

Originator. Eric Berne (1910–1970); popularised in Games People Play.

Theory of personality. Personality has three ego states: Parent (taught, the internalised authority — nurturing or critical), Adult (the rational, here-and-now data processor), and Child (felt, the spontaneous or adapted child). Transactions between people occur between ego states and can be complementary (smooth), crossed (conflict), or ulterior (hidden agenda). Early decisions form a life script; we exchange strokes (units of recognition); and we play repetitive games (e.g. "Why Don't You — Yes But") that pay off in bad feelings. A person's basic life position is one of four: I'm OK–You're OK (healthy), I'm OK–You're not OK, I'm not OK–You're OK, I'm not OK–You're not OK.

Goals & techniques. Reach autonomy via awareness, spontaneity, intimacy; strengthen the Adult; rewrite the script. Techniques: structural analysis (of ego states), transactional analysis proper (of exchanges), game analysis, script analysis; usually contract-based and often done in groups.

Criticisms. Jargon-heavy, popularised; uneven evidence.

Integrative / eclectic approaches

Most practising counsellors are not "pure" anything. Sharf distinguishes routes to combining theories: technical eclecticism (pick whatever techniques work, regardless of theory — e.g. Lazarus's multimodal therapy, the BASIC ID), theoretical integration (blend two or more theories into a new whole — e.g. Wachtel's cyclical psychodynamics), the common-factors route (use what all therapies share — see NIMHANS box), and assimilative integration (work from one home theory while importing techniques). The aim is to fit the therapy to the client, not the client to one theory.

Ego states (Parent–Adult–Child) · games · scripts · strokes · life positions = TA / Berne. Multimodal therapy / BASIC ID / technical eclecticism = Arnold Lazarus.
Recap Ch 15. Berne/TA: Parent-Adult-Child ego states; complementary/crossed/ulterior transactions; games, scripts, strokes; "I'm OK–You're OK." Integration: technical eclecticism (Lazarus, BASIC ID) vs theoretical integration vs common factors vs assimilative.

Ch 16. Comparison & integration — how the theories line up

Sharf closes by lining the theories up on the recurring axes. Reading these axes off, you can place any theory and predict its "which approach" answer.

  • View of human nature. Determined / pessimistic at one end (psychoanalysis), free / optimistic / growth-oriented at the other (person-centred, existential, Adlerian, humanistic). Behavioural = neutral (a product of learning, neither good nor bad).
  • Role of the past. Past-focused: psychoanalysis, Jungian, (Adlerian uses early recollections but aims forward). Present/future-focused: behavioural, cognitive, REBT, reality, Gestalt (radically here-and-now), solution-focused, narrative.
  • Directiveness. Highly directive: behaviour therapy, REBT, reality therapy, strategic family. Non-directive: person-centred (the extreme). In between / collaborative: Beck's CT, Adlerian, solution-focused.
  • The relationship. The cure itself: person-centred (necessary and sufficient). A collaboration: cognitive, Adlerian, the postmodern therapies. The site of the work via transference: psychoanalysis. A means to an end / working alliance: behaviour therapy, REBT.
  • Insight vs action. Insight-based: psychoanalysis, Jungian. Action/doing-based: behavioural, reality, solution-focused, strategic. Cognitive-and-both: REBT and Beck (insight into thinking, then behavioural action).
NIMHANS — the four big "force" eras. The first force = psychodynamic; the second = behavioural/cognitive-behavioural; the third = humanistic-existential; the (sometimes-cited) fourth = multicultural / social-justice (and/or transpersonal). Useful for "which wave / force" stems.

All the MCQ traps, collected

  • REBT (Ellis) vs Cognitive Therapy (Beck): Ellis = A-B-C(DE), irrational beliefs / musturbation, disputing, directive & confrontational. Beck = automatic thoughts / cognitive distortions / schemas, collaborative empiricism, Socratic, the client tests the thought as a hypothesis. REBT predates Beck.
  • Cognitive triad (negative self / world / future) = Beck (depression), NOT Ellis.
  • Systematic desensitisation (Wolpe) = graded hierarchy + relaxation (reciprocal inhibition). Flooding = intense, no hierarchy, no relaxation, extinguish anxiety. Both are exposure; opposite intensities.
  • Token economy = operant / Skinnerian. Modeling / observational learning = Bandura. Aversion therapy = pair behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus.
  • Person-centred core triad (congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard, empathy) = Rogers. "Six necessary and sufficient conditions," "fully functioning person," "actualising tendency" = Rogers.
  • Empty chair · top-dog vs under-dog · here-and-now awareness · unfinished business = Gestalt / Perls.
  • Miracle question · exception · scaling questions = SFBT / de Shazer & Berg. (Most-tested: miracle question is de Shazer, not Gestalt.)
  • Externalising the problem · re-authoring · unique outcomes = Narrative / White & Epston.
  • Logotherapy · will to meaning · existential vacuum · paradoxical intention · dereflection = Frankl. The four givens (death/freedom/isolation/meaninglessness) = Yalom. Rollo May brought existentialism to US therapy.
  • Will to pleasure = Freud · will to power / superiority = Adler · will to meaning = Frankl.
  • WDEP · choice theory · total behavior · five needs (survival, love/belonging, power, freedom, fun) = Glasser (WDEP formalised by Wubbolding). Glasser's five needs ≠ Maslow's hierarchy.
  • Inferiority complex · style of life · social interest · birth order · early recollections · encouragement = Adler.
  • Collective unconscious · archetypes · individuation · amplification = Jung (NOT Freud's personal unconscious / free association).
  • Free association · dream analysis (manifest/latent) · transference · interpretation = Freud / psychoanalysis.
  • Family swaps: genogram + differentiation + triangulation = Bowen · boundaries (enmeshed/disengaged) + enactment + subsystems = Minuchin (structural) · directives + paradox / prescribe the symptom = Haley (strategic) · family sculpting + placater/blamer/computer/distractor = Satir (experiential).
  • "Identified patient · homeostasis · circular causality" = family systems generally.
  • "The personal is political" · gender-role & power analysis · egalitarian relationship = feminist therapy.
  • Parent-Adult-Child ego states · games · scripts · strokes · "I'm OK–You're OK" = TA / Berne.
  • Multimodal therapy / BASIC ID / technical eclecticism = Lazarus.
  • "The relationship is necessary and sufficient" = Rogers; "necessary but not sufficient" = Beck / CBT (a common stem flip).

Beyond Sharf — what NIMHANS adds

Common factors & the Dodo-bird verdict (Wampold). Across the bona-fide therapies, much of the outcome is explained by common factors shared by all (the therapeutic alliance, empathy, expectancy/hope, a believable rationale + ritual, goal consensus) rather than the specific techniques. The "Dodo-bird verdict" (from Rosenzweig, championed by Luborsky and Bruce Wampold's contextual model): "everyone has won and all must have prizes," i.e. all therapies achieve broadly comparable outcomes. The working/therapeutic alliance (Bordin: bond + goals + tasks) is the most robust predictor of outcome.
Stages of change — the Transtheoretical Model (Prochaska & DiClemente). Change moves through stages: precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance (with relapse possible). Match the intervention to the stage; "the client isn't resistant, they're precontemplative."
Motivational Interviewing (Miller & Rollnick). A client-centred but directive method to resolve ambivalence and elicit change talk. Spirit = partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation (PACE); core skills = OARS (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, Summaries); "roll with resistance," develop discrepancy, support self-efficacy. Pairs naturally with the stages of change.
Multicultural counselling competencies (Sue & Sue). The "fourth force." A competent counsellor has awareness (of own biases/worldview), knowledge (of the client's culture), and skills (culturally appropriate interventions). Watch for cultural encapsulation, etic vs emic, and the limits of imposing Western individualistic therapy.
Ethics of the counselling frame. Core principles (Beauchamp & Childress / Kitchener): autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, fidelity (and veracity). The frame: informed consent, confidentiality and its limits (Tarasoff duty to warn/protect; harm to self/others; abuse), clear boundaries (avoid dual relationships), competence, and supervision. India: RCI registration for clinical psychologists; MHCA 2017 frame.

Self-test (active recall, by theory)

Cover the answers, ask each aloud, then check. Miss one → jump back to its chapter. Answers follow in green.

Psychoanalysis, Jung, Adler

  1. The defining technique and the defining relationship mechanism of psychoanalysis? Free association; the transference.
  2. Collective unconscious + individuation + amplification belong to whom? Jung (NOT Freud, who has only the personal unconscious + free association).
  3. Adler's mark of mental health, and his two assessment tools? Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl); early recollections and birth order.
  4. Match the three "wills": pleasure, power, meaning. Freud (pleasure), Adler (power), Frankl (meaning).

Existential & person-centred

  1. Yalom's four givens? Death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness.
  2. Name the two logotherapy techniques and their author. Paradoxical intention and dereflection (Frankl).
  3. Rogers' core triad, and how many conditions in total? Congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard, empathy; six necessary-and-sufficient conditions (therapist supplies three).
  4. What does the client take on that produces incongruence? Conditions of worth (Rogers).

Gestalt, behaviour, REBT, cognitive

  1. Empty chair and top-dog/under-dog belong to whom? Perls / Gestalt.
  2. Systematic desensitisation vs flooding — the two differences? Desensitisation = graded hierarchy + relaxation (Wolpe); flooding = intense, no hierarchy, no relaxation.
  3. Spell out Ellis's model and what truly causes C. A-B-C-D-E; it's B (the belief), not A (the event), that causes C.
  4. Beck's hallmark relationship stance and his depression triad? Collaborative empiricism; the cognitive triad (negative self, world, future).
  5. One line: REBT vs Beck. Ellis disputes irrational beliefs (directive); Beck tests automatic thoughts as hypotheses (collaborative/Socratic).

Reality, constructivist, feminist

  1. Expand WDEP and name its author(s). Wants, Doing, Evaluation, Plan (Glasser; formalised by Wubbolding).
  2. Glasser's five needs? Survival, love/belonging, power, freedom, fun.
  3. Miracle question vs externalising — which theory and founder each? Miracle question = SFBT (de Shazer & Berg); externalising = narrative (White & Epston).
  4. The feminist slogan and signature relationship stance? "The personal is political"; an egalitarian relationship.

Family systems & TA

  1. Genogram and differentiation = whose model? Bowen (multigenerational).
  2. Enactment and enmeshed/disengaged boundaries = whose? Minuchin (structural).
  3. Prescribing the symptom / directives = whose? Haley (strategic).
  4. The four Satir stress stances plus the healthy one? Placater, blamer, super-reasonable (computer), irrelevant (distractor); healthy = congruent/leveller.
  5. Berne's three ego states and the healthy life position? Parent, Adult, Child; "I'm OK–You're OK."

NIMHANS extensions

  1. What does the Dodo-bird verdict claim, and who champions the contextual/common-factors model? All bona-fide therapies achieve roughly equal outcomes; Wampold (alliance is the top predictor).
  2. List the stages of change and their authors. Precontemplation→contemplation→preparation→action→maintenance (Prochaska & DiClemente).
  3. Spell OARS and name MI's authors. Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, Summaries (Miller & Rollnick).
