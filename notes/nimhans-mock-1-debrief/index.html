A) Urea
B) Tyrosine  ← correct
C) Tryptophan  ← you picked
D) Histidine

Your note: "DOnt know this. need to learn"
Why B. PKU = autosomal recessive deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH). The enzyme normally converts phenylalanine → tyrosine. With PAH gone, phenylalanine piles up (toxic to brain in infancy) and tyrosine drops. The downstream consequence is intellectual disability, fair skin / hair (tyrosine is the precursor for melanin), and a musty body odour. The amino-acid → neurotransmitter chain (memorise this entire row):
  • Phenylalanine → Tyrosine → L-DOPA → Dopamine → Norepinephrine → Epinephrine. The whole catecholamine pathway. PKU breaks step 1. Parkinson's loses dopamine at step 4 (treat with L-DOPA, not dopamine, because dopamine doesn't cross the BBB).
  • Tryptophan → 5-HTP → Serotonin → Melatonin. The indolamine pathway. SSRIs raise serotonin synaptic levels by blocking reuptake. Tryptophan is also the precursor for niacin (B3); pellagra = niacin deficiency.
  • Histidine → Histamine. CNS arousal NT (one reason antihistamines sedate, they cross the BBB and block H1).
  • Glutamate → GABA via glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), B6 cofactor. Single most important inhibitory NT; benzos + alcohol potentiate GABA-A.

What is the alternative name for adrenaline, the hormone released by the adrenal medulla during the fight-or-flight response?
B) Epinephrine  ← correct, you picked
Your note: "What about norepinephrine?"

n1_bst01Biostatsguessed-rightNIMHANS 2024
Risk ratio = 1.0 in a cohort study. Means: C) no difference in risk between groups  ← correct, you guessed.
Your note: "wtf is risk ratio?"
Risk ratio (RR) = relative risk = incidence in exposed / incidence in unexposed.
  • RR = 1 → no association (incidence equal in both groups).
  • RR > 1 → exposure raises risk. RR = 2 means exposed group has twice the risk.
  • RR < 1 → exposure protects. RR = 0.5 means exposed group has half the risk.
Used in cohort studies and RCTs (you know who developed the outcome over time).
RR vs OR vs HR, the trio that owns biostats MCQs:
MeasureWhatWhen used
RR (risk ratio)incidence in exposed / incidence in unexposedCohort, RCT
OR (odds ratio)odds of exposure in cases / odds in controlsCase-control (you can't compute incidence)
HR (hazard ratio)instantaneous risk ratio over timeSurvival analysis, Cox regression
ARR (absolute risk reduction)risk-unexposed minus risk-exposedRCTs
NNT1 / ARRHow many you treat to prevent 1 outcome
Rule of thumb: When outcome is rare (<10%), OR ≈ RR. When outcome is common, OR exaggerates the effect.
n1_bst04BiostatsrightNIMHANS 2024
Multi-stage cluster sampling (5 districts → 10 schools → 30 students) — you got C right but wrote "need to understand what makes it the right answer."
Your note: "Need to understand what exactly makes it the right answer"
Why cluster (specifically multi-stage cluster), not stratified.
  • Cluster sampling: you sample entire groups (clusters) at each level, not individuals. Districts are clusters of schools, schools are clusters of students. The question samples randomly at each stage, all the way down. That's textbook multi-stage cluster.

n1_bst06Biostatsguessed-rightNIMHANS 2024
Scree plot: eigenvalues 3.5 → 1.8 → 0.9 → 0.7 → 0.5. Kaiser-Guttman + elbow says: C) 2 factors. You guessed and got it.
Your note: "Dont know anything about this"
This is a factor-analysis question. Two rules combined:
  • Kaiser-Guttman rule: retain factors with eigenvalue > 1.0. Eigenvalue = variance explained by that factor (in standardised units). >1 means the factor explains more variance than a single observed variable would, so it's "worth keeping." In your data, only 3.5 and 1.8 qualify. The rest are below 1. → 2 factors.
  • Scree (Cattell): plot eigenvalues against factor number, find the "elbow" where the line flattens. Keep factors above the elbow. The big drop here is from 1.8 to 0.9, so the elbow is at factor 2. Agrees with Kaiser.
Factor analysis vocabulary (NIMHANS test-construction territory):
  • EFA (exploratory) = no a-priori structure, let the data tell you. Common in scale development.
  • CFA (confirmatory) = you have a model, test it. Uses SEM (structural equation modelling).
  • Rotation: orthogonal (varimax) = factors stay uncorrelated. Oblique (promax, oblimin) = factors allowed to correlate. Psych constructs usually correlate, so oblique is more realistic but harder to interpret.
  • Communality (h²) = variance of an item explained by all retained factors.
  • Loading = correlation of an item with a factor. ≥0.30 = weak, ≥0.40 = moderate, ≥0.50 = strong.
  • PCA vs FA: PCA = data reduction, uses total variance. FA = latent structure, uses common variance. Often interchanged in MCQs, NIMHANS doesn't usually split hairs.

Ernst Weber (1834): ΔI / I = k. The smallest detectable change in stimulus intensity (JND, just-noticeable-difference) is a constant proportion of the original intensity. Example: in a room with one candle, adding a second is obvious. In a room with 100 candles, adding one is invisible — you'd need to add ~10 (assuming Weber fraction 0.1 for light).
  • Fechner's Law (1860): extends Weber. Sensation = k × log(intensity). The subjective magnitude grows as the logarithm of physical intensity.
  • Stevens' Power Law (1957): psychophysics revision. Sensation = k × (intensity)^a. Different exponents for different modalities (brightness ~0.33, length ~1.0, electric shock ~3.5).
Classical psychophysics methods (Fechner):
  • Method of limits: ascending / descending series, find the threshold crossover.
  • Method of constant stimuli: random presentation, percent detected → psychometric function.
  • Method of adjustment: subject sets the stimulus to match.
  • Signal detection theory → Green & Swets. d' = sensitivity (separates signal from noise). β (or c) = response criterion (bias). 4 outcomes: hit, miss, false alarm, correct rejection. Threshold is not a fixed value — it's a decision under uncertainty.
