How abnormal psychology is tested. (1) Name the disorder from a vignette ("she restricts food, BMI 16, terrified of weight gain, sees herself as fat" = anorexia nervosa). (2) Pin the cut-off (panic disorder needs 1 month of worry after the attacks; GAD needs 6 months; schizophrenia needs 6 months in DSM but only 1 month in ICD). (3) Spot the DSM-vs-ICD difference (DSM keeps a 6-month duration for schizophrenia, ICD-11 needs 1; ICD-11 has Complex PTSD, DSM doesn't; ICD-11 made personality disorders dimensional, DSM-5 kept the 10 categories). (4) Tell look-alikes apart, that's where the distractor sits: delirium vs dementia, conversion vs factitious vs malingering, schizoaffective vs psychotic depression, bipolar II vs cyclothymia. Learn every disorder in its contrast pair and learn its number (the cut-off).
The frame that organises the whole field. Abnormality itself is judged on the "4 Ds" — Deviance (statistically rare / norm-violating), Distress (it hurts), Dysfunction (it impairs work/relationships/self-care), Danger (to self or others) — with a fifth lens, cultural/contextual relativity, always layered on top (a behaviour normal in one culture is pathology in another; criteria are guides, not verdicts). Every modern explanation is some mix of the biopsychosocial model run through diathesis-stress: a predisposition (genetic / temperamental / cognitive) that a stressor converts into an episode. Hold those two and you can reconstruct almost any "which model / what causes it" answer.

Part One · Foundations

Ch 1. What counts as abnormal, and the models that explain it

No single criterion defines abnormality; psychology uses a convergence of indicators, the "4 Ds". Deviance — the behaviour breaks the statistical or social norm (rare, or violates the rules a society lives by). Distress — it causes the person suffering (but not always: a person in a manic high feels great, so distress alone is neither necessary nor sufficient). Dysfunction — it interferes with daily functioning (work, relationships, self-care): this is the heaviest single criterion, and the one DSM/ICD both require. Danger — risk of harm to self or others (least common, often over-weighted by laypeople). A fifth qualifier rides on all of them: cultural and contextual relativity — the same act is healthy in one context and pathological in another (grief, trance, religious experience), so the clinician asks "is this deviant for this person's culture and situation?"

The single best one-word criterion = dysfunction / impairment (harmful dysfunction, Wakefield). Distress is not required (mania, antisocial PD), and statistical rarity alone is not abnormality (genius is rare and not a disorder).

The models of abnormality (each = a cause + a cure)

ModelCause of disorderTreatment it implies
Biological / medicalGenes, neurotransmitters, brain structure, endocrineDrugs, ECT, rTMS
Psychodynamic (Freud)Unconscious conflict, early-childhood, defencesPsychoanalysis, insight

Diathesis-stress is the integrating idea. A diathesis (vulnerability: a gene, a temperament, a cognitive style) sits silent until a stressor activates it; the disorder is the product, not either alone. This is why identical-twin concordance for schizophrenia is ~50% not 100% (genes load the gun, environment pulls the trigger), and why it underlies nearly every "what causes X" answer in the modern syllabus.
Recap Ch 1. Abnormality = 4 Ds (Deviance, Distress, Dysfunction, Danger) + cultural relativity; dysfunction is the core. Seven models, each pairing a cause with a cure; the biopsychosocial model run through diathesis-stress integrates them.

Ch 2. Classification — the two systems, and the gap MCQs live in

Two manuals classify mental disorders. DSM-5-TR (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th ed, Text Revision 2022) — published by the American Psychiatric Association (APA), used mainly for research and in the US. ICD-11 (International Classification of Diseases, 11th rev) — published by the WHO, the global standard, came into effect 1 January 2022; its mental-disorders section is Chapter 06. India follows ICD officially, but exams test DSM criteria heavily, so you carry both.

FeatureDSM-5-TR (APA)ICD-11 (WHO)
ScopeMental disorders onlyAll diseases; mental disorders = Chapter 06
ApproachLargely categorical, criteria-based, operationalisedPrototype/description-based; more clinician judgment
AxesMultiaxial system abandoned in DSM-5 (the old 5 axes are gone)Never used DSM-style axes
Schizophrenia duration6 months (incl. 1 month active)1 month of active symptoms
Schizophrenia subtypesDropped (no more paranoid/catatonic/hebephrenic)Dropped too; uses symptom specifiers
Personality disordersKept the 10 categorical types (Section II); a dimensional model sits in Section IIIFully dimensional — severity + 5 trait domains + a borderline pattern
PTSDOne PTSD (4 symptom clusters)Simplified PTSD + a separate Complex PTSD
BereavementAdded Prolonged Grief Disorder (new in DSM-5-TR)Has Prolonged Grief Disorder too
Gaming / genderInternet gaming in appendix (for study)Added Gaming disorder; moved gender incongruence OUT of mental disorders
NIMHANS / RCI loves the deltas. The most-asked classification facts: (1) DSM-5 abandoned the multiaxial system; (2) both manuals dropped schizophrenia subtypes; (3) schizophrenia is 6 months DSM vs 1 month ICD; (4) ICD-11 made PDs dimensional and added Complex PTSD; (5) ICD-11 de-pathologised gender incongruence (no longer a mental disorder) and added gaming disorder. ICD-11 also folded gender, sexual conditions and reorganised them, and moved OCD and PTSD into their own groupings just as DSM-5 did.
Recap Ch 2. DSM-5-TR (APA, categorical, no axes) vs ICD-11 (WHO, global, in force 2022, more dimensional). Memorise the deltas: schizophrenia 6 vs 1 month, subtypes gone in both, ICD-11 dimensional PDs + Complex PTSD + gaming disorder + gender incongruence removed.

Ch 3. The Mental State Examination (how the disorder is observed)

Ahuja's MSE is the clinician's structured snapshot of the patient now. Learn the headings in order; vignette MCQs quote one cell and ask which heading it belongs to.

HeadingWhat's assessed · key terms
1. General appearance & behaviourGrooming, eye contact, rapport, psychomotor activity (retardation / agitation), mannerisms, catatonia
2. SpeechRate, volume, tone; pressure of speech (mania), poverty of speech (depression/negative)
3. Mood & affectMood = sustained subjective ("how do you feel"); affect = observed, moment-to-moment (blunted, flat, labile, incongruent)
4. ThoughtStream (flight of ideas, retardation), form (loosening of associations, tangentiality, circumstantiality, word salad, neologisms), content (delusions, obsessions, overvalued ideas)
5. PerceptionHallucinations (modality), illusions, depersonalisation/derealisation
6. CognitionConsciousness, orientation (time/place/person), attention & concentration, memory (immediate/recent/remote), MMSE
7. InsightAwareness of being ill (graded I–VI); poor in psychosis/mania, retained in neurosis
8. JudgementPersonal, social, test judgement
Mood vs affect: mood is the climate (sustained, subjective), affect is the weather (immediate, observed). Form vs content of thought: form = how thoughts connect (loosening, tangentiality → schizophrenia); content = what they are about (delusions). Illusion vs hallucination: illusion = misperception of a real stimulus; hallucination = perception with no stimulus.
Recap Ch 3. MSE = appearance/behaviour → speech → mood & affect → thought (stream/form/content) → perception → cognition → insight → judgement. Mood vs affect and form vs content of thought are the two cells most asked.

Part Two · Psychotic disorders

Ch 4. Schizophrenia

Core. A chronic psychotic disorder of disturbed thought, perception, affect and behaviour with relatively preserved consciousness and intellect. Lifetime risk ~1%, equal in both sexes (earlier onset in men, late teens–20s; women mid-20s–30s). The term is Eugen Bleuler's (1911, "splitting of psychic functions"); Emil Kraepelin earlier called it dementia praecox (early dementia, poor outcome).

Clinical picture — three classic frameworks.

FrameworkContent
Bleuler's 4 A's (fundamental sx)Affect (blunted/inappropriate), Associations (loosening), Ambivalence, Autism (withdrawal into inner world). Hallucinations/delusions are accessory, not fundamental.
Schneider's First-Rank SymptomsThought echo / insertion / withdrawal / broadcast; delusional perception; passivity (made acts, feelings, impulses, somatic passivity); third-person auditory hallucinations (running commentary, voices arguing/discussing the patient). Highly suggestive but not pathognomonic.
Positive vs negative (Crow's Type I/II)Positive (excess): delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech/behaviour — respond to drugs, dopamine-linked. Negative (deficit): 5 A's — Affective flattening, Alogia, Avolition, Anhedonia, Asociality (+ attention) — drug-resistant, worse prognosis.

Diagnostic criteria. DSM-5: ≥2 of [delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised/catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms], at least one being one of the first three; continuous signs ≥ 6 months with ≥ 1 month of active-phase symptoms; social/occupational decline. ICD-11: ≥2 core symptoms (incl. ≥1 of delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking, experiences of influence/passivity) for ≥ 1 month; subtypes dropped, replaced by symptom specifiers (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive).

The duration delta: schizophrenia = 6 months in DSM, only 1 month in ICD. Under 1 month with full recovery = brief psychotic disorder (DSM) / acute & transient psychotic disorder (ICD). 1–6 months = schizophreniform (DSM).
Subtypes are abolished in both DSM-5 and ICD-11. The old paranoid, hebephrenic/disorganised, catatonic, undifferentiated, residual, simple types are legacy ICD-10. "Hebephrenic" (disorganised, early onset, poor prognosis) and "simple" (insidious negative-only) were ICD-only; don't pick a subtype as a current DSM-5 diagnosis.

Etiology (one line each). Genetic — strongest risk factor; ~50% MZ-twin concordance, ~10% in a sibling/child. Dopamine hypothesis — mesolimbic hyperactivity → positive symptoms (all antipsychotics block D2); mesocortical hypoactivity → negative symptoms; glutamate (NMDA) and serotonin also implicated. Neurodevelopmental — enlarged ventricles, reduced grey matter, winter births, obstetric complications. Expressed Emotion (EE) — high family criticism/over-involvement predicts relapse (social maintenance factor).

Course & prognosis. Usually chronic with relapses. Good-prognosis features: acute/sudden onset, late onset, clear precipitant, prominent positive/mood symptoms, good premorbid functioning, married, female, paranoid picture. Poor-prognosis: insidious onset, young onset, no precipitant, prominent negative symptoms, poor premorbid adjustment, single, male.

Treatment. Antipsychotics: typical/first-gen (haloperidol, chlorpromazine — strong D2 block, extrapyramidal side-effects, tardive dyskinesia) and atypical/second-gen (risperidone, olanzapine, clozapine for treatment-resistant — needs WBC monitoring for agranulocytosis). Plus psychosocial: CBT for psychosis, family therapy (lowers EE), social-skills training, rehabilitation.

Recap Ch 4. Schizophrenia (~1%): Bleuler 4 A's (fundamental) + Schneider FRS + positive/negative (Crow I/II, negative = 5 A's). DSM 6 months / ICD 1 month; subtypes abolished. Dopamine + genes + neurodevelopment + high EE. Good prognosis = acute onset, positive/mood sx, female; treat with D2-blocking antipsychotics (clozapine if resistant) + psychosocial.

Schizoaffective vs psychotic depression/mania. If psychotic symptoms occur only during mood episodes → it's a mood disorder with psychotic features. If there's a stretch of ≥2 weeks of psychosis with the mood normal → schizoaffective. Mood-congruent delusions (guilt, poverty, nihilism in depression; grandeur in mania) point to a mood disorder.
Recap Ch 5. By duration: brief psychotic <1mo → schizophreniform 1–6mo → schizophrenia ≥6mo (DSM). Delusional disorder = isolated delusion, function intact. Schizoaffective = needs 2 weeks of psychosis without mood. Folie à deux = induced/shared delusion.

Part Three · Mood (affective) disorders

Ch 6. Depressive disorders

Core. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD): pervasive low mood and/or loss of interest with vegetative and cognitive symptoms. Lifetime prevalence ~15%, ~2× more common in women. Mean onset late 20s.

DSM-5 criteria — ≥5 of 9 symptoms over the same 2 weeks, ≥1 being depressed mood or anhedonia. Mnemonic SIG E CAPS + mood: Sleep (insomnia/hypersomnia), Interest loss (anhedonia), Guilt/worthlessness, Energy loss/fatigue, Concentration impaired, Appetite/weight change, Psychomotor agitation/retardation, Suicidality, + depressed mood. Causes distress/impairment; not due to substance/medical cause. ICD-11 groups by severity (mild/moderate/severe ± psychotic) and lists affective, cognitive and neurovegetative clusters.

Specifiers / subtypes. Melancholic (anhedonia, early-morning waking, diurnal variation worse in AM, marked guilt, psychomotor change — the "biological" depression). Atypical (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, hyperphagia/weight gain, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity). Psychotic (mood-congruent delusions: guilt, nihilism/Cotard, poverty). Seasonal (winter, light-responsive). Peripartum (within 4 weeks/pregnancy). Catatonic.

Other depressive disorders. Persistent Depressive Disorder (PDD / dysthymia) — depressed mood most days for ≥2 years (1 year in children), milder but chronic. Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) — luteal-phase mood symptoms (new DSM-5 category). Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD) — chronic irritability + temper outbursts in children (created to curb paediatric bipolar over-diagnosis).

Etiology. Monoamine hypothesis — deficit of serotonin / noradrenaline / dopamine (antidepressants raise them). HPA-axis hyperactivity (raised cortisol, non-suppression on dexamethasone). Genetics (heritability ~40%). Cognitive: Beck's negative cognitive triad (negative view of self, world, future) + Seligman's learned helplessness / hopelessness.

Treatment. SSRIs first-line (fluoxetine, sertraline), SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs; ECT for severe/psychotic/suicidal/refractory cases. Psychotherapy: CBT and IPT have the strongest evidence.

MDD vs grief vs PDD. Normal grief centres on the loss and comes in waves with preserved self-esteem; MDD is pervasive with worthlessness and anhedonia. DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion (you can diagnose MDD even soon after a death). PDD = ≥2 years, chronic-but-milder; "double depression" = MDD superimposed on PDD.
Recap Ch 6. MDD = ≥5/9 (SIGECAPS + mood), 2 weeks, ≥1 core. Melancholic = biological/early-waking; atypical = mood-reactive + hypersomnia + weight gain. PDD/dysthymia = ≥2 years. Monoamine deficit + HPA + Beck's triad. SSRIs first-line, ECT for severe; CBT/IPT.

Ch 7. Bipolar & related disorders

Mania = a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood + increased energy, lasting ≥1 week (or any duration if hospitalised), with ≥3 symptoms (4 if mood is only irritable). Mnemonic DIG FAST: Distractibility, Indiscretion/impulsivity (risky pleasure: spending, sex), Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase/goal-directed, Sleep decreased (no need for sleep), Talkativeness (pressured speech). Marked impairment, may be psychotic. Hypomania = same symptoms but ≥4 days, no marked impairment, no psychosis, no hospitalisation.

DisorderDefining requirement
Bipolar I≥1 manic episode (depression not even required for the diagnosis)
Bipolar II≥1 hypomanic + ≥1 major depressive episode, and never a full manic episode
Cyclothymia≥2 years of fluctuating subthreshold hypomanic and depressive symptoms (never meeting full episode criteria)
Bipolar I vs II vs cyclothymia: one full mania = BP-I (forever, even if it never recurs). Hypomania + depression, no mania = BP-II. Chronic subthreshold ups-and-downs ≥2 years = cyclothymia (the bipolar analogue of dysthymia). A manic switch triggered by an antidepressant counts toward bipolarity.

Etiology & treatment. Highly heritable (MZ concordance ~60–70%, the most genetic of the mood disorders). Lithium is the prototype mood stabiliser (also anti-suicidal; needs level monitoring, narrow therapeutic index); alternatives valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine; atypical antipsychotics for acute mania. Antidepressant monotherapy is avoided (can flip to mania). Psychoeducation about relapse signs and sleep regulation is central.

Recap Ch 7. Mania = ≥1 week, DIGFAST, impairment/psychosis; hypomania = ≥4 days, no impairment. BP-I needs one mania; BP-II needs hypomania + depression and no mania; cyclothymia = ≥2 years subthreshold. Lithium is first-line + anti-suicidal; don't use an antidepressant alone.

Part Four · The "neurotic" cluster — anxiety, OCD, trauma, dissociation, somatic

Ch 8. Anxiety disorders

Anxiety disorders share excessive fear (response to real/perceived imminent threat) and anxiety (anticipation of future threat). DSM-5 split off OCD and PTSD into their own chapters, so the anxiety group now is: GAD, panic disorder, agoraphobia, specific phobia, social anxiety, separation anxiety, selective mutism.

DisorderCore + the cut-offTrap
Generalised anxiety (GAD)Excessive, hard-to-control worry about many things, ≥6 months, + ≥3 of [restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance]Free-floating, no single trigger; 6-month rule
Panic disorderRecurrent unexpected panic attacks (peak in minutes: palpitations, choking, derealisation, fear of dying/going mad) + ≥1 month of worry about more attacks or behaviour changeThe attack ≠ the disorder; you need 1 month of anticipatory worry
AgoraphobiaFear of ≥2 situations (crowds, transport, open/enclosed spaces, queues, being outside alone) where escape is hardNow a standalone diagnosis in DSM-5, not just a panic add-on
Specific phobiaMarked fear of a specific object/situation (animal, blood-injection-injury, situational, natural environment), ≥6 monthsBlood-injection-injury phobia uniquely causes a vasovagal faint (biphasic BP/HR)
Social anxiety (social phobia)Fear of scrutiny/negative evaluation in social/performance settings, ≥6 monthsFear of embarrassment/judgement, not the situation itself
Separation anxietyExcessive fear of separation from attachment figures; now recognised in adults too (DSM-5)No longer childhood-only

Etiology & treatment. Behavioural: classical conditioning of fear + operant maintenance by avoidance (Mowrer's two-factor theory); preparedness (Seligman) explains why phobias cluster on evolutionarily dangerous stimuli. Biological: amygdala hyperactivity, GABA/serotonin. Treatment: exposure therapy (systematic desensitisation, flooding) is first-line for phobias; CBT for GAD/panic; SSRIs/SNRIs; short-term benzodiazepines (GABA agonists) for acute relief only.

Recap Ch 8. GAD = 6 months free-floating worry; panic disorder = unexpected attacks + 1 month worry; agoraphobia now standalone; specific phobia (BII type → fainting); social anxiety = fear of evaluation. Two-factor learning + avoidance; treat with exposure + CBT + SSRIs.

Ch 9. Obsessive-Compulsive & Related Disorders

DSM-5 carved OCD out of the anxiety chapter into its own "OCD and Related Disorders" grouping (so did ICD-11). OCD = obsessions (recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts/images/urges causing anxiety — contamination, doubt, symmetry, taboo) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to neutralise the anxiety — washing, checking, counting, ordering), recognised (in adults) as excessive, time-consuming (>1 hr/day) or impairing. Insight is usually retained (ego-dystonic), unlike a delusion.

Related disorderCore
Body dysmorphic disorderPreoccupation with an imagined/slight physical defect + repetitive checking/mirror behaviours
Hoarding disorderPersistent difficulty discarding possessions, distress at parting, clutter (new DSM-5 category)
TrichotillomaniaRecurrent hair-pulling
ExcoriationRecurrent skin-picking
Obsession vs compulsion vs OCPD vs psychotic insight. Obsession = the intrusive thought; compulsion = the act that relieves it. OCD ≠ OCPD: OCD is ego-dystonic anxiety with true obsessions/compulsions (an Axis-I/clinical disorder); Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder (OCPD) is an ego-syntonic personality style (perfectionism, orderliness, control, no true obsessions). If the patient is distressed by the thoughts → OCD; if they think their rigidity is correct → OCPD.

Etiology & treatment. Cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop overactivity; serotonin dysregulation. First-line treatment = Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP), a specific behavioural therapy (expose to the trigger, prevent the compulsion, let anxiety habituate) + SSRIs (often higher doses than for depression). Clomipramine (a TCA) is the classic anti-obsessional drug.

Recap Ch 9. OCD = obsessions (thoughts) + compulsions (acts), ego-dystonic, >1hr/day; now its own DSM-5/ICD-11 chapter. Relatives: BDD, hoarding, trichotillomania, excoriation. ERP is the treatment of choice + SSRIs/clomipramine. Don't confuse OCD with OCPD.

Ch 10. Trauma- & Stressor-Related Disorders

DSM-5 made trauma its own chapter (out of anxiety). All require an identifiable stressor.

DisorderCore + duration
PTSDAfter exposure to actual/threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence: 4 symptom clusters — intrusion (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, arousal (hypervigilance, startle) — lasting >1 month
Acute stress disorderSame symptom pool but 3 days–1 month after the trauma (the <1 month window before it becomes PTSD)
Adjustment disorderEmotional/behavioural symptoms within 3 months of an ordinary stressor (job loss, breakup), out of proportion, resolving within 6 months of the stressor ending
Reactive attachment / disinhibited social engagementChildhood disorders from grossly inadequate caregiving
Prolonged grief disorderPersistent, disabling grief beyond ~12 months (new in DSM-5-TR; also in ICD-11)
ICD-11 split PTSD in two. ICD-11 has a simplified PTSD (just three clusters: re-experiencing in the here and now, avoidance, persistent sense of current threat) PLUS a separate Complex PTSD (CPTSD) for prolonged/repeated trauma (childhood abuse, captivity): PTSD plus "Disturbances in Self-Organisation" (DSO) — affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship disturbances. DSM-5 has no Complex PTSD. This DSM-vs-ICD difference is a favourite stem.
ASD vs PTSD vs adjustment: same trauma symptoms but ASD = 3 days–1 month, PTSD = >1 month; adjustment disorder follows an ordinary stressor (not life-threatening) and is the residual diagnosis when nothing else fits.
Recap Ch 10. PTSD (DSM 4 clusters, >1 month) vs Acute Stress (3 days–1 month) vs Adjustment (ordinary stressor, within 3 months). ICD-11 adds Complex PTSD (+ DSO triad); DSM doesn't have it. Prolonged Grief Disorder is the newest addition.

Ch 11. Dissociative disorders

A disruption of the normally integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity, perception — psychologically driven (often post-trauma), not organic.

DisorderCore
Dissociative amnesiaInability to recall important (usually traumatic) autobiographical information, too extensive for ordinary forgetting; dissociative fugue (sudden purposeful travel + amnesia for identity) is now a specifier of it in DSM-5
Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID)≥2 distinct personality states ("alters") with recurrent gaps in recall; formerly "multiple personality disorder"; strongly linked to severe childhood trauma
Depersonalisation / derealisation disorderPersistent detachment from one's self (depersonalisation) or surroundings feel unreal (derealisation), with intact reality testing
Dissociative (psychogenic) amnesia vs organic amnesia: dissociative = selective, autobiographical, identity-linked, reversible, normal new learning; organic (e.g. Korsakoff, head injury) = anterograde failure to form new memories, gradient, with neurological cause. Depersonalisation ≠ psychosis: the person knows it feels unreal (insight intact).
Recap Ch 11. Dissociation = split in consciousness/memory/identity, psychogenic. Amnesia (fugue = a specifier), DID (≥2 alters, childhood trauma), depersonalisation/derealisation (reality testing intact). Separate psychogenic from organic amnesia.

Ch 12. Somatic Symptom & Related Disorders

Physical symptoms or health worries that dominate life, with no proportionate organic explanation. DSM-5 reorganised this whole group (the old "somatoform" cluster), shifting the emphasis from "medically unexplained" to the excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviours about the symptoms.

DisorderCore · DSM-5 change
Somatic symptom disorder≥1 distressing somatic symptom + disproportionate thoughts/anxiety/time devoted to it (≥6 months). Merged the old somatisation, pain and undifferentiated somatoform disorders
Illness anxiety disorderPreoccupation with having/getting a serious illness with minimal or no somatic symptoms — replaced "hypochondriasis"
Conversion disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder)Neurological-type deficits (paralysis, blindness, non-epileptic seizures, aphonia) incompatible with known disease; classic "la belle indifférence" and primary/secondary gain
Factitious disorderDeliberately feigning/inducing symptoms to assume the sick role (no external reward); "imposed on another" = Munchausen by proxy
The three-way intent trap — conversion vs factitious vs malingering. Conversion = unconscious production (the patient is not faking; symptom relieves a conflict). Factitious = conscious production for the internal reward of being a patient (the sick role). Malingering = conscious production for an external reward (money, drugs, avoiding work/jail) — and is NOT a mental disorder, it's a V-code/condition. Ask: faking? (no = conversion) → if faking, why? (sick role = factitious; tangible gain = malingering).
Recap Ch 12. Somatic symptom disorder (real symptom + disproportionate worry), illness anxiety (fear of illness, replaced hypochondriasis), conversion = FND (unconscious neuro deficit, la belle indifférence). Intent ladder: conversion (unconscious) → factitious (sick role) → malingering (external gain, not a disorder).

Part Five · The other major groups

Ch 13. Feeding & Eating Disorders

DisorderCore features
Anorexia nervosaRestriction of intake → significantly low body weight; intense fear of weight gain; disturbed body image. Two types: restricting and binge-eating/purging. DSM-5 dropped the amenorrhoea requirement. Highest mortality of any psychiatric disorder (starvation + suicide)
Bulimia nervosaRecurrent binge eating + recurrent compensatory behaviour (self-induced vomiting, laxatives, fasting, exercise), ≥1×/week for 3 months; weight usually normal or above. Signs: Russell's sign (knuckle calluses), parotid swelling, dental erosion, hypokalaemia
Binge-eating disorderRecurrent binges with loss of control, no compensatory behaviour → often overweight (new full diagnosis in DSM-5)
Pica / Rumination / ARFIDEating non-food (pica); regurgitation (rumination); avoidant/restrictive intake without body-image disturbance (ARFID)
Anorexia vs bulimia: the discriminator is weight and body image. Anorexia = underweight, denies the problem, distorted body image. Bulimia = normal/above weight, knows it's a problem, ego-dystonic. A binge-purger who is underweight is anorexia (binge-purge type), not bulimia. BED = binges with no purging.
Recap Ch 13. Anorexia (low weight, fear of gain, body-image distortion, highest mortality, amenorrhoea no longer required), bulimia (binge + compensate, normal weight, Russell's sign), BED (binge, no compensation). Weight + body image separate the first two.

Ch 14. Substance Use & Addictive Disorders

Two systems classify differently. ICD-10/11 dependence syndrome (Edwards & Gross) = ≥3 of 6 over a year: (1) compulsion/craving, (2) impaired control, (3) withdrawal, (4) tolerance, (5) neglect of other interests / salience, (6) persistence despite harm. DSM-5 merged "abuse" and "dependence" into one "Substance Use Disorder" on a severity spectrum (≥2 of 11 criteria → mild 2–3 / moderate 4–5 / severe ≥6). DSM-5 also added Gambling disorder as a behavioural addiction; ICD-11 added Gaming disorder.

SubstanceIntoxication / Withdrawal highlights
AlcoholWithdrawal: tremor → seizures → delirium tremens (autonomic storm, confusion, hallucinations, can be fatal). Wernicke's encephalopathy (thiamine deficiency: confusion + ophthalmoplegia + ataxia) → Korsakoff's psychosis (anterograde amnesia + confabulation). Screen with CAGE. Treat withdrawal with benzodiazepines + thiamine
OpioidsIntoxication: pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression (reverse with naloxone); withdrawal: dysphoric but not life-threatening (lacrimation, piloerection, cramps). Maintenance: methadone/buprenorphine
Stimulants (cocaine, amphetamine)Intoxication: euphoria, dilated pupils, paranoia, formication; withdrawal: "crash" — dysphoria, hypersomnia, craving
CannabisIntoxication: conjunctival injection, increased appetite, altered time, anxiety/paranoia
BenzodiazepinesWithdrawal (like alcohol): anxiety, seizures — dangerous; taper, don't stop abruptly
Tolerance vs dependence vs withdrawal vs addiction: tolerance = needing more for the same effect; withdrawal = the physical syndrome on stopping; physical dependence = tolerance + withdrawal (can occur with legitimate medication); addiction = compulsive use despite harm + loss of control (the behavioural disorder). Wernicke (acute, reversible) → Korsakoff (chronic, confabulation, often irreversible) — both from thiamine deficiency.
Recap Ch 14. ICD dependence = 3/6 (compulsion, control, withdrawal, tolerance, salience, harm); DSM-5 = one Substance Use Disorder, severity by count. Alcohol: DTs, Wernicke→Korsakoff (thiamine). Opioids: pinpoint pupils + naloxone. Gambling (DSM) + Gaming (ICD-11) are behavioural addictions.

Ch 15. Personality Disorders

An enduring, pervasive, inflexible pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from cultural expectation, is stable from adolescence/early adulthood, and causes distress or impairment. DSM-5 (Section II) keeps 10 categorical PDs in 3 clusters:

ClusterThemeDisorders (one-line)
A — "odd / eccentric"The "mad" clusterParanoid (distrust, suspiciousness) · Schizoid (detached, no desire for relationships) · Schizotypal (odd beliefs, magical thinking, eccentric — on the schizophrenia spectrum)
B — "dramatic / erratic"The "bad" clusterAntisocial (disregard for/violation of others' rights, since age 15; needs conduct disorder before 15) · Borderline (instability of affect, relationships, self-image; impulsivity, self-harm, fear of abandonment) · Histrionic (excessive emotionality + attention-seeking) · Narcissistic (grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of empathy)
C — "anxious / fearful"The "sad/scared" clusterAvoidant (social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, hypersensitive to rejection) · Dependent (excessive need to be cared for, clinging) · Obsessive-Compulsive PD (perfectionism, orderliness, control — ego-syntonic)
ICD-11 scrapped the categories. ICD-11 abolished the 10 types and made personality disorder fully dimensional: first rate severity (mild / moderate / severe), then add up to 5 trait domain qualifiers — Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, Anankastia — plus an optional Borderline pattern qualifier (the one categorical remnant, kept because it guides treatment). This severity-first, trait-based model is the single biggest ICD-11 change and a guaranteed stem.
The look-alikes. Schizoid (doesn't want relationships, indifferent) vs avoidant (wants them but fears rejection) vs schizotypal (odd/magical thinking, eccentric). Antisocial (callous, exploitative, no remorse) vs borderline (unstable, self-harming, fears abandonment) vs narcissistic (grandiose, entitled). OCPD (ego-syntonic rigidity, a personality) vs OCD (ego-dystonic obsessions/compulsions, a clinical disorder).
Recap Ch 15. DSM-5 = 10 PDs in clusters A (odd: paranoid/schizoid/schizotypal), B (dramatic: antisocial/borderline/histrionic/narcissistic), C (anxious: avoidant/dependent/OCPD). ICD-11 = dimensional: severity + 5 trait domains + borderline pattern. Schizoid vs avoidant and OCD vs OCPD are the classic traps.

Delirium vs dementia, the one-line discriminator: delirium = clouded consciousness + acute + fluctuating + reversible; dementia = clear consciousness + insidious + progressive + memory-led. A demented patient who suddenly becomes confused and drowsy has a superimposed delirium — find the medical cause.
Dementia typeSignature

Recap Ch 16. Delirium = acute, fluctuating, clouded consciousness, inattention, reversible. Dementia = insidious, progressive, clear consciousness, memory-led. Alzheimer (plaques + tangles, ACh, donepezil) / vascular (stepwise) / Lewy body (hallucinations + parkinsonism) / FTD (behaviour change).

Ch 17. Neurodevelopmental Disorders

DisorderCore
Intellectual Disability (intellectual developmental disorder)Deficits in intellectual functioning (reasoning, ~IQ<70) AND adaptive functioning (conceptual/social/practical), onset in the developmental period. DSM-5 grades severity by adaptive function, not IQ alone
Autism Spectrum DisorderTwo domains: (1) persistent deficits in social communication/interaction; (2) restricted, repetitive behaviours/interests (stereotypy, insistence on sameness, sensory issues). DSM-5 merged autism, Asperger's and PDD-NOS into one spectrum
ADHDInattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, ≥6 symptoms, several present before age 12, in ≥2 settings. Three presentations: inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, combined. Treat with stimulants (methylphenidate) + behavioural
Specific Learning DisorderDyslexia (reading), dyscalculia (maths), dysgraphia (writing) — unexpected for age/IQ
Tic disorders / Tourette'sMotor + vocal tics >1 year = Tourette's

ASD vs social anxiety vs intellectual disability: ASD has the restricted/repetitive domain (not present in social anxiety or pure ID). DSM-5 abolished Asperger's as a separate label — it's "ASD without language/intellectual impairment." ADHD's gate is onset before age 12 (was 7 in DSM-IV — a classic "what changed" item).
Recap Ch 17. ID (intellectual + adaptive deficits, developmental onset, graded by adaptive function). ASD (social-communication + restricted/repetitive; Asperger's merged in). ADHD (inattention/hyperactivity, before age 12, ≥2 settings; stimulants). Specific learning disorder, tics/Tourette's.

Ch 18. Sexual dysfunctions, gender & sleep (brief)

Sexual dysfunctions — disturbance in the sexual response cycle (desire → arousal → orgasm → resolution; Masters & Johnson / Kaplan): erectile disorder, female sexual interest/arousal, orgasmic disorders, genito-pelvic pain, premature/delayed ejaculation. Paraphilic disorders — atypical sexual interest that causes distress or harm to others (exhibitionism, voyeurism, frotteurism, paedophilic, sexual sadism/masochism, fetishism): a paraphilia is only a disorder when it distresses the person or harms a non-consenting other. Gender dysphoria (DSM-5) = distress from incongruence between experienced and assigned gender; ICD-11 renamed it "gender incongruence" and moved it OUT of the mental-disorders chapter (into sexual-health conditions), de-pathologising it.

Sleep-wake — insomnia (commonest), hypersomnolence, narcolepsy (sleep attacks + cataplexy + sleep paralysis + hypnagogic hallucinations; orexin/hypocretin loss), breathing-related (sleep apnoea), circadian-rhythm disorders, parasomnias (sleepwalking, night terrors in NREM; nightmares + REM-sleep behaviour disorder in REM).

Gender incongruence: the headline DSM-vs-ICD difference is that ICD-11 removed it from mental disorders (DSM-5 still lists Gender Dysphoria, framed around the distress, not the identity). Night terror vs nightmare: terror = NREM, early night, no recall, child sits up screaming; nightmare = REM, late night, vivid recall.
Recap Ch 18. Sexual dysfunctions map onto the response cycle; paraphilia → disorder only with distress/harm. ICD-11 de-pathologised gender incongruence; DSM keeps Gender Dysphoria (distress-based). Narcolepsy tetrad; NREM (terrors/sleepwalking) vs REM (nightmares/RBD) parasomnias.
