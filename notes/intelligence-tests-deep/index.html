Intelligence Tests, Deep Reference

Built 2026-05-20, companion to the personality + projective deep reference. Same job, different test family: for every individual + group + infant intelligence scale in the MET/NIMHANS pool, lock the item/subtest count, scales/indices, scoring, author + year, and age range. Cross-link with the read-first primer (reliability/validity/IQ history) and the revision pack (one-page table).

How MCQ-writers attack intelligence tests. Five recurring moves: (1) swap the SD between Wechsler (15) and old Binet (16); (2) swap subtest count between SPM/CPM/APM, or between Bhatia (5) and Army Beta (7); (3) swap the year (Binet-Simon 1905 vs Terman 1916 vs Stern's IQ formula 1912); (4) swap which index belongs to which Wechsler version (WAIS-IV has 4, WISC-V has 5); (5) swap a theorist with their model (Spearman g, Thurstone PMA, Guilford SOI, Gardner MI, Sternberg triarchic, Cattell-Horn Gf-Gc). Memorize those five swaps cold.

1. Stanford-Binet Family

Origin: Binet-Simon (1905, France)

  • Authors: Alfred Binet & Théodore Simon, commissioned by the French Ministry of Public Instruction to identify children needing special schooling.
  • Year: 1905 (original 30-item scale), revised 1908 (introduced mental-age concept) and 1911.
  • Items: 30 tasks of graded difficulty in the 1905 version. Range from "follow a light with eyes" to "abstract definitions."
  • Key contribution: the concept of mental age (MA) in the 1908 revision - child's performance level matched to the average chronological age that performs at that level.
  • Did NOT yield IQ. Binet himself rejected a single global score; he wanted a clinical descriptive profile.

Stanford revisions - the IQ-formula era

  • 1916 - Stanford-Binet, 1st edition. Lewis M. Terman, Stanford University. First English-language adaptation. Adopted William Stern's 1912 ratio IQ formula: IQ = (MA / CA) × 100. Mean ≈ 100.
  • 1937 - SB-LM (2nd edition). Terman & Maud Merrill. Two equivalent forms L and M. Still ratio IQ.
  • 1960 - SB-LM revised (3rd edition). Terman & Merrill. Switched from ratio IQ to deviation IQ (Pinneau's tables): M = 100, SD = 16. Memorize this 16 - it is the classic Wechsler-vs-Binet trap.
  • 1986 - SB-IV (4th edition). Thorndike, Hagen & Sattler. First time the test moved to a hierarchical structure with 4 area scores: Verbal Reasoning, Abstract/Visual Reasoning, Quantitative Reasoning, Short-Term Memory. 15 subtests. Age 2 to adult.
  • 2003 - SB-5 (current edition). Gale H. Roid, Riverside Publishing. SD changed to 15 to align with Wechsler. Age range 2 to 85+.

SB-5 structure (the version exam-writers mean today)

  • 10 subtests, 5 factors × 2 domains.
  • 5 factors: Fluid Reasoning (FR), Knowledge (KN), Quantitative Reasoning (QR), Visual-Spatial Processing (VS), Working Memory (WM). Identical names across the verbal and nonverbal domains.
  • 2 domains: Verbal (5 subtests) and Nonverbal (5 subtests). Each factor has one verbal + one nonverbal subtest.
  • 3 IQ scores yielded: Full-Scale IQ (FSIQ), Verbal IQ (VIQ), Nonverbal IQ (NVIQ). All M = 100, SD = 15.
  • Routing subtests: Object Series/Matrices (nonverbal) and Vocabulary (verbal) determine entry difficulty for the rest.
  • Administration time: ~45–75 min full battery; 15–20 min abbreviated.

2. Wechsler Family

David Wechsler (Bellevue Hospital, NY) built his scales explicitly to fix two SB problems: (a) it was age-stratified and clunky for adults, and (b) ratio IQ broke down past age 16 because MA stops climbing linearly. His fix was the deviation IQ (1939) and a point-scale structure where every adult takes the same subtests.

Edition lineage

  • Wechsler-Bellevue I (W-B I, 1939) - David Wechsler. First deviation-IQ test. 11 subtests.
  • W-B II (1946) - alternate form.
  • WAIS (1955) - adults 16–64.
  • WAIS-R (1981) - restandardised.
  • WAIS-III (1997) - added Processing Speed Index, 4-index structure introduced.
  • WAIS-IV (2008) - current MET/NIMHANS reference; ages 16:0–90:11.
  • WAIS-5 (2024) - rolled out in US/UK; exams still default to WAIS-IV unless otherwise specified.
  • WISC (1949) → WISC-R (1974) → WISC-III (1991) → WISC-IV (2003) → WISC-V (2014 US, 2016 UK, 2019 India adaptation). Ages 6:0–16:11.
  • WPPSI (1967) → WPPSI-R (1989) → WPPSI-III (2002) → WPPSI-IV (2012). Ages 2:6–7:7.

WAIS-IV - the canonical exam answer

  • Age range: 16:0 to 90:11.
  • Total subtests: 15 (10 core + 5 supplemental).
  • 4 index scores (memorize): Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Perceptual Reasoning (PRI), Working Memory (WMI), Processing Speed (PSI). FSIQ derived from the 10 core subtests across these 4 indices.
  • Subtest scaled scores: M = 10, SD = 3, range 1–19.
  • FSIQ and index scores: M = 100, SD = 15.
  • Administration time: ~60–90 minutes for FSIQ.

WISC-V - the children's scale

  • Age range: 6:0 to 16:11.
  • Total subtests: 21 (10 primary + 11 secondary, including 5 complementary subtests scored separately).
  • 5 primary indices: Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Visual Spatial (VSI), Fluid Reasoning (FRI), Working Memory (WMI), Processing Speed (PSI). The big WISC-IV → WISC-V change was splitting old PRI into VSI + FRI, giving 5 primary indices instead of 4.
  • 5 ancillary indices: Quantitative Reasoning Index (QRI), Auditory Working Memory Index (AWMI), Nonverbal Index (NVI), General Ability Index (GAI), Cognitive Proficiency Index (CPI).
  • 5 complementary subtests (CHC narrow abilities): Naming Speed Literacy, Naming Speed Quantity, Immediate Symbol Translation, Delayed Symbol Translation, Recognition Symbol Translation.

Secondary subtests: Information, Comprehension, Picture Concepts, Arithmetic, Letter-Number Sequencing, Cancellation.

WPPSI-IV - the preschool scale

  • Age range: 2:6 to 7:7. Split into two batteries: 2:6–3:11 and 4:0–7:7.
  • Ages 2:6 to 3:11: 7 subtests, 3 primary indices (VCI, VSI, WMI). No FRI or PSI at this age (cognitive abilities not yet differentiated).
  • Ages 4:0 to 7:7: 15 subtests, 5 primary indices (VCI, VSI, FRI, WMI, PSI) - same structure as WISC-V.
  • Primary subtests (4:0–7:7): Information, Similarities, Block Design, Object Assembly, Matrix Reasoning, Picture Concepts, Picture Memory, Zoo Locations, Bug Search, Cancellation.

3. Raven's Progressive Matrices

  • Author: John C. Raven.
  • Year: SPM published 1938 (developed 1936). CPM 1947. APM 1943.
  • Nature: nonverbal, multiple-choice, pattern-completion. Targets Spearman's g via eductive (meaning-extracting) reasoning.
  • "Culture-fair": commonly described that way because items are abstract shapes, but research shows cultural loading still exists (familiarity with paper-pencil format, formal schooling). Treat "culture-reduced" as the more honest phrasing.

MCQ trap: SPM = 60, CPM = 36, APM (scored) = 36. The 36 on CPM and APM is the swap that wrecks people. Lock both.

4. Indian Intelligence Tests

Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence

  • Author: Chandra Mohan Bhatia.
  • Year: 1955.
  • Age range: originally 11 to 16 years; extended use up to early adulthood.
  • Subtests (5 - memorize the count):
    1. Koh's Block Design Test (10 designs)
    2. Alexander's Pass-Along Test (8 problems)
    3. Pattern Drawing Test (8 designs)
    4. Immediate Memory Test - digits forward + digits backward + sentence repetition
    5. Picture Construction Test (5 pictures)
  • Use case: built specifically for the Indian context, suitable for illiterate and rural populations because of its performance (nonverbal) orientation.
  • MCQ trap: the Pass-Along is Alexander's (1932), incorporated into Bhatia's battery. Don't say "Bhatia Pass-Along."

Binet-Kamat Test (BKT)

  • Author: V.V. Kamat.
  • Year: 1934 (Marathi adaptation), later Kannada version.
  • Age range: 3 to 22 years.
  • Indian Binet adaptation, still used in NIMHANS clinical assessment for clinical IQ estimation in Indian children.

Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC)

  • Author: Arthur J. Malin.
  • Year: 1969.
  • Age range: 6 to 15 years.
  • Indian adaptation of WISC (the 1949 original, not WISC-R). 11 subtests in the original Indian version.

Other Indian instruments

  • Mohsin General Intelligence Test (S.M. Mohsin, 1959) - verbal group test, Hindi.
  • Jalota's Group Test of General Mental Ability (S. Jalota, 1972) - verbal, ages 13+, 100 items.
  • Seguin Form Board (1907 origin, used in India for ages 3–7) - nonverbal performance, 10 cut-outs. Often misattributed to Indian authors; it is Edouard Seguin's, used in Indian developmental screening.
  • Developmental Screening Test (DST) - Bharath Raj, 1977 - Indian developmental screen, ages 0–15.
  • Vineland Social Maturity Scale, Indian adaptation (A.J. Malin, 1965) - Indian SQ (Social Quotient).

5. Kaufman Family

  • Authors: Alan S. Kaufman & Nadeen L. Kaufman.
  • K-ABC (Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children, 1983) → KABC-II (2004). Ages 3:0–18:11.
    • Built on Luria's neuropsychological model (simultaneous + sequential processing) and updated in KABC-II to allow CHC interpretation.
    • KABC-II yields two global scores: Mental Processing Index (MPI) for Luria-style use and Fluid-Crystallized Index (FCI) for CHC-style use.
    • 5 scales: Sequential, Simultaneous, Planning, Learning, Knowledge.
    • Designed to reduce verbal/cultural loading and detect minority and ESL children fairly.
  • KAIT (Kaufman Adolescent & Adult Intelligence Test, 1993): ages 11–85, separates Fluid IQ vs Crystallized IQ explicitly.
  • KBIT-2 (Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test, 2004): 2 subtests (Verbal Knowledge + Matrices), 20 min, screening tool.

6. Cattell's Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT)

  • Author: Raymond B. Cattell.
  • Year: first published 1949; revised 1973.
  • Purpose: measure fluid intelligence (Gf) per Cattell's Gf-Gc theory, with reduced cultural and language loading.
  • 3 scales:
    • Scale 1: ages 4–8 and intellectually disabled adults. 8 subtests.
    • Scale 2: ages 8–13 and average adults. 4 subtests.
    • Scale 3: ages 14+ and above-average adults. 4 subtests.
  • Scales 2 & 3 subtests (same 4): Series, Classifications, Matrices, Conditions (Topology).
  • Form A + Form B available for retest reliability.
  • Scoring: M = 100, SD = 24 in original; renormed editions use SD = 15 or 16 depending on manual.

7. Group Intelligence Tests

Army Alpha (1917)

  • Chair / committee: Robert M. Yerkes (APA president, chair) with Arthur S. Otis, Lewis Terman, E.L. Thorndike, Henry Goddard, Walter V. Bingham, Guy Whipple. World War I, US Army.
  • Items: 212 questions across 8 subtests.
  • Subtests: Oral Directions, Arithmetic Problems, Practical Judgment, Synonym-Antonym, Disarranged Sentences, Number Series Completion, Analogies, Information.
  • Population: literate, English-speaking recruits.

Army Beta (1917)

  • Same committee, Yerkes-led.
  • 7 subtests: Maze, Cube Analysis, X-O Series, Digit Symbol, Number Checking, Pictorial Completion, Geometrical Construction.
  • Population: illiterate or non-English-speaking recruits. Administered via pantomime and demonstration.
  • Trap: Beta has 7 subtests, Alpha has 8. Bhatia also has 5. Don't conflate.

Other group tests

  • Otis Self-Administering Tests of Mental Ability (Arthur S. Otis, 1922) - civilian descendant of Army Alpha; Otis was on the Yerkes committee.
  • Otis-Lennon School Ability Test (OLSAT) - current K–12 group ability screen.
  • Wonderlic Personnel Test (1937) - 50 items, 12 minutes, employment screening; famous for being used by the NFL combine.
  • Multidimensional Aptitude Battery (MAB-II, Jackson, 1998) - group-administrable Wechsler-style.
  • California Test of Mental Maturity (CTMM), Henmon-Nelson Tests, Cognitive Abilities Test (CogAT) - older / current school-grade group ability tests.

8. Infant and Developmental Scales

  • Bayley Scales of Infant Development (BSID) - Nancy Bayley. BSID-I (1969), BSID-II (1993), Bayley-III (2006), Bayley-4 (2019). Ages 1 to 42 months. Bayley-III has 5 scales: Cognitive, Language (Receptive + Expressive), Motor (Fine + Gross), Social-Emotional, Adaptive Behavior.
  • Gesell Developmental Schedules - Arnold Gesell, 1940s. Ages 4 weeks to 6 years. 5 fields: Adaptive, Gross Motor, Fine Motor, Language, Personal-Social. Yields a Developmental Quotient (DQ) = (Developmental Age / Chronological Age) × 100.
  • Cattell Infant Intelligence Scale (Psyche Cattell, 1940) - downward extension of Stanford-Binet, ages 2–30 months.
  • Brunet-Lézine Scale (1951) - French infant scale.
  • Mullen Scales of Early Learning (1995) - ages birth to 68 months.
  • Denver Developmental Screening Test (DDST, 1967; Denver-II, 1992) - screening, not diagnostic; ages 0–6.

9. Special-Purpose and Nonverbal Scales

  • Leiter International Performance Scale - Russell G. Leiter, 1936 → Leiter-R (1997) → Leiter-3 (2013). Fully nonverbal, no language required; used with deaf, ASD, non-English speakers. Ages 3–75+.
  • Goodenough Draw-A-Man Test (Florence Goodenough, 1926) → Goodenough-Harris Drawing Test (Harris, 1963). Children draw a man (and woman, and self in Harris); scored on 64+ body-detail criteria. Ages 3–15.
  • Slosson Intelligence Test (SIT, Slosson, 1963; SIT-R3, 2002) - brief individual screen.
  • Differential Ability Scales (DAS-II, Elliott, 2007) - ages 2:6–17:11; CHC-aligned, popular for LD identification.
  • Woodcock-Johnson Tests of Cognitive Abilities (WJ-IV, 2014) - explicitly built on CHC theory; ages 2–90+.
  • Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales for Early Childhood (SB5: Early SB-5) - ages 2:0–7:3 abbreviated form.
  • Comprehensive Test of Nonverbal Intelligence (CTONI-2, Hammill et al., 2009).
  • Universal Nonverbal Intelligence Test (UNIT-2, Bracken & McCallum, 2016).
  • Wide Range Intelligence Test (WRIT, Glutting et al., 2000).
  • Reynolds Intellectual Assessment Scales (RIAS-2, Reynolds & Kamphaus, 2015).

11. IQ Score Mechanics

The formula history (memorize this sequence)

  1. Mental Age introduced by Binet, 1908 revision.
  2. Ratio IQ = (MA / CA) × 100: William Stern, 1912. Applied by Terman in the Stanford-Binet 1916.
  3. Deviation IQ: Wechsler 1939 for adults. Compares the examinee to age-mates via standardized score, fixed mean and SD.
  4. Stanford-Binet switches to deviation IQ in 1960 (Pinneau's tables), SD = 16.
  5. SB-5 (2003) drops SD to 15 to match Wechsler.

15. MCQ Traps and One-Line Stems

  • Trap 1: Wechsler SD = 15, old Stanford-Binet (1960–1986) SD = 16, SB-5 (2003+) SD = 15. The 16 still surfaces because textbooks lag.
  • Trap 2: SPM = 60, CPM = 36, APM scored = 36 (don't include the 12 practice). Most common item-count swap on Indian PG papers.
  • Trap 3: Bhatia has 5 subtests (Koh, Pass-Along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory, Picture Construction). Army Beta has 7. Army Alpha has 8.
  • Trap 4: WAIS-IV has 4 indices (VCI/PRI/WMI/PSI). WISC-V has 5 (VCI/VSI/FRI/WMI/PSI). The WISC-V split was: old PRI broke into VSI + FRI.
  • Trap 5: Ratio IQ = (MA/CA)×100. Stern (1912) wrote the formula; Terman (1916) put it into Stanford-Binet. Don't credit Binet for the IQ formula - Binet rejected it.
  • Trap 6: Stanford-Binet 1st edition = 1916, not 1905. 1905 = Binet-Simon (France).
  • Trap 7: Cattell-Horn Gf-Gc is 1966. Cattell's CFIT is 1949. Raymond Cattell is also 16PF (1949). Three things by the same author.
  • Trap 8: Gardner originally proposed 7 intelligences (1983), added Naturalist in 1995 → 8. Existential is proposed but not formally accepted. "9 intelligences" is debated and usually wrong on Indian exams.
  • Trap 9: KABC-II yields two global indices (MPI and FCI) because it supports both Luria's model and CHC. The original K-ABC (1983) was pure Luria.
  • Trap 10: Bayley-III is ages 1–42 months, NOT just infants. Gesell goes to 6 years. Stanford-Binet starts at 2. Cattell Infant Scale 2–30 months. Don't mix these.
  • Trap 11: Goodenough Draw-A-Man (1926) vs Harris-Goodenough Drawing Test (1963). Both names show up; the 1963 version also has a woman and a self drawing.
  • Trap 12: Yerkes was the chair of the Army testing committee - not Terman. Terman was on the committee but is more often associated with Stanford-Binet 1916.
  • Trap 13: Bhatia is 1955, not 1949. 1949 belongs to WISC (Wechsler) and CFIT (Cattell). 1955 belongs to WAIS and Bhatia.
  • Trap 14: Malin's MISIC adapted WISC (1949), not WAIS or WISC-R. Year 1969.
  • Trap 15: Spearman invented factor analysis (1904) for the two-factor theory; the order is theory → method, not the other way.

16. Likely Exam Stems (rehearse the lead-ins)

  3. "Stanford-Binet was developed by" → Terman (1916).
  4. "Standard Deviation of old Stanford-Binet IQ is" → 16.
  5. "Number of items in Standard Progressive Matrices" → 60.
  6. "Number of subtests in Bhatia's Battery" → 5.
  7. "Which battery was developed for Indian illiterate population" → Bhatia (1955).
