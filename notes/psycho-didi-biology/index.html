1 · Development & Organization of the Nervous System

The Nervous System is divided into the Central Nervous System (CNS) and the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS).

  • Central Nervous System (CNS) — the Brain and the Spinal Cord.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) — includes the Autonomic Nervous System (with its Sympathetic and Parasympathetic divisions) and the Somatic Nervous System (with its Afferent and Efferent nervous systems).
  • Enteric Nervous System — controls the smooth muscle and glandular tissue in the digestive system.

The neural tube picture. The coloured drawing in the diagram shows the early embryonic brain at the three-vesicle stage: the prosencephalon (forebrain, blue) at the front, the mesencephalon (midbrain, magenta) in the middle and the rhombencephalon (hindbrain, green) behind it, running on into the spinal cord (yellow). The neural tube itself forms when the neural plate (from the embryo's outer layer, the ectoderm) folds up and closes, by about the end of the fourth week of pregnancy. Around the fifth week the forebrain splits into telencephalon and diencephalon and the hindbrain into metencephalon and myelencephalon, giving the five secondary vesicles in the table. The midbrain does not split.

Exam trap. Count the vesicles: 3 primary (pros-, mes-, rhomb-encephalon) and 5 secondary (tel-, di-, mes-, met-, myel-encephalon). The mesencephalon is the only one that keeps its name. If the tube fails to close, the result is a neural tube defect: anencephaly at the head end, spina bifida at the tail end.

Ventricles in the diagram. The neural tube is hollow, and each part's hollow becomes a fluid-filled space (last column of the table): the telencephalon's becomes the two lateral ventricles, the diencephalon's the third ventricle, the mesencephalon's the narrow cerebral aqueduct, and the hindbrain's the fourth ventricle. Below the brain the same channel continues as the central canal of the spinal cord. The diagram draws the 4th ventricle from the myelencephalon only; strictly it is the cavity of the whole hindbrain, lying between the cerebellum behind and the pons and upper medulla in front.

Limbic system branch. The diagram also branches the limbic system off the telencephalon, listing under it the basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, substantia nigra, nucleus accumbens, subthalamic nuclei), the amygdala, the hippocampus and the cingulate gyrus. The amygdala, hippocampus, cingulate gyrus and the striatum (caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens) do develop from the telencephalon. Two exceptions: the substantia nigra is a midbrain structure and the subthalamic nucleus belongs to the diencephalon.

Heads up: the diagram files the basal ganglia inside the limbic system. Standard texts treat the basal ganglia as a motor system and the limbic system as the emotion and memory system. The nucleus accumbens is where they meet: it links emotion and reward to action.
Don't confuse. Afferent nerves carry sensory information toward the CNS; Efferent nerves carry motor commands away from the CNS. Similarly, the Sympathetic system drives "fight or flight" (Walter Cannon, 1915) while the Parasympathetic system drives "rest and digest."

Who → what. James Papez (1937) — the Papez circuit, a loop of structures he proposed as the brain's emotion circuit (paper: "A proposed mechanism of emotion"): hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies (hypothalamus) → anterior nucleus of the thalamus → cingulate gyrus → parahippocampal gyrus → back to the hippocampus. Exam trap. The amygdala is not part of Papez's original circuit; MacLean added it. And although Papez proposed the circuit for emotion, it is now linked mainly to memory: damage to its parts (hippocampus, fornix, mammillary bodies, as in Korsakoff syndrome) causes amnesia.

4 · The Basal Ganglia

The Basal Ganglia handle motor coordination and inhibition; they prevent unnecessary motor movements and activity in the white matter of the frontal lobe. Dysfunction: tremors, involuntary muscle movements, abnormal increase in tone, difficulty initiating movements, and abnormal posture.

Components of the basal ganglia: caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, substantia nigra, nucleus accumbens, and subthalamic nuclei.

Reading the cross diagram. The cross is a memory aid for which passage joins which ventricles. The horizontal arm joins V1 and V2 (the two lateral ventricles, one in each hemisphere) and is labelled "intraventricular formation"; the correct term is the interventricular foramen, which the labelled drawing beside it calls the foramen of Monro. The vertical arm joins V3 (in the diencephalon) to V4 (between the cerebellum and the pons and medulla) through the cerebral aqueduct (aqueduct of Sylvius), which runs through the midbrain. "Choroid plexus" is written under V4.

Heads up: the cross makes it look as if V1 and V2 open into each other. They do not: each lateral ventricle opens separately into the third ventricle through its own interventricular foramen. Also, choroid plexus is found in all four ventricles (most of it in the lateral ventricles), not only in V4. Classic question: a blocked cerebral aqueduct (aqueductal stenosis) swells the lateral and third ventricles but not the fourth, because the fluid is trapped above the block (obstructive hydrocephalus).

9 · Important Broadmann's Areas

Alongside the Broadmann's areas, the note maps the visual system stream: areas V1, V2, V3, V3A, V4, V5, running from the occipital lobe toward the temporal and parietal lobes. In the diagram, V3 (dynamic form) and V4 (recognition) sit under the temporal lobe, and V5 (motion) and V3A (shape) sit under the parietal lobe. A two-way arrow joins the temporal and parietal lobes at the top.

The idea that each visual area handles one feature (form, colour, motion) is Semir Zeki's functional specialization theory. The split toward two lobes is the two visual streams idea: Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin (1982) described a ventral "what" stream (occipital to inferior temporal lobe: recognising objects) and a dorsal "where" stream (occipital to posterior parietal lobe: location and motion); Melvyn Goodale and David Milner (1992) later recast the dorsal stream as "how" (vision for guiding action).

Exam trap. Exams key V4 = colour and V5/MT = motion. MT stands for middle temporal (not medial temporal), and despite the "temporal" in its name V5/MT belongs to the dorsal (parietal) stream, not the temporal "what" stream. The diagram agrees with the standard view on these two: V4 on the temporal side, V5 on the parietal side.

How the diagram is wired. V1 and V2 are each drawn as two boxes to show that separate channels leave from different parts of them. Following the arrows:

  • V1 → V2: the main route; both halves of V1 feed V2.
  • V1 → V5 and V1 → V3A directly: a shortcut that skips V2, so motion signals reach V5 fast.
  • V2 → V3 and V2 → V4 (temporal side), and V2 → parietal lobe (the other half of V2 sends a line straight up to the parietal lobe).
  • V4 ↔ V5: a two-way arrow, so the colour/form side and the motion side exchange information.
  • Temporal lobe ↔ parietal lobe: the two streams also talk to each other at the top.

The point of the wiring is that vision is processed in parallel: different features travel along different routes at the same time and are combined later.

10 · Techniques to Study the Brain

History

  • Trephination: creating a hole in the skull or nail to relieve pressure or treat a health condition.
  • Autopsy: a medical examination of a body after death.
  • Ablation: complete removal of a part of the brain (mostly for treatment).
  • Gene knockout: use of genetic engineering to inactivate or remove one or more specific genes from an organism.

Imaging techniques

  • Computed Axial Tomography (CAT): introduced in the early 1970s (Godfrey Hounsfield, first patient scan 1971); 360-degree X-ray; 3D/2D structural image.
  • EEG: electrical activity.
  • EMG (electromyography): records the electrical activity of muscles, i.e. muscle tension; used in sleep studies (muscle tone drops in REM sleep) and in biofeedback.
  • MEG (magnetoencephalography): records the tiny magnetic fields produced by the electrical activity of neurons; millisecond timing like EEG, with better localization than EEG.
  • EOG: sleep studies, eye movement.
  • PET: introduced in 1973; functional image of the brain (2D); fluorodeoxyglucose (a radioactive tracer: active neurons take up more of it) injected in carotid artery.
  • MRI: uses a strong magnetic field plus radio-wave pulses; hydrogen nuclei (mostly in water) line up in the field and give off radio signals that are turned into an image; 3D image of structure; high spatial resolution. No radioactivity and no X-rays.
  • fMRI: traces BOLD (Blood Oxygen Level Dependent) signal; gives structural as well as functional image. BOLD contrast: checks difference between active and inactive areas.
  • Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS): non-invasive; uses magnetic pulses to stimulate the brain and treat conditions like depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder.
  • Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT): 2D structural, 3D functional image; location of seizures can be checked.
  • Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS): optical imaging technique; light measures blood flow changes; good temporal resolution.
  • Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI): better spatial resolution than MRI.
Heads up: the diagram above swaps two labels and garbles a third. It prints EMG as "magnetic activity" and MEG as "muscle tension"; the correct pairs are EMG = electrical activity of muscles (muscle tension) and MEG = magnetic fields from neural activity. Break the names up to remember: electro-myo-graphy (myo = muscle), magneto-encephalo-graphy (encephalo = brain). It also says MRI traces a "radioactive field of hydrogen": MRI uses a magnetic field and radio waves and involves no radioactivity. The techniques that use a radioactive tracer are PET (and SPECT).

Surgical & stimulation techniques

  • Stereotaxic surgery: uses an atlas and an instrument; useful for lesion and surgery; invasive.
  • Optogenetic stimulation: a genetic technique that enables scientists to activate or inhibit the activity of specific neuron populations using light.
  • Neurosurgery: done for treatment.

Lesions (controlled destruction)

  • Aspiration lesion: for suction action; only for shallow areas and cerebral cortex.
  • Knife cut lesion: severing, tissue sectioning (for biopsy).
  • Reversible lesion: functional lesion; cryogenic (put freezing agents); uses a cryoprobe (cryogenic blockade).
  • Chemical lesion: excitotoxic lesions performed through stereotaxis — kainic acid (glutamate neuron), ibotenic acid, 6-hydroxy-dopamine (monoamine).
  • Radiofrequency lesion: a high-frequency current is passed through the tip of an electrode placed by stereotaxic surgery, and the heat destroys the tissue around the tip. Used for small, deep (subcortical) lesions; the size depends on the current's strength and duration and on the electrode tip. It destroys everything near the tip, including axons just passing through, which excitotoxic chemical lesions spare.
Applies to all lesions. The diagram's note "bilateral lesions show better effect than unilateral" sits beside the Lesions heading, so it is a general point about lesion studies, not about radiofrequency lesions only. Destroying a structure on both sides gives a much clearer effect on behaviour; after a unilateral (one-sided) lesion the intact twin structure in the other hemisphere can partly compensate, so the effect may be mild or hard to detect (Pinel, Biopsychology).

11 · Dysfunctions of the Brain

Lateralization and Localization

Specific parts of the brain control specific functions.

Who → what. Franz Gall and Johann Casper — studies of localization. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga — the Split Brain Experiment: severing the corpus callosum leads to the binding problem. Karl Lashley — controlled lesioning; concept of equipotentiality: each area of the brain has an equal role in each function.

Lateralization = functional dominance of one hemisphere over the other.

  • Broca's area: expressive aphasia. (Left inferior frontal gyrus, areas 44 and 45: frontal lobe.)
  • Wernicke's area: fluent aphasia. (Left posterior superior temporal gyrus, back of area 22: temporal lobe.)

The diagram lists these two under lateralization because language is the textbook example of hemispheric dominance: speech is controlled by the left hemisphere in about 95% of right-handers and roughly 70% of left-handers. Paul Broca (1861) described a patient, Leborgne (nicknamed "Tan"), who understood speech but could say almost nothing. Carl Wernicke (1874) described patients who spoke fluently but made little sense and understood poorly.

Don't confuse. Broca's aphasia = non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic speech with agrammatism; comprehension fairly good; the patient knows and is frustrated. Wernicke's aphasia = fluent but empty speech (word salad, made-up words, wrong words); comprehension poor; the patient is often unaware. Broca's area is in the frontal lobe, not the temporal lobe. Damage to the arcuate fasciculus that links the two areas gives conduction aphasia (cannot repeat what is heard).

Heads up: in the diagram, "Balint's syndrome" and "can't adjust to sharpen movement accuracy skills" are two separate cerebellum bullets; the second is not a definition of the first. Also, Balint's syndrome is not a cerebellar disorder. Balint's syndrome (Rezső Bálint, 1909) comes from damage to both parietal (parieto-occipital) regions and has three parts: simultanagnosia (cannot see more than one object at a time), ocular apraxia (cannot shift gaze at will) and optic ataxia (misreaching for things seen). That is why the source also lists it under Parietal. The optic ataxia can look like cerebellar clumsiness, but the cerebellar signs proper are ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor (worse as the hand nears the target, unlike the resting tremor of Parkinson's), nystagmus and low muscle tone.

Serotonin trap. MAOIs (monoamine oxidase inhibitors) are serotonin agonists, not antagonists, as the diagram shows. They block the enzyme monoamine oxidase, which breaks down serotonin (and noradrenaline and dopamine), so more serotonin stays in the synapse. SSRIs raise serotonin too, by blocking its reuptake. Both are indirect agonists, which is why both are antidepressants (and why combining them risks serotonin syndrome). The "-setrons" (ondansetron, granisetron, dolasetron, palonosetron) are the true antagonists here: they block 5-HT3 receptors and are used to stop nausea and vomiting, e.g. during chemotherapy. In both drug names, "inhibitor" refers to blocking an enzyme or a reuptake pump, not the serotonin receptor.

14 · Glial Cells

CNS glial cells

  • Astrocytes: maintain the structure of the cell; control concentration of neurotransmitters; maintain blood brain barrier
  • Ependymal cells: produce CSF
  • Oligodendrocytes: myelinate neurons of the CNS
  • Microglia: brain's immune cell; soldiers of the nervous system

PNS glial cells

  • Satellite cells: similar function to astrocytes, but in the PNS
  • Schwann cells: myelinate neurons of the PNS
Don't confuse. Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS neurons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS neurons.

Heads up: the diagram gives the two systems as two separate lists, not matched pairs, so do not read across them line by line. In particular, "metabolic activity decreases" and "brain activity decreases" are general parasympathetic effects; they are not the opposite of "inhibits digestion" (that opposite is stimulates digestion). The table above lines each organ up with the standard textbook effect on each side. Easy-to-misread pair: the source's "constricts bladder" (sympathetic) and "contracts bladder" (parasympathetic). Standard texts say the sympathetic system relaxes the bladder and holds urine, and the parasympathetic system contracts it to empty it.
Don't confuse. Sympathetic = Thoracic and Lumbar nerves, "fight or flight (Walter Cannon)," pupils dilate, heart rate up, digestion inhibited, GSR increases. Parasympathetic = Cranial and Sacral nerves, "rest and digest," slows heartbeat, stimulates saliva, GSR decreases.
Mechanism. In sympathetic arousal, galvanic skin response increases because sweat gland activity rises, which decreases skin resistance. Under parasympathetic dominance, GSR decreases as skin resistance increases.
