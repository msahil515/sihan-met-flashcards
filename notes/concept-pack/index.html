High-Yield Concept Pack

A batch of the exact concepts that keep showing up and keep costing you: Lewin's whole toolkit, the motivation theories (Herzberg, McClelland), attachment (Bowlby, Ainsworth, the four styles), the Johari window, proactive vs retroactive interference, and the full Indian psychology load (trigunas, pancha kosha, purusha/prakriti, the four avasthas, antahkarana, RCI 1986 vs 1993). Each one is widened out the same way as the rest of the site: the core idea, the why so it actually sticks, and the precise trap they use to catch you. Read top to bottom once, then re-pass the trap boxes before the exam.

1. Kurt Lewin: the one name behind half the social/org questions

Lewin is the founder of social psychology, group dynamics, and organisational change. If a question is about change in groups, leadership climate, or "behaviour comes from the present situation," it is almost certainly tracing back to Lewin. Five of his ideas are tested again and again: field theory (B = f(P,E)), action research, force field analysis, unfreeze / change / refreeze, and the three leadership styles.

Field theory: B = f(P, E)

Behaviour is a function of the Person and the Environment acting together. P and E are not separate causes you add up; they form one interdependent whole that Lewin called the life space (the totality of everything influencing the person at that moment). Behaviour is driven by the present field of forces, not by buried childhood drives or fixed traits.

Why it matters. This is Lewin's break from both psychoanalysis (which looks to the past) and trait theory (which looks to stable dispositions). For Lewin the explanation of why you act is "here and now": what forces are pushing on you in this situation right now. Change the field, change the behaviour. That is the bridge to his change models below.
The trap: B = f(P,E) is pure Lewin. Do not read P and E as "nature vs nurture", and do not attribute the formula to anyone else. P (person) and E (environment) are interdependent parts of one present life space, not two historical causes.

Action research

Lewin coined action research: a cyclical, participatory method where you solve a real social problem and build knowledge at the same time, working with the people in the situation rather than studying them from outside. The cycle spirals: plan → act → observe → reflect, then re-plan and go again.

His line for it: "No action without research, no research without action." And the much-quoted "Nothing is as practical as a good theory" is also Lewin.
The trap: action research is applied, cyclical, and participatory, the opposite of detached basic/lab research. If a stem describes practitioners studying and fixing their own setting in repeating cycles, that is action research, and the name to pick is Lewin.

Force field analysis

Any stable situation is a quasi-stationary equilibrium: it sits still because two sets of forces balance out. Driving forces push toward change; restraining forces push back and hold the status quo. To produce change you can either add driving forces or weaken restraining forces.

The key finding: it is more effective to reduce the restraining forces than to pile on more driving forces. Adding driving force raises tension and provokes resistance; removing a restraint lets change happen with less conflict.
The trap: the "best way to create change" answer is weaken/reduce the restraining forces, not "increase the driving forces." That second option is the distractor.

Unfreeze → change (move) → refreeze

Lewin's three-stage model of planned change, and it maps straight onto force field analysis.

StageWhat happensForce-field link
UnfreezeCreate the motivation to change; destabilise the old equilibrium; build awareness that the current state is not working.Tip the balance by reducing restraining forces.
Change / MoveThe transition itself: new behaviours, attitudes, and ways of working are adopted; cognitive restructuring.The system moves to a new position.
RefreezeStabilise and lock in the new state so it does not slide back; embed it in norms, habits, and structures.A new equilibrium is established and held.
The trap: order and meaning. Refreeze = making the change permanent, not "going back." Unfreeze is first and is about loosening the status quo, not implementing the change yet. Stems love to scramble the three.

The three leadership / group-climate styles (Lewin, Lippitt & White, 1939)

StyleHow the leader actsWhat happens in the group
Autocratic
(authoritarian)		Leader decides alone, dictates tasks, stays apart.High output only while watched; more hostility/aggression or apathy; work stops when the leader leaves; dependent members.
DemocraticDecisions shared, leader guides and explains, members participate.Best quality work, highest satisfaction and cohesion; output continues even when the leader is away. Generally the most effective.
Laissez-faireHands-off, little guidance or feedback, leaves the group to it.Lowest productivity, low satisfaction, members directionless and frustrated.
The trap: "most effective" is democratic (best quality + morale + self-sustaining), but watch the nuance: autocratic can give high quantity in the short term while supervised. Laissez-faire is the least productive, not democratic. Do not mix laissez-faire ("hands-off") with democratic ("shared").
NIMHANS depth (other Lewin items worth knowing): he founded group dynamics and sensitivity training / T-groups. The Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks remembered better) came out of his circle, named for his student Bluma Zeigarnik. He also gave the three conflict types: approach-approach (two goods), avoidance-avoidance (two bads, "between a rock and a hard place"), and approach-avoidance (one goal with both attraction and repulsion). And the concept of valence (the positive or negative pull of a goal in the life space).
Lewin in one line: behaviour comes from the present field (B = f(P,E)); you change a group by unfreezing it (weaken the restraining forces), moving it, and refreezing the new state; and democratic leadership gives the best quality and morale.

2. Motivation at work: Herzberg and McClelland

Herzberg's two-factor (motivation-hygiene) theory

Herzberg's big claim: satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not two ends of one scale, they are two separate scales driven by two different sets of factors. Fixing what makes people unhappy does not make them motivated; it only makes them "not unhappy."
Factor typeExamplesWhat it controls
Hygiene factors
(extrinsic, "maintenance", dissatisfiers)		Salary/pay, company policy, supervision, working conditions, interpersonal relations, job security, status.Their absence causes dissatisfaction. Their presence only removes dissatisfaction; it does not motivate.
Motivators
(intrinsic, satisfiers)		Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth.Their presence produces real satisfaction and motivation; their absence leaves people neutral, not dissatisfied.
Why the two-scale idea is the whole point. The opposite of "dissatisfied" is not "satisfied", it is "not dissatisfied." So you can have a well-paid, comfortable job (good hygiene) and still feel zero drive, because none of the motivators are present. You raise motivation through job enrichment (building in more responsibility, achievement, and growth), not through better pay.
The trap: salary is a HYGIENE factor, not a motivator. This is the single most common Herzberg question. Also do not confuse job enrichment (adding motivators, vertical) with job enlargement (more tasks of the same level, horizontal).

McClelland's acquired-needs theory and nAch

McClelland said motivation runs on three learned needs, present in different strengths in everyone, and measured projectively with the TAT (Thematic Apperception Test).

NeedWhat the person wants
nAch (achievement)To accomplish, master, meet a standard of excellence; driven by the success itself.
nPow (power)To influence, control, have impact on others.
nAff (affiliation)To be liked, belong, maintain warm relationships.

The high nAch profile is the one they test: prefers tasks of moderate difficulty / calculated risk, wants personal responsibility for outcomes, and wants immediate, concrete feedback on performance. Driven by the achievement, not by the money or praise. McClelland linked high national nAch to economic development in The Achieving Society.

The trap: high nAch prefers moderate risk, not high risk (too random, no credit for skill) and not low risk (no real achievement). Also: the need is measured by the TAT, a projective test. And the best managers tend to be high in socialised nPow, not necessarily highest in nAch.

3. The Johari window

A model of self-awareness and interpersonal disclosure by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham ("Joe" + "Hari" = Johari). A 2 × 2 grid: what is known/unknown to self crossed with what is known/unknown to others.
Known to selfUnknown to self
Known to othersOpen / Arena: shared, public self.Blind spot: others see it, you don't.
Unknown to othersHidden / Façade: you know it, you conceal it.Unknown: neither you nor others know (latent).
How the quadrants move: feedback from others shrinks the Blind area; self-disclosure shrinks the Hidden area. Both enlarge the Open quadrant, which is the goal of healthy communication and good teamwork.
The trap: do not flip Blind and Hidden. Blind = others know, you don't (reduced by feedback). Hidden/Façade = you know, others don't (reduced by self-disclosure). The "Unknown" quadrant is known to neither.

4. Attachment: Bowlby, Ainsworth, and the four styles

Bowlby: the theory

John Bowlby is the father of attachment theory. Drawing on ethology and evolution, he argued attachment is an innate survival system: infants are biologically driven to keep proximity to a caregiver because it protects them. Key Bowlby concepts:

ConceptMeaning
MonotropyOne primary attachment figure sits at the top of a hierarchy of figures.
Internal working modelA mental template of relationships, built from early care, that shapes later relationships.
Critical / sensitive periodRoughly 6 months to ~2.5 years for forming the primary bond.
Maternal deprivationProlonged disruption of the bond can cause lasting harm (his "44 thieves" study linked it to affectionless psychopathy).
Separation responseThree stages on separation: protest → despair → detachment.
The trap: Bowlby = the theory; Ainsworth = the measurement (the Strange Situation). Do not credit the Strange Situation to Bowlby. Monotropy means one main figure, not "many equal figures."

Ainsworth: the Strange Situation

Mary Ainsworth (Bowlby's collaborator) built the empirical test. The Strange Situation is a structured lab procedure for infants around 12 to 18 months, run as eight short episodes involving separations from and reunions with the caregiver, plus a stranger. It scores: use of the caregiver as a secure base for exploration, stranger anxiety, separation anxiety, and most importantly the infant's reunion behaviour.

The classifier: it is the reunion behaviour (how the baby responds when the caregiver comes back) that mainly decides the attachment style, not the crying at separation.

The four attachment styles

StyleBehaviour in the Strange SituationCaregiving linked to it
Secure (Type B)
~60-65%		Explores using caregiver as a base, distressed at separation, easily comforted at reunion.Sensitive, responsive.
Insecure-Avoidant (Type A)
~20%		Little distress at separation, avoids/ignores caregiver at reunion, treats stranger much the same.Unresponsive, rejecting.
Insecure-Resistant / Ambivalent (Type C)
~10-15%		Intense distress, clingy AND resistant at reunion: seeks contact then pushes it away, hard to soothe.Inconsistent.
Disorganised (Type D)Confused, contradictory, freezing, fearful, no coherent strategy.Frightening / abusive (often added later by Main & Solomon).
The trap: Type A = avoidant, Type C = resistant/ambivalent. The "ambivalent / clingy-but-rejecting" one is C, not A. Disorganised (Type D) was added by Main & Solomon, not part of Ainsworth's original three. And classification rests on reunion behaviour.
NIMHANS depth: the adult parallels (Hazan & Shaver, and the secure / dismissing / preoccupied / fearful four-category adult model) extend the same logic to romantic relationships. Avoidant infants minimise attachment needs; resistant infants maximise them.

5. Proactive vs retroactive interference

Interference theory says we forget not because the trace fades but because other learning gets in the way. The only thing to get right is the direction: which set of information is doing the interfering, old or new.
TypeDirectionWhat you cannot recallEveryday example
Proactive interferenceOld → new (forward). Previously learned material blocks new learning.You struggle to recall the NEW info.Your old phone number keeps coming out instead of the new one.
Retroactive interferenceNew → old (backward). Recently learned material blocks older memories.You struggle to recall the OLD info.A new password makes you forget the old one.
The mnemonic that fixes it for good: PROactive = the PRior (past) learning reaches forward. RETROactive = the recent learning reaches back ("retro" = backward). Or simply: Proactive = Past interferes; Retroactive = Recent interferes.
The trap: the direction gets flipped under time pressure. Always ask two things: (1) which info is being recalled, and (2) which info is doing the interfering. In proactive you fail to recall the new (old intrudes); in retroactive you fail to recall the old (new intrudes).

6. Indian psychology: the cluster MET over-weights

MET leans on Indian psychology harder than NIMHANS does, often a whole cluster in one paper. Over-prepare this section, do not just touch it.

Trigunas: the three gunas

From Samkhya philosophy (and the Bhagavad Gita), the three fundamental qualities of prakriti (nature, and therefore of the mind and personality). Everyone has all three; your makeup is their proportion.

GunaQuality
SattvaPurity, clarity, harmony, knowledge, calm, balance, light.
RajasActivity, passion, restlessness, desire, drive, movement.
TamasInertia, dullness, darkness, ignorance, lethargy.
The trap: map each one exactly. Sattva = clarity/balance, Rajas = activity/passion, Tamas = inertia/ignorance. The yogic goal is to cultivate sattva and ultimately go beyond all three (gunatita).

Pancha kosha: the five sheaths

From the Taittiriya Upanishad: five layers or "coverings" wrapped around the true Self (Atman), running from the grossest (outer, physical) to the subtlest (inner, bliss).

#KoshaLayer
1AnnamayaFood / physical body (outermost).
2PranamayaVital energy, breath (prana).
3ManomayaMind, emotions (manas).
4VijnanamayaIntellect, wisdom, discernment (buddhi).
5AnandamayaBliss (innermost).
The trap: do not confuse Annamaya (food/body, outermost) with Anandamaya (bliss, innermost). They sound alike and sit at opposite ends. Order is physical → bliss, gross → subtle.

Purusha and Prakriti: the Samkhya dualism

Samkhya (attributed to the sage Kapila) is a dualistic system built on two ultimate realities.

PurushaPrakriti
What it isPure consciousness, the Self, the witness.Primordial matter/nature, the source of everything material and mental.
NaturePassive, eternal, unchanging, plural, just observes.Active, unconscious, ever-changing; made of the three gunas.
ContainsNothing; it is the silent observer.Even mind, intellect, and ego evolve out of Prakriti.
Liberation (kaivalya) comes from viveka: discriminating Purusha (consciousness) from Prakriti (matter), realising the Self is the witness, not the changing mind.
The trap: Purusha = consciousness (inactive witness); Prakriti = matter/nature (active, carries the gunas). The mind, intellect, and ego are part of Prakriti, not Purusha. And Samkhya is dualistic, in contrast to Advaita Vedanta's monism.

The four avasthas: states of consciousness

From the Mandukya Upanishad, the four states of the Self.

#AvasthaState
1JagratWaking: outward awareness of gross objects.
2Swapna (taijasa)Dream: inward awareness of subtle impressions.
3SushuptiDeep dreamless sleep: no desires, undifferentiated, blissful.
4TuriyaThe "fourth": pure transcendental consciousness, the witness underlying the other three.
The trap: Turiya is the fourth, transcendent state, not deep sleep. Deep dreamless sleep is Sushupti. Keep the order jagrat → swapna → sushupti → turiya.

Antahkarana: the "inner instrument"

Antahkarana ("antah" = inner, "karana" = instrument) is the inner mental apparatus in Vedanta, classically fourfold.

FacultyFunction
ManasMind: takes in the senses, doubts, deliberates (the lower, indecisive mind).
BuddhiIntellect: discriminates, decides, judges, the seat of wisdom.
AhamkaraEgo, the "I-maker": the sense of a separate self/identity.
ChittaMemory store: the reservoir of impressions (samskaras), "mind-stuff."
The trap: Buddhi decides/discriminates; Manas doubts and handles the senses; Ahamkara is the ego/I-sense; Chitta is the memory storehouse. Note that in Patanjali's Yoga, "chitta" means the whole mind-field (as in yogah chitta-vritti-nirodhah, yoga is the stilling of the modifications of the mind), whereas Vedanta lists chitta as just one of the four.

RCI: 1986 vs 1993

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) regulates the training and registration of rehabilitation professionals, including clinical and rehabilitation psychologists. The "1986 vs 1993" point is about its change in legal status.
YearWhat happened
1986RCI is first set up as a registered society (voluntary, non-statutory body).
1992 / 1993The RCI Act, 1992 is passed by Parliament and comes into force on 1 July 1993, making RCI a statutory regulatory body with legal teeth.
2000Act amended to add promotion of research in rehabilitation and special education.
So: 1986 = a society (no legal authority); 1992 Act / 1993 in force = a statutory council that maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register. Practising as a rehab professional without RCI registration is an offence.
The trap: the Act is dated 1992 (passed) but came into force in 1993, and the council itself predates the Act (1986, as a society). Do not confuse RCI with the MCI/NMC (doctors), the RPwD Act 2016, the older PWD Act 1995, or the Mental Healthcare Act 2017. RCI is specifically about training and registering rehabilitation personnel.

Self-test: answer before opening

What does B = f(P,E) mean and whose is it?
Lewin's field theory: behaviour is a function of the Person and the Environment together (the life space), driven by the present field of forces, not the past.
To create change, is it better to add driving forces or reduce restraining forces?
Reduce the restraining forces. Adding driving force raises tension and resistance. (Force field analysis.)
Put Lewin's change stages in order and say what "refreeze" means.
Unfreeze → change/move → refreeze. Refreeze = locking in the new state so it becomes permanent, not reverting.
Which leadership style gives the best quality and morale, and which is least productive?
Democratic gives best quality + morale (and continues without the leader). Laissez-faire is least productive.
Is salary a motivator or a hygiene factor (Herzberg)?
Hygiene factor. Its absence causes dissatisfaction; its presence does not motivate. Motivators are achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth.
What level of risk does a high-nAch person prefer, and how is the need measured?
Moderate / calculated risk (not high, not low). Measured by the TAT. McClelland's three needs: achievement, power, affiliation.
In the Johari window, which quadrant is reduced by feedback, and which by self-disclosure?
Feedback shrinks the Blind area (others know, you don't). Self-disclosure shrinks the Hidden/Façade area (you know, others don't). Both grow the Open area.
Who built the Strange Situation, and what behaviour mainly classifies the infant?
Ainsworth (Bowlby gave the theory). The reunion behaviour is the main classifier.
Type A vs Type C attachment?
Type A = insecure-avoidant (ignores caregiver at reunion). Type C = insecure-resistant/ambivalent (clingy but rejects comfort). Type D (disorganised) was added later by Main & Solomon.
Proactive vs retroactive interference: which blocks which?
Proactive = Past/old learning blocks the new (you can't recall the new). Retroactive = Recent/new learning blocks the old (you can't recall the old).
Name the three gunas with their qualities.
Sattva = clarity/balance; Rajas = activity/passion; Tamas = inertia/ignorance.
Pancha kosha: outermost and innermost sheaths?
Outermost = Annamaya (food/body). Innermost = Anandamaya (bliss). Order: anna → prana → mano → vijnana → ananda.
Purusha vs Prakriti?
Purusha = pure consciousness, passive witness. Prakriti = active matter/nature made of the gunas; mind, intellect and ego belong to Prakriti. Samkhya is dualistic.
The four avasthas in order, and which is the transcendent one?
Jagrat (waking) → Swapna (dream) → Sushupti (deep sleep) → Turiya (the transcendent fourth, the witness). Turiya is NOT deep sleep.
Antahkarana: which faculty decides, and which is the ego?
Buddhi decides/discriminates. Ahamkara is the ego ("I-maker"). Manas handles senses and doubts; Chitta is the memory store.
RCI: what changed between 1986 and 1993?
1986 = set up as a registered society (non-statutory). The RCI Act 1992 came into force on 1 July 1993, making it a statutory regulatory body that maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register.
The whole pack in one line: Lewin owns change and leadership (B=f(P,E), reduce restraining forces, unfreeze-move-refreeze, democratic wins); Herzberg splits hygiene from motivators (salary is hygiene); McClelland's high-nAch wants moderate risk; Johari grows the Open area via feedback + disclosure; attachment is Bowlby's theory + Ainsworth's Strange Situation with four styles judged at reunion; interference is just old-blocks-new vs new-blocks-old; and the Indian-psych cluster is gunas, five koshas, Purusha/Prakriti, four avasthas, antahkarana, and RCI's 1986-society-to-1993-statutory shift.
