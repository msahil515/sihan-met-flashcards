MPhil Mock Review + Your Coverage Gaps

An honest read on the PsychEntrancePrep mocks I pulled, what the MPhil layer actually tests, and the topics that aren't in your notes yet · Built 2026-05-26

The short version. The PEP site is a decent supplementary drill for the MPhil-specific layer (ethics, professional regulation, MSE, trauma therapy) that NET-style material skips. It is not a clean answer key, so treat its themes as a checklist, not gospel. I went through all 40 MPhil-specific questions across the two mocks, mapped them to your notes, and found 8 clusters you have zero or thin coverage on. Those are filled in below, exam-ready.

1. Verdict on PsychEntrancePrep (the site I pulled the mocks from)

What it actually is

A Google Sites page hosting mock tests as Google Forms and notes as Google Drive PDFs. The 120-question mocks split into 80 common (NET + MPhil) + 20 MPhil-specific + 20 UGC-NET-specific. I extracted both mocks' questions straight from the form data, but the forms are ungraded surveys with no official answer key — every correct answer and explanation on our quiz pages was authored by me with reasoning, not lifted from the site.

Where it's good. Genuinely India-flavoured MPhil content (RCI, Indian Acts, MSE, ethics codes) that you won't get from generic NET PDFs. Free till the 31st. Wide topical spread. The MPhil-specific 20 per mock is the most useful slice for you because it targets the clinical-psych entrance layer specifically.
Where to be careful. (1) No real answer key — a handful of items have defensible alternative answers, so if an explanation feels wrong, trust your reasoning and flag it. (2) Sloppy phrasing — stray "Q)" prefixes, typos, occasionally two arguable options. (3) The actual study notes are login-gated Drive PDFs scoped to one Workspace account, so I could only map their titles, not rip the text. (4) It's a coaching aggregator, not a primary source — for anything load-bearing, your NIMHANS PYQs and standard texts win.
How to use it. Run the two MPhil sections as a themes checklist. The point isn't the score, it's surfacing the topics below that NET-style prep never made you study.

2. What the MPhil side actually tests

Across the 40 MPhil-specific questions (20 in each mock), the weighting is heavily toward the professional / ethical / legal scaffolding of clinical practice — the stuff that separates an MPhil Clinical entrance from a general psych exam. Breakdown:

Question style is roughly half straight definitional recall ("who coined X", "what is Y") and half short applied vignette ("which principle is violated", "what is the correct sequence"). Difficulty is moderate — no trick stats, no deep neuroscience, but it assumes you've read the professional-practice and ethics chapters that NET prep tends to skip.

4. Filling the gaps (study these)

§A — APA Ethics Code + ethical theory (deontology vs utilitarianism)

Don't confuse the two ethics frameworks they both test:

APA General Principles (5)Beauchamp & Childress (4)
WhatAspirational principles of the APA Ethics CodeFour principles of biomedical ethics
ListA) Beneficence & Nonmaleficence · B) Fidelity & Responsibility · C) Integrity · D) Justice · E) Respect for People's Rights & DignityAutonomy · Beneficence · Non-maleficence · Justice
APA = 5, "BFIJR". Integrity (C) = the one about accuracy, honesty, truthfulness in science/teaching/practice. Fidelity & Responsibility (B) = trust + professional obligations. "Equity" is NOT an APA principle — the relevant one is Justice (a classic distractor).

Ethical theory (meta-ethics):

Deontology (Kant)Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill)
Judges actions by their intrinsic rightness/wrongness and adherence to duty. "Categorical imperative." Consequences don't change whether the act is right.Judges actions by their consequences — a cost-benefit calculus for the greatest good of the greatest number.
The swap they bait: deontology = duty/intrinsic, utilitarian = consequences/cost-benefit. If an option says "deontology = cost-benefit," it's the reversed (wrong) one.

§B — Ethical competency, gifts & boundaries

  • Competence: practising only within the boundaries of your training, supervised experience, and credentials. Taking on a case outside your competence (without supervision/referral) is the violation.
  • Ethical competency = the clinician's ability to recognise and reason through ethical dilemmas and apply the code in practice — not just knowing the rules, but applying them.
  • Accepting gifts: generally discouraged because of boundary/dual-relationship risk. Acceptable only when the gift is of nominal/token value and refusing would harm the therapeutic relationship or be culturally insulting. Large or quid-pro-quo gifts = never.
  • Multiple/dual relationships: avoid roles that could impair objectivity or exploit the client (e.g., therapist + business partner). Boundary crossing (minor, sometimes benign) vs boundary violation (harmful, exploitative — e.g., sexual contact, always prohibited).

§D — RCI + the Indian Acts that show up

Act / bodyWhat to remember
Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI)Statutory body under the Ministry of Social Justice & Empowerment (Dept. of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities). Not Health, not Education. Regulates training + maintains the register of rehab professionals (incl. clinical psychologists).
RCI Act, 1992Created the RCI; mandates that all rehabilitation professionals be registered with the RCI. Practising without registration is an offence. This is why an MPhil Clinical Psychology must be RCI-recognised.
Mental Healthcare Act, 2017Rights-based; decriminalised suicide; introduced advance directive + nominated representative; right to access mental healthcare. Replaced the Mental Health Act 1987.
RPwD Act, 2016Rights of Persons with Disabilities; expanded disabilities from 7 to 21; replaced the PwD Act 1995.
Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013Raised the age of consent / statutory rape to 18 (post-Nirbhaya). Sex with anyone under 18 = rape regardless of consent.
POCSO Act, 2012Protection of Children from Sexual Offences; child = under 18; mandatory reporting.
UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC)Child = under 18. Core idea: children are capable of forming and expressing their own views, with evolving capacities; decisions guided by the best interests of the child.

§E — Minority stress + LGBTQ affirmative practice

  • Minority stress model (Ilan Meyer, 2003): the excess stress that stigmatised minority groups face explains their higher rates of mental-health problems. Defining features: it is unique (added on top of general stressors), chronic (tied to stable social structures), and socially based (not individual pathology).
  • Distal stressors = external events: discrimination, prejudice, violence, rejection. Proximal stressors = internal: internalised stigma/homophobia, concealment, and expectation of rejection (vigilance).
  • Depathologization: homosexuality removed from the DSM in 1973 (DSM-II); ICD-10 still had "ego-dystonic sexual orientation"; ICD-11 removed it entirely. "Pathologization" = treating it as a disorder; "depathologization" = the shift to seeing it as a normal variant.
  • Affirmative therapy self-acceptance sequence (rough order): awareness/acknowledging same-sex feelings → exploring & reducing internalised stigma → disclosure/coming out to safe others → building community/connectedness → integrating identity into a positive self-concept.

§F — Psychodynamic process terms (the thin ones)

  • Transference: client redirects feelings about a past figure onto the therapist. Countertransference: the therapist's own emotional reactions (conscious or unconscious) toward the client — to be monitored and used, not acted on.
  • Primary defense against anxiety (psychodynamic): repression — pushing unacceptable thoughts/impulses out of awareness. It's the foundational defense the others build on.

§G — MSE depth + how clinicians read assessment data

Assessment data is conceived as: samples (a direct sample of behaviour), signs (an indirect indicator of an underlying trait/state), or correlates (statistically linked to a criterion). Sample = behaviour for its own sake; sign = behaviour points to something hidden.

MSE domains often under-studied:

  • Consciousness — level of alertness. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) grades it via Eye (4) + Verbal (5) + Motor (6), score 3–15. GCS measures consciousness, NOT attention/memory/intelligence.
  • Insight — the patient's degree of awareness of their own illness (distinct from orientation, consciousness, recall).
  • Affect vs mood: affect = the immediate, outward, observable emotional expression — described by quality, range, and reactivity. Mood = the sustained, pervasive internal emotional climate — described by its persistence. So "persistence" belongs to mood, not affect (a NOT-question trap).
  • Thought form: flight of ideas (rapid shifting, loose links) ≠ perseveration (repeating one idea) ≠ circumstantiality (over-detail, eventually gets there) ≠ tangentiality (never gets there).
