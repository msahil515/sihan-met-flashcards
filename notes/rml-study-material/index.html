1. Logical & Analytical Reasoning

The CET-166 paper (and the older GGSIPU CET = Guru Gobind Singh Indraprastha University Common Entrance Test pattern it inherits) uses a fixed menu of item types. Learn the trick behind each.

Series

Find the rule linking consecutive terms, then extend it. Rules are arithmetic (+/−), geometric (×/÷), squares/cubes, or alternating (two interleaved series).

2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?, differences are 4, 6, 8, 10, so next gap is 12 → 42.

Analogies

"A is to B as C is to D." Name the exact relationship in A:B, then force the same relationship on C:D.

Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : School (worker : workplace).

Syllogisms

From scratch: A syllogism gives you premises ("All A are B", "Some B are C") and asks which conclusion necessarily follows. Draw overlapping circles (Venn diagrams). A conclusion is valid ONLY if it is true in every possible diagram, not just one. "Some" means at least one and possibly all; "All A are B" does NOT let you say "All B are A".

All cats are animals; all animals are mortal → All cats are mortal (valid).

Trap: "Some A are B" never guarantees "Some A are not B". Existence of an overlap does not prove a non-overlap. The paper plants this "illicit converse" repeatedly.

Odd-one-out (classification)

Four items share a property; one breaks it. Rose, Lotus, Lily, Mango, Mango is the odd one (it is a fruit/tree, the rest are flowers).

Coding-Decoding

A word is enciphered by a rule (shift letters, reverse, number-for-letter). Discover the rule from the given pair, apply it to the target.

If CAT = DBU (each letter +1), then DOG = EPH.

Basic Data Interpretation

A table/bar/pie gives numbers; questions ask totals, differences, percentages, or ratios. If a pie shows 90° for "Clinical", that slice is 90/360 = 25% of the total. Read the axis and units first, compute second.

RML tilt: Reasoning items are non-verbal-friendly and answerable in under a minute; do not burn exam time deriving elegant formulas. Spot the pattern, mark, move.

2. Research Aptitude

Types of research

  • Basic (pure) vs Applied, theory-building vs solving a real problem.
  • Descriptive (describes "what is") vs Experimental (manipulates to test cause).
  • Qualitative (meaning, themes) vs Quantitative (numbers, statistics).
  • Exploratory, correlational, longitudinal, cross-sectional designs.

Hypotheses

The null hypothesis (H₀) states "no effect / no difference / no relationship." The alternative hypothesis (H₁) states there IS one. A directional (one-tailed) hypothesis predicts the direction (Group A scores higher); a non-directional (two-tailed) hypothesis predicts only that a difference exists.

Key. You never "prove" H₁, you reject or fail to reject H₀. A Type I error is rejecting a true H₀ (false positive, α); a Type II error is failing to reject a false H₀ (false negative, β).

Variables

VariableRole
Independent (IV)Manipulated / presumed cause
Dependent (DV)Measured / the effect
ConfoundUnwanted variable that co-varies with the IV and offers a rival explanation
ModeratorChanges the strength/direction of the IV→DV link (a "for whom / when")
MediatorThe mechanism through which IV affects DV (a "how / why")
Trap: Moderator vs mediator. A moderator sits alongside and interacts; a mediator sits in the causal chain (IV → mediator → DV). Stress is a mediator if IV causes stress which then lowers DV.

Sampling

Probability sampling (every unit has a known, non-zero chance; allows generalisation):

  • Simple random, pure lottery.
  • Systematic, every k-th unit from a random start.
  • Stratified, divide into strata, sample within each proportionally.
  • Cluster, sample whole groups (e.g. clinics), study all within.

Non-probability sampling (chance not known; cheaper, weaker generalisation):

  • Convenience, whoever is available.
  • Purposive (judgmental), chosen for a trait.
  • Quota, fill fixed counts per category.
  • Snowball, participants refer others (hidden populations).

Reliability & Validity

Reliability = consistency. Types: test-retest (same test, two times), inter-rater (two scorers agree), internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha), parallel/alternate forms, split-half.

Validity = measures what it claims. Types: content (covers the domain), construct (measures the trait; includes convergent and discriminant), criterion (concurrent and predictive), plus face validity (looks right).

Key. A test can be reliable but NOT valid (a scale consistently 2 kg heavy). It cannot be valid without being reliable. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

Basic designs

Between-subjects (different people per condition) vs within-subjects/repeated-measures (same people, all conditions). Randomised control trial (RCT) is the gold-standard experiment. Quasi-experiment lacks random assignment; case study is single-unit depth.

3. General Awareness, Indian Mental-Health System

Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI)

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) is a statutory body created by the RCI Act, 1992. It regulates training of rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists) and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register.

Key. To practise as a Clinical Psychologist in India you must hold RCI registration. RML's CET-166 admits to an RCI-recognised MA Clinical Psychology, which is why this is the "RCI-licensing route."

Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (MHCA 2017)

Replaced the Mental Health Act, 1987. Rights-based. Key provisions:

  • Advance directive, a person may state in advance how they wish (or do not wish) to be treated.
  • Nominated representative, a person appointed to take decisions on their behalf.
  • Decriminalised suicide attempt, a person attempting suicide is presumed to be under severe stress and shall not be punished under the IPC.
  • Right to community living, protection from cruel/degrading treatment, and free treatment for the poor/homeless.
Trap: The advance directive belongs to MHCA 2017, NOT to the RCI Act or the RPwD Act. Do not confuse MHCA 2017 with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016.

National & District programmes

The National Mental Health Programme (NMHP) was launched in 1982 to make mental-health care accessible and integrate it into general health services. Its field arm, the District Mental Health Programme (DMHP), began in 1996 (Bellary model) to deliver care at the district level.

4. More Reasoning Item Types

The paper can rotate across many logical-reasoning families. Below are the ones most likely to appear beyond the basics already covered, each with the quick method that cracks it.

4.1 Blood Relations

Decode a family chain by replacing each link with a symbol as you read it. Draw a small tree: put males and females on rows and connect with lines.

From scratch: "Pointing to a man, Ravi said, 'His mother is the only daughter of my mother.'" Only daughter of Ravi's mother = Ravi's sister (or Ravi's own generation female). So the man's mother is Ravi's sister, making the man Ravi's nephew.
Tip: "only daughter of my mother" often resolves to the speaker's sister or the speaker herself; test both.

4.2 Direction Sense

Track movement on a compass grid (North up). Sum the net East–West and North–South displacement; final distance from start uses the Pythagorean shortcut when the two legs are perpendicular.

Walk 3 km North, then 4 km East: straight-line distance = √(3²+4²) = 5 km, and you are North-East of start.
Trap: Left/right turns depend on the facing direction, not on the page. Turning "right" while facing South sends you West, not East.

4.3 Seating Arrangement (Linear & Circular)

  • Linear: everyone faces the same way; "left" and "right" are the observer's, unless stated otherwise.
  • Circular, facing centre: a person's right hand points anticlockwise; facing outward reverses this.
Key. Fix one definite clue first (an absolute position), then place relative clues around it. Keep a light pencil diagram; do not solve circular puzzles purely in your head.

4.4 Calendar & Clock

Calendar: use odd days (remainder after dividing total days by 7). An ordinary year has 1 odd day, a leap year 2. A century leap-year rule: divisible by 400 is a leap year (2000 yes), a plain century (1900) is not.

Clock: the minute hand gains 5.5° per minute over the hour hand. Hands overlap 11 times in 12 hours, and form a right angle 22 times in 12 hours.

4.5 Series Depth & Venn Set-Counting

Number/alphabet series may mix two interleaved patterns (alternate terms) or use differences of differences. For letters, convert to position numbers (A=1 … Z=26) and read the gaps.

Venn set-counting: for two sets, |A∪B| = |A| + |B| − |A∩B|. For three, add the three singles, subtract the three pair-overlaps, add back the triple overlap.

In a class of 40, 25 play cricket, 20 play hockey, 10 play both: at least one sport = 25 + 20 − 10 = 35; neither = 5.

5. Research Aptitude: Going Deeper

5.1 Purpose-Based Types of Research

  • Fundamental (basic/pure): builds theory and general knowledge, no immediate application (testing a memory model).
  • Applied: solves a specific practical problem (does a coping programme cut relapse?).
  • Action research: a practitioner studies and improves their own practice in cycles of act → observe → reflect.

By objective, research is also exploratory (map an unfamiliar area, generate hypotheses), descriptive (describe what exists, the "what"), or explanatory (test cause, the "why").

5.2 Steps of the Research Process

  1. Identify and define the problem.
  2. Review the existing literature.
  3. Formulate hypotheses.
  4. Choose the research design and sample.
  5. Collect the data.
  6. Analyse and interpret the data.
  7. Draw conclusions and report.

5.3 A Good Hypothesis and Research Ethics

Key. A good hypothesis is clear and specific, testable (falsifiable), states a relationship between variables, is grounded in theory, and is stated before data collection.
  • Informed consent: participants agree freely after understanding the purpose, procedure, and risks; they may withdraw anytime.
  • Confidentiality & anonymity: protect identities and keep data secure.
  • Plagiarism: presenting others' work as one's own; avoid by citing every borrowed idea.
  • Referencing awareness: psychology uses APA style (author, year, in-text; full list at the end). Institutional Ethics Committee approval is required for research on humans.

2 General & Basic Psychology

From scratch: Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. This section builds the "core" academic base the RML CET-166 paper draws on: the schools, then the big four content areas (learning, memory, attention/perception, motivation/emotion) and intelligence. Learn each theorist as a who → what pair; the exam almost always tests matching.

1. Schools & History of Psychology

Modern psychology begins in 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt opened the first psychology laboratory at Leipzig, Germany. Each school below is a reaction to the one before it.

Who → what:
  • Wilhelm Wundt & Edward Titchener → Structuralism: break conscious experience into basic elements (sensations, feelings) using introspection. Titchener coined the term.
  • William James → Functionalism: don't ask what the mind is, ask what it is for, how consciousness helps us adapt. Wrote Principles of Psychology (1890).
  • Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, Kurt Koffka → Gestalt: "the whole is different from the sum of its parts." Wertheimer's phi phenomenon (apparent motion) launched it; Köhler showed insight learning in chimpanzees.
  • John B. Watson & B.F. Skinner → Behaviourism: study only observable behaviour, not the mind. Watson's 1913 "behaviourist manifesto" is the founding statement.
  • Sigmund Freud → Psychoanalysis: behaviour driven by the unconscious and early childhood conflict.
  • Carl Rogers & Abraham Maslow → Humanistic ("third force"): free will, growth, self-actualization.
  • Ulric Neisser & George A. Miller → Cognitive revolution (1950s–60s): the mind is back, studied as information processing. Neisser's 1967 book Cognitive Psychology named the field; Miller's "magical number seven, plus or minus two" set short-term memory span.
Trap: Titchener (not Wundt) coined "structuralism," and Titchener was American-based; Wundt is the founder of experimental psychology. Also, Gestalt is perception-focused, behaviourism is learning-focused, don't swap.

2. Learning

Learning = a relatively permanent change in behaviour due to experience.

Classical conditioning, Ivan Pavlov

Ivan Pavlov (Russian physiologist) discovered that a neutral stimulus, paired with one that already triggers a reflex, comes to trigger that reflex itself.

TermMeaningPavlov's dog
US (unconditioned stimulus)triggers a reflex naturallyfood
UR (unconditioned response)the natural reflexsalivation to food
CS (conditioned stimulus)was neutral, now triggers responsebell
CR (conditioned response)learned response to CSsalivation to bell
  • Acquisition: learning the CS–US link.
  • Extinction: CS repeatedly without US → CR fades.
  • Spontaneous recovery: after a rest, the extinguished CR briefly reappears.
  • Generalization: similar stimuli also trigger the CR (a different bell).
  • Discrimination: responding only to the specific CS, not similar ones.
  • Higher-order conditioning: a well-learned CS acts as a US to condition a new CS.

Operant conditioning, Thorndike & Skinner

Edward L. Thorndike's Law of Effect: responses followed by satisfaction are strengthened; those followed by discomfort are weakened (from his puzzle box cats). B.F. Skinner extended this: behaviour is shaped by its consequences, studied in the Skinner box.

Key. Reinforcement always increases a behaviour; punishment always decreases it. "Positive" = add a stimulus, "negative" = remove one.
  • Positive reinforcement: add something pleasant (give a treat).
  • Negative reinforcement: remove something unpleasant (seatbelt beep stops).
  • Positive punishment: add something aversive (a scolding).
  • Negative punishment: remove something pleasant (take away the phone).
  • Primary reinforcer: satisfies a biological need (food, water). Secondary reinforcer: learned value (money, praise).
  • Shaping: reinforcing successive approximations toward a target behaviour.

Schedules of reinforcement:

ScheduleRuleFeature
Fixed Ratio (FR)after a set number of responseshigh rate, post-reward pause
Variable Ratio (VR)after an unpredictable numberhighest, steadiest rate
Fixed Interval (FI)first response after a set timescalloped pattern
Variable Interval (VI)first response after variable timeslow, steady
Key. Variable Ratio (VR) produces the highest response rate and is most resistant to extinction (why gambling is so persistent).

Observational learning, Albert Bandura

Albert Bandura's Bobo doll experiment showed children imitate aggression they merely watch, learning by modelling, no direct reinforcement needed. His broader social learning theory adds thought between stimulus and response.

3. Memory

Who → what:
  • Richard Atkinson & Richard Shiffrin → multi-store model: sensory memory → short-term memory (STM) → long-term memory (LTM), with rehearsal moving info along.
  • Alan Baddeley & Graham Hitch → working memory: replaces passive STM with an active system, central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad (later, the episodic buffer).
  • Fergus Craik & Robert Lockhart → levels of processing: deeper semantic (meaning) processing is remembered better than shallow (looks, sound).
  • Endel Tulving → memory types: episodic (personal events), semantic (facts), both declarative; plus procedural (skills, non-declarative).
  • Hermann Ebbinghaus → the forgetting curve: memory drops sharply then levels off; also the spacing effect.

Forgetting theories: decay (unused traces fade), interference, proactive (old learning blocks new) vs retroactive (new learning blocks old), and retrieval failure.

Key. Serial position effect: we best recall the first items (primacy, rehearsed into LTM) and last items (recency, still in STM); the middle is worst.
Trap: Proactive = pro/forward, old interferes forward onto new; retroactive = new acts backward on old. Easy to reverse under time pressure.

4. Attention & Perception

Who → what:
  • Donald Broadbent → filter (bottleneck) model: an early filter selects one channel by physical features; unattended info is blocked.
  • Anne Treisman → attenuation model: the filter turns unattended input down rather than off, so a salient word (your name) can still break through.

Gestalt laws of perceptual organization: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity (good continuation), figure–ground, common fate.

Depth cues: monocular (one eye, relative size, interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient) and binocular (two eyes, retinal disparity, convergence). Perceptual constancies keep size, shape, and colour stable despite changing retinal images.

5. Motivation & Emotion

Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull): a need creates a drive that we act to reduce, restoring homeostasis. Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs stacks motives: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualization; lower needs dominate until met.

Theories of emotion (a classic RML matching item):

6. Intelligence

Who → what:
  • Charles Spearman → two-factor theory: a general factor g underlies all abilities plus task-specific s factors (from factor analysis).
  • Louis Thurstone → seven primary mental abilities (verbal, numerical, spatial, memory, reasoning, word fluency, perceptual speed), no single g.
  • Raymond B. Cattell → fluid intelligence (reasoning with novel problems, declines with age) vs crystallized intelligence (learned knowledge, holds up).
  • Howard Gardner → multiple intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic).
  • Robert Sternberg → triarchic theory: analytical, creative, practical.
  • J.P. Guilford → Structure of Intellect: up to 150+ abilities from operations × contents × products; source of divergent thinking.

7. Sensation & Psychophysics

Everything you know about the world arrives first as raw energy hitting a receptor. Sensation is the pickup of that physical energy (light, sound, pressure, chemicals) by specialised receptor cells; perception is the brain's organisation and interpretation of those signals into meaningful objects and events. Sensation asks "is something there?"; perception asks "what is it?".

From scratch: a receptor cannot pass physical energy directly into the nervous system. It must convert light or sound or pressure into nerve impulses (action potentials). That conversion is called transduction, and it is the single most important idea in sensation: every sense is a transducer.

7.1 Thresholds

Not every stimulus is strong enough to notice. Psychophysics (founded by Gustav Fechner) measures the relationship between physical stimuli and the sensations they produce.

  • Absolute threshold: the smallest amount of stimulus energy detectable 50% of the time (e.g. a candle flame seen on a dark clear night from about 30 miles).
  • Difference threshold (just-noticeable-difference, JND): the smallest change in a stimulus that can be detected 50% of the time. Also called the difference limen.
  • Subliminal stimulus: one below the absolute threshold, not consciously detected.

7.2 The three classic laws

LawTheoristCore statement
Weber's lawErnst WeberThe JND is a constant proportion of the original stimulus, not a fixed amount. A heavier weight needs a bigger change to feel different.
Fechner's lawGustav FechnerSensation grows as the logarithm of stimulus intensity (built on Weber's constant). Big physical increases yield smaller sensory increases.
Stevens' power lawStanley Smith StevensSensation is a power function of intensity; the exponent varies by sense (electric shock grows faster than brightness). Often considered more accurate than Fechner's.
Who → what: Weber → constant fraction (JND/stimulus = k); Fechner → logarithmic sensation; Stevens → power-function sensation.

Weber fraction for weight is about 1/50: you can just detect a 2g change on a 100g load, but need a 20g change on a 1000g load.

7.3 Signal detection theory

Detection is not just about the stimulus; it also depends on the observer's expectations, motivation and the background "noise". Signal detection theory (SDT) replaces the fixed threshold with two independent factors: sensitivity (how well signal is told from noise) and response criterion / bias (how willing the person is to say "yes"). Outcomes fall into hits, misses, false alarms and correct rejections.

RML tilt: expect a one-liner match item: "detection depends on both stimulus intensity and decision criterion" → signal detection theory. Know that SDT explains why a tired radiologist reports more false alarms.

7.4 Sensory adaptation and the senses

Sensory adaptation is the reduced sensitivity to an unchanging stimulus (you stop smelling a room, stop feeling your clothes). It keeps us tuned to change.

  • Vision: rods and cones in the retina transduce light (photoreception).
  • Audition: hair cells on the basilar membrane (cochlea) transduce sound vibration.
  • Gustation and olfaction: chemoreceptors on the tongue and in the nose transduce dissolved and airborne chemicals.
  • Somatosensation: mechanoreceptors, thermoreceptors and nociceptors handle touch, temperature and pain; the vestibular and kinaesthetic senses handle balance and body position.

8. Thinking, Cognition & Problem Solving

8.1 Concepts and categories

A concept is a mental grouping of similar objects, events or ideas; it lets us handle the world without treating every item as brand new. We organise concepts around a prototype: the best, most typical example of a category (a robin is a more prototypical "bird" than a penguin). Faster categorisation of items close to the prototype is a classic exam fact (Eleanor Rosch's work).

8.2 Reasoning

  • Deductive reasoning: from general premises to a specific, logically certain conclusion (top-down). If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.
  • Inductive reasoning: from specific observations to a probable general rule (bottom-up). Conclusions are likely, not guaranteed.

8.3 Problem-solving strategies

Two classic blocks to problem solving:

  • Mental set: the tendency to keep using a strategy that worked before, even when a simpler one exists.
  • Functional fixedness: inability to see a novel use for an object (Karl Duncker's candle problem: not seeing the matchbox as a shelf).
From scratch: the Gestalt school treated problem solving as restructuring the whole problem, so insight was a perceptual reorganisation, not trial and error. This is why insight and functional fixedness sit together in the syllabus.

8.4 Judgement and decision-making heuristics

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that everyday judgement leans on heuristics that produce predictable errors (biases).

  • Availability heuristic: judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind (plane crashes feel common because they are vivid).
  • Representativeness heuristic: judging by how well something matches a stereotype, ignoring base rates.
  • Anchoring and adjustment: starting from an initial value and adjusting insufficiently.
  • Framing effect: the same choice pulls different decisions depending on wording ("90% survive" vs "10% die").
  • Confirmation bias: seeking evidence that fits an existing belief and ignoring the rest.
Trap: availability = ease of recall; representativeness = resemblance to a stereotype. Exams swap the two. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel in Economics for this work (Tversky had died).

8.5 Creativity

J.P. Guilford distinguished convergent thinking (narrowing to one correct answer, what IQ tests measure) from divergent thinking (generating many possible answers, the engine of creativity). Divergent thinking is judged on fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration.

9. Language & Psycholinguistics

Language is a rule-governed system for combining symbols to communicate meaning. Psycholinguists break it into levels:

  • Phonology: the sound system; phonemes are the smallest distinctive sound units.
  • Morphology: word structure; morphemes are the smallest meaning units.
  • Syntax: rules for combining words into grammatical sentences.
  • Semantics: meaning of words and sentences.
  • Pragmatics: use of language in social context (tone, turn-taking, implied meaning).

9.1 How is language acquired? The great debate

Who → what: Noam Chomsky → nativist / innate view; B.F. Skinner → learning / behaviourist view.

B.F. Skinner (in Verbal Behavior, 1957) argued that language is learned like any behaviour, through imitation, reinforcement and shaping. Noam Chomsky attacked this, arguing that children produce novel sentences they were never taught and learn grammar too fast for reinforcement alone. He proposed:

  • Language acquisition device (LAD): an innate brain mechanism for grasping grammar.
  • Universal grammar: a common grammatical blueprint underlying all human languages.
  • Transformational grammar: deep structure (meaning) transformed into surface structure (spoken form).

9.2 Linguistic relativity

Benjamin Lee Whorf and Edward Sapir proposed the linguistic relativity hypothesis (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis): the language we speak shapes how we think and perceive the world. The strong form (language determines thought) is largely rejected; the weak form (language influences thought) has some support.

9.3 Stages of language acquisition

  1. Cooing (around 2 months): vowel-like sounds.
  2. Babbling (around 4 to 6 months): consonant-vowel repetitions, initially universal.
  3. One-word / holophrastic (around 12 months): single words carry whole meanings.
  4. Two-word / telegraphic (around 18 to 24 months): "want milk", grammar words dropped.
  5. Sentences (from about 2.5 years): rapid grammar growth, including overregularisation ("goed").

10. Consciousness & Cognitive Psychology as a Field

Consciousness is our awareness of ourselves and our environment. Psychologists describe varied states of consciousness: normal waking awareness, sleep and dreaming, hypnosis, meditation, and drug-altered states.

10.1 The cognitive revolution

From roughly the 1950s, psychology turned away from strict behaviourism and back to the study of internal mental processes. This cognitive revolution borrowed the computer as a metaphor and gave rise to the information-processing model: the mind takes in information (input), encodes and transforms it (processing / storage), and produces behaviour (output), much like a computer.

10.2 Cognitive psychology as a school

Who → what: Ulric Neisser → "father of cognitive psychology"; his 1967 book Cognitive Psychology named and organised the field.

Cognitive psychology studies how people perceive, attend, remember, think, reason and use language, treating these as information-processing operations that can be modelled and tested experimentally.

Key. Sensation transduces energy; perception interprets it. Weber = constant fraction, Fechner = log, Stevens = power. Kahneman and Tversky = heuristics and biases. Chomsky (innate LAD, universal grammar) vs Skinner (learned verbal behaviour). Neisser = father of cognitive psychology; the information-processing model is its master metaphor.
Recap: This block bridged the raw senses (psychophysics, thresholds, three laws, signal detection) to higher cognition (concepts and prototypes, reasoning, problem-solving blocks, judgement heuristics, creativity), then to language (five levels, the Chomsky-Skinner debate, Sapir-Whorf, acquisition stages) and finally to consciousness and the cognitive revolution that made cognitive psychology a school.

3 Biopsychology & Physiological Psychology

From scratch: Biopsychology asks how the brain and body produce behaviour and mental life. Every thought, mood, and symptom you will study in clinical work has a physical substrate: cells that fire, chemicals that cross gaps, and structures that specialise. This section builds that substrate from the single cell upward.

3.1 The Neuron & the Synapse

The neuron is the basic signalling cell of the nervous system. Santiago Ramón y Cajal established the neuron doctrine: the nervous system is made of discrete individual cells (not a continuous net), for which he shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with Camillo Golgi.

Parts of a neuron. Dendrites are branching fibres that receive incoming signals. The soma (cell body) contains the nucleus and integrates inputs. The axon carries the signal away toward other cells. The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer (made by Schwann cells in the periphery, oligodendrocytes centrally) with gaps called nodes of Ranvier; it speeds conduction via saltatory conduction. The axon terminals (terminal buttons) release chemical messengers.

Resting potential. At rest the inside of the axon is about −70 mV relative to outside, kept negative by the sodium-potassium pump and selective permeability. This is polarisation.

Action potential. When input pushes the membrane past its threshold (about −55 mV), voltage-gated sodium (Na+) channels open and Na+ floods in: depolarisation. Then K+ exits, causing repolarisation and a brief hyperpolarisation. Key properties: it is all-or-none (a spike either fires fully or not at all, so intensity is coded by rate, not size); during the absolute refractory period no new spike can fire, and during the relative refractory period only a stronger-than-usual stimulus can.

Synaptic transmission. The spike reaches the terminal, triggering calcium influx, which makes vesicles fuse and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. The transmitter binds receptors on the next (postsynaptic) neuron. It is then cleared by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion. An EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential) depolarises and makes firing more likely; an IPSP (inhibitory postsynaptic potential) hyperpolarises and makes it less likely. The neuron sums all EPSPs and IPSPs (spatial and temporal summation).

Key. Resting −70 mV → threshold −55 mV → Na+ in (depolarise) → K+ out (repolarise). All-or-none; strength is coded by frequency.

3.3 Brain Anatomy

Hindbrain. The medulla controls vital autonomic functions (heartbeat, breathing). The pons bridges the halves and relays signals, and helps regulate sleep. The cerebellum governs balance, coordination, and motor timing.

Midbrain. Contains relay centres for vision and hearing and dopamine pathways; with parts of the hindbrain it forms the reticular activating system for arousal and attention.

Forebrain. The largest region, housing the thalamus, hypothalamus, limbic system, and the cerebral cortex. The cortex is divided into four lobes:

  • Frontal lobe, voluntary movement, planning, and personality. The prefrontal cortex handles executive function, judgment, and impulse control. Broca's area (usually left frontal) controls speech production; damage causes non-fluent Broca's aphasia.
  • Parietal lobe, the somatosensory cortex processes touch, temperature, and body position.
  • Temporal lobe, hearing and language comprehension. Wernicke's area (usually left) handles language understanding; damage gives fluent-but-meaningless Wernicke's aphasia. The hippocampus (deep temporal) forms new long-term memories.
  • Occipital lobe, visual processing.

Limbic system, the emotion-and-memory network: the amygdala (fear, aggression, emotional salience), the hippocampus (memory formation), the hypothalamus (drives, hormones, homeostasis), and the thalamus (the sensory relay station, routing all senses except smell to the cortex).

Lateralization. The left hemisphere is typically dominant for language, logic, and analysis; the right hemisphere for spatial ability, faces, and holistic processing. The hemispheres communicate through the corpus callosum (studied via Roger Sperry's split-brain work).

RML tilt: The Phineas Gage case (1848) is a near-certain factual item. A railroad tamping iron destroyed his frontal lobe; his intellect survived but his personality and impulse control changed drastically ("no longer Gage"). It became early evidence that the frontal lobe governs personality and social judgment.

3.4 Neuroendocrine System

Hormones are chemical messengers released by glands into the blood. The pituitary ("master gland") is directed by the hypothalamus; the adrenal glands release stress hormones; the thyroid sets metabolism; the pancreas regulates blood sugar; the gonads produce sex hormones.

The HPA axis (HPA = hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) is the core stress system. Under stress the hypothalamus releases CRH → the pituitary releases ACTH → the adrenal cortex releases cortisol. Cortisol mobilises energy and suppresses non-essential functions. It also gives negative feedback to shut the axis off. Chronic HPA activation and high cortisol are linked to depression, anxiety, and PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder).

3.5 Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) controls involuntary organs and has two opposing branches.

3.7 Psychopharmacology Basics

  • Antipsychotics, dopamine antagonists (block D2 receptors), reducing positive symptoms of schizophrenia. Typicals like haloperidol; atypicals like risperidone (also block serotonin).
  • Antidepressants, SSRIs (SSRI = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor) block serotonin reuptake to raise its level (fluoxetine); TCAs (tricyclic antidepressants) raise serotonin and norepinephrine but have more side effects; MAOIs (monoamine oxidase inhibitors) block the enzyme that breaks these transmitters down (need dietary tyramine caution).
  • Anxiolytics, benzodiazepines enhance GABA (the main inhibitory transmitter), calming the nervous system (diazepam, lorazepam).
  • Mood stabilisers, lithium is first-line for bipolar disorder, needing blood-level monitoring for a narrow therapeutic window.

Recap: Neurons fire all-or-none action potentials (−70→−55 mV, Na+ in) and talk chemically across synapses via EPSPs/IPSPs. Cajal gave the neuron doctrine. Key transmitters: dopamine (schizophrenia positive symptoms), serotonin/norepinephrine (mood), GABA (inhibitory/anxiety), glutamate (excitatory), ACh (memory), endorphins (pain). Brain builds from hindbrain (medulla, pons, cerebellum) to forebrain and four lobes (frontal/Broca's, parietal/somatosensory, temporal/Wernicke's + hippocampus, occipital); limbic system runs emotion and memory; Gage showed the frontal lobe shapes personality. The HPA axis pumps cortisol under stress; the ANS balances sympathetic fight-or-flight against parasympathetic rest-and-digest. Sleep runs N1–N3 (theta→delta) plus REM, on a circadian clock. Drugs map to transmitters: antipsychotics→dopamine, SSRIs→serotonin, benzodiazepines→GABA, lithium→bipolar.

3.8 Methods of Studying the Brain

How do we know what a brain region does when we cannot open a living person's skull and watch? Physiological psychology answers this by pairing older lesion logic with modern imaging. The methods split into two families: those that measure activity (recording) and those that disrupt or destroy tissue to see what is lost.

From scratch: Two opposite questions drive brain science. "What happens if this part stops working?" is answered by lesions and stimulation. "What lights up while a person does a task?" is answered by EEG, PET and fMRI. Structure questions ("what does it look like?") are answered by CT and MRI.

Lesion and ablation studies

A lesion is damaged tissue; ablation is the deliberate surgical removal or destruction of a region (in animals, or observed in human injury). The reasoning: if removing area X abolishes behaviour Y, area X is implicated in Y. This is the oldest tool (recall Paul Broca's patient "Tan" and speech production). Limits: damage is rarely clean, and loss of a function does not prove the region alone produced it.

Electrical recording and stimulation

  • Electroencephalography (EEG): scalp electrodes record summed electrical activity of large neuron populations. Excellent time resolution (milliseconds), poor spatial resolution (cannot localise deep sources). Reads sleep stages, seizures, wakefulness rhythms (alpha, beta, delta, theta).
  • Event-related potentials (ERP): EEG signals averaged across many repeats of a stimulus, cancelling background noise to reveal the response locked to that event (for example the P300 wave to a surprising target).
  • Single-cell / microelectrode recording: a fine electrode records firing of one neuron. Highest precision, but invasive (mostly animals). Gave us David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel's feature-detector cells in the visual cortex.
  • Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS): a magnetic coil over the scalp induces current that briefly excites or silences a cortical patch, creating a temporary "virtual lesion" in a healthy person. Establishes that a region is causally necessary, not just correlated.

Structural and functional imaging

3.9 Behaviour Genetics

Behaviour genetics asks how much of a trait's variation is inherited. Start with the physical units. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the double-helix molecule coiled into chromosomes (humans have 23 pairs, 46 total). A gene is a segment of DNA coding for a protein. The 23rd pair are the sex chromosomes: XX female, XY male.

  • Genotype: the actual genetic code a person carries.
  • Phenotype: the observable trait that results (genotype plus environment).
  • Dominant allele: expressed even with one copy. Recessive: expressed only with two copies.
  • Heritability: the proportion of variation in a trait, within a population, attributable to genetic differences. It is a group statistic, not a personal percentage.

Twin and adoption studies

Monozygotic (identical) twins share ~100% of genes; dizygotic (fraternal) twins share ~50%. If identical twins resemble each other more than fraternal twins on a trait, genes are implicated. Adoption studies separate nature from nurture: an adopted child resembling biological parents points to genes, resembling adoptive parents points to environment.

Trap: High heritability does not mean "unchangeable." Height is highly heritable yet responds to nutrition. Heritability describes variation in a population, never the fate of one individual.

3.10 Hemispheric Lateralization and the Split Brain

The two cerebral hemispheres are joined by the corpus callosum, a thick band of fibres carrying information between them. Each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body: this is contralateral control (the left hemisphere moves the right hand, and vice versa).

Who → what: Roger Sperry (Nobel Prize, 1981) and his student Michael Gazzaniga studied "split-brain" patients whose corpus callosum was surgically cut to control severe epilepsy. With the bridge severed, the two hemispheres could no longer share information, revealing what each does alone.

Findings: an object shown only to the left visual field (right hemisphere) could be picked out by the left hand but could not be named, because speech is usually a left-hemisphere function. This demonstrated lateralization: functions are not evenly spread across both sides.

3.11 Physiology of Motivation and Biological Rhythms

Hypothalamic control of hunger

The hypothalamus regulates eating through two centres:

  • Lateral hypothalamus (LH): the "start eating" centre. Stimulation induces eating; damage causes an animal to stop eating (aphagia).
  • Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH): the "stop eating" (satiety) centre. Damage causes overeating and obesity (hyperphagia).
Key. LH = Lights on (start). VMH = Very much full (stop). Lesioning them produces the opposite of their normal job.

Thirst and sexual behaviour

The hypothalamus also monitors thirst, triggered by cellular dehydration (osmotic) and by drops in blood volume. Sexual behaviour is driven by hormones: androgens such as testosterone and oestrogens, released under hypothalamic and pituitary control. Motivation here is both hormonal (internal drive) and neural (brain circuits).

Pain: gate-control theory

Who → what: Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall (1965) proposed the gate-control theory: a neural "gate" in the spinal cord can open or close to let pain signals reach the brain. Rubbing an injury or psychological factors can "close the gate," explaining why pain is not a simple one-to-one readout of tissue damage.

Biological rhythms and the circadian clock

Circadian rhythms are roughly 24-hour cycles (sleep, temperature, hormones). The master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus, which reads light through the eyes. The SCN signals the pineal gland to release melatonin in darkness, promoting sleep.

RML tilt: Remember the chain: light → SCN → pineal gland → melatonin. The SCN is the pacemaker; the pineal gland only secretes the hormone.

3.12 Stress Physiology

When a stressor is perceived, the body mounts a coordinated response. Two systems fire. The fast one is the sympathetic nervous system (adrenaline, "fight or flight"). The slower, sustained one is the HPA axis.

The HPA axis

  • Hypothalamus releases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone).
  • CRH signals the Pituitary to release ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone).
  • ACTH signals the Adrenal cortex to release cortisol.

Cortisol mobilises energy and dampens non-essential functions. Chronically high cortisol harms immunity, memory (hippocampus) and mood, linking stress to illness.

General Adaptation Syndrome

Who → what: Hans Selye described the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS), the body's stereotyped physiological response to any prolonged stressor, in three stages.
  • Alarm: initial shock, sympathetic activation, resources mobilised.
  • Resistance: the body adapts and copes; cortisol stays elevated to sustain the effort.
  • Exhaustion: resources deplete, resistance collapses, and illness or breakdown can follow.
Key. GAS order is Alarm → Resistance → Exhaustion. Selye called stress "the non-specific response of the body to any demand."
Recap: Brain methods trade time against space (EEG fast, fMRI/PET localised). Behaviour genetics separates genotype from phenotype using twin and adoption studies, with PKU, Down (trisomy 21) and Huntington's as key conditions. Sperry and Gazzaniga's split-brain work showed lateralization across the corpus callosum. The hypothalamus runs hunger (LH start, VMH stop) and the SCN drives circadian rhythm via pineal melatonin. Stress runs through the HPA axis and cortisol, unfolding as Selye's alarm, resistance and exhaustion.

4 Developmental & Child Psychology

Developmental psychology studies how thinking, emotion, morality, and relationships change across the lifespan. For the RML CET-166, the examiners lean on the classic stage theorists (who moves through what, and in what order) and on the attachment experiments. Learn the names, the sequence, and the one-line "signature" idea for each.

4.1 Jean Piaget, Cognitive Development

From scratch: Jean Piaget argued that a child is not a "small adult" but a little scientist who actively constructs knowledge. Hence his theory is called cognitive constructivism. The child builds mental structures called schemas (organised patterns of knowing).

Two processes drive change. Assimilation = fitting new information into an existing schema (a child calls a cat "doggy" because it has four legs and fur). Accommodation = changing the schema to fit new information (learning that cats and dogs are different categories). The push toward balance between the two is equilibration; a mismatch causes disequilibrium, which motivates learning.

Object permanence is tested by hiding a toy; before ~8 months, "out of sight is out of mind." Egocentrism means the child cannot take another's viewpoint (the three-mountains task). Animism = attributing life to objects ("the sun follows me"). Conservation = understanding quantity stays the same despite a change in appearance (the same water poured into a tall thin glass "looks like more" to a preoperational child).

Trap: Conservation is failed in the preoperational stage and gained in the concrete operational stage. Object permanence belongs to sensorimotor, not preoperational. Do not swap these.

4.2 Lev Vygotsky, Sociocultural Theory

Lev Vygotsky stressed that cognition grows out of social interaction and culture, not solo discovery. His key idea is the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the gap between what a child can do alone and what she can do with help. Learning happens in that zone through scaffolding (temporary, tailored support that is withdrawn as competence grows), provided by a More Knowledgeable Other (MKO), a parent, teacher, or abler peer.

Private speech (children talking aloud to themselves) is, for Vygotsky, a tool for self-guidance that later becomes silent inner speech, a positive sign of thinking, not mere immaturity.

Who → what: Piaget = child as lone scientist, development drives learning, universal stages. Vygotsky = child as social apprentice, learning drives development, culture-dependent, ZPD + scaffolding.

4.4 Sigmund Freud, Psychosexual Stages

Sigmund Freud held that libido (sexual energy) centres on a different erogenous zone at each stage. Being over- or under-gratified causes fixation, energy "stuck" at that stage, shaping adult personality.

  • Oral (0–1): mouth; fixation → dependency, smoking, overeating.
  • Anal (1–3): bowel control; fixation → anal-retentive (orderly, stingy) or anal-expulsive (messy).
  • Phallic (3–6): genitals; the Oedipus complex in boys (with castration anxiety) and the Electra complex in girls; resolved by identifying with the same-sex parent → superego forms.
  • Latency (6–puberty): sexual urges dormant; focus on skills, same-sex friends.
  • Genital (puberty on): mature adult sexuality.
Trap: The phallic stage carries the Oedipus/Electra conflict and forms the superego, not the anal or latency stage.

4.5 Lawrence Kohlberg, Moral Development

Lawrence Kohlberg, using the Heinz dilemma, proposed three levels (six stages) of moral reasoning, judged by the reasoning given, not the choice.

  • Pre-conventional: morality = avoiding punishment (Stage 1) and self-interest/reward (Stage 2). Typical of children.
  • Conventional: morality = social approval, being a "good boy/girl" (Stage 3) and upholding law and order (Stage 4). Most adults.
  • Post-conventional: morality = social contract (Stage 5) and universal ethical principles (Stage 6). Reached by few.
Who → what: Carol Gilligan critiqued Kohlberg for a male-biased "justice" emphasis built on all-male samples, and proposed an ethic of care (morality as responsibility and relationships) alongside his ethic of justice.

4.6 Attachment

From scratch: Attachment is the enduring emotional bond between infant and caregiver, a survival system, not just "spoiling."

John Bowlby founded attachment theory: the infant is biologically primed to seek proximity to one primary figure (monotropy), and early experience builds an internal working model, a mental template of relationships that guides later bonds. Prolonged separation, he warned, causes lasting harm (maternal deprivation).

Mary Ainsworth tested this with the Strange Situation (structured separations and reunions), classifying three patterns: secure (distressed at separation, comforted at reunion), anxious-ambivalent/resistant (very distressed, hard to soothe, clingy-and-angry), and avoidant (little distress, ignores caregiver). Later, Mary Main added a fourth: disorganized (confused, contradictory behaviour, often linked to frightening care).

Harry Harlow's rhesus monkey studies showed infants clung to a soft cloth "mother" over a wire mother that fed them, proving that contact comfort, not food, is the basis of attachment, a direct blow to the behaviourist "cupboard love" view.

RML tilt: A favourite matching item: Bowlby = theory/monotropy, Ainsworth = Strange Situation & the three styles, Main = disorganized, Harlow = monkeys/contact comfort. Keep the person-to-contribution links crisp.

4.7 Diana Baumrind, Parenting Styles

Diana Baumrind crossed two dimensions, demandingness (control) and responsiveness (warmth), to describe parenting.

  • Authoritative: high warmth + high control; firm but reasoning. Best child outcomes.
  • Authoritarian: low warmth + high control; strict, "because I said so."
  • Permissive (indulgent): high warmth + low control; few demands.

Maccoby & Martin added a fourth style: neglectful/uninvolved (low warmth + low control), associated with the poorest outcomes.

Trap: Authoritative (best, balanced) is easily confused with authoritarian (harsh, controlling). The neglectful style was added by Maccoby & Martin, not Baumrind.

4.8 Arnold Gesell, Maturation & Milestones

Arnold Gesell championed maturation: development unfolds in a fixed, biologically pre-programmed sequence, relatively independent of environment. From detailed observation he produced developmental norms (age-based milestones) still echoed in modern screening, sits ~6 months, stands ~9–10 months, walks ~12 months, first words ~12 months, two-word phrases ~24 months. His stance is the classic nature pole against Watson's environmentalism.

4.9 Common Developmental Disorders

  • Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD): persistent deficits in social communication plus restricted, repetitive behaviours; onset in early childhood.
  • Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD): age-inappropriate inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity across settings.
  • Intellectual Disability (ID): significantly below-average intellectual functioning and adaptive deficits, with onset in the developmental period.
Recap: Piaget = 4 cognitive stages (object permanence → conservation), schema/assimilation/accommodation. Vygotsky = ZPD, scaffolding, MKO, private speech (social > solo). Erikson = 8 psychosocial crises with virtues (Trust→Integrity). Freud = 5 psychosexual stages, phallic = Oedipus/Electra, fixation. Kohlberg = pre-/conventional/post-conventional; Gilligan = ethic of care. Attachment: Bowlby (monotropy, internal working model), Ainsworth (Strange Situation: secure/ambivalent/avoidant), Main (disorganized), Harlow (contact comfort). Baumrind parenting (+ neglectful by Maccoby & Martin). Gesell = maturation & norms.

4.10 Nature vs Nurture and Developmental Research Designs

From scratch: Development is the study of how a person changes across the lifespan. Before we can study any theory, we need two foundations: what drives change (biology or experience), and how we actually measure change over time.

Nature vs Nurture (Heredity-Environment Interaction)

The oldest question in the field asks whether behaviour is shaped mainly by nature (genes, heredity, biological maturation) or by nurture (environment, learning, culture, upbringing). The modern answer rejects the either/or framing: nearly every trait emerges from an interaction of both.

  • Range of reaction: genes set a range of possible outcomes; the environment decides where within that range a person lands.
  • Canalization: some traits (for example motor milestones like walking) are so strongly guided by genes that they follow a narrow path despite wide environmental variation.
  • Gene-environment correlation: genes and environments are not independent, for example a musically gifted child is often given music lessons (an environment that matches the gene).
  • Epigenetics: environment can switch genes on or off without altering the DNA sequence itself.

Key. The exam-safe stance is interactionism: heredity and environment work together, not in opposition.

Continuity vs Discontinuity, and Critical vs Sensitive Periods

  • Continuity view: development is gradual and quantitative, like a smooth ramp (learning theorists).
  • Discontinuity view: development moves through distinct qualitative stages, like steps on a staircase (Piaget, Freud, Erikson).
  • Critical period: a rigid window during which a specific input must occur or the outcome is lost forever (Konrad Lorenz imprinting in geese).
  • Sensitive period: a softer, optimal window when the organism is especially receptive, but learning can still happen later (much of human language).

4.12 Temperament: Thomas and Chess

From scratch: Temperament is the biologically based, early-appearing style of emotional and behavioural reactivity, the raw material on which personality is later built.

Who → what: Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess, in the New York Longitudinal Study, identified three temperament types.

  • Easy child (~40%): regular routines, positive mood, adapts readily to new situations.
  • Difficult child (~10%): irregular routines, intense negative reactions, slow to accept change.
  • Slow-to-warm-up child (~15%): mild, low activity, withdraws at first but warms up gradually with gentle exposure.

Key. Goodness of fit is the match between a child's temperament and the demands and parenting style of their environment. Good outcomes depend on fit, not on the temperament type alone.

4.14 Language Development

From scratch: Language unfolds in a predictable universal sequence in the first years, regardless of the specific language being learned.

  • Cooing (~2 months): vowel-like sounds, the first playful vocalisation.
  • Babbling (~6 months): repeated consonant-vowel strings (ba-ba-ba); becomes tuned to the native language.
  • Holophrastic / one-word stage (~12 months): single words carry whole-sentence meaning (milk means "I want milk").
  • Telegraphic speech (~18–24 months): two-word utterances with only content words, function words dropped (want cookie).

Trap: Do not confuse cooing (vowels) with babbling (consonant-vowel repetition). Cooing comes first.

Puberty is the biological onset of sexual maturity, driven by hormones, and includes primary sex characteristics (reproductive organs) and secondary sex characteristics (body hair, voice change, breast development).

4.16 Adulthood and Aging

Who → what: Daniel Levinson, in "The Seasons of a Man's Life", described adult development as alternating stable structure-building periods and transitional periods, including the midlife transition around ages 40 to 45.

Who → what: Robert Havighurst proposed developmental tasks, age-linked challenges (for example choosing a career, marriage, adjusting to retirement) that a person must master at each life stage for healthy development.

Intelligence in Aging

Who → what: Raymond Cattell and John Horn distinguished two kinds of intelligence.

  • Fluid intelligence: reasoning, speed, and novel problem solving; declines with age.
  • Crystallized intelligence: accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, and skills; stays stable or rises with age.

From scratch: Broad theories of aging include the disengagement theory (older adults and society mutually withdraw), the activity theory (staying active supports wellbeing), and the socioemotional selectivity theory (with less time perceived ahead, older adults prioritise emotionally meaningful relationships).

5 Social Psychology

Social psychology is the scientific study of how our thoughts, feelings, and behaviour are shaped by the real, imagined, or implied presence of others. It sits between the individual (personality) and the group (sociology): the same person behaves very differently alone versus in a crowd, and that difference is the subject matter here.

From scratch: Three questions organise the whole field. How do we evaluate the social world (attitudes)? How do we explain it (attribution)? And how does it change us (influence, groups, prejudice, aggression, helping)? Keep those buckets in mind and the classic studies slot in cleanly.

Attitudes

An attitude is a learned, relatively stable evaluation of a person, object, or idea. The classic tri-component (ABC) model breaks it into Affect (feelings), Behaviour (action tendencies), and Cognition (beliefs). "I feel disgust at smoking (A), I refuse cigarettes (B), and I believe it causes cancer (C)."

Attitudes form through classical conditioning (pairing), operant conditioning (reward/punishment), observational learning, and mere exposure (Robert Zajonc: familiarity breeds liking). But attitudes predict behaviour weakly, the attitude-behaviour gap. Richard LaPiere's 1934 study is the classic demonstration: a Chinese couple was served at nearly every establishment they visited, yet those same places later said in writing they would refuse Chinese guests.

Persuasion: the Elaboration Likelihood Model

Richard Petty and John Cacioppo proposed two routes to persuasion. The central route works through careful thinking about argument quality (used when motivation and ability are high) and produces lasting change. The peripheral route relies on surface cues, source attractiveness, length of message, number of arguments, and produces flimsy, temporary change.

Cognitive dissonance

Leon Festinger argued that holding two inconsistent cognitions creates an uncomfortable tension (dissonance) that we reduce by changing an attitude. In the famous Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) study, people paid only $1 to lie that a boring task was fun later rated it as more enjoyable than those paid $20, because the $1 group had no external justification and had to change their attitude to resolve the tension.

Trap: Lower reward produces more attitude change in dissonance (insufficient justification). Bigger incentive gives an external excuse, so no internal change is needed.

Attribution

Attribution is how we infer the causes of behaviour. Fritz Heider, the founder, distinguished internal (dispositional) from external (situational) causes, calling ordinary people "naive scientists."

Who → what: Heider → naive-scientist attribution; Jones & Davis → correspondent inference; Harold Kelley → covariation model.

Edward Jones and Keith Davis's correspondent inference theory asks when an act reflects a stable trait: we infer disposition when behaviour is freely chosen, unexpected (low social desirability), and produces non-common effects. Harold Kelley's covariation model says we weigh three kinds of information: consensus (do others act this way?), consistency (does this person always act this way?), and distinctiveness (only towards this target?). High consensus + high distinctiveness + high consistency → external attribution; low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency → internal.

Three biases recur:

  • Fundamental attribution error (FAE): over-crediting disposition and under-weighting situation when explaining others (Lee Ross's term).
  • Actor-observer bias: we explain our own behaviour situationally but others' dispositionally. "I failed because the exam was unfair; he failed because he is lazy."
  • Self-serving bias: we attribute our successes internally and our failures externally, protecting self-esteem.

Social influence

Conformity is yielding to real or imagined group pressure. Solomon Asch's line-judgement study had participants match a line to one of three comparisons; with confederates giving obviously wrong answers, about 75% conformed at least once, and roughly a third of responses matched the majority. Two motives underlie it: informational influence (others are a source of truth, especially in ambiguity) and normative influence (wanting acceptance).

Obedience is following a direct order from authority. Stanley Milgram's shock experiments found 65% of ordinary people delivered the maximum 450 volts to a screaming "learner" when instructed by an experimenter, demonstrating the power of situational authority over conscience.

Compliance techniques exploit consistency and contrast: the foot-in-the-door (a small request first makes a later large one likely) and the door-in-the-face (an unreasonable request first makes a modest follow-up seem fair).

Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971) assigned students to be guards or prisoners; guards turned cruel and the study was halted after six days, showing how roles and situations override individual character. Muzafer Sherif earlier used the autokinetic effect (an illusory moving dot) to show groups converge on a shared norm, and his Robbers Cave study created intergroup hostility between boys' camps, then dissolved it through superordinate goals (shared tasks needing cooperation).

Group processes

Norman Triplett (1898) ran what is usually called the first social psychology experiment: cyclists rode faster with others present. This is social facilitation, the presence of others improves performance on easy/well-learned tasks but impairs it on hard ones (Zajonc explained it via heightened arousal boosting the dominant response).

Its mirror image is social loafing: people exert less effort in a group when individual contributions cannot be identified (Max Ringelmann's rope-pulling). Irving Janis coined groupthink, the drive for consensus in cohesive groups that suppresses dissent and produces bad decisions (symptoms: illusion of invulnerability, self-censorship, pressure on dissenters). Group polarization is the tendency for group discussion to push members towards a more extreme version of their initial leaning. Deindividuation is the loss of self-awareness and restraint in crowds and anonymity.

The bystander effect, that people are less likely to help as the number of onlookers grows, was studied by John Darley and Bibb Latané after the 1964 Kitty Genovese case. The mechanism is diffusion of responsibility: with many present, each person assumes someone else will act.

Prejudice

Distinguish three levels: a stereotype is a belief (cognition), prejudice is a negative attitude/feeling (affect), and discrimination is unfair behaviour. Realistic conflict theory traces prejudice to competition over scarce resources (Sherif's Robbers Cave is the evidence). Henri Tajfel and John Turner's social identity theory shows that merely categorising people into groups, even arbitrary "minimal groups," produces in-group favouritism, because we derive self-esteem from group membership. Gordon Allport's contact hypothesis holds that prejudice falls when groups meet under four conditions: equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support.

Aggression

John Dollard and Neal Miller's frustration-aggression hypothesis stated that frustration (a blocked goal) always leads to aggression, later softened by Leonard Berkowitz to "frustration creates readiness for aggression, triggered by cues." Albert Bandura's Bobo doll study showed social learning: children who watched an adult beat an inflatable doll imitated that aggression, proving behaviour is learned by observation without direct reinforcement.

Prosocial behaviour

Prosocial behaviour is any act intended to benefit another; altruism is helping with no expectation of reward. Daniel Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis argues that when we feel genuine empathy for someone, we help for their sake, not merely to relieve our own distress, supporting the existence of true altruism against purely egoistic accounts.

The Self

Social psychology treats the self as both the knower (the "I" who experiences) and the known (the "me" that is thought about). The topics below cover how people build a picture of themselves, evaluate it, and manage it in front of others.

From scratch: A self-concept is the total set of beliefs a person holds about who they are. It is organised into self-schemas: mental frameworks about specific traits (for example "I am athletic" or "I am shy") that guide how quickly and confidently we process self-relevant information. If a trait is central to you, you are said to be schematic on it; if it is irrelevant, you are aschematic.

Self-esteem is the evaluative and emotional part of the self: how positively or negatively a person judges their own worth. It differs from self-concept (the description) by adding a value judgement (the appraisal). Self-awareness is the state of focusing attention on the self as an object; it rises in front of a mirror, a camera, or an audience and tends to make behaviour match personal standards more closely.

Key theorists of the self

Who → what: Daryl Bem → self-perception theory: when internal cues are weak or ambiguous, people infer their own attitudes and feelings by observing their own behaviour, exactly as an outside observer would ("I have eaten three biscuits, so I must like them"). This is the reverse of the usual assumption that attitudes drive behaviour, and it is the main rival explanation to cognitive dissonance.
Who → what: Leon Festinger → social comparison theory: people evaluate their own opinions and abilities by comparing themselves with others, especially when objective yardsticks are missing. Upward comparison (with someone better) can motivate or deflate; downward comparison (with someone worse) can protect self-esteem.
Who → what: Mark Snyder → self-monitoring: a personality dimension describing how much people adjust their behaviour to fit the social situation. High self-monitors read the room and change accordingly; low self-monitors act from inner attitudes regardless of audience.

Self-serving biases and strategies

  • Self-serving bias: the tendency to take credit for successes (internal attribution) but blame failures on external causes. It protects self-esteem.
  • Self-fulfilling prophecy: an initially false expectation that causes the very behaviour that makes it come true. Demonstrated by Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson in the "Pygmalion in the classroom" study, where teachers' raised expectations lifted pupils' actual performance.
  • Self-handicapping: creating an obstacle to one's own success (for example not studying, or drinking before a test) so that failure can be blamed on the obstacle rather than on low ability, while success looks even more impressive.
Trap: Do not confuse self-serving bias (protecting the self after an outcome) with the fundamental attribution error (misjudging other people). Self-serving bias is about you; the attribution error is about them.

Social Cognition & Perception

Social cognition is the study of how people select, store, and use information about other people and social situations. Because we cannot process everything, we rely on schemas (organised knowledge structures) and mental shortcuts.

Impression formation

Who → what: Solomon Asch → classic impression-formation work. He showed a primacy effect: traits presented first weigh more heavily than later ones. He also identified central traits (such as "warm" versus "cold") that reshape the whole impression, versus peripheral traits (such as "polite" versus "blunt") that change it little.

The halo effect is a related bias: one positive quality (often physical attractiveness) leads us to assume other unrelated positive qualities, so good-looking people are rated as kinder or more competent without evidence.

Heuristics in social judgement

Heuristics are quick rules of thumb, described by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, that usually work but can bias judgement.

HeuristicRule of thumbExample error
AvailabilityJudge frequency by how easily examples come to mindOverrating plane-crash risk after news coverage

Stereotypes can be seen as cognitive shortcuts: category-based beliefs that save effort but overgeneralise and feed prejudice. Priming is the process by which a recent or frequent concept becomes temporarily more accessible and shapes how the next person or event is interpreted, often outside awareness.

Attribution, re-framed as social cognition

Two well-known attribution biases are really products of how we process social information:

  • Fundamental attribution error: overestimating personality and underestimating the situation when explaining others' behaviour.
  • Actor-observer bias: attributing our own behaviour to the situation, but others' behaviour to their character, partly because we literally see the situation from our own viewpoint.
RML tilt: Expect a matching-type item pairing each heuristic or bias with its definition, and a single-line question naming the theorist (Asch for central traits, Bem for self-perception, Snyder for self-monitoring). Learn the names, not just the ideas.

Interpersonal Attraction & Close Relationships

Determinants of attraction

  • Proximity and mere exposure: we like those who are physically near, largely through the mere exposure effect (Robert Zajonc): repeated contact with a stimulus increases liking.
  • Physical attractiveness: a powerful first-stage cue, amplified by the halo effect.
  • Similarity: shared attitudes, values, and background predict attraction far more than the folk idea that "opposites attract".
  • Reciprocity: we tend to like those who like us.

Theories of love and bonding

Who → what: Robert Sternberg → the triangular theory of love. Three components combine into different kinds of love: intimacy (closeness, warmth), passion (physical and emotional arousal), and commitment (the decision to maintain the relationship). All three together give consummate love.

A common distinction is between passionate love (intense, arousing, often early in a relationship) and companionate love (steady affection and deep friendship that endures).

Who → what: Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver → extended attachment theory to adults, proposing that adult romantic bonds echo infant attachment. Adults show secure, anxious (ambivalent), or avoidant styles that shape trust and intimacy.

Economic models of relationships

Social exchange theory analyses relationships as a ledger of rewards minus costs, judged against a comparison level (what we expect) and a comparison level for alternatives (what else is available). Equity theory adds that people are most satisfied when the ratio of their own outcomes to inputs feels roughly fair compared with the partner's.

Key. Attraction is driven by proximity, similarity, physical cues, and reciprocity; love is best split into Sternberg's intimacy, passion, and commitment; long relationships run on companionate love, fair exchange, and secure attachment.

Applied Social Psychology (Brief)

Applied social psychology uses these principles to solve real problems in specific settings.

  • Health: attitude-change and social-norm campaigns promote vaccination, exercise, and safe behaviour; perceived social support buffers stress and improves recovery.
  • Environment: feedback, commitment, and descriptive norms ("most guests reuse their towels") are used to cut energy use and encourage conservation.
  • Law: research on eyewitness memory, line-up construction, jury decision-making, and conformity within juries informs fairer legal procedures.
Recap: The self is described by self-concept and self-schemas, valued through self-esteem, and inferred through Bem's self-perception and Festinger's social comparison, then defended by self-serving bias and self-handicapping. Social cognition runs on schemas, Asch's primacy and central traits, the halo effect, and the availability, representativeness, and anchoring heuristics. Attraction rests on proximity, similarity, and reciprocity; Sternberg's triangle and Hazan and Shaver's adult attachment explain love; and applied social psychology carries all of this into health, environment, and law.

6 Personality

Personality is the enduring, organised pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviours that makes a person distinctive and relatively consistent across time and situations. The word comes from the Latin persona (the mask worn by an actor). For the RML CET-166 paper the safest way to master this domain is by theorist → construct: they rarely ask "what is personality?" and very often ask "who proposed the inferiority complex?" This section is organised into four families plus a short assessment link.

From scratch: Two research styles run through all trait work. The nomothetic approach (from Greek nomos, law) seeks universal dimensions that apply to everyone; the idiographic approach studies the unique individual. Trait psychologists differ mainly in how many universal dimensions they claim exist.

1. Trait & Type Theories

Who → what: Gordon Allport → the first modern trait theory. He distinguished cardinal traits (a single dominating disposition that rules a life, e.g. a "Scrooge-like" greed, rare), central traits (the 5–10 core descriptors we would list in a reference letter) and secondary traits (situational preferences, e.g. anxiety only in exams). Allport stressed the idiographic study of the individual and coined the term functional autonomy (a motive can outlive its original cause).
Who → what: Raymond B. Cattell → used factor analysis to reduce Allport's list to 16 source traits. He separated surface traits (clusters of behaviour we observe directly) from source traits (the deeper underlying factors that cause them). His test is the 16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire).
Who → what: Hans Eysenck → argued personality reduces to just a few biologically-based dimensions. His PEN model has three: Psychoticism, Extraversion and Neuroticism (originally only E and N, plus Psychoticism added later). He gave each a biological basis: Extraversion reflects low cortical arousal (extraverts seek stimulation to reach an optimum), and Neuroticism reflects a labile autonomic/limbic system. His measures include the EPQ (Eysenck Personality Questionnaire).
Who → what: Paul Costa & Robert McCrae → the Big Five / Five-Factor Model (FFM), remembered by the acronym OCEAN: Openness to experience, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness and Neuroticism. Their test is the NEO-PI-R (Revised NEO Personality Inventory).
Who → what: William Sheldon → somatotypes, a constitutional (body-type) theory linking physique to temperament: the round endomorph (viscerotonic, sociable, food-loving), the muscular mesomorph (somatotonic, assertive, energetic) and the thin ectomorph (cerebrotonic, restrained, introverted).
Key. Count the dimensions: Cattell = 16, Eysenck = 3 (PEN), Costa & McCrae = 5 (OCEAN). Only Neuroticism and Extraversion appear in all the modern models.
Trap: The 16PF belongs to Cattell, not Eysenck; the NEO-PI belongs to Costa & McCrae; the EPQ belongs to Eysenck. Examiners love to swap the test onto the wrong theorist.

2. Psychodynamic Theories

From scratch: Psychodynamic theories assume behaviour is driven by unconscious forces and early experience. They begin with Freud, and the "neo-Freudians" are those who trained in or reacted against him and then softened his emphasis on sexuality.
Who → what: Sigmund Freud → the founder of psychoanalysis. His structural model has three parts: the id (unconscious, pleasure principle, primary process), the ego (reality principle, mediator), and the superego (conscience and ego-ideal, morality). His topographical model divides the mind into conscious, preconscious (accessible) and unconscious (repressed). The energy driving all this is the libido (sexual/life energy). When the ego is threatened it deploys defence mechanisms such as repression, projection, denial, displacement, reaction formation, rationalisation and sublimation.

3. Humanistic Theories

From scratch: The "third force" (after psychoanalysis and behaviourism) rejected determinism and stressed free will, growth and the healthy person's potential.
Who → what: Carl Rogers → person-centred theory. The self-concept is our organised view of ourselves; wellbeing depends on congruence between the real self and the ideal self. Growth requires unconditional positive regard (acceptance without conditions of worth); the goal is the fully functioning person.
Who → what: Abraham Maslow → the hierarchy of needs, usually drawn as a pyramid: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualization at the top. Lower deficiency needs must be reasonably met before the higher growth need to fulfil one's potential emerges.
Trap: Self-actualization as the peak of the need hierarchy is Maslow; the fully functioning person is Rogers. Both are humanists, but the labelled endpoints differ.

4. Social-Cognitive & Behavioural Theories

Who → what: Albert Bandura → social-cognitive theory. His central concept is reciprocal determinism: person, behaviour and environment continuously influence one another. He is also known for self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to succeed at a task) and observational learning (the Bobo doll studies).
Who → what: Julian Rotter → locus of control (internal: outcomes depend on my own effort; external: outcomes depend on luck, fate or powerful others) and expectancy (behaviour potential = expectancy × reinforcement value).
Who → what: Walter Mischel → launched the person–situation debate (in Personality and Assessment, arguing behaviour is less cross-situationally consistent than traits imply), and ran the marshmallow test on delay of gratification in children.
Who → what: George Kelly → personal construct theory. People are "scientists" who anticipate events through bipolar personal constructs (e.g. kind–cruel); assessed with the Rep Test (Role Construct Repertory Test).
Key. Self-efficacy = Bandura; locus of control = Rotter; marshmallow / person-situation debate = Mischel; personal constructs = Kelly.

5. Assessment Link (brief)

Self-report inventories present structured items scored against norms: the MMPI (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory) for clinical psychopathology, the 16PF (Cattell) and the NEO-PI-R (Costa & McCrae). Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli onto which the person "projects" inner content: the Rorschach inkblot test (Hermann Rorschach) and the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) (Henry Murray & Christiana Morgan).

Trap: Self-report inventories are objective/structured; the Rorschach and TAT are projective. Do not label the MMPI "projective." Full scoring and validity detail sits in the Assessment section.
RML tilt: Expect one-mark "match the theorist to the construct" items far more than essay-style definitions. Drill the acronyms and their owners: PEN → Eysenck, OCEAN/FFM → Costa & McCrae, 16PF → Cattell. Recurrent RML-style hooks: id/ego/superego and defence mechanisms (Freud), collective unconscious (Jung), inferiority complex & birth order (Adler), womb envy (Horney), unconditional positive regard (Rogers), hierarchy of needs (Maslow), self-efficacy (Bandura) and locus of control (Rotter).
Recap: Four families. Trait/type: Allport (cardinal/central/secondary, idiographic), Cattell (16 source traits, 16PF), Eysenck (PEN, biological arousal), Costa & McCrae (Big Five/OCEAN, NEO-PI), Sheldon (endo/meso/ecto somatotypes). Psychodynamic: Freud (id/ego/superego, conscious–preconscious–unconscious, libido, defences), then Jung (collective unconscious, archetypes), Adler (inferiority, birth order), Horney (basic anxiety, womb envy), Erikson (psychosocial), Sullivan (interpersonal), Fromm (freedom vs security). Humanistic: Rogers (real vs ideal self, unconditional positive regard, fully functioning person) and Maslow (hierarchy of needs, self-actualization). Social-cognitive/behavioural: Bandura (reciprocal determinism, self-efficacy), Rotter (locus of control, expectancy), Mischel (person–situation debate, marshmallow test), Kelly (personal constructs). Assessment: self-report (MMPI, 16PF, NEO-PI) vs projective (Rorschach, TAT).

6. Biological & Genetic Approaches

Where trait theories describe personality and psychodynamic theories root it in early conflict, biological approaches ask a blunter question: what in the nervous system and the genome makes one person reliably calmer, bolder, or more anxious than another? The answer is that stable individual differences have measurable physiological and inherited substrates.

From scratch: A "biological substrate" simply means a bodily mechanism (a brain circuit, a chemical, an inherited tendency) that a psychological trait sits on top of. If a trait is biological, it should appear early, be fairly stable, and run in families.

Hans Eysenck's PEN model and cortical arousal

Hans Eysenck argued that personality reduces to a small number of biologically based dimensions. His mature model, PEN, has three: Psychoticism (tough-minded, aggressive, cold), Extraversion (sociable, stimulation-seeking), and Neuroticism (emotionally unstable, anxious). Each is a continuum on which everyone falls somewhere.

His signature claim was about the biology of extraversion. The ascending reticular activating system (ARAS), a network in the brainstem, regulates cortical arousal (how "switched on" the cortex is). Eysenck proposed that introverts have chronically higher baseline cortical arousal, so they are already near their comfortable ceiling and avoid extra stimulation. Extraverts have lower baseline arousal, so they seek stimulation (company, noise, risk) to top themselves up. Neuroticism he tied to the limbic system and its threshold for emotional reactivity.

Jeffrey Gray's Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (BIS/BAS)

Jeffrey Gray, a student of Eysenck, proposed that arousal is the wrong axis. He described two brain systems sensitive to reward and punishment. The Behavioural Activation System (BAS) responds to signals of reward and drives approach, impulsivity, and positive affect. The Behavioural Inhibition System (BIS) responds to signals of punishment, novelty, and non-reward, producing anxiety, caution, and stopping. (Gray later added a Fight-Flight-Freeze System for outright threat.)

Gray rotated Eysenck's axes: high BAS activity underlies impulsivity (roughly extraversion plus neuroticism) and high BIS activity underlies anxiety (roughly introversion plus neuroticism). Crucially, Gray's theory is about sensitivity to reinforcement, not general arousal, which is why it is called Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory.

Who → what: Eysenck → PEN, cortical arousal, ARAS. Gray → BIS/BAS reinforcement sensitivity. Friedman & Rosenman → Type A/B behaviour pattern.

Behaviour genetics and heritability

Behaviour genetics uses twin and adoption studies to partition trait variance into genetic and environmental sources. Heritability is the proportion of variation in a trait, within a population, attributable to genetic differences. For the Big Five and Eysenck's dimensions, heritability estimates typically fall around 0.40 to 0.60, meaning roughly half of the variation is genetic.

Trap: Heritability describes variation across a population, not how much of one person's personality is "genetic." A heritability of 0.5 does not mean half of your extraversion came from your genes.

A robust and counter-intuitive finding is that the shared environment (growing up in the same home) contributes little to adult personality, while the non-shared environment (unique experiences, peers, chance) matters much more. Siblings resemble each other mainly through shared genes, not shared upbringing.

Temperament as the biological substrate

Temperament is the set of early-appearing, biologically based individual differences in emotional reactivity and self-regulation, visible in infancy before personality proper forms. Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess described three infant patterns, easy, difficult, and slow-to-warm-up, and stressed goodness of fit between a child's temperament and the caregiving environment. Temperament is best seen as the raw biological material that experience later shapes into personality.

Type A and Type B behaviour pattern

Cardiologists Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman described a behaviour pattern, not a trait dimension, from clinical observation. Type A people are competitive, time-urgent, hard-driving, and easily provoked to hostility. Type B people are relaxed, patient, and less hostile. Their long-term data linked Type A to higher coronary heart disease risk; later work narrowed the toxic ingredient to hostility and anger rather than ambition or busyness as such.

RML tilt: Expect one-liners pairing a theorist to a construct: Eysenck-ARAS, Gray-BIS/BAS, Friedman/Rosenman-Type A. Also know that Type A's cardiac danger is specifically hostility.

7. Cognitive Approaches

Cognitive approaches locate personality in how a person thinks: the categories they use to interpret events, their expectancies about outcomes, and the mental representations that guide behaviour. Personality is a knowledge system, not a bundle of drives or reinforced habits.

George Kelly's Personal Construct Theory

George Kelly saw every person as a "scientist" who forms hypotheses about the world and tests them against experience. His core idea is constructive alternativism: reality can always be construed (interpreted) in more than one way, so people are not prisoners of the facts but of their own constructs. A personal construct is a bipolar dimension of judgment (for example kind versus cruel) that a person uses to make sense of people and events. The system of constructs a person carries is their personality.

Kelly built the Repertory Grid (Rep Test) to elicit these constructs: the person compares triads of significant people (mother, boss, self) and states how two are alike and different from the third, revealing the personal dimensions they habitually use.

Julian Rotter: locus of control and expectancy

Julian Rotter, a social-learning theorist, argued that behaviour depends on expectancy (the belief that an action will lead to a given outcome) times reinforcement value (how much that outcome is wanted). His famous individual-difference variable is locus of control: whether a person generally believes outcomes are controlled by their own actions (internal locus) or by luck, fate, and powerful others (external locus). Internals tend to take initiative and cope actively; externals are more prone to helplessness.

Walter Mischel: the person-situation debate

Walter Mischel lit the fuse of a decades-long debate. In 1968 he pointed out that trait scores predict behaviour across situations only modestly (correlations often around 0.30), the so-called "personality coefficient." He argued that behaviour is heavily situation-dependent, challenging the assumption of broad, stable traits.

This produced the consistency paradox: people intuitively feel that personality is consistent, yet measured cross-situational consistency is low. Mischel's resolution was that consistency is real but lives in if-then situation-behaviour signatures (Priya is aggressive if criticised by a peer, but not if criticised by a boss). Aggregated across situations, behaviour is stable; situation by situation, it varies meaningfully.

He and Yuichi Shoda formalised this as the Cognitive-Affective Personality System (CAPS): personality is a network of cognitive-affective units (encodings, expectancies, goals, competencies, self-regulatory plans) that different situations activate differently, generating the stable if-then patterns.

Key. Mischel did not deny personality; he relocated consistency from "the same behaviour everywhere" to "the same distinctive if-then pattern across situations."

8. Indian Approaches

Indian psychology frames personality not as fixed traits but as a dynamic balance of underlying qualities, and it treats the self as layered rather than singular. These models are constitutional and spiritual, aimed at self-transformation, where Western trait models are descriptive and aimed at prediction.

The Triguna theory

From Samkhya philosophy and the Bhagavad Gita, all of nature (prakriti) is woven from three gunas (qualities) present in everyone in varying proportion: Sattva (purity, clarity, calm, balance), Rajas (activity, passion, restlessness, ambition), and Tamas (inertia, dullness, ignorance, lethargy). Personality reflects the shifting dominance among the three; the ideal is to cultivate sattva. Because the balance can change through diet, conduct, and practice, the triguna model is dynamic, unlike a fixed Western trait score.

The Panchakosha (five sheaths) model

From the Taittiriya Upanishad, the self is described as five concentric koshas (sheaths) wrapping the innermost being: Annamaya (the physical/food sheath), Pranamaya (the vital-energy/breath sheath), Manomaya (the mental-emotional sheath), Vijnanamaya (the intellect/discernment sheath), and Anandamaya (the bliss sheath). Personality and wellbeing arise from the harmony of these layers, with the true self lying beyond identification with any single sheath.

The Ayurvedic tridosha (constitutional types)

Ayurveda classifies constitution (prakriti) by three doshas: Vata (air/space: quick, changeable, anxious), Pitta (fire: sharp, driven, irritable), and Kapha (earth/water: steady, calm, slow, attached). A person's dominant dosha describes a body-mind type, an early trait-like typology of temperament grounded in physiology.

Trap: Do not confuse the three gunas (sattva/rajas/tamas, mental qualities) with the three doshas (vata/pitta/kapha, bodily constitution). Different systems, different triads.

9. Culture, and the Nomothetic vs Idiographic Debate

Culture and personality

The culture-and-personality tradition holds that a society shapes typical personality through childrearing and shared meanings. A central contrast is between individualist cultures (self as independent, autonomous, defined by inner traits) and collectivist cultures (self as interdependent, defined by relationships and roles). Indian selfhood is often described as relational and contextual, which is one reason Western trait inventories can miss culturally central qualities.

Nomothetic vs idiographic

Gordon Allport drew this key distinction. The nomothetic approach seeks general laws and common dimensions on which everyone can be compared (the Big Five, Eysenck's PEN, most questionnaires). The idiographic approach studies the unique individual in depth, capturing the pattern that makes this one person unlike anyone else (case studies, personal documents, Kelly's rep grid).

Person-situation interactionism as the integrating idea

The debate Mischel opened resolved not into "traits win" or "situations win" but into interactionism: behaviour is a joint product of the person (traits, constructs, expectancies) and the situation, and the two continuously influence each other. People also select and shape the situations they enter, so person and situation are not independent. This interactionist stance now integrates the biological, cognitive, cultural, and trait approaches into one framework: stable dispositions expressed through, and modified by, the situations a person meets and makes.

Recap: Biology supplies the substrate (Eysenck's arousal, Gray's BIS/BAS, heritable temperament, Type A hostility). Cognition supplies the interpreting mind (Kelly's constructs, Rotter's locus of control, Mischel's if-then CAPS). Indian models supply a dynamic, layered, constitutional self (triguna, panchakosha, tridosha). Nomothetic versus idiographic frames the method, and person-situation interactionism ties it all together.

7 Abnormal Psychology (ICD-deep)

From scratch: Abnormal psychology studies patterns of thought, feeling and behaviour that are dysfunctional and distressing. There is no single fact that makes something "abnormal"; instead clinicians triangulate several markers, then match the picture to a named category in a classification system. For the RML paper you must know two systems (WHO's ICD and APA's DSM), the descriptive vocabulary used to describe symptoms before you name a disorder, and the theories of why disorders arise.

7.1 Defining abnormality, the 4 Ds

The most examinable framework is the Four Ds:

  • Deviance, behaviour that departs from social/cultural norms. A person who stops speaking to avoid "contaminating" others with their thoughts.
  • Distress, subjective suffering (though not universal: a person in a manic episode may feel euphoric).
  • Dysfunction, impaired ability to carry out daily roles (work, self-care, relationships).
  • Danger, risk to self or others (suicidality, aggression). Least common, most over-weighted by the public.

Competing models of abnormality: the statistical model (abnormal = statistically rare, i.e. far from the mean; weakness: genius and rare virtue are also "abnormal"); the social-norm / cultural-relativism model (abnormal = norm-violating; weakness: norms shift and can pathologise dissent); and Jerome Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction model (a condition is a disorder only if it is both a failure of an evolved mental function and harmful to the person by social standards).

Key. No single D is sufficient. Diagnosis requires a syndrome (a cluster of signs and symptoms) plus clinically significant distress or impairment, that impairment clause appears in almost every DSM-5-TR = Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th ed., Text Revision (APA) criteria set.

7.2 Classification, DSM-5-TR vs ICD-11

Two authoritative systems exist. DSM-5-TR is APA's, used mainly in the USA and in research. ICD-11 = International Classification of Diseases 11th revision (WHO) is the global standard and the official system in India for morbidity coding and government/hospital reporting, which is why RML leans ICD.

7.3 Descriptive psychopathology, signs & symptoms (Fish)

From scratch: Before naming a disorder, clinicians describe the phenomena. This descriptive tradition (Karl Jaspers, Frank Fish) is what RML fishes for. A symptom is reported by the patient; a sign is observed.

Disorders of thought

Split into form (how thoughts connect) and content (what they are about).

  • Content, delusions (fixed false beliefs, not culturally sanctioned, held with certainty): persecutory, grandiose, delusion of reference, nihilistic, somatic/hypochondriacal, delusion of control (passivity), delusion of jealousy, erotomania. A primary (autochthonous) delusion arises fully formed out of nowhere.
  • Form, formal thought disorder: loosening of associations, tangentiality, circumstantiality, flight of ideas (mania), perseveration, neologisms, word salad, and Knight's move thinking (illogical jumps between unrelated ideas, classic in schizophrenia).
  • Thought alienation / passivity: thought insertion, thought withdrawal, thought broadcasting.
Key. Kurt Schneider's first-rank symptoms of schizophrenia: (1) audible thoughts / thought echo, (2) voices arguing or discussing in the third person, (3) voices commenting on one's actions (running commentary), (4) somatic passivity, (5) thought insertion, (6) thought withdrawal, (7) thought broadcasting, (8) made feelings, (9) made impulses, (10) made volitional acts, and (11) delusional perception. Attributed to Kurt Schneider.
RML tilt: First-rank symptoms are highly suggestive but not pathognomonic (they occur in mania/organic states too), and both DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 have demoted them, they no longer carry extra diagnostic weight. RML still asks who described them: Kurt Schneider.

Disorders of perception

  • Illusion, misperception of a real external stimulus.
  • Hallucination, perception without a stimulus, experienced as real and in external space. Types: auditory, visual, olfactory, gustatory, tactile. Special: hypnagogic (falling asleep), hypnopompic (waking), functional, reflex, extracampine (outside the sensory field).
  • Pseudohallucination, experienced in inner subjective space, insight partly retained.

Mood, affect, speech, memory, consciousness

  • Mood/affect: anhedonia, blunted/flat affect, incongruity of affect, lability, alexithymia, and La belle indifférence (bland unconcern about a serious deficit, classic in conversion/dissociative disorder).
  • Speech: pressure of speech (mania), poverty of speech (alogia), echolalia (echoing others' words), palilalia, mutism.
  • Memory: confabulation (gap-filling with false memory, e.g. Korsakoff), déjà vu, jamais vu.
  • Consciousness/motor: delirium (clouding), stupor, and catatonic signs, waxy flexibility (cerea flexibilitas), echopraxia (mimicking movements), negativism, posturing.

7.4 The disorder families (ICD-11 & DSM-5-TR framing)

Schizophrenia & other primary psychotic disorders

Positive symptoms: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech/behaviour. Negative symptoms: affective flattening, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, asociality (the "5 A's"). Classic subtypes, paranoid, hebephrenic/disorganised, catatonic, simple, residual.

RML tilt: DSM-5-TR abolished the subtypes (paranoid, catatonic etc.) and rates symptom dimensions instead. ICD-11 also dropped them as formal types but keeps a symptom-domain qualifier. If the option lists "paranoid schizophrenia" as a current DSM diagnosis, it's a trap, it is the older ICD-10/descriptive tradition.

Mood (affective) disorders

Major depressive disorder (SIG-E-CAPS: sleep, interest, guilt, energy, concentration, appetite, psychomotor, suicidality), persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia), bipolar I (mania) and bipolar II (hypomania + depression), cyclothymia. ICD-11 requires a manic/hypomanic episode for a bipolar diagnosis, same as DSM.

Anxiety, OCD-related, trauma-related, dissociative, somatic

  • Anxiety: generalized anxiety disorder (free-floating worry), panic disorder (recurrent panic attacks), agoraphobia, specific phobia, social anxiety disorder.
  • Obsessive-compulsive & related: OCD, body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding, trichotillomania, hypochondriasis (ICD-11 places health anxiety here).
  • Trauma & stressor-related: PTSD, acute stress reaction, adjustment disorder, and ICD-11's unique Complex PTSD.
  • Dissociative: dissociative amnesia, dissociative identity disorder, depersonalization/derealization.
  • Somatic: somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety disorder, and conversion disorder (the old hysteria; ICD-11 = dissociative neurological symptom disorder).
RML tilt: Since DSM-5, OCD and PTSD are NOT anxiety disorders, OCD sits in its own Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders chapter and PTSD in Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders. Both ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR make this split. A question asking "which is an anxiety disorder?" listing OCD/PTSD is testing exactly this.
Trap: The word hysteria is obsolete but RML still uses it, it maps to today's conversion / dissociative disorders, not to a single modern label. La belle indifférence is its hallmark sign.

Feeding & eating, personality, neurodevelopmental, substance, neurocognitive

  • Feeding & eating: anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder.
  • Neurodevelopmental: intellectual developmental disorder, autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, specific learning disorder.
  • Substance-use: dependence, withdrawal (ICD-11 uses "disorders due to substance use").
  • Neurocognitive: delirium, major & mild neurocognitive disorder (old "dementia"), e.g. Alzheimer's.

7.5 Etiology models

  • Biological: genetics, neurotransmitters (dopamine in psychosis, serotonin in depression/OCD), brain structure, endocrine.
  • Psychodynamic (Freud): symptoms as expressions of unconscious conflict and defence mechanisms.
  • Cognitive-behavioural: learning (classical/operant), and Beck's cognitive triad / maladaptive schemas.
  • Diathesis-stress model: a predisposing vulnerability (diathesis) + environmental stress together precipitate disorder, the integrative model most examiners endorse.
Recap: Abnormality = the 4 Ds plus clinically significant impairment, judged against statistical, social-norm, and harmful-dysfunction models. Two systems: DSM-5-TR (APA, criteria-based, categorical) and ICD-11 (WHO, prototypic, used in India), RML leans ICD, which uniquely keeps Complex PTSD, dissociative neurological symptom disorder, and a fully dimensional personality model while both systems have dropped schizophrenia subtypes and demoted Kurt Schneider's first-rank symptoms. Master the descriptive phenomenology (form vs content, illusion vs hallucination) and the eponyms DSM dropped (Capgras, Fregoli, Cotard, Ganser, folie à deux, Othello, Ekbom, La belle indifférence, Knight's move, waxy flexibility). Remember OCD and PTSD are no longer anxiety disorders, "hysteria" = conversion/dissociative, and the diathesis-stress model integrates biology with environment.

7.6 History & Evolving Models of Abnormality

From scratch: How a society explains madness decides how it treats the mad. The story of abnormal psychology is a swing between supernatural, biological (somatogenic) and psychological (psychogenic) explanations, and the same three currents still shape today's debates.

Ancient & humoral models

The Greek physician Hippocrates (c. 460–377 BCE) rejected demonic explanations and located mental illness in the body, specifically an imbalance of the four humours: blood (sanguine), yellow bile (choleric), black bile (melancholic) and phlegm (phlegmatic). Excess black bile (Greek melan chole) produced melancholia, the ancestor of our word for depression. Galen later systematised this humoral theory, which dominated Western medicine for over a thousand years.

The demonological / supernatural era

In the medieval and early-modern West, disturbed behaviour was widely read as possession, witchcraft or divine punishment. Treatments included exorcism, and at the extreme, trials and executions codified in texts like the Malleus Maleficarum (1486). Care such as it was fell to asylums like Bethlem ("Bedlam") in London, which became bywords for neglect.

Moral treatment & asylum reform

Who → what: Philippe Pinel (France) → unchained patients at the Bicetre and Salpetriere hospitals and championed traitement moral, humane care with routine, dignity and conversation. William Tuke (England) → founded the York Retreat (1796), a Quaker institution built on kindness rather than restraint. Dorothea Dix (USA) → a schoolteacher whose 19th-century campaigns exposed the abuse of the mentally ill in jails and poorhouses and drove the building of dozens of state hospitals.

Kraepelin & the birth of nosology

Who → what: Emil Kraepelin (Germany) → the father of modern psychiatric classification (nosology). By grouping symptoms that clustered and ran a common course, he split the major psychoses into dementia praecox (later renamed schizophrenia by Eugen Bleuler) and manic-depressive insanity (today's bipolar/mood disorders). His descriptive, course-and-outcome method is the direct ancestor of DSM and ICD.

Biological vs psychological models & deinstitutionalisation

The 20th century saw the somatogenic tradition strengthen (the discovery that general paresis was caused by syphilis proved a "mad" condition could be an infection) alongside the psychogenic tradition of Sigmund Freud and later behaviourism and cognitivism. From the 1950s, the arrival of chlorpromazine and other antipsychotics, plus civil-rights concern over warehousing, drove deinstitutionalisation: the mass discharge of patients from large asylums into (often under-resourced) community care.

Trap: Do not credit Kraepelin with unchaining patients (that is Pinel) or with naming schizophrenia (that is Bleuler). Kraepelin's contribution is classification.

7.7 Culture-Bound Syndromes & Cultural Concepts of Distress

From scratch: A culture-bound syndrome is a recurring pattern of distressing symptoms recognised as an illness within a specific culture, but not neatly mapped onto a standard diagnosis. Modern classifications prefer the umbrella term cultural concepts of distress, which DSM-5-TR breaks into cultural syndromes, cultural idioms of distress (shared ways of talking about suffering) and cultural explanations (perceived causes).

Dhat syndrome (in depth)

Dhat (from Sanskrit dhatu, meaning the elixir that constitutes the body) is the classic South Asian culture-bound syndrome. Young men present with vague somatic and anxious complaints (weakness, palpitations, poor concentration, sexual worry) that they attribute to the passage of semen in urine or through nocturnal emission. It reflects the folk belief that semen is a precious vital essence whose loss depletes the body. Management is chiefly psychoeducation, correcting the belief, and treating any comorbid depression or anxiety.

Possession & dissociative trance

Across India and many cultures, distress may present as spirit possession. ICD-11 lists trance disorder and possession trance disorder under dissociative disorders: a temporary, marked alteration of identity and awareness, experienced as being taken over by a spirit, power or other person, that is unwanted and causes distress or impairment. It is only a disorder when it is involuntary and dysfunctional, not when it is a sanctioned part of religious or ritual practice.

Key. Both ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR keep culture central: DSM-5-TR uses the Cultural Formulation Interview and "cultural concepts of distress"; ICD-11 threads cultural context through its clinical descriptions. A behaviour is only pathological when it exceeds cultural norms and causes genuine distress or dysfunction.

7.8 Sleep-Wake Disorders

From scratch: Sleep disorders are grouped into problems of getting/staying asleep, excessive sleepiness, and abnormal events during sleep (parasomnias).

  • Insomnia disorder: difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early waking, at least 3 nights a week for 3 months, with daytime impairment.
  • Hypersomnolence disorder: excessive sleepiness despite a main sleep period of 7 hours or more.
  • Narcolepsy: irresistible sleep attacks, often with cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion), sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations; linked to loss of hypocretin (orexin) neurons.
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea hypopnoea: repeated upper-airway collapse causing breathing pauses, snoring and daytime fatigue.
  • Parasomnias – NREM (deep-sleep) arousal disorders: sleepwalking (somnambulism) and sleep terrors, with amnesia for the event, typical in children.
  • Nightmare disorder: repeated frightening dreams in REM sleep, with full recall on waking.
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder: loss of normal REM muscle paralysis so the person acts out dreams; a marker of later Parkinson's and Lewy body disease.
  • Restless legs syndrome: an urge to move the legs with unpleasant sensations, worse at rest and at night.

Trap: Sleep terrors (NREM, no dream recall, child screams but is not truly awake) are not the same as nightmares (REM, vivid recall).

7.9 Sexual Dysfunctions, Paraphilic Disorders & Gender Incongruence

Sexual dysfunctions are persistent problems in desire, arousal, orgasm or pain: examples include hypoactive sexual desire, erectile dysfunction, female orgasmic dysfunction, premature (early) ejaculation and genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder (dyspareunia, vaginismus).

Paraphilic disorders: a paraphilia (an atypical object or focus of arousal) becomes a disorder only when it causes distress, harm or involves non-consenting persons. Examples: exhibitionistic, voyeuristic, frotteuristic, paedophilic, sexual sadism and masochism, and fetishistic disorders.

Key. ICD-11 renamed "gender identity disorder" to gender incongruence and moved it out of the mental-disorders chapter into a new chapter on sexual health, explicitly de-pathologising being transgender. DSM-5-TR retains gender dysphoria, where the diagnosis rests on the distress, not on the identity itself.

7.10 Impulse-Control, Disruptive, Conduct & Elimination Disorders

  • Oppositional defiant disorder: a pattern of angry, argumentative, defiant behaviour toward authority.
  • Conduct disorder: repeated violation of others' rights and social norms (aggression, destruction, deceit, serious rule-breaking); a childhood risk pathway to antisocial personality disorder.
  • Intermittent explosive disorder: recurrent, disproportionate aggressive outbursts.
  • Pyromania (deliberate fire-setting for pleasure/relief) and kleptomania (failure to resist stealing objects not needed for use or value).
  • Elimination disorders: enuresis (repeated urination into clothes/bed after age 5) and encopresis (passing faeces in inappropriate places after age 4), each by developmental age.

7.11 Suicide & Risk Assessment

From scratch: Suicidal behaviour runs along a continuum: ideation → plan → attempt → completed suicide. Assessment aims to gauge risk and mobilise safety, not to predict with certainty.

Risk factors: previous attempt (the single strongest predictor), depression and other mental illness, substance use, hopelessness, chronic pain or illness, access to lethal means, recent loss, and social isolation. Protective factors: strong social support, religious or cultural prohibitions, responsibility for children, reasons for living and access to care.

Trap: The belief that "asking about suicide plants the idea" is a myth; direct, calm questioning does not increase risk and is essential. Another myth: people who talk about suicide do not do it, whereas most who die by suicide gave prior warning.

RML tilt: India carries one of the world's highest absolute suicide burdens, with young adults and, notably, the young-female and farmer sub-groups over-represented. Ingestion of pesticides is a leading method, so means restriction is a key public-health lever.

7.12 Epidemiology Basics for Psychopathology

From scratch: Epidemiology studies how often disorders occur in populations.

  • Prevalence: the proportion of a population with a condition at a point (point prevalence) or over a period (lifetime prevalence); it reflects existing cases.
  • Incidence: the rate of new cases arising over a period; it reflects onset/risk.

Key. Chronic disorders (like schizophrenia) can have low incidence but high prevalence because cases accumulate; brief disorders show the reverse. Mental and substance-use disorders are a leading global cause of years lived with disability. India's National Mental Health Survey estimated a lifetime prevalence of mental disorders near 13.7% and a wide treatment gap (most cases untreated).

Recap: Abnormality has been explained by turns as humoral, demonic, biological and psychological; Pinel and Tuke humanised care, Dix reformed asylums, and Kraepelin founded classification. Culture-bound concepts (Dhat, Koro, Amok, Latah, Piblokto, possession trance) are high-yield and now framed as cultural concepts of distress. Round out the chapter with sleep-wake disorders, sexual and gender presentations, impulse-control and elimination disorders, suicide risk assessment, and the prevalence/incidence backbone of epidemiology.

8 Clinical Psychology, scope, history & method

From scratch: Psychology is the science of behaviour and mental processes. Clinical psychology is the branch that applies that science to people who are actually suffering: it studies, assesses, and treats psychological distress and mental disorders. So the word "clinical" (from a Greek root meaning "at the bedside") signals a practical, patient-facing job, not just a laboratory one.

1. What clinical psychology is

Clinical psychology is the field concerned with the assessment, diagnosis, formulation, treatment, and prevention of psychological disorders and distress across the lifespan. A clinical psychologist studies how disorders arise, measures them with tests and interviews, builds an explanation of the individual case, and delivers psychological interventions (chiefly psychotherapy). Its scope spans mental illness (depression, anxiety, psychosis), developmental and neuropsychological problems, addictions, and everyday adjustment difficulties, in hospitals, clinics, schools, courts, and private practice.

Key. The four verbs to memorise: assess, formulate, diagnose, treat (plus prevent and research). Assessment + psychotherapy are the two signature skills that distinguish clinical psychology from its neighbours.

Trap: A psychiatrist is a medical doctor and can prescribe drugs; a clinical psychologist is not and (in India) generally cannot. Do not treat the two as interchangeable, and note that psychiatric social work is a separate RCI-recognised profession, not a synonym for clinical psychology.

Training models

Two philosophies define how clinical psychologists are trained:

  • Scientist-practitioner (Boulder) model (1949): the clinician is both a researcher and a therapist, trained to produce research and to use evidence in practice. This is the dominant model.
  • Scholar-practitioner (Vail) model (1973): emphasises practice over research, awarding the PsyD (Doctor of Psychology) for those who mainly want to deliver clinical services.

2. History

Who → what: Lightner Witmer → founded the world's first psychological clinic at the University of Pennsylvania in 1896 and coined the term "clinical psychology." He treated a boy with a spelling difficulty, marking the field's birth in applied, individual case work.

Key milestones:

  • 1896, Lightner Witmer: first clinic; the term "clinical psychology" is born.
  • World War I & II, the assessment boom: the army needed to screen and place millions of recruits. Psychologists built the Army Alpha (group test for literate recruits) and Army Beta (nonverbal test for illiterate or non-English recruits). After WWII, huge numbers of veterans with psychological trauma created demand for treatment, pushing psychologists from testing into psychotherapy.
  • Boulder Conference, 1949: formalised the scientist-practitioner model as the standard for doctoral training.
  • Rise of psychotherapy: through the second half of the 20th century clinical psychology grew from a mainly diagnostic role into a treatment profession.
RML tilt: Expect direct factual items: Who founded the first psychological clinic? (Witmer), Where and when? (University of Pennsylvania, 1896), Which conference established the scientist-practitioner model? (Boulder, 1949), and the Army Alpha/Beta pairing.

Clinical psychology in India

The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI), a statutory body under the RCI Act, 1992, regulates training and maintains the register of qualified clinical psychologists. Historically, the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology (a two-year RCI-recognised course after a master's) was the licensing route to practise. This is now being complemented and replaced by the RCI-recognised MA in Clinical Psychology, a direct two-year professional degree that itself confers RCI registration. Premier training centres include NIMHANS (National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences, Bengaluru), CIP (Central Institute of Psychiatry, Ranchi), and IHBAS (Institute of Human Behaviour and Allied Sciences, Delhi).

3. The clinical method (the process)

Clinical work follows an ordered sequence from first contact to review:

  1. Referral: the patient arrives via a doctor, family, court, or self-referral, with a presenting complaint.
  2. Clinical interview: the central assessment tool. Three types:
    • Structured: fixed questions in fixed order (high reliability; used in research and formal diagnosis).
    • Semi-structured: a core set of questions the clinician can probe and expand (the common practical middle ground).
    • Unstructured: free-flowing, follows the patient's lead (flexible but less reliable).
  3. Mental status examination (MSE): a structured snapshot of the patient's current functioning. Its components: appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought (form and content, e.g. delusions), perception (e.g. hallucinations), cognition (orientation, attention, memory), insight, and judgement. A patient who denies being unwell shows poor insight.
  4. Case history: a full developmental, family, medical, and social account of how the problem arose.
  5. Psychological assessment: standardised tests of intelligence, personality, and neuropsychological function to complement the interview.
  6. Case formulation: a working explanation of this person's problem, organised by the 4 Ps.
  7. Diagnosis: matching the picture to a classification system (ICD or DSM).
  8. Intervention: the treatment plan (usually psychotherapy).
  9. Evaluation: reviewing whether the intervention worked and revising as needed.
Key, the 4 Ps of formulation. Predisposing (long-standing vulnerabilities, e.g. genetics, early trauma), Precipitating (the recent trigger, e.g. a job loss), Perpetuating (what keeps it going, e.g. avoidance), Protective (strengths and supports, e.g. a stable family). Formulation explains the case; diagnosis merely labels it.

From scratch: No single model explains everything, so modern practice integrates them. The biopsychosocial model holds that biological, psychological, and social factors all contribute. The diathesis-stress model refines this: a person carries a diathesis (a predisposition or vulnerability) that only produces disorder when enough stress is applied. Vulnerability plus trigger equals disorder.

5. Ethics and professional issues

  • Confidentiality: keep the patient's information private, with narrow exceptions (serious risk of harm to self or others).
  • Informed consent: the patient must understand and freely agree to assessment and treatment.
  • Competence: practise only within one's training; refer on when a case exceeds it.
  • RCI code of conduct: registered clinical psychologists in India are bound by the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) professional and ethical code.
  • Scope of practice: work within the legal boundaries of the profession (for example, not prescribing medication).
Recap: Clinical psychology assesses, formulates, diagnoses, and treats psychological disorders; it is distinct from psychiatry (a medical, prescribing profession), counselling, and psychiatric social work. Lightner Witmer founded the first psychological clinic (University of Pennsylvania, 1896) and coined the term; the World Wars drove the assessment boom (Army Alpha and Beta); the Boulder Conference (1949) set the scientist-practitioner model, with the Vail scholar-practitioner model as the alternative. In India the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) regulates the field, with the M.Phil giving way to the RCI MA, trained at NIMHANS, CIP, and IHBAS. The clinical method runs referral to interview (structured / semi-structured / unstructured) to MSE (mental status examination) to case history to assessment to formulation (the 4 Ps) to diagnosis to intervention to evaluation. Abnormality is read through biological, psychodynamic, behavioural, cognitive, humanistic-existential, and sociocultural models, integrated by the biopsychosocial and diathesis-stress frameworks, all bounded by confidentiality, informed consent, competence, and the RCI code.

6. The Clinical Interview & the Mental Status Examination (MSE)

Before any test is scored, the clinician's first and most powerful instrument is a structured conversation. The clinical interview is the purposeful, face-to-face exchange through which the psychologist gathers information, builds rapport, observes behaviour, and forms early hypotheses about a client's problem.

From scratch: An interview is not a chat. It has an aim (assessment, diagnosis, therapy planning, or research), a structure the clinician controls, and a running observation of how the person answers, not only what they say.

6.1 Purposes of the clinical interview

  • Rapport: establishing a working alliance so the client discloses freely.
  • Information gathering: presenting complaint, history, symptoms, functioning.
  • Diagnosis: mapping reported symptoms onto ICD-11 / DSM-5 criteria.
  • Observation: live sampling of behaviour, speech, and affect (the MSE).
  • Planning: deciding on further testing, formulation, and management.

6.2 Types of interview by structure

TypeFormatStrength / weakness
StructuredFixed questions, fixed order, standardised (e.g. SCID, MINI)High reliability, comparable; but rigid, low rapport
Semi-structuredCore questions plus freedom to probeBalances reliability and flexibility (most clinical use)
UnstructuredOpen, guided by the clinician's judgementRich rapport and depth; low reliability, hard to compare

Interviews are also classified by aim: intake, diagnostic, crisis, and mental status interviews.

6.3 The Mental Status Examination (MSE)

The Mental Status Examination is a systematic, cross-sectional snapshot of a person's psychological functioning at the moment of examination. It is to psychiatry what the physical examination is to medicine: partly observed, partly elicited. Learn its components in order.

Who → what: Adolf Meyer is credited with formalising the systematic mental status examination in early twentieth-century American psychiatry.

Trap: Thought form (how ideas connect) is not thought content (what the ideas are). Flight of ideas is a form disturbance; a persecutory delusion is a content disturbance. Do not merge them.
Trap: An illusion is a misperception of a real stimulus; a hallucination is a perception without any stimulus. Only the latter signals more severe pathology.
RML tilt: The MSE is the single most testable clinical-skills topic. Memorise the nine headings in sequence and the mood/affect and illusion/hallucination pairs; one-word "which MSE domain?" items appear often.

7. Case History, History-taking & Case Formulation

Where the MSE is a snapshot, the case history is the longitudinal story: how the problem arose and evolved over the person's life.

7.1 Components of history-taking

  • Identifying data and chief / presenting complaint (in the client's words).
  • History of present illness (onset, course, duration, precipitants).
  • Past psychiatric and medical history; treatment history.
  • Family history; personal history (birth, development, education, occupation, marriage).
  • Premorbid personality; substance use; forensic history.

7.2 The biopsychosocial model

Proposed by George Engel (1977), the biopsychosocial model holds that health and illness arise from the interaction of biological (genes, neurochemistry), psychological (thoughts, coping, personality), and social (family, culture, poverty) factors. It replaced the purely biomedical model and underpins modern formulation.

7.3 The 5 Ps of case formulation

Key. A diagnostic formulation assigns a category (an ICD-11 / DSM-5 label). A case formulation is an individualised, explanatory story of why this person has this problem now, integrating the 5 Ps and the biopsychosocial model. Diagnosis classifies; formulation explains.

8. Psychological Report Writing

The report translates assessment findings into a clear, useful document for a referral source (a psychiatrist, court, school, or the client).

  • Identifying data and reason for referral.
  • Tests administered and behavioural observations during testing.
  • Relevant history and background.
  • Results and interpretation (integrated, not a list of raw scores).
  • Summary, diagnosis / impression, and recommendations.
Trap: A good report is referral-question driven and jargon-light; simply dumping scores without integration and recommendations is poor practice.

9. Community Psychology & Prevention

Community psychology shifts the focus from the individual in the clinic to systems, settings, and social change, stressing prevention over cure and a sense of community and empowerment (helping people gain control over their own lives).

Who → what: Gerald Caplan gave the classic three levels of prevention, and the U.S. Community Mental Health Act (1963) launched the community mental health movement, moving care out of asylums into local centres.
LevelTargetExample
PrimaryPrevent onset in the healthy populationMental-health education, reducing stressors
SecondaryEarly detection and prompt treatmentScreening, crisis intervention
TertiaryReduce disability from established illnessRehabilitation, relapse prevention
Trap: Primary prevention reduces incidence (new cases); secondary reduces prevalence by shortening episodes; tertiary reduces disability, not case numbers.

10. Health Psychology & Behavioural Medicine

Health psychology applies psychology to physical health, illness, and the health-care system, using the biopsychosocial model to explain how mind and behaviour shape bodily illness (e.g. how stress affects cardiovascular disease).

Who → what: Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman proposed the transactional model of stress: stress is the outcome of primary appraisal (Is this a threat?) and secondary appraisal (Can I cope?). Coping is then problem-focused (change the stressor) or emotion-focused (manage the distress).
  • Behavioural medicine: interdisciplinary field integrating behavioural and biomedical knowledge for prevention and treatment.
  • Adherence (compliance): the extent to which patients follow medical advice; poor adherence is a major cause of treatment failure.
Key. In the Lazarus and Folkman model, appraisal (not the event itself) determines the stress response, and coping style is chosen accordingly.

11. Rehabilitation Psychology (RCI-relevant)

Rehabilitation psychology helps people with disability or chronic illness achieve optimal functioning and participation. The World Health Organization distinguishes three levels:

12. Forensic & Clinical-Legal Basics

Forensic psychology sits at the interface of clinical practice and the law.

  • Competency / fitness to stand trial: whether an accused can understand the proceedings and assist in their own defence; distinct from the insanity defence, which concerns the mental state at the time of the offence.
  • Expert testimony: the psychologist gives the court an opinion within their competence, based on assessment, while remaining objective and staying inside their expertise.
Who → what: The Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA) 2017 reframed Indian mental-health law around rights: it recognises a person's right to access care, the advance directive, the nominated representative, and it effectively decriminalised suicide (presuming severe stress) and bars most inhuman treatments.
Trap: Fitness to stand trial (present ability to participate) is not the same as criminal responsibility / insanity (mental state during the crime). Exams love to swap these.
Recap: The interview and MSE capture the present; history-taking and the 5 Ps explain the past-to-present; the report communicates it; community, health, and rehabilitation psychology extend clinical work into prevention, physical illness, and disability; and forensic practice plus the MHCA 2017 and RCI licensing set its legal frame.

9 Counselling & Psychotherapy (who developed what)

From scratch: A psychotherapy is a planned, structured psychological treatment in which a trained clinician uses a relationship and a set of techniques to relieve distress and change thinking, feeling or behaviour. Each therapy grew out of a particular school (a theory of what goes wrong and how change happens), and almost every school is tied to one or two named founders. The RML CET-166 paper loves exactly this fact. It rarely asks you to do therapy; it asks who developed which therapy and what does the acronym stand for. So learn each pairing until it is reflexive.
RML tilt: Expect stems like "The ABC model belongs to ___", "EMDR was developed by ___", "Person-Centred Therapy was founded by ___", "DBT was developed by ___". Master the two tables at the end and you have banked a cluster of marks.

1. Psychoanalytic & psychodynamic therapies

Who → what: Sigmund Freud → Psychoanalysis, the original insight therapy. Core techniques: free association (say whatever comes to mind), dream analysis (dreams as "the royal road to the unconscious"), analysis of transference (the patient projects feelings for early figures onto the analyst), resistance, and interpretation of unconscious conflict.
From scratch: Freud held that symptoms come from unconscious conflict, often rooted in childhood and sexuality. The cure is to make the unconscious conscious, so the ego can master what was repressed. This is insight-oriented and long-term.
Who → what: Carl Gustav Jung → Analytical Psychology (collective unconscious, archetypes, individuation). Alfred Adler → Individual Psychology (inferiority/superiority, style of life, social interest).
Who → what: Melanie Klein → object relations/play therapy; Donald Winnicott → "good-enough mother", transitional object; Heinz Kohut → self psychology; David Malan and Peter Sifneos → brief/short-term psychodynamic therapy. Modern psychodynamic therapy keeps interpretation and transference but is shorter and more focused.
Key. Freud = free association + dream analysis + transference + interpretation. That four-word chain is the single most examined item in this school.

2. Behaviour therapy

From scratch: Behaviour therapy assumes maladaptive behaviour is learned and can be unlearned using classical and operant conditioning. It targets observable behaviour, not the unconscious.
Who → what: Joseph Wolpe → Systematic Desensitization, built on his principle of reciprocal inhibition (a fear response cannot occur at the same time as relaxation, so pairing a feared image with deep relaxation, moving up a graded fear hierarchy, extinguishes the anxiety). A snake-phobic learns relaxation, then imagines snakes from a distant photo up to holding one.
Who → what: Mary Cover Jones → the "mother of behaviour therapy"; she deconditioned little Peter's fear of rabbits (early counter-conditioning), the practical ancestor of desensitization.
Who → what: Burrhus Frederic (B.F.) Skinner → operant conditioning, and its clinical child the token economy (earned tokens exchanged for privileges reinforce target behaviour, classically on inpatient wards).
Who → what: Exposure therapies (in-vivo, imaginal, flooding, graded exposure, exposure-and-response-prevention for OCD) → grounded in extinction; aversion therapy pairs an unwanted behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus. Flooding = full-intensity exposure with no gradual hierarchy, the opposite of Wolpe's graded method.
Trap: Systematic desensitization is graded and paired with relaxation (Wolpe); flooding is maximal and un-graded. Do not swap them. Also: Mary Cover Jones (not Watson) did the counter-conditioning of fear; Watson only created a fear (Little Albert).

3. Cognitive therapy & CBT

From scratch: The cognitive model says it is not events but our interpretations of events that drive emotion. Change the thinking and the feeling follows. Two founders anchor this, and RML routinely pits them against each other.
Who → what: Aaron Temkin Beck → Cognitive Therapy (CT). Key constructs: the negative cognitive triad (negative view of the self, world and future, central to depression), automatic thoughts, schemas, and cognitive distortions (e.g. all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralization, catastrophizing, personalization). Beck's method is collaborative empiricism, testing thoughts like hypotheses.
Who → what: Albert Ellis → Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT) and the ABC model: A = Activating event, B = Belief (especially irrational beliefs), C = Consequence (emotion/behaviour). Ellis added D = Disputing the irrational belief and E = Effective new belief. REBT is the earliest cognitive therapy (1955).
Trap: ABC model + REBT + "irrational beliefs" → Albert Ellis. Cognitive triad + cognitive distortions → Aaron Beck. These two are the most commonly confused pair on the paper. Ellis disputes; Beck tests.
Who → what: Donald Meichenbaum → Self-Instructional Training and Stress Inoculation Training (SIT) (teaching coping self-talk in a "conceptualization → skills → application" sequence, a cognitive-behavioural way of vaccinating against stress).

4. Humanistic & existential therapies

From scratch: The "third force" rejects both the deterministic unconscious and mechanical conditioning. People are seen as growth-seeking; therapy provides conditions for that self-actualizing tendency to work.
Who → what: Carl Rogers → Person-Centred Therapy (also called Client-Centred Therapy). The three core conditions the therapist must offer are empathy (accurate empathic understanding), unconditional positive regard (non-judgemental warmth) and congruence (genuineness/authenticity). These three words are examined verbatim.
Who → what: Fritz (Frederick) Perls → Gestalt Therapy; here-and-now awareness, "unfinished business", and the famous empty chair technique.
Who → what: Viktor Frankl → Logotherapy (therapy through meaning; "will to meaning", from his concentration-camp experience in Man's Search for Meaning). Techniques: paradoxical intention, dereflection.
Who → what: Rollo May (father of American existential therapy) and Irvin Yalom → Existential Therapy. Yalom's four "ultimate concerns": death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness.
Key. Empathy + Unconditional Positive Regard + Congruence = Carl Rogers' core conditions. Empty chair = Perls / Gestalt. Meaning = Frankl / Logotherapy.

5. Family & systemic therapies

From scratch: Systemic therapy treats the family or system as the client. The identified patient's symptom is understood as serving, or being maintained by, the family's patterns.
Who → what: Salvador Minuchin → Structural Family Therapy (family structure, subsystems, boundaries that are rigid, clear or diffuse, enactment).
Who → what: Murray Bowen → Bowenian / Intergenerational Family Therapy; key ideas differentiation of self, triangulation, and the genogram (family-tree map).
Who → what: Virginia Satir → Experiential / Conjoint (Communication) Family Therapy; growth, self-esteem, communication stances (placater, blamer, etc.), family sculpting.
Who → what: Jay Haley (with Cloe Madanes) → Strategic Family Therapy (directives, paradox, changing the problem-maintaining sequence). Mara Selvini Palazzoli → the Milan systemic approach.
Trap: Genogram + differentiation → Bowen. Boundaries + structure → Minuchin. Communication stances / sculpting → Satir. Directives / strategic → Haley. The four family names are a classic matching item.

6. Third-wave & modern therapies (acronym central)

From scratch: "Third-wave" CBTs shift from changing thoughts to changing one's relationship to thoughts, using acceptance, mindfulness and values. This is where nearly all the acronyms live, so this is RML's favourite hunting ground.
Who → what: Marsha Linehan → Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), developed for borderline personality disorder and chronic suicidality; balances acceptance and change; four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness.
Who → what: Steven C. Hayes → Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT); psychological flexibility, acceptance, defusion, and committed values-based action.
Who → what: William Miller and Stephen Rollnick → Motivational Interviewing (MI); a collaborative, client-centred method for resolving ambivalence, rolling with resistance and eliciting "change talk" (big in addictions).
Who → what: Francine Shapiro → Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR); bilateral stimulation (guided eye movements) while recalling trauma, used for PTSD.
Who → what: Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman → Interpersonal Therapy (IPT); a time-limited therapy for depression targeting grief, role disputes, role transitions and interpersonal deficits.
Who → what: Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg → Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT); the miracle question, exception-finding and scaling questions, focus on solutions not causes.
Who → what: Jon Kabat-Zinn → Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), an 8-week secular mindfulness programme. Building on it, Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale → Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), which adds cognitive therapy to prevent depressive relapse.
RML tilt: The single most likely acronym questions are DBT→Linehan, ACT→Hayes, REBT→Ellis, EMDR→Shapiro and MBSR→Kabat-Zinn. Over-learn those five.

7. Common (curative) factors & the therapeutic alliance

From scratch: Beyond any one school, research (the "Dodo bird verdict": all therapies work about equally) suggests much of the benefit comes from factors shared across therapies rather than the specific technique.
Who → what: Jerome Frank (Persuasion and Healing) → the classic common-factors account: an emotionally charged confiding relationship, a healing setting, a rationale/myth, and a ritual/procedure, all instilling hope.
Key. Common factors: the therapeutic alliance (bond + agreement on goals and tasks, per Edward Bordin), therapist empathy (Carl Rogers again), client expectancy/hope, and the healing ritual. These outweigh brand-name technique for many outcomes.

8. Group Therapy: Irvin Yalom's Therapeutic Factors

From scratch: Group therapy treats several clients together in a facilitated group (usually 6 to 12 members), on the premise that the group itself is the healing agent. The person often cited for the modern theory of group psychotherapy is Irvin Yalom, an American existential psychiatrist, whose textbook The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy is the standard reference.

Who → what: Irvin Yalom → the 11 therapeutic (curative) factors of group therapy, and the modern interpersonal-existential model of group work.

Yalom's 11 therapeutic factors

  • Instillation of hope: seeing others improve gives faith in recovery.
  • Universality: realising "I am not the only one" (my problems are shared).
  • Imparting information: teaching, advice, and psychoeducation.
  • Altruism: helping fellow members raises self-worth.
  • Corrective recapitulation of the primary family group: the group re-enacts the family of origin so old conflicts can be worked through.
  • Development of socialising techniques: learning basic social skills.
  • Imitative behaviour (modelling): copying healthier members and the therapist.
  • Interpersonal learning: gaining insight from feedback about how one relates to others.
  • Group cohesiveness: the sense of belonging and acceptance (Yalom's analogue of the therapeutic alliance, considered the most important factor).
  • Catharsis: the ventilation of strong emotion within a safe group.
  • Existential factors: accepting responsibility, mortality, and the limits of life.

RML tilt: The count "11" and the definitions of universality, instillation of hope, and cohesiveness are the classic single-word items. Cohesiveness is the group parallel of the alliance.

Types of groups and process

  • Types: psychoeducational, support/self-help, process (interpersonal), psychodynamic, cognitive-behavioural, and encounter/T-groups. Alcoholics Anonymous is a self-help group.
  • Group stages (Bruce Tuckman): forming → storming → norming → performing → adjourning.

Trap: Tuckman gave the stages of group development (forming, storming, norming, performing); Yalom gave the therapeutic factors. Do not swap them.

9. Play Therapy

From scratch: Play therapy uses play, a child's natural medium of expression, as the vehicle for therapy because young children cannot verbalise conflicts the way adults can. There are two broad traditions: psychoanalytic and non-directive/humanistic.

Who → what: Virginia Axline → non-directive (child-centred) play therapy and its 8 principles; Melanie Klein → psychoanalytic play technique (play as free association); Anna Freud → play used to build the therapeutic alliance before analysis; Margaret Lowenfeld → the "World Technique" that became sandplay (later developed by Dora Kalff).

Virginia Axline's non-directive play therapy

Axline applied Carl Rogers' client-centred principles to children: the child leads the play, the therapist follows. Her 8 basic principles are the exam favourite: (1) build a warm relationship, (2) accept the child exactly as they are, (3) establish permissiveness so the child expresses freely, (4) recognise and reflect feelings, (5) respect the child's ability to solve their own problems, (6) let the child lead (do not direct), (7) do not hurry the process, and (8) set only necessary limits.

Psychoanalytic play and sandplay

  • Melanie Klein: treated the child's play as the equivalent of adult free association and interpreted it directly, often around early aggressive and depressive anxieties.
  • Anna Freud: more cautious; used play to form a relationship and strengthen the ego before interpreting.
  • Sandplay: the child builds scenes with miniatures in a tray of sand; rooted in Lowenfeld's World Technique and formalised by Dora Kalff on a Jungian basis.

Trap: Axline = non-directive/child-centred (Rogerian). Klein = directive interpretation. The Klein vs Anna Freud debate is about whether the child's play can be interpreted like free association (Klein: yes; Anna Freud: not immediately).

10. Behavioural Techniques in Depth

Who → what: Joseph Wolpe → systematic desensitization and reciprocal inhibition; Thomas Stampfl → implosive therapy; Edmund Jacobson → progressive muscular relaxation; B. F. Skinner → operant basis of token economy; Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin → the first token economy; Albert Bandura → modelling (observational learning); Neal Miller → early biofeedback demonstrations.

Anxiety-reduction techniques

  • Systematic desensitization (Wolpe): pair deep relaxation with a graded anxiety hierarchy so the feared stimulus stops evoking anxiety. Its mechanism is reciprocal inhibition (relaxation and anxiety cannot coexist, so relaxation inhibits anxiety).
  • Flooding: in-vivo exposure to the full-intensity feared stimulus with no gradation, held until anxiety extinguishes.
  • Implosive therapy (Stampfl): like flooding but imaginal and psychodynamically themed; the client imagines the worst-case scene.
  • Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP): the frontline behavioural treatment for OCD; the client faces the trigger and is prevented from performing the compulsion.
  • Progressive muscular relaxation (Jacobson): tense then release muscle groups in sequence to produce deep relaxation.

Trap: Systematic desensitization is graded and uses relaxation; flooding/implosion are ungraded and use maximum intensity. Reciprocal inhibition is Wolpe's term, not extinction.

Operant and learning-based techniques

  • Token economy (Ayllon & Azrin, on Skinner's operant principles): desired behaviours earn tokens that are exchanged for backup reinforcers; used on inpatient and developmental-disability wards.
  • Aversion therapy: pairs an unwanted behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus (classical conditioning). Antabuse (disulfiram) for alcohol; covert sensitization uses imagined aversive scenes.
  • Modelling (Bandura): the client learns by watching a model; includes participant modelling for phobias.
  • Biofeedback: electronic feedback of a physiological signal (heart rate, muscle tension) lets the client learn voluntary control.
  • Contingency management: systematically rewarding target behaviours (and withholding reward for others); widely used in substance-use treatment.

11. Motivational Interviewing and the Stages of Change

Who → what: William Miller and Stephen Rollnick → Motivational Interviewing (MI); James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente → the Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change).

From scratch: Motivational Interviewing is a collaborative, client-centred style for strengthening a person's own motivation to change, developed for addictions. Its spirit is often summarised as PACE (Partnership, Acceptance, Compassion, Evocation), and it works by rolling with resistance and eliciting "change talk" rather than confronting.

12. Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

Who → what: Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman → Interpersonal Therapy (IPT).

From scratch: IPT is a time-limited, evidence-based therapy developed for depression that links mood symptoms to current relationships. It works on four problem areas: (1) grief (complicated bereavement), (2) interpersonal role disputes, (3) role transitions, and (4) interpersonal deficits (loneliness/isolation). It focuses on the here-and-now, not childhood.

Trap: IPT (Klerman & Weissman) is not the same as CBT (Beck) or psychoanalysis; it is interpersonal and present-focused, and its four foci are the give-away detail.

13. Solution-Focused and Narrative Therapies

Who → what: Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg → Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT); Michael White and David Epston → Narrative Therapy.

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy

SFBT (de Shazer & Berg, Milwaukee) ignores the origins of the problem and builds on exceptions and future solutions. Signature techniques: the miracle question ("if you woke and the problem were gone, what would be different?"), scaling questions (rate 0 to 10), and exception questions (times the problem was absent).

Narrative Therapy

Narrative Therapy (White & Epston) sees problems as stories that dominate a person's identity. Its core move is externalisation ("the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem"), followed by finding unique outcomes and re-authoring a preferred story.

Trap: Miracle question → de Shazer/Berg (SFBT). Externalisation → White/Epston (Narrative). Both are postmodern/social-constructionist, but the techniques distinguish them.

14. Psychodrama

Who → what: Jacob Levy Moreno → Psychodrama (and sociometry).

From scratch: Psychodrama is a group method in which clients act out situations from their lives rather than only talking about them. Key elements: the protagonist (whose story is enacted), auxiliary egos (members who play other people), the director (therapist), the stage, and the audience. Signature techniques include role reversal, doubling, mirroring, and soliloquy. Moreno also coined "group psychotherapy" and invented sociometry.

15. Crisis Intervention, Supportive Therapy, and Evidence-Based Practice

  • Crisis intervention: short, immediate, present-focused help to restore functioning after an acute crisis (bereavement, disaster, suicidality); Gerald Caplan's crisis theory and models such as Albert Roberts' seven-stage model are the reference points. The aim is stabilisation, not personality change.
  • Supportive psychotherapy: strengthens existing defences and coping through reassurance, encouragement, and advice rather than uncovering the unconscious; used when insight-oriented work is unsuitable (for example, in severe or acute illness).
  • Evidence-based practice (EBP): integrates the best available research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values/preferences (the three-legged stool, from the American Psychological Association's 2006 policy).

RML tilt: Distinguish crisis intervention (acute, time-limited, restore prior functioning) from supportive therapy (ongoing, bolster coping, no deep uncovering).

10 Psychological Assessment & Tests

A psychological test is a standardized sample of behaviour, scored against norms, used to make an inference about a person. For the RML (Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia Hospital) CET-166 paper the questions are almost always reflexive: name the test, name its author (full name), say what it measures, and list its subtests. Train yourself to fire in that exact order.

RML tilt: Test → author → what it measures → subtests. If you can rattle off all four for every test below, you have covered the bulk of the assessment marks.

1. Psychometric foundations

From scratch: Before a test can be trusted, two properties must be shown. Reliability = consistency (does it give the same answer twice?). Validity = truth (does it measure what it claims?). A test can be reliable but not valid (a bathroom scale that is always 5 kg heavy), but it cannot be valid without being reliable.

Reliability types:

  • Test-retest reliability, same test given twice, correlate the scores; measures stability over time.
  • Split-half reliability, split the items into two halves (e.g. odd vs even), correlate; the Spearman-Brown formula corrects for the halving.
  • Internal consistency, do the items hang together? Indexed by Cronbach's alpha (α), the average of all possible split-halves; α > 0.7 is acceptable. Kuder-Richardson (KR-20) is its dichotomous cousin.
  • Inter-rater reliability, agreement between two scorers (crucial for projectives); indexed by Cohen's kappa.

Validity types:

  • Content validity, do the items cover the whole domain? (judged by experts).
  • Criterion validity, does the test predict an external criterion? Two flavours: concurrent (criterion measured now) and predictive (criterion measured later, an entrance test predicting first-year marks).
  • Construct validity, does it measure the theoretical trait? Shown by convergent validity (correlates with tests of the same trait) and discriminant validity (does not correlate with unrelated traits).

Norms are the reference scores of a representative sample; standardization means fixing the administration, scoring and norms so every testee is treated identically. Standard scores re-express a raw score relative to the norm: the z-score (mean 0, standard deviation 1), the T-score (mean 50, standard deviation 10, used on the MMPI), and the deviation IQ (mean 100, standard deviation 15, used on modern intelligence tests).

2. Intelligence tests

Who → what: Alfred Binet & Théodore Simon built the first practical intelligence scale (1905, Paris) to identify schoolchildren needing help; Lewis Terman of Stanford revised it into the Stanford-Binet and imported the Intelligence Quotient (IQ) = (mental age / chronological age) × 100, using mental age.

David Wechsler rejected a single ratio IQ and built a family of tests giving a deviation IQ plus index scores:

  • WAIS (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale), ages 16+.
  • WISC (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children), roughly ages 6–16.
  • WPPSI (Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence), preschool/early childhood.

Modern Wechsler tests yield four index scores: VCI (Verbal Comprehension Index), PRI (Perceptual Reasoning Index, called VSI, Visual Spatial Index, in the newest editions), WMI (Working Memory Index) and PSI (Processing Speed Index).

Raven's Progressive Matrices (John C. Raven) is a culture-fair, nonverbal test of abstract reasoning: the testee picks the missing piece of a visual pattern. It is taken as a purified measure of Spearman's general factor, g.

Indian intelligence tests are RML favourites:

  • Bhatia Battery (Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence, C. M. Bhatia, 1955), a performance battery of five subtests: Koh's Block Design, Alexander's Pass-Along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory, and Picture Construction; designed for the largely non-literate Indian population.
  • MISIC (Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children, Arthur J. Malin), the Indian adaptation of the WISC, giving Verbal and Performance IQs.
  • Seguin Form Board (Édouard Séguin), a speeded placing of shaped blocks into matching recesses; a performance test of visuo-motor speed used in children and in intellectual-disability screening.
  • DST (Developmental Screening Test, J. Bharat Raj), screens developmental level / gives a developmental quotient.
Trap: The Stanford-Binet introduced the ratio IQ; the Wechsler scales use the deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15). Do not swap them.

3. Personality tests, objective / self-report

These use fixed items and objective scoring, usually true/false or Likert:

  • MMPI (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, Starke Hathaway & J. C. McKinley), the dominant clinical inventory; 10 clinical scales plus validity scales L (Lie), F (Infrequency) and K (Correction/defensiveness) that detect faking; scored as T-scores.
  • 16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire, Raymond B. Cattell), measures 16 source traits derived by factor analysis.
  • NEO-PI-R (Revised NEO Personality Inventory, Paul Costa & Robert McCrae), measures the Big Five: Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, each with six facets.
  • EPQ (Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, Hans Eysenck), measures three dimensions: Extraversion, Neuroticism and Psychoticism, plus a Lie scale.
  • CPI (California Psychological Inventory, Harrison Gough), a "sane person's MMPI" measuring normal interpersonal traits (dominance, sociability, responsibility).

4. Projective tests

Who → what: The projective hypothesis was named by Lawrence K. Frank (1939): faced with an ambiguous stimulus, a person projects unconscious needs, conflicts and structure onto it, revealing personality that a self-report cannot reach.
  • Rorschach Inkblot Test (Hermann Rorschach), 10 symmetrical inkblots; the modern standardized scoring is John Exner's Comprehensive System, coding location, determinants, form quality and content.
  • TAT (Thematic Apperception Test, Henry Murray & Christiana Morgan), ambiguous interpersonal picture cards; the testee tells a story, which is analysed for needs and press (Murray's need-press theory).
  • Sentence Completion tests (e.g. the Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank), the testee finishes stems like "My greatest fear…"; semi-structured, easy to quantify.
  • DAP (Draw-A-Person Test, Karen Machover), interpret the human figure drawing as a projection of the self-image and body image.
  • HTP (House-Tree-Person Test, John Buck), the testee draws a house, a tree and a person, tapping self-perception and family/environment attitudes.
  • CAT (Children's Apperception Test, Leopold Bellak), the child version of the TAT, using animal figures in the original form.

5. Neuropsychological batteries

These localize and quantify brain dysfunction:

  • Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery (Ward Halstead & Ralph Reitan), a fixed battery (Category Test, Tactual Performance, Trail Making, etc.) giving an Impairment Index.
  • Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery (based on Alexander Luria, standardized by Charles Golden), scales for motor, rhythm, tactile, visual, receptive/expressive speech, reading, writing, arithmetic, memory and intellectual processes.
  • Bender-Gestalt Test (Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test, Lauretta Bender), copying nine geometric designs; screens visuo-motor / constructional impairment and organicity.
  • NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery (National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences, Bangalore), an Indian-normed adult battery across attention, executive function, learning, memory and comprehension.
  • MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination, Marshal Folstein), a brief 30-point bedside screen for cognitive impairment/dementia (orientation, registration, attention, recall, language).

6. Objective vs projective, the distinction

Key. Objective tests use structured items and fixed scoring keys, are high in reliability, and are vulnerable to faking (hence MMPI L/F/K scales). Projective tests use ambiguous stimuli, rest on the projective hypothesis, are hard to fake but weaker in reliability and validity, and lean heavily on inter-rater reliability.

7. Test Construction & Standardization

A psychological test is not written in one sitting; it is engineered through a fixed sequence so that the final scores mean the same thing across people. Knowing these steps and the two item-analysis indices is high-yield.

7.1 Steps in building a test

  • Planning: define the construct (what trait or ability), the target population, and the purpose (screening, diagnosis, selection).
  • Item writing: generate an item pool larger than the final test. Rules: one idea per item, simple language, avoid double negatives, avoid double-barrelled items, balance keyed direction to reduce acquiescence.
  • Item analysis: try items on a sample, keep the good ones (see below).
  • Standardization: fix administration, scoring, and time so conditions are identical for all.
  • Norming: administer to a large representative sample to build reference tables.
  • Reliability & validity checks, then publish the manual.

7.2 Item analysis: the two indices

IndexWhat it tells youRule of thumb
Item difficulty index (p)Proportion who answered correctly. p = 0.00 (nobody) to 1.00 (everybody).High p = easy item. Best around 0.50 for maximum discrimination; keep a spread.
Item discrimination index (D)How well an item separates high scorers from low scorers (upper vs lower group).D ranges −1 to +1; keep items above ~0.30. Negative D = flawed item, discard.
Key. Difficulty = how many got it right; Discrimination = whether the right people got it right. A high p can still be a bad item if D is low or negative.

7.3 Item Response Theory (IRT), briefly

Classical Test Theory treats difficulty as a property of the sample. Item Response Theory instead models the probability of a correct answer as a function of the person's latent ability and item parameters (difficulty, discrimination, guessing), plotted as an item characteristic curve. IRT underlies computer-adaptive testing, where the next item is chosen from the last response.

7.4 Norming, standardization & cross-cultural adaptation

Norms convert a raw score into a relative one (percentile, standard score, deviation IQ) by comparison with the standardization sample. A test imported from the West cannot be used as-is; it needs cross-cultural adaptation. The standard method is translation and back-translation: one bilingual translates into the target language, a second independently translates back to the source, and discrepancies are reconciled so meaning (not just words) is preserved. Re-norming on the local population is essential.

Trap: Merely translating a test is not adaptation. Without local re-norming and cultural item review, an American IQ table applied to Indian children inflates or deflates scores.

8. Ethics in Psychological Testing

  • Informed consent: the testee (or guardian) must understand the purpose, use, and limits before testing.
  • Confidentiality: results are shared only with authorised persons; records are protected.
  • Test security: items, manuals, and scoring keys are restricted so the test is not compromised; access is limited to qualified users.
  • Competence: a tester uses only instruments they are trained and qualified to administer and interpret.
  • Cultural fairness / bias: avoid items that penalise a group for reasons unrelated to the construct; report limits of the norms used.
RML tilt: In India the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) regulates who may practise clinical psychology and use clinical tests; the American Psychological Association (APA) Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing are the classic reference for test quality and ethics.

11. Aptitude, Interest & Achievement Tests, and Adaptive-Behaviour Scales

  • Aptitude tests predict future capacity to learn a specific skill (for example the Differential Aptitude Tests, General Aptitude Test Battery); used in guidance and selection.
  • Interest inventories map preferences to occupations (for example the Strong Interest Inventory, Kuder Preference Record); vocational, not ability, measures.
  • Achievement tests measure what has already been learned (present attainment), unlike aptitude which forecasts future learning.
  • Adaptive-behaviour scales assess everyday functioning (self-care, communication, social skills): the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales and the AAIDD Adaptive Behavior Scales, central to diagnosing intellectual disability alongside an IQ test.
From scratch: aptitude looks forward (can they learn it?), achievement looks back (have they learned it?), interest asks what they like, and adaptive behaviour asks how well they cope with daily life. Intellectual disability needs deficits in both IQ and adaptive behaviour, which is why these scales pair with intelligence tests.
Recap: Tests are engineered (item difficulty p, discrimination D, IRT, norming, translation-back-translation) and used ethically under RCI and APA standards. 11 Research Methodology & Statistics

From scratch: Research is a systematic, self-correcting way of answering a question so that the answer does not depend on who is asking. In clinical psychology we want to know things like "does cognitive behavioural therapy reduce depression more than a waitlist?" To answer that credibly we must define our terms precisely, design a fair comparison, sample people properly, measure carefully, and then use statistics to decide whether what we saw is real or just chance. This section builds that whole machine, piece by piece.

1. The research process & variables

A variable is anything that can take different values. The independent variable (IV) is what the researcher manipulates or groups by (the presumed cause); the dependent variable (DV) is what is measured (the presumed effect). Therapy type is the IV; depression score is the DV.

  • Extraneous variable, any variable other than the IV that could affect the DV. If it is not controlled it becomes a confounding variable: it varies systematically with the IV, so you cannot tell whether the IV or the confound caused the effect. If everyone in the therapy group is also younger, age confounds the result.
  • Moderator, a variable that changes the strength or direction of the IV–DV relationship (an interaction; "for whom / when" the effect holds). Therapy works better for mild than severe cases: severity moderates.
  • Mediator, a variable that explains how the IV affects the DV; it sits in the causal chain IV → mediator → DV. Therapy reduces depression by first changing negative thoughts.
Key. Moderator = when/for whom (interaction). Mediator = how/why (mechanism, in the middle).

An operational definition states a variable in terms of the exact operations used to measure it, making it observable and repeatable. "Depression = score on the Beck Depression Inventory."

Hypotheses

The null hypothesis (H0) states there is no effect or difference (any observed difference is chance). The alternative hypothesis (H1) states there is an effect. A non-directional / two-tailed H1 predicts a difference in either direction (μ1 ≠ μ2). A directional / one-tailed H1 predicts the direction (μ1 > μ2). We never "prove" H1; we reject or fail to reject H0.

2. Research designs

  • True (experimental) design, the researcher manipulates the IV and uses random assignment to conditions. Only this design supports strong causal claims.
  • Quasi-experimental design, an IV is compared across pre-existing groups with no random assignment. Comparing two intact hospital wards. Weaker causal inference (selection confounds).
  • Correlational design, no manipulation; two measured variables are related. Establishes association, not causation.
  • Descriptive / survey design, describes a population's characteristics without testing relationships.
  • Case study, intensive study of one individual/unit; rich detail, poor generalisability.
  • Longitudinal, same people followed over time (tracks change, but attrition and cost). Cross-sectional, different age/cohort groups measured once (fast, but cohort effects confound age).

Between vs within subjects

Between-subjects: different participants in each condition (no carry-over, but individual differences add noise). Within-subjects (repeated measures): same participants in all conditions (controls individual differences, more power, but risks order/carry-over effects).

Key. Control of confounds: randomization (random assignment spreads unknown confounds evenly across groups), counterbalancing (varying the order of conditions to cancel order effects in within-subjects designs), and matching (pairing participants on a key variable so groups are equated on it).

3. Sampling

Probability sampling, every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection, so results generalise and sampling error can be estimated.

  • Simple random, every member equally likely (lottery).
  • Systematic, every k-th member from a list after a random start.
  • Stratified, divide the population into strata (e.g. sex) and sample randomly within each; ensures representation.
  • Cluster, divide into naturally occurring clusters (e.g. hospitals), randomly select whole clusters.
  • Multistage, sampling in nested stages (states → districts → clinics → patients).

Non-probability sampling, selection chances unknown; cheaper but biased.

  • Convenience, whoever is available.
  • Purposive, deliberately chosen for a characteristic (expert judgement).
  • Quota, fill preset counts per subgroup, non-randomly (the non-random cousin of stratified).
  • Snowball, participants refer others (for hidden populations).
Trap: Stratified vs cluster: in stratified you sample from every stratum and want them internally homogeneous; in cluster you sample only some clusters, each meant to mirror the whole. Quota = non-probability version of stratified.

5. Descriptive statistics

Central tendency: the mean (arithmetic average) suits symmetric interval/ratio data but is pulled by outliers; the median (middle value) is robust and preferred for skewed data or ordinal scales; the mode (most frequent value) is the only option for nominal data.

Dispersion: the range (max − min) is crude; the variance is the mean of squared deviations from the mean; the standard deviation (SD) is the square root of variance, back in the original units and the most used spread measure.

The normal distribution

The normal distribution is a symmetric, bell-shaped curve where mean = median = mode. Under the empirical rule, about 68% of scores fall within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD (±1.96 exactly), and 99.7% within ±3 SD of the mean. Many parametric tests assume it.

Skewness measures asymmetry: positive (right) skew has a long right tail (mean > median), negative (left) skew a long left tail (mean < median). Kurtosis measures tailedness/peakedness: leptokurtic = tall, heavy tails; platykurtic = flat, thin tails; mesokurtic = normal.

Trap: In a positively skewed distribution the order is Mode < Median < Mean (the mean chases the tail). Report the median for skewed income-type data.

6. Reliability & validity of measurement

Reliability = consistency of measurement (test–retest, internal consistency via Cronbach's alpha, inter-rater); validity = whether it measures what it claims (content, criterion, construct). A test can be reliable but not valid (consistently wrong), but not valid without being reliable. (See the Assessment section for full detail.)

7. Inferential statistics, the logic

We study a sample but want to speak about a population. The sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic (e.g. the mean) over all possible samples; its spread is the standard error (SE) = SD ÷ √n. Larger samples shrink the SE, giving more precise estimates. A confidence interval (CI) gives a range that would capture the true parameter in, say, 95% of repeated samples.

Significance testing: assume H0 is true, compute how surprising the data are under it. The p-value is the probability of results at least as extreme as observed if H0 were true. If p < alpha (α) (the pre-set threshold, usually .05), we reject H0 and call the result "statistically significant."

Key. Type I error = rejecting a true null = seeing an effect that isn't there (false positive), probability α. Type II error = failing to reject a false null = missing a real effect (false negative), probability β. Statistical power = 1 − β = the probability of detecting a real effect; it rises with larger n, larger effect, and higher α. Effect size (e.g. Cohen's d = mean difference ÷ pooled SD; d ≈ 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) reports the magnitude of an effect, independent of sample size, unlike the p-value.
Trap: "Convict an innocent person" = Type I; "let a guilty person go free" = Type II. A tiny p-value does NOT mean a big effect: with huge n, trivial effects become "significant", always read the effect size.

8. The statistical tests

Parametric tests assume interval/ratio data, normality, and (often) homogeneity of variance.

  • t-test compares means: one-sample (sample vs known value), independent-samples (two separate groups), paired/dependent (same group twice or matched pairs).
  • ANOVA (analysis of variance) compares means of three or more groups using the F ratio (between-group variance ÷ within-group variance). One-way = one IV; factorial = two+ IVs (tests interactions); repeated-measures = within-subjects.
  • ANCOVA (analysis of covariance) = ANOVA that statistically removes the effect of a continuous covariate (e.g. adjusting post-scores for pre-scores).
  • Pearson's r, linear correlation between two interval/ratio variables (−1 to +1). Regression predicts a DV from one (simple) or more (multiple) predictors, giving a line Y = a + bX.

Non-parametric tests are used for nominal/ordinal data, small samples, or violated assumptions.

  • Chi-square (χ²), association between categorical variables (goodness-of-fit or independence).
  • Mann-Whitney U, two independent groups (non-parametric independent t).
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank, two related/paired conditions (non-parametric paired t).
  • Kruskal-Wallis, three+ independent groups (non-parametric one-way ANOVA).
  • Friedman, three+ related conditions (non-parametric repeated-measures ANOVA).
  • Spearman's rho, correlation of ranked/ordinal data.

10. Qualitative Research Methods

The statistics you have met so far belong to the quantitative tradition: it measures variables as numbers, tests hypotheses, and prizes large samples that let findings generalise. Qualitative research asks a different kind of question. Instead of "how much" or "how many", it asks "what is this experience like" and "how do people make meaning". It works with words, images and observations rather than numbers, uses small purposive samples, and builds understanding from the ground up.

From scratch: Quantitative research is usually deductive (start with a theory, deduce a hypothesis, test it) and aims for objectivity and generalisation. Qualitative research is usually inductive (start with data, build a theory upward) and aims for depth, context and the participant's own point of view. Neither is "better"; they answer different questions.

The main qualitative designs

  • Grounded theory (Barney Glaser & Anselm Strauss, 1967): builds a theory grounded in the data itself through constant comparison and coding (open → axial → selective), collecting data until theoretical saturation (no new categories emerge).
  • Phenomenology (rooted in Edmund Husserl): describes the lived experience of a phenomenon from the first-person view; the researcher brackets (sets aside) prior assumptions. Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) is a common clinical variant.
  • Case study: an intensive, holistic study of a single unit (person, family, institution, event) using many sources; strong on depth, weak on generalisation.
  • Ethnography: immersion in a culture or group over time, often by participant observation, to describe it from the insider (emic) view.
  • Narrative analysis: studies the stories people tell to understand how they construct identity and meaning over time.

Ways to gather and analyse qualitative data

  • Interviews: structured, semi-structured (fixed core questions plus probes, the clinical workhorse), or unstructured.
  • Focus groups: a moderated group discussion (usually 6 to 10 people) that harvests interaction and shared meaning, not just individual answers.
  • Thematic analysis: identifies, analyses and reports patterns (themes) across a data set; flexible and widely used (Braun & Clarke).
  • Content analysis: systematically codes text into categories; can be quantitative (counting code frequencies) or qualitative.
Key. Trustworthiness is the qualitative answer to reliability and validity. Its four criteria: credibility (are the findings believable, checked via member checking and triangulation), transferability (do they apply to other contexts, aided by thick description), dependability (are they consistent over time), and confirmability (do they reflect the participants, not the researcher's bias).

Mixed-methods designs deliberately combine both traditions. Common types: explanatory sequential (quantitative first, then qualitative to explain it), exploratory sequential (qualitative first to build an instrument, then quantitative), and convergent parallel (both at once, then compare). Triangulation across methods strengthens confidence in a finding.

Trap: Do not equate "qualitative" with "no analysis" or "just anecdote". Rigorous qualitative work has explicit sampling, coding and audit trails. Also, a large sample is not a goal in qualitative work; saturation, not size, decides when to stop.

11. Correlation in Depth

A correlation coefficient summarises the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables in a single number between −1 and +1. Zero means no linear relationship; the sign gives direction; the absolute value gives strength.

Key. The coefficient of determination is r squared: it is the proportion of variance in one variable explained by the other. If r = 0.60, then r squared = 0.36, so 36 percent of the variance is shared. This is why a "moderate" r of 0.5 explains only a quarter of the variance.
Trap: Correlation is not causation. A correlation can arise from A causing B, B causing A, a third (confounding) variable causing both, or coincidence. Correlation also detects only linear (Pearson) or monotonic (Spearman) trends; a strong curved relationship can still give r near zero.

12. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) in Depth

From scratch: A t-test compares two means. If you have three groups and run three separate t-tests, each at alpha = 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive across the set (the familywise error rate) climbs well above 5 percent. ANOVA compares all the means in one test at a controlled alpha, which is why it is preferred over multiple t-tests.

ANOVA works by partitioning the total variance into between-groups variance (differences among the group means, the "signal") and within-groups variance (variability inside each group, the "noise"). The F-ratio is their quotient (between / within). A large F means the group differences are large relative to random noise, pointing to a real effect.

The family of ANOVA designs

  • One-way ANOVA: one independent variable (factor) with three or more levels.
  • Two-way ANOVA: two factors at once. It yields two main effects (the effect of each factor alone) and an interaction (does the effect of one factor depend on the level of the other).
  • Repeated-measures ANOVA: the same participants measured under every condition (the within-subjects analogue), which controls individual differences.
  • ANCOVA (analysis of covariance): ANOVA that statistically removes the effect of a continuous nuisance variable (a covariate) before comparing means.
  • MANOVA (multivariate ANOVA): two or more dependent variables tested together.
Key. A significant ANOVA tells you the means differ somewhere, not where. Post-hoc tests locate the specific pairs: Tukey's HSD (honestly significant difference, balanced designs), Scheffe (conservative, flexible for complex comparisons), and Bonferroni (divides alpha by the number of comparisons to hold familywise error).

13. Non-parametric Tests in Depth

From scratch: Parametric tests assume the data are roughly normal, measured on an interval/ratio scale, with reasonably equal variances. When those assumptions fail (small samples, skewed data, ordinal or ranked scores, outliers), you switch to a non-parametric test, which usually works on ranks rather than raw values. Non-parametric tests are more robust but have slightly less power when the parametric assumptions actually hold.

14. Regression and Multivariate Methods

Correlation asks whether two variables move together; regression goes further and lets you predict one variable from another. Simple linear regression fits a straight line, the regression line, of the form Y = a + bX, where a is the intercept and b is the slope (how much Y changes per unit of X). The line is placed to minimise the squared distances of the points from it (the least squares criterion).

  • Multiple regression: predicts one outcome from several predictors at once (Y = a + b1X1 + b2X2 + ...), and reports how much variance the whole set explains (R squared).
  • Factor analysis: a data-reduction method that finds a small number of underlying factors (latent dimensions) behind many correlated items; central to test construction (for example, identifying the factors behind an intelligence or personality scale).
RML tilt: Expect one-liners that pair a purpose with a method: prediction → regression; data reduction / test construction → factor analysis; theory built from data → grounded theory. Know that r squared and R squared both mean "variance explained".

15. Effect Size, Power, Confidence Intervals and Meta-analysis

A p-value tells you whether an effect is likely real; it does not tell you how big it is. Effect size answers the size question. Common measures: Cohen's d (a standardised mean difference, roughly 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) and eta squared for ANOVA. Because a huge sample can make a trivial difference "significant", effect size is essential for judging practical importance.

  • Statistical power is the probability of detecting an effect that is truly there (1 − beta). It rises with larger sample size, larger effect size and larger alpha. Convention targets power of at least 0.80.
  • Sample-size (power) analysis uses effect size, alpha and desired power to work out how many participants a study needs before it begins.
  • Confidence intervals give a range that would contain the true population value in a stated percentage (usually 95 percent) of repeated studies; a narrow interval means a more precise estimate.
  • Meta-analysis statistically combines effect sizes from many studies into a single pooled estimate, giving greater power and a view of the overall evidence.

16. Research Ethics

Who → what: Nuremberg Code (1947): the first international code, born from Nazi medical trials, establishing voluntary consent as essential. Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 1964): ethical principles for medical research on humans. Belmont Report (USA, 1979): named the three core principles, respect for persons, beneficence and justice.

Every study with human participants must protect them. The core safeguards:

  • Informed consent: participants freely agree after being told the purpose, procedures, risks and their right to withdraw at any time.
  • Institutional ethics committee / Institutional Review Board (IRB): an independent body that reviews and approves the protocol before data collection begins.
  • Debriefing: after the study, telling participants its true purpose and correcting any misunderstanding.
  • Deception: withholding or misstating information; permitted only when unavoidable, minimally harmful, approved by the committee, and followed by debriefing.
  • Confidentiality and anonymity: protecting participants' data and identity.
  • Plagiarism: presenting another's words or ideas as your own; a serious breach of research integrity, avoided by proper citation.
Key. In India, the two codes to name are the APA Ethics Code (American Psychological Association, the standard psychology reference) and the ICMR (Indian Council of Medical Research) National Ethical Guidelines for biomedical and health research on human participants.
Recap: Qualitative work seeks meaning and depth (grounded theory, phenomenology, thematic analysis) judged by trustworthiness. Correlation (Pearson, Spearman, point-biserial, phi) gives relationship and r squared is variance shared, never causation. ANOVA beats multiple t-tests by controlling familywise error, yields an F-ratio, and uses post-hoc tests to locate differences. Every parametric test has a rank-based non-parametric twin for when assumptions fail. Regression predicts; factor analysis reduces. Effect size, power and confidence intervals qualify the p-value; meta-analysis pools across studies. Ethics rests on consent, committee review and the Nuremberg, Helsinki and Belmont lineage.

12 Indian Psychology (past & present)

RML tilt: The RML CET-166 tests Indian psychology as a past AND present subject. Expect one cluster on the history of the discipline in India (firsts, founders, institutions) and another on classical Eastern frameworks (gunas, koshas, Yoga, Buddhist psychology). This is a recurring leak, so over-learn both halves.
From scratch: "Indian psychology" means two overlapping things. (1) The history of the modern academic discipline as it grew in India from 1915 onward. (2) The indigenous psychological knowledge embedded in Samkhya, Yoga, Vedanta, Ayurveda and Buddhism, which long predates Western scientific psychology. Keep the two senses separate in your head and you will not mix up an exam item.

1. History of psychology in India

Modern experimental psychology reached India through the University of Calcutta. The first psychology laboratory in India was set up at Calcutta in 1915, and the first Department of Psychology in India was founded there in 1916 by Narendra Nath Sen Gupta. Sen Gupta had trained at Harvard under Hugo Münsterberg (a student of Wilhelm Wundt), so the German experimental tradition entered India through him. He is rightly called the founder of academic psychology in India.

Who → what: Narendra Nath Sen Gupta → first Department of Psychology (Calcutta, 1916); trained under Hugo Münsterberg at Harvard.

The towering early figure in Indian psychoanalysis was Girindrasekhar Bose, the first Indian psychoanalyst and the first Indian to earn a doctorate in psychology (Calcutta). In 1922 he founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society, one of the earliest such societies outside the West. Bose corresponded with Sigmund Freud for many years, and though the two respected each other they disagreed on theory. Bose proposed his theory of opposite wishes: every wish coexists with its opposite in the mind, and repression is really the management of these paired opposing wishes rather than simple burial of a single drive. Bose argued the wish "to be a man" and the wish "to be a woman" can both be present, which he linked to Indian ideas of identification.

Who → what: Girindrasekhar Bose → first Indian psychoanalyst; founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society (1922); corresponded with Sigmund Freud; "opposite wish" theory.

Other builders of the discipline:

  • Gunamudian David Boaz, a pioneer of psychology in South India, associated with the University of Madras, who helped establish applied and psychological-testing work.
  • Durganand Sinha, the leading voice of the indigenization of psychology, author of Psychology in the Indian Context. He argued that psychology in India had been a borrowed, imitative Western import and needed to be rooted in Indian social reality.
  • Jadunath Sinha, scholar of classical Indian Psychology, author of a major multi-volume study of cognition, emotion and will (conation) as treated in Indian philosophical systems.
  • H. P. Maiti, an early experimental and applied psychologist in the Calcutta tradition.

Institutionally, psychology gained a foothold when a dedicated psychology section of the Indian Science Congress was recognised, giving the field a scientific platform. Applied and clinical psychology then grew rapidly around mental-health institutions. The National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS) in Bangalore became the flagship for clinical training, and in 1992 the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) was established by an Act of Parliament to regulate and standardise the training of clinical psychologists and other rehabilitation professionals.

Firsts in Indian psychology (compact list)

  • First psychology laboratory: University of Calcutta, 1915.
  • First Department of Psychology: University of Calcutta, 1916, founded by Narendra Nath Sen Gupta.
  • First Indian psychoanalyst: Girindrasekhar Bose.
  • First psychoanalytical society: Indian Psychoanalytical Society, 1922 (Bose).
  • Regulatory body for clinical psychology: Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI), 1992.
Trap: Do not confuse the laboratory (1915) with the department (1916), and do not credit the department to Wundt or Münsterberg, Münsterberg was Sen Gupta's teacher, not the Indian founder. Also, RCI was founded in 1992, not with NIMHANS.

Samkhya, one of the oldest Indian schools, splits reality into two ultimate principles: purusha (pure consciousness, the witnessing self) and prakriti (primordial matter/nature, the source of mind and body). All experience arises when purusha appears entangled with prakriti; liberation is discriminating one from the other.

Patanjali's Yoga Sutras define yoga as "chitta vritti nirodha", the stilling of the modifications of the mind. The path is ashtanga yoga, the eight limbs: yama (ethical restraints), niyama (observances), asana (posture), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (withdrawal of the senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation) and samadhi (absorption). The last three combined form samyama.

The Bhagavad Gita offers an applied psychology of action and motivation. Its central teaching is nishkama karma, performing one's duty without attachment to the fruits of action, which reduces anxiety and stabilises the mind. The Gita also applies the three gunas to classify personality, food, work and even faith, giving a full trait-like typology.

Buddhist psychology centres on the Four Noble Truths: there is suffering (dukkha), it has a cause (craving, tanha), it can cease (nirodha), and there is a path to its cessation (the Eightfold Path). Two ideas are heavily tested: anatta (no-self, there is no fixed, permanent self, only changing processes) and mindfulness / vipassana (non-judgemental moment-to-moment awareness), the direct ancestor of modern mindfulness-based clinical interventions.

3. Indigenous concepts & therapies

The self in Indian thought is relational and interdependent, not the bounded, autonomous individual of Western psychology. Identity is embedded in family, community, duty (dharma) and the cosmos, which is why collectivist and role-based framings recur in Indian personality research. Ayurveda adds a mind-body model built on the tridosha, vata (air/movement), pitta (fire/metabolism) and kapha (earth-water/structure), alongside the mental gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas) as determinants of temperament and disorder. Interventions include yoga and meditation as psychological treatments (now evidence-based for anxiety, depression and stress), plus indigenous and faith-based healing practices that many Indian clients use in parallel with clinical care.

4. The present & new developments

The modern field is defined by the indigenization movement that Durganand Sinha led: building a psychology fitted to Indian culture rather than importing Western tools wholesale. Classical Indian frameworks map neatly onto positive psychology, sattva, well-being, mindfulness, meaning and self-regulation are shared ground, and the Gita's nishkama karma anticipates ideas of flow and non-attachment. Clinically, the RCI licensing framework now governs training: an RCI-recognised M.Phil or the newer RCI MA in Clinical Psychology, plus registration, is required to practise. The living institutional map includes NIMHANS (Bangalore), the Central Institute of Psychiatry (CIP), Ranchi, the Institute of Human Behaviour and Allied Sciences (IHBAS), Delhi, and RML Hospital, Delhi, the very centres whose entrance you are preparing for.

Trap: "Indigenization" is Durganand Sinha, not Jadunath Sinha (classical Indian Psychology scholar), different people, similar surname. And anatta means no-self, not non-violence (that is ahimsa).
Recap: Calcutta gave India its first psychology lab (1915) and first department (1916) under Narendra Nath Sen Gupta (pupil of Hugo Münsterberg); Girindrasekhar Bose was the first Indian psychoanalyst, founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society (1922), corresponded with Freud, and proposed the opposite-wish theory; Durganand Sinha drove indigenization, and the RCI (1992) regulates clinical training. Classically: Samkhya's purusha/prakriti and the three gunas; the five koshas; the four-fold antahkarana and four avasthas (ending in turiya); Patanjali's ashtanga yoga; the Gita's nishkama karma; and Buddhist Four Noble Truths, anatta and mindfulness/vipassana, now flowing into positive psychology and RCI-governed practice at NIMHANS, CIP, IHBAS and RML.

5. The Six Darshanas (Orthodox Schools of Indian Philosophy)

The word darshana means "a way of seeing" or a vision of reality. The six orthodox (astika) schools accept the authority of the Vedas. They are conventionally grouped in three pairs: Nyaya and Vaisheshika, Samkhya and Yoga, Mimamsa and Vedanta. Each carries an implicit psychology, a theory of the mind (manas), the self (atman or purusha), and how valid knowledge (pramana) is obtained.

From scratch: "Orthodox" here is a technical label meaning Veda-accepting (astika); it does not mean religious or conservative. The heterodox (nastika) schools that reject Vedic authority are Buddhism, Jainism, and the materialist Charvaka. RML questions often ask which schools are astika versus nastika.

Nyaya and Vaisheshika: logic, atoms, and the mind

Nyaya is the school of logic and epistemology. Its lasting psychological contribution is the theory of pramanas (valid means of knowledge): pratyaksha (perception), anumana (inference), upamana (comparison or analogy), and shabda (verbal testimony from a reliable source). This is effectively an early theory of cognition and how the mind acquires reliable beliefs. Vaisheshika, closely allied, is an atomistic metaphysics: all physical reality reduces to indivisible atoms (paramanu). For psychology, its key claim is that manas (mind) is itself a subtle, atomic internal organ that connects the self (atman) to the senses; because the mind is atomic and can attend to only one thing at a time, attention is serial.

Samkhya: purusha and prakriti (the most weighted)

Samkhya, attributed to the sage Kapila, is one of the oldest and most influential systems, and it supplies the metaphysics that Yoga later builds on. It is strictly dualist but not a mind-body dualism in the Western sense. The two ultimate realities are:

  • Purusha: pure consciousness, the witness (sakshi), inactive, plural, and eternal. It is the "seer."
  • Prakriti: primordial matter or nature, unconscious, active, the source of everything mental and physical. It is the "seen."

Crucially, in Samkhya the mind (including buddhi/intellect, ahamkara/ego, and manas) belongs to prakriti, not to purusha. The mind is material and unconscious; it only appears conscious because purusha's light is reflected in it. Prakriti evolves through the interplay of the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas). Bondage and suffering arise from the false identification of purusha with prakriti; liberation (kaivalya) is the discriminative knowledge (viveka) that they are separate.

Trap: Samkhya is atheistic (nirishvara): it explains creation through prakriti's evolution and needs no God. Yoga (seshvara Samkhya) adds Ishvara. Do not attribute a creator God to Samkhya.

Vedanta: Advaita and the four states of consciousness (also heavily weighted)

Vedanta ("end of the Vedas") centres on the Upanishads and the nature of the self. The most examined branch is Advaita Vedanta (non-dualism), systematised by Shankara (Adi Shankaracharya, c. 8th century CE). Its central teaching is that atman (individual self) and Brahman (ultimate reality) are one and the same; the perceived plurality of the world is maya (appearance superimposed by ignorance, avidya). Liberation (moksha) is realising this identity.

Advaita's key psychological model is the four states of consciousness (from the Mandukya Upanishad):

  • Jagrat: waking state, awareness of the external world.
  • Svapna: dreaming state, awareness of internal mental images.
  • Sushupti: deep dreamless sleep, undifferentiated consciousness with no objects.
  • Turiya: literally "the fourth," pure consciousness underlying and witnessing the other three; the true self.

RML tilt: The four avasthas overlap with the "avasthas" mentioned in the base section. Here anchor them to Mandukya Upanishad and Advaita, and remember turiya = the witnessing fourth state, not a phase of sleep.

6. Yoga Psychology in Depth (Patanjali)

Patanjali compiled the Yoga Sutras (c. 2nd century BCE to 2nd century CE), the foundational text of Raja Yoga. Yoga shares Samkhya's metaphysics but is theistic and, above all, practical: it is a systematic psychology of stilling the mind.

Key. Patanjali defines yoga in the second sutra: "Yogah chitta-vritti-nirodhah" = yoga is the restraint (nirodha) of the modifications (vrittis) of the mind-stuff (chitta). When the mind's fluctuations cease, the seer rests in its own true nature.

Chitta and the chitta-vrittis

Chitta is the total mind-field (comprising buddhi, ahamkara, and manas). Its activities are the vrittis, five kinds of mental modification: pramana (valid cognition), viparyaya (error), vikalpa (imagination or verbal delusion), nidra (sleep), and smriti (memory). Yoga aims to still all vrittis.

The five kleshas (afflictions)

The kleshas are the root causes of suffering, the mental afflictions that drive the vrittis:

  • Avidya: ignorance (the root of the other four).
  • Asmita: egoism, false identification of the seer with the instrument of seeing.
  • Raga: attachment to pleasure.
  • Dvesha: aversion to pain.
  • Abhinivesha: clinging to life, fear of death.

Ashtanga: the eight limbs

The path to stilling the mind has eight limbs (ashtanga yoga):

Types of samadhi

Samadhi is graded. Samprajnata samadhi (with cognition) still has an object of awareness; it passes through subtler stages. Asamprajnata samadhi (without cognition) has no object and no seed. The highest is nirbija samadhi ("seedless"), in which even the latent impressions (samskaras) are dissolved and kaivalya (liberation) is attained.

7. Buddhist Psychology in Depth

Buddhism is a nastika (Veda-rejecting) system founded by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha. It is intensely psychological: it analyses suffering, the mind, and the self without positing a permanent soul.

The Four Noble Truths

  • Dukkha: life involves suffering or unsatisfactoriness.
  • Samudaya: suffering has a cause, namely craving (tanha) and attachment.
  • Nirodha: suffering can cease (nirvana).
  • Magga: there is a path to its cessation, the Noble Eightfold Path.

The Noble Eightfold Path

The path has eight factors, grouped into wisdom (prajna), ethical conduct (sila), and mental discipline (samadhi): right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration.

The three marks of existence

  • Anicca: impermanence; all conditioned things are in flux.
  • Dukkha: unsatisfactoriness inherent in clinging to the impermanent.
  • Anatta: no-self; there is no permanent, unchanging soul.

Trap: Anatta (no-self) is the sharpest contrast with the Hindu darshanas, which affirm an eternal atman or purusha. A common exam item pairs "Buddhism → anatta (no permanent self)" against "Vedanta/Samkhya → eternal self."

The five skandhas (aggregates)

What we call "self" is, in Buddhism, only a bundle of five ever-changing skandhas: rupa (form or body), vedana (feeling or sensation tone), samjna (perception), samskara (mental formations or volitions), and vijnana (consciousness). No aggregate is the self, and no self stands behind them.

Abhidhamma, dependent origination, and mindfulness

The Abhidhamma is the third "basket" of Buddhist scripture, a systematic analysis of mind and mental factors (a kind of early phenomenological psychology). Dependent origination (paticca-samuppada) is the doctrine that all phenomena arise in dependence on conditions, classically the twelve-linked chain from ignorance (avidya) through to suffering; nothing exists independently. Sati (mindfulness) is present-moment, non-judgemental awareness, cultivated through vipassana (insight meditation) to see the three marks directly. This lineage is the acknowledged root of modern mindfulness-based interventions (MBSR, MBCT).

RML tilt: Expect a question linking vipassana/sati to contemporary mindfulness-based cognitive therapy. Know that Jon Kabat-Zinn's MBSR secularised the Buddhist sati tradition.

8. Bhagavad Gita Psychology

The Bhagavad Gita (Krishna's counsel to Arjuna) offers a practical psychology of action and motivation. It presents three complementary yogas (paths to liberation): karma yoga (the path of selfless action), bhakti yoga (the path of devotion), and jnana yoga (the path of knowledge).

Key. The central motivational teaching is nishkama karma: acting without attachment to the fruits of action ("you have a right to action alone, never to its fruits"). This decouples effort from outcome-anxiety, a theme that resonates with intrinsic motivation and non-attached engagement.

The ideal person is the sthitaprajna, the one of steady, established wisdom: unshaken by pleasure or pain, free from craving and aversion, with senses under control (compared to a tortoise withdrawing its limbs). The sthitaprajna is the Gita's model of emotional equanimity and self-regulation.

9. Sri Aurobindo, Integral Psychology, and Ayurveda

Sri Aurobindo's integral psychology

Sri Aurobindo developed integral psychology, mapping consciousness as a graded hierarchy rather than a single layer. Below ordinary awareness lies the subconscient and inconscient; ordinary mind sits in the middle; above it rise the higher mind, illumined mind, intuitive mind, overmind, and finally the supermind (supramental consciousness). The self also has an inner being (behind the surface) and a central psychic being (the evolving soul). His practice, Integral Yoga, aims not to escape the world but to bring the supramental consciousness down into life. His work is a cornerstone of the modern Indian psychology movement.

Ayurveda's tridosha and the mind

Ayurveda, the classical Indian medical system, models the body through three doshas (biological energies): vata (air/movement), pitta (fire/metabolism), and kapha (earth-water/structure). For the mind (manas), Ayurveda uses the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas) as the mental equivalent of the doshas, giving a constitutional temperament theory (prakriti as personality type) that predates and parallels later trait models.

10. Contemporary Indian Psychology and Psychologists

Beyond the classical firsts, a distinct movement toward indigenous, culturally grounded psychology emerged from the 1970s onward, reacting against the wholesale import of Western frameworks.

Who → what:

  • Durganand Sinha → the leading voice of indigenization; argued Indian psychology must fit Indian social reality, not simply replicate American paradigms.
  • Jai B.P. Sinha → the nurturant-task (NT) leadership model, an Indian leadership style blending personal care (nurturance) with task orientation, suited to the Indian work context.
  • Girishwar Misra → edited major surveys of Indian psychology and advanced culture-and-cognition and indigenous well-being research.
  • Anand C. Paranjpe → bridged Indian thought and Western self-psychology; comparative work on the self and identity across traditions.
  • K. Ramakrishna Rao → systematised "Indian psychology" as a field, drawing on consciousness studies and yoga; a founder of the contemporary movement.

A parallel Indian positive-psychology and well-being movement reframes classical constructs (sukha, ananda, equanimity, the sthitaprajna ideal) as models of flourishing, and studies indigenous coping, spirituality, and resilience. Applied and clinical research is concentrated at the premier institutions: NIMHANS (National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences, Bengaluru), CIP Ranchi (Central Institute of Psychiatry), and IHBAS (Institute of Human Behaviour and Allied Sciences, Delhi), which anchor clinical psychology training, mental-health research, and culturally adapted therapy in India.
