1. Subject-Verb Agreement

Core rule

The verb agrees with the subject in number, not with the nearest noun.

Patterns to know

  • Each, every, either, neither, one, anyone, everyone, nobody → singular verb. (mock-1 Q5: "Each of the participants was given a consent form.")
  • Either / or, neither / nor → verb agrees with the nearer subject. (mock-1 Q11: "Neither the patient nor the family members were informed.")
  • Collective nouns (committee, team, government) → singular when acting as a unit, plural when members act individually. British English often plural; American often singular.
  • 'A number of' = plural verb. 'The number of' = singular verb.
  • None can be either; default to singular in formal writing.
  • The data are / the data is — data is technically plural (datum singular). Modern usage allows singular but treat as plural in formal exam contexts.
  • Plural-looking but singular nouns: news, mathematics, physics, politics, statistics, measles, mumps. ("Mathematics is...")
  • Plural-only nouns: scissors, trousers, glasses, premises. Take plural verb.
  • Two singulars joined by 'and' = plural ("Tom and Jerry are friends"), unless they refer to one entity ("Bread and butter is my breakfast" = singular).

5. Punctuation

Comma

  • Series: "apples, oranges, and bananas" (Oxford comma optional but consistent).
  • Before coordinating conjunctions joining independent clauses: "I came, but he left."
  • Restrictive vs non-restrictive relative clauses (mock-1 Q1):
    • Non-restrictive (extra info, removable) → use commas. "The patient, who was anxious, refused medication." Here we are talking about the only patient; the anxiety is added detail.
    • Restrictive (defines which) → no commas, use 'that' for things, 'who' for people. "The patient who was anxious refused medication" implies multiple patients and we're picking out the anxious one.
  • Around appositives, after introductory clauses, and direct address ("Sihan, come here").

Semicolon

  • Joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction. "She studied; he slept."
  • Separates items in a complex list where items themselves contain commas. "We invited Tom, our neighbour; Maya, the doctor; and Raj, the teacher."

Colon

  • Introduces a list, explanation, or quotation after an independent clause. "She had one goal: to win."

Apostrophe

  • Possession: dog's bowl (singular), dogs' bowls (plural). It's = it is; its = possessive.
  • Contractions: don't, can't, you're.

Quotation marks

  • British: single ('like this'). American: double ("like this"). Be consistent. Punctuation goes inside (US) or by logic (UK).

Hyphen vs en-dash vs em-dash

  • Hyphen (-) joins compound modifiers (well-known author).
  • En-dash (–) for ranges (1990–2000).
  • Em-dash (—) for parenthetical breaks. Avoid in your writing if asked.

8. Modifiers + Parallelism

Misplaced + dangling modifiers

  • Misplaced: modifier next to wrong word. Wrong: "She nearly drove the car for six hours." Right: "She drove the car for nearly six hours."
  • Dangling: modifier has no clear noun to attach to. Wrong: "Walking down the street, the building looked tall." (The building isn't walking.) Right: "Walking down the street, I thought the building looked tall."

Parallelism

  • Items in a series should share grammatical form. Wrong: "She likes reading, writing, and to swim." Right: "She likes reading, writing, and swimming."
  • With correlative conjunctions (both/and, either/or, neither/nor, not only/but also): structures on both sides must mirror.

11. Spelling Patterns (mock-1 Q14)

  • i before e except after c (when sounding 'ee'): receive, ceiling, but neighbour and weigh.
  • -ious / -eous / -uous: conscious, righteous, virtuous.
  • -tion vs -sion: -sion after l, n, r and after vowels (collision); -tion otherwise (motion).
  • Conscientious (correct, mock-1 Q14) — note the -sci- after 'con' and the -tious ending.
  • Common confusables: accept/except, affect/effect (verb usually affect, noun usually effect), principal/principle, complement/compliment, stationary/stationery (a = building, e = paper), discreet/discrete, council/counsel.
  • Doubling rule: in CVC verbs ending in stressed syllable, double the consonant before -ing/-ed: stop → stopped; refer → referring (stress on 2nd syllable). Not for unstressed: open → opening.

12. Reading Comprehension Strategy (mock-1 Q9, Q10)

Mock-1 had a short passage on CBT with two questions: main idea + author's tone.

Main idea questions

  • Re-read the first and last sentences of each paragraph.
  • Look for the thesis or controlling claim, often in the first sentence.
  • Eliminate options that are too narrow (covers only one detail) or too broad (overstates the passage).
  • Mock-1 Q9 example: the passage said CBT works by changing distorted thoughts and that empirical trials support it across "a range of disorders". Main idea = "CBT works by changing distorted thoughts that maintain distress." Wrong options: "cures every disorder" (overreach), "triggers are easy to remove" (contradicts), "trials unnecessary" (opposite).

Tone questions

  • Look at qualifiers ("a range of", "can alleviate" — measured), adjectives, contrast words (however, but).
  • Common tone options: supportive, critical, neutral, dismissive, sarcastic, polemical, optimistic, pessimistic, ambivalent.
  • Watch for hedging language → likely "supportive but qualified" or "cautious".
  • Mock-1 Q10: positive framing + qualifiers → "supportive but qualified".

Other RC question types

  • Inference — what is implied but not stated. Stay close to the text.
  • Vocabulary in context — substitute the option into the sentence; the meaning shouldn't change.
  • Author's purpose — to inform, persuade, critique, define, illustrate.
  • Specific detail — go back and find it; don't rely on memory.

13. Common Sentence-Correction Patterns (top 12)

  1. Subject-verb agreement with intervening phrases. ("The list of names is long," not 'are').
  2. Pronoun antecedent agreement. "Each student must bring their book" (singular they accepted) vs "his or her book" (formal).
  3. Pronoun ambiguity. "John told Sam he was wrong" — who is 'he'? Rephrase.
  4. Tense consistency within a sentence/paragraph.
  5. Parallel structure in lists and correlative pairs.
  6. Comparison errors: "Anil's marks are higher than Sunil's" (not 'than Sunil').
  7. 'Like' vs 'as': like (preposition, before a noun); as (conjunction, before a clause). "He sings like Elvis" / "He sings as Elvis sings".
  8. 'Who' vs 'whom': who = subject; whom = object. "Whom did you meet?"
  9. 'Less' vs 'fewer': less for uncountable, fewer for countable.
  10. 'Between' vs 'among': between two; among three or more.
  11. Double negatives. Avoid: "I don't know nothing."
  12. Misuse of 'the reason is because' (redundant). Use "the reason is that".

14. Mock-1 Trap Recap (15 questions)

  1. Non-restrictive relative clause needs commas on both sides ("The patient, who was anxious, refused medication").
  2. Antonym of GREGARIOUS = RECLUSIVE.
  3. "Insist" takes the preposition "on".
  4. Synonym of EPHEMERAL = TRANSIENT.
  5. "Each of the participants was given..." (each = singular).
  6. "Inconclusive" in "the data were inconclusive" = predicate adjective.
  7. Person fluent in many languages = POLYGLOT.
  8. "Bury the hatchet" = make peace.
  9. RC main idea: CBT works by changing distorted thoughts.
  10. RC tone: supportive but qualified (hedged language).
  11. "Neither the patient nor the family members were informed" — verb agrees with nearer subject.
  12. Active future simple → passive future simple: "will be announced".
  13. AMBIVALENT = having mixed feelings (NOT indifferent).
  14. CONSCIENTIOUS — correct spelling.
  15. "Had she taken... she would have recovered" — third conditional.
