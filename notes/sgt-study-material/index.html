2 Biopsychology & Physiological Psychology

Biopsychology (also called physiological psychology, biological psychology, or behavioural neuroscience) asks one question: how does the physical machinery of the body, above all the nervous system, produce behaviour, thought, emotion, and experience? To answer an entrance paper on this topic you must be able to build the brain from its smallest working part upward. We therefore start with the single cell, the neuron, and end with whole-brain imaging. Nothing here needs prior biology; every term is defined as it appears.

2.1 The Neuron: The Unit of the Nervous System

The nervous system is built from billions of specialised cells called neurons (nerve cells) that receive, integrate, and transmit information as electrical and chemical signals. A neuron is not like an ordinary body cell; it is shaped for communication, with parts that collect signals at one end and pass them on at the other.

Parts of a neuron

  • Soma (cell body): the metabolic centre. It holds the nucleus and keeps the cell alive. Incoming signals are summed here.
  • Dendrites: short, branching fibres that spread out from the soma like the branches of a tree (the word means "little tree"). They are the receiving surface; they collect signals from other neurons.
  • Axon: a single long fibre that carries the outgoing signal away from the soma toward other cells. One neuron has many dendrites but usually just one axon (which may branch at its end).
  • Myelin sheath: a fatty white insulating layer wrapped in segments around many axons. It speeds up signal conduction and stops the signal leaking. Myelin is why "white matter" is white.
  • Nodes of Ranvier: the small unmyelinated gaps between segments of myelin. The signal jumps from node to node (see saltatory conduction).
  • Terminal buttons (axon terminals, end feet): the swollen tips at the end of the axon branches. They store and release chemical messengers onto the next cell.
Why. The neuron's shape is a receive-integrate-transmit pipeline: dendrites in, soma decides, axon out, terminals hand off. Every fact about signalling maps onto this direction of flow, so memorise the direction, not just the parts.

Glial cells (neuroglia): the support staff

Neurons are outnumbered by glial cells ("glia" means glue), which support, feed, insulate, and protect neurons. They do not fire signals themselves but are essential.

  • Astrocytes: star-shaped cells that hold neurons in place, supply nutrients, mop up excess chemicals, and help form the blood-brain barrier.
  • Oligodendrocytes: make the myelin sheath in the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord). One oligodendrocyte myelinates several axons.
  • Schwann cells: make the myelin sheath in the peripheral nervous system (nerves outside the brain and cord). One Schwann cell myelinates one segment of one axon, and Schwann cells help peripheral nerves regenerate.
  • Microglia: the immune cells of the nervous system; they clean up dead cells and fight infection.
Trap. Papers love to swap myelin-makers: oligodendrocytes myelinate the CNS, Schwann cells myelinate the PNS. If a question mentions the brain or spinal cord, the answer is oligodendrocyte; if it mentions a peripheral nerve, the answer is Schwann.

The neuron doctrine: Golgi versus Cajal

In the late 1800s scientists argued whether the nervous system was one continuous web (the "reticular theory") or a set of separate cells. Camillo Golgi, an Italian physician, invented a silver-staining method (the "black reaction") that made single neurons visible, yet he believed the network was continuous. The Spaniard Santiago Ramon y Cajal used Golgi's own stain to show that neurons are separate, individual cells that merely communicate across tiny gaps. Cajal's view, the neuron doctrine, won.

Who → what. Camillo Golgi → the silver stain that revealed neurons (but he backed the continuous-network theory). Santiago Ramon y Cajal → the neuron doctrine (neurons are discrete cells). They shared the 1906 Nobel Prize despite disagreeing.

2.2 Resting and Action Potentials: How a Neuron Fires

The resting potential

A neuron at rest is electrically charged, like a tiny battery. The inside of the axon is about −70 millivolts (mV) relative to the outside; this is the resting membrane potential. It exists because charged particles (ions) are unevenly spread across the membrane: sodium (Na+) is concentrated outside, potassium (K+) inside, and the inside carries a net negative charge.

Key. Resting potential = about −70 mV, inside negative. The neuron is "polarised" and ready to fire.

This imbalance is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, a protein in the membrane that constantly pushes 3 sodium ions out for every 2 potassium ions it pulls in, using energy. Because it moves more positive charge out than in, it helps keep the inside negative.

The action potential (the nerve impulse)

When enough incoming signals push the membrane past a threshold (about −55 mV), the neuron fires an action potential, a rapid electrical spike that travels down the axon. It has clear phases:

  1. Depolarisation: sodium channels open, Na+ rushes in, and the inside shoots up toward +30 to +40 mV (briefly positive).
  2. Repolarisation: sodium channels close, potassium channels open, K+ flows out, and the charge drops back toward negative.
  3. Hyperpolarisation (undershoot): the charge briefly overshoots below −70 mV before the pump restores the resting state.
Key. The all-or-none law: a neuron either fires a full action potential or none at all. The strength of a stimulus is coded by how often the neuron fires (frequency), not by making a bigger spike. Every spike is the same size.

Refractory periods

  • Absolute refractory period: immediately after firing, the neuron cannot fire again no matter how strong the stimulus (sodium channels are inactivated).
  • Relative refractory period: shortly after, the neuron can fire again but only with a stronger-than-usual stimulus.
Why. The refractory period forces the impulse to travel in one direction (down the axon, not backward) and sets an upper limit on firing rate.

Saltatory conduction

In myelinated axons the impulse does not crawl smoothly; it jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next (Latin saltare, to leap). This saltatory conduction is much faster and more energy-efficient than conduction along an unmyelinated fibre.

Trap. When myelin is destroyed (as in multiple sclerosis), conduction slows and fails; saltatory conduction cannot occur without intact myelin and nodes. Do not confuse the nodes (bare gaps) with the myelin (insulation): the impulse jumps to the nodes because the myelinated stretches are skipped.

2.3 Synaptic Transmission: How Neurons Talk to Each Other

Neurons do not touch. Between the terminal button of one neuron and the dendrite of the next lies a microscopic gap, the synapse (synaptic cleft). The signal crosses this gap chemically.

  1. The action potential reaches the terminal button of the presynaptic (sending) neuron.
  2. Calcium enters and causes tiny sacs called synaptic vesicles to move to the membrane.
  3. By exocytosis the vesicles fuse with the membrane and dump their neurotransmitter (chemical messenger) into the cleft.
  4. The neurotransmitter drifts across and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic (receiving) neuron, like a key in a lock.
  5. This opens channels and changes the postsynaptic cell's charge.
  6. The signal is then cleared by reuptake (the presynaptic neuron sucks the transmitter back in for reuse) or by enzymes that break it down.

Excitation and inhibition

  • EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential): the transmitter makes the postsynaptic cell more likely to fire (depolarises it toward threshold).
  • IPSP (inhibitory postsynaptic potential): the transmitter makes it less likely to fire (hyperpolarises it away from threshold).
Why. A neuron is a voting machine: it constantly adds up EPSPs and IPSPs arriving on its dendrites and soma (this is "summation"). Only if excitation outweighs inhibition past threshold does it fire. Behaviour is the net result of this balance across billions of cells.

The discovery of neurotransmission

Who → what. Otto Loewi → first proof of chemical neurotransmission. In a famous 1921 frog-heart experiment (reportedly designed in a dream) he stimulated the vagus nerve of one heart, collected its fluid, applied it to a second heart, and slowed that heart too. He named the substance "Vagusstoff" ("vagus substance"), later identified as acetylcholine, the first known neurotransmitter. Charles Sherrington → coined the term "synapse" and described reflex integration.

Also note dopamine is the classic "two-disease" transmitter: excess → schizophrenia, deficit → Parkinson’s, which is why anti-schizophrenia drugs can cause Parkinson-like side effects.

2.5 Organisation of the Nervous System

The whole nervous system splits into two great divisions:

  • Central nervous system (CNS): the brain and the spinal cord. This is the control centre and integrator.
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS): all the nerves outside the CNS that connect it to the body and outside world.

The PNS divides again:

  • Somatic nervous system: carries sensory information in and controls voluntary skeletal muscles (moving your arm).
  • Autonomic nervous system (ANS): controls involuntary functions of glands and internal organs (heartbeat, digestion). It has two opposing branches.

Trap. Sympathetic and parasympathetic are opposites, but both belong to the autonomic system. A common wrong answer treats the sympathetic system as "voluntary"; it is involuntary. Voluntary muscle control is the somatic system.

The spinal cord and the reflex arc

The spinal cord relays messages between brain and body, but it can also act on its own in a reflex arc, a fast, automatic response that bypasses the brain. When you touch a hot stove: a sensory (afferent) neuron carries the pain signal to the spinal cord, an interneuron passes it across, and a motor (efferent) neuron orders the muscle to pull back, all before the brain even registers the pain.

Why. Routing simple protective reflexes through the spinal cord alone saves the time it would take to consult the brain; the brain is informed a fraction of a second later. This is why you yank your hand away before you feel the burn.

2.6 The Brain

The brain is traditionally divided into hindbrain, midbrain, and forebrain, roughly from bottom-and-back (older, survival functions) to top-and-front (newer, higher functions).

Hindbrain

  • Medulla (medulla oblongata): controls vital automatic functions, heartbeat, breathing, blood pressure. Damage is fatal.
  • Pons: a bridge relaying signals between higher and lower brain; involved in sleep, arousal, and facial movement.
  • Cerebellum ("little brain"): coordinates balance, posture, and smooth, well-timed voluntary movement; also involved in some learning. Damage causes clumsy, jerky movement.

Midbrain

A relay hub for vision and hearing reflexes and for movement. It contains parts of the reticular formation, a network running through the brainstem that controls arousal, alertness, and the sleep-wake cycle.

Forebrain and the limbic system

The forebrain contains the most advanced structures. Beneath the cortex sits the limbic system, the emotional and motivational core:

  • Amygdala: the fear and emotion centre; tags experiences as threatening; central to aggression and emotional learning.
  • Hippocampus: forms new long-term memories and supports spatial navigation. Damage causes an inability to form new memories.
  • Hypothalamus: the body’s regulator; controls hunger, thirst, temperature, sex drive, and it commands the pituitary gland (the link between brain and hormones). Remember the "four Fs": feeding, fighting, fleeing, and sexual behaviour.
  • Thalamus: the great relay station; nearly all sensory information (except smell) passes through the thalamus on its way to the cortex.
Trap. Do not confuse hippocampus (memory formation) with hypothalamus (drives and hormones), or thalamus (sensory relay) with hypothalamus (regulation and pituitary control). Amygdala = emotion/fear; hippocampus = memory. These four are the most-tested structures in the whole section.

The cerebral cortex: four lobes

The wrinkled outer layer of the forebrain, the cerebral cortex, is where perception, thought, and language happen. Each hemisphere has four lobes:

Language areas and hemispheric specialisation

Who → what. Paul Broca → Broca’s area (in the left frontal lobe), for speech production; damage causes Broca’s aphasia (halting, effortful speech, comprehension intact). Carl Wernicke → Wernicke’s area (in the left temporal lobe), for language comprehension; damage causes Wernicke’s aphasia (fluent but meaningless speech, poor comprehension). Roger Sperry → split-brain research on hemispheric specialisation (Nobel Prize 1981).

The two hemispheres are somewhat specialised (lateralisation): for most people the left hemisphere handles language, logic, and analysis, while the right hemisphere handles spatial ability, faces, and holistic processing. They are joined by the corpus callosum, a thick band of fibres. Roger Sperry studied "split-brain" patients whose corpus callosum was cut to treat epilepsy; his experiments showed the two halves could then work independently, revealing what each side does on its own.

Trap. Broca = broken, halting speech (production); Wernicke = wordy, fluent nonsense (comprehension). Both areas are in the left hemisphere for most people. Also: each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body (contralateral control).

2.7 The Endocrine System

Alongside the fast electrical nervous system runs a slower chemical one: the endocrine system, a set of glands that release hormones directly into the bloodstream to act on distant organs.

  • Pituitary gland: the "master gland", sitting under the hypothalamus. It releases hormones that control the other endocrine glands (and it is itself controlled by the hypothalamus).
  • Adrenal glands: release adrenaline (epinephrine) for fight-or-flight and cortisol for stress.
  • Thyroid gland: releases thyroxine, which sets the body’s metabolic rate.
  • Pineal gland: releases melatonin, which regulates sleep and the day-night (circadian) cycle.

The HPA axis and key hormones

The stress response runs on the HPA axis: the Hypothalamus signals the Pituitary, which signals the Adrenal glands to release cortisol. This is the body’s slow, sustained stress system.

  • Cortisol: the main stress hormone; mobilises energy but harms health if chronically high.
  • Adrenaline (epinephrine): the fast surge that races the heart in an emergency.
  • Oxytocin: the "bonding" hormone; released in childbirth, breastfeeding, and social attachment.
  • Thyroxine: controls metabolism and energy use.
  • Melatonin: promotes sleep and tracks the light-dark cycle.
Why. Nervous versus endocrine is a speed-versus-duration trade-off. The nervous system sends fast electrochemical signals down fixed wires for immediate, brief effects; the endocrine system sends slower hormones through the blood for widespread, longer-lasting effects. The hypothalamus is the bridge that lets the brain command the hormones.
Trap. The pituitary is the "master gland", but it takes orders from the hypothalamus (a brain structure, not a gland in the usual sense). So the true top of the chain is the hypothalamus, which is why the same structure keeps appearing in both the brain and endocrine sections.

2.8 Sensation and Perception: The Basics

Sensation is the raw detection of a stimulus by a sense organ; perception is the brain’s interpretation of that sensation into a meaningful experience. Sense organs first perform transduction: converting physical energy (light, sound, pressure) into the neural signals the brain can read.

Thresholds and psychophysics

  • Absolute threshold: the smallest amount of a stimulus a person can detect 50% of the time (e.g. a candle flame seen far away on a dark night).
  • Difference threshold (just noticeable difference, JND): the smallest change in a stimulus that can be detected.
Who → what. Ernst Weber → Weber’s law: the JND is a constant proportion of the original stimulus, not a fixed amount (adding one candle to ten is noticeable; adding one to a hundred is not). Gustav Fechner → founded psychophysics and extended Weber’s work into a law relating physical stimulus strength to perceived sensation.

The visual pathway

Light enters the eye and lands on the retina, which holds two kinds of receptor cells:

  • Rods: many, at the edges; work in dim light; see in black and white; give night vision.
  • Cones: concentrated in the centre (fovea); work in bright light; give colour and sharp detail.

Signals leave the eye by the optic nerve and travel to the visual cortex in the occipital lobe. Two classic theories explain colour vision:

Who → what. Thomas Young and Hermann von Helmholtz → the trichromatic (three-colour) theory: the retina has three cone types tuned to red, green, and blue, and all colours arise from their combined activity. Ewald Hering → the opponent-process theory: colour is coded by opposing pairs (red-green, blue-yellow, black-white); one member of a pair is excited while the other is inhibited.
Trap. The two colour theories are not rivals; both are correct at different stages. Trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz) operates at the cones (retina); opponent-process (Hering) operates later, in the retinal ganglion cells and brain. Opponent-process best explains afterimages (stare at green, look away, see red).

Auditory basics

Sound waves enter the ear, vibrate the eardrum, pass through three tiny bones, and reach the fluid-filled cochlea in the inner ear. Hair cells in the cochlea transduce the vibrations into neural signals sent along the auditory nerve to the temporal lobe. Pitch corresponds to the frequency of the wave and loudness to its amplitude (measured in decibels).

To study a brain we cannot open, biopsychologists use recording, imaging, and damage-based methods, plus genetics to separate nature from nurture.

Behaviour genetics

Behaviour genetics studies how much of a trait comes from genes versus environment.

  • Twin studies: compare identical (monozygotic, 100% shared genes) with fraternal (dizygotic, ~50% shared) twins. If identical twins are far more alike on a trait, genes matter more.
  • Adoption studies: compare adopted children with both their biological and their adoptive parents to separate genetic from environmental influence.
  • Heritability: the proportion of the variation in a trait across a population that is due to genetic differences. It ranges from 0 to 1.
Trap. Heritability is about variation within a population, not about a single individual. A heritability of 0.5 does not mean "half of your intelligence comes from genes"; it means half of the differences among people in that group trace to genetic differences. This distinction is a favourite trick question.

3 Developmental Psychology

Developmental psychology studies how humans change and stay the same across the entire lifespan, from conception to death. It asks three linked questions: what changes (body, thinking, emotion, personality, social ties), when it changes (the timing and order of milestones), and why it changes (the mechanisms behind growth). This section builds every major theory from zero, then flags the exact contrasts and confusions that entrance papers love to test.

3.1 The Big Debates & Research Designs

Before any single theory, developmental science is organised around a handful of recurring arguments. Every theorist you meet later takes a side (often implicitly) on each of these, so learn the debate first and the theorists become easy to sort.

Nature vs nurture

This is the oldest question in the field: does development come mainly from nature (genes, biological maturation, inherited temperament) or from nurture (environment, learning, culture, parenting)? The modern answer is both, interacting. Genes set a range of possibilities (a reaction range); environment decides where in that range a person lands. Related ideas: epigenetics (experience switches genes on or off without changing the DNA code) and gene–environment correlation (a child's genes shape the environments they are exposed to, e.g. a musically gifted child seeks out instruments).

Key. Almost no serious theory is purely nature or purely nurture. The exam-correct framing is interactionism: genes and environment continuously shape each other.

Continuity vs discontinuity

Is development a smooth, gradual, quantitative build-up (continuity, like a ramp), or a series of distinct qualitative jumps into new stages (discontinuity, like a staircase)? Learning theorists (Skinner) lean continuity; stage theorists (Piaget, Erikson, Freud, Kohlberg) lean discontinuity.

Trap. "Stage theory" is a strong marker of the discontinuity side. If a question names Piaget, Erikson, Freud, or Kohlberg and asks about the nature of change, the answer is discontinuous / qualitative / stage-like, not gradual.

Critical vs sensitive periods

A critical period is a rigid, narrow window during which a specific experience must occur for normal development, or the ability is lost for good (Konrad Lorenz's imprinting in geese; some aspects of vision). A sensitive period is softer: a window when the organism is especially receptive and learning is easiest, but development can still happen (harder, less complete) outside it (first-language and accent acquisition).

Key. Critical = rigid, all-or-none, permanent loss. Sensitive = optimal-but-flexible. Human language is usually called a sensitive period, not a strict critical one.

Stability vs change

Do early traits (temperament, attachment style, intelligence) persist across life (stability), or can people fundamentally transform (change)? Most evidence shows moderate stability with real room for change, especially with new relationships and environments.

Research designs in developmental psychology

Because the subject is change over time, the study design is itself an exam favourite.

Teratogens

A teratogen is any environmental agent (drug, virus, chemical, radiation) that causes harm to the developing embryo or fetus. Key principles: harm depends on timing (the embryonic period is most sensitive because organs are forming), dose, and the genetic vulnerability of mother and child. Common teratogens: alcohol (causes Fetal Alcohol Syndrome, marked by facial abnormalities and intellectual disability), nicotine, cocaine and other drugs, the rubella virus, and high-dose radiation.

Key. The embryonic stage is the period of maximum teratogen vulnerability because that is when organs are being built. Same insult in the fetal stage usually does less structural damage.

3.3 Jean Piaget: Cognitive Development

Who → what. Jean Piaget, a Swiss psychologist, proposed that children are active "little scientists" who construct knowledge by acting on the world, passing through four fixed, universal, qualitatively distinct stages of thinking (cognitive constructivism).

The engine: how thinking grows

  • Schema (plural schemas / schemata): a mental framework or "file" for organising information (a child's "dog" schema = four legs, fur, barks).
  • Assimilation: fitting new information into an existing schema (seeing a cat and calling it "dog").
  • Accommodation: changing the schema to fit new information (learning cats are a separate category and updating the schema).
  • Equilibration: the drive to balance assimilation and accommodation; disequilibrium (when old schemas fail) pushes the child to a higher level of understanding.
Trap. Do not swap these two. Assimilation = the world bends to fit your existing schema (schema stays the same). Accommodation = your schema bends to fit the world (schema changes).

The four stages

StageAge rangeKey features & the achievement
SensorimotorBirth – ~2 yrsKnows the world through senses and movement. Key gain: object permanence (objects still exist when out of sight; before this, "out of sight, out of mind"). Beginnings of symbolic thought at the end.
Preoperational~2 – 7 yrsSymbolic thinking, language, pretend play. But thinking is limited by egocentrism (cannot take another's viewpoint), centration (focusing on one feature), and lack of conservation. No true logical operations yet. The "three-mountains task" shows egocentrism.
Concrete operational~7 – 11 yrsLogical thinking about concrete objects. Masters conservation (quantity stays the same despite a change in shape), reversibility (mentally undoing an action), classification, and seriation (ordering). Still struggles with abstract, hypothetical ideas.
Formal operational~11 yrs → adulthoodAbstract reasoning, hypothetical-deductive logic, systematic problem solving, thinking about "what if" and ideals. Adolescent egocentrism may reappear (imaginary audience, personal fable).
Why. The classic conservation test: pour water from a short wide glass into a tall thin glass. A preoperational child says the tall glass "has more" (fooled by height, i.e. centration). A concrete-operational child knows the amount is unchanged, because they grasp reversibility. This single demonstration is the boundary between the two stages and is heavily tested.

Critiques of Piaget

  • He underestimated young children; with simpler methods, object permanence and some abilities appear earlier than he claimed.
  • Development is more continuous and domain-specific than his neat universal stages suggest; not everyone reaches formal operations in every area.
  • He neglected culture and social interaction as engines of cognitive growth (the gap that Lev Vygotsky fills).

3.4 Lev Vygotsky: Sociocultural Theory

Who → what. Lev Vygotsky, a Russian psychologist, argued that cognitive development is driven by social interaction and culture. Children learn through guided participation with more skilled partners; thinking is first social ("between people") and then becomes internal ("within the person").
  • Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help from a skilled partner. Learning happens best inside this zone.
  • Scaffolding: the temporary, adjustable support a teacher or peer gives, gradually withdrawn as the child becomes competent (like scaffolding removed once a building stands). Note: the term "scaffolding" was coined by Jerome Bruner and colleagues, building on Vygotsky's ZPD.
  • More Knowledgeable Other (MKO): anyone with more skill or knowledge on the task (a parent, teacher, older peer, even a tool).
  • Private speech: children talk out loud to themselves to guide their own behaviour; this later goes underground as silent inner speech that regulates thought. Vygotsky saw this as helpful self-guidance; Piaget dismissed similar talk as merely "egocentric" and non-social.

Who → what. Erik Erikson, a German-born ego psychologist, proposed eight psychosocial stages spanning the whole lifespan. 3.6 Sigmund Freud: Psychosexual Development

Who → what. Sigmund Freud, founder of psychoanalysis, proposed five psychosexual stages. At each stage libidinal (sexual) energy centres on a different erogenous zone; how conflicts there are handled shapes adult personality.

Erikson vs Freud (contrast)

  • Motivation: Freud = sexual/biological drives; Erikson = social relationships and identity.
  • Span: Freud stops at puberty (5 stages, ending in the genital stage); Erikson covers the whole lifespan (8 stages).
  • Focus: Freud on the id and unconscious conflict; Erikson on the ego and healthy adaptation.
  • Tone: Freud emphasises pathology from early fixation; Erikson emphasises growth and strengths (virtues).
Trap. Erikson was a neo-Freudian who expanded Freud, keeping stages but making them psychosocial and lifelong. Their childhood stages roughly align in age; the giveaway is that only Erikson has stages for adulthood and old age.

3.7 Lawrence Kohlberg: Moral Development

Who → what. Lawrence Kohlberg extended Piaget's ideas to moral reasoning. Using dilemmas (famously the Heinz dilemma: should a man steal an over-priced drug to save his dying wife?), he judged not the yes/no answer but the reasoning behind it, yielding three levels of two stages each.
LevelStageReasoning basis
I. Preconventional (self-interest; typical of children)1. Obedience & punishment"Avoid punishment." Right = not getting caught
2. Instrumental / self-interest"What's in it for me?" Right = fair exchange, rewards

4. Law & orderRight = obeying rules, laws, duty, authority
III. Postconventional (abstract principles)5. Social contractLaws are social agreements that can be changed for the greater good

Key. Kohlberg scores the reasoning, not the choice. Two people who both say "Heinz should steal the drug" can be at completely different stages depending on why.

Carol Gilligan's critique

Carol Gilligan, once Kohlberg's collaborator, argued his theory was gender-biased: it was built on male samples and treated abstract justice as the highest morality, thereby scoring women as "lower." She proposed that women tend to reason from an ethic of care (relationships, compassion, responsibility to others), which is different, not inferior, to the male-typical ethic of justice.

Trap. Gilligan = ethic of care and the charge of gender bias against Kohlberg. Kohlberg = ethic of justice. Do not credit "care ethics" to Kohlberg.

3.8 Attachment

Attachment is the deep, enduring emotional bond between a child and caregiver. Three names dominate: Harlow (that it is not about food), Bowlby (the theory), and Ainsworth (how to measure the types).

Who → what. Harry Harlow raised infant rhesus monkeys with two wire "mothers": one made of bare wire that dispensed milk, and one covered in soft cloth with no milk. The babies clung to the cloth mother and ran to her when frightened, going to the wire mother only to feed. This proved attachment is built on contact comfort, not just food, demolishing the earlier "cupboard love" idea.
Who → what. John Bowlby founded attachment theory: attachment is an evolved, biological system that keeps the infant close to a caregiver for survival. The infant builds an internal working model, a mental template of relationships ("Am I worthy of care? Are others reliable?") that guides later relationships. He also emphasised a sensitive period for forming attachments and the damage of maternal deprivation.
Who → what. Mary Ainsworth devised the Strange Situation, a lab procedure of separations and reunions between an infant, caregiver, and stranger. How the infant behaves, especially at reunion, reveals the attachment style.
Attachment styleBehaviour in the Strange SituationCaregiving linked to it

Insecure–avoidantLittle distress at separation; ignores/avoids caregiver at reunionUnresponsive, rejecting
Insecure–ambivalent / resistantVery distressed at separation; at reunion seeks yet resists comfort (clingy and angry), hard to sootheInconsistent

3.9 Language Development

Language emerges in a strikingly universal order across cultures, which is itself evidence in the nature–nurture debate.

A common error here is overregularisation (applying a grammar rule too widely, e.g. "goed," "foots"), which actually shows the child has learned the rule rather than merely imitating.

Chomsky vs Skinner (the nativist–behaviourist debate)

  • B.F. Skinner (behaviourist): language is learned like any behaviour, through imitation, reinforcement, and shaping by parents.
  • Noam Chomsky (nativist): children learn language far too fast and too creatively for pure reinforcement to explain, and produce sentences they have never heard (the "poverty of the stimulus"). He proposed an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD), a built-in biological readiness for grammar, and a universal grammar common to all languages.
Trap. LAD and universal grammar = Chomsky (innate/nativist). Reinforcement/imitation = Skinner (learned/behaviourist). Overregularisation errors ("goed") are strong evidence against pure imitation and thus favour Chomsky.

3.10 Lifespan, Adolescence & Aging

Urie Bronfenbrenner: ecological systems theory

Who → what. Urie Bronfenbrenner pictured the developing child at the centre of nested environmental "systems," like a set of Russian dolls, each layer influencing development.
SystemWhat it includes
MicrosystemImmediate, direct settings: family, school, peers, the child's actual daily contacts

Trap. Meso = links between the child's own settings; exo = a setting the child is not directly in but which still affects them. Chronosystem is the time layer, added later.

James Marcia: identity statuses (adolescence)

James Marcia operationalised Erikson's stage 5 using two dimensions: exploration (actively trying out options) and commitment (settling on a choice). Their combinations give four identity statuses:

StatusExplorationCommitment
Identity diffusionNoNo
ForeclosureNoYes (commitment without exploring, e.g. adopting parents' plan)

Key. The maturity order is diffusion → foreclosure → moratorium → achievement. Foreclosure = commitment without exploration; moratorium = exploration without commitment yet. These two are the most confused pair.

Paul Baltes: lifespan development principles

Paul Baltes argued development is a lifelong process best summarised by a few principles:

  • Lifelong: development continues from birth to death; no age dominates.
  • Multidimensional & multidirectional: it involves many domains, and abilities can rise and fall at the same time (some grow, some decline).
  • Gain–loss balance: every stage brings both gains and losses together.
  • Plasticity: capacities remain modifiable across life.
  • Contextual: shaped by history, culture, and non-normative life events.

Baltes also described healthy aging as Selective Optimisation with Compensation (SOC): selecting fewer goals, optimising effort on them, and compensating for losses. In cognition he distinguished fluid intelligence (raw processing speed, declines with age) from crystallised intelligence (accumulated knowledge, stable or rising).

Elisabeth Kubler-Ross: stages of grief

Who → what. Elisabeth Kubler-Ross, studying the terminally ill, described five stages of coping with dying and grief: Denial → Anger → Bargaining → Depression → Acceptance (mnemonic DABDA).
Trap. The stages are not a rigid, universal sequence; people skip stages, repeat them, or reorder them. Modern view treats them as common reactions, not a fixed ladder. The ordered five for recall: Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance.
Recap. The big debates (nature/nurture, continuity/discontinuity, critical/sensitive periods) and designs (cross-sectional cohort effect vs longitudinal attrition) frame everything. Prenatal = germinal, embryonic (peak teratogen risk), fetal. Piaget = 4 cognitive stages (object permanence → egocentrism → conservation/reversibility → abstract) driven by assimilation/accommodation; Vygotsky counters with ZPD, scaffolding, MKO, and "learning leads development." Erikson = 8 lifelong psychosocial crises with virtues (identity vs role confusion in adolescence); Freud = 5 psychosexual stages plus fixation. Kohlberg = pre/conventional/postconventional (6 stages, judges reasoning); Gilligan adds the ethic of care. Attachment: Harlow (contact comfort), Bowlby (internal working model), Ainsworth's Strange Situation (secure/avoidant/ambivalent) plus Main's disorganised. Language runs cooing → babbling → holophrases → telegraphic, with Chomsky's LAD against Skinner's reinforcement. Lifespan: Bronfenbrenner's nested systems, Marcia's four identity statuses, Baltes's lifespan principles, and Kubler-Ross's DABDA grief stages.

4 Abnormal & Clinical Psychology

Abnormal psychology studies patterns of thought, feeling and behaviour that fall outside the ordinary range and cause problems for the person or those around them. Clinical psychology takes that knowledge and applies it: assessment, diagnosis, formulation and treatment of psychological distress. This section builds from the ground up – what "abnormal" even means, how disorders are classified, why they arise, and the main families of disorder you must recognise for the entrance.

4.1 What counts as "abnormal"? The 4 Ds

There is no single line that separates the normal from the abnormal. Instead, clinicians weigh several overlapping criteria, remembered as the 4 Ds. The more of them that apply, and the more strongly, the more confidently we call a pattern disordered.

  • Deviance – the behaviour departs from what a society considers acceptable or statistically usual. Hearing voices no one else hears deviates from shared norms of perception.
  • Distress – the person suffers. Someone with severe anxiety feels genuine anguish. (Note: distress is not always present; a person in a manic episode may feel wonderful.)
  • Dysfunction – the pattern interferes with daily life: work, study, relationships, self-care. This is the most clinically weighted of the four.
  • Danger – the behaviour poses risk of harm to self or others. This is the least common criterion and is over-emphasised in popular thinking.
Key. No single D is necessary or sufficient. A skydiver deviates and courts danger yet has no disorder; a grieving widow feels intense distress that is not pathological. Diagnosis rests on the pattern across the Ds, its persistence, and its context.
Trap. "Abnormal = statistically rare" is only one narrow model. Very high intelligence is statistically rare but not a disorder; depression is common yet clearly pathological. Rarity alone never defines abnormality.

4.2 Models of abnormality

Before modern classification, different lenses competed to define what "abnormal" was. Each still shapes how we think today.

  • Statistical model – abnormality as deviation from the average (e.g. an IQ two standard deviations below the mean). Clean but blind to whether the deviation is harmful.
  • Sociocultural model – abnormality is defined by violation of a culture's norms and roles; what is disordered in one society may be accepted in another. Highlights labelling and cultural relativity.
  • Biological (medical) model – disorders are illnesses with organic causes (genes, neurotransmitters, brain structure) treated like physical disease.
  • Psychological model – abnormality arises from mental processes: unconscious conflict, faulty learning, distorted cognition, or blocked growth, depending on the school.

A short history

Who → what. Philippe Pinel (France, late 1700s) pioneered moral treatment – he unchained inmates at the Bicêtre and Salpêtrière asylums and insisted the mentally ill be treated humanely rather than as prisoners or the possessed. He shifted madness from a moral or demonic problem to a medical one.
Who → what. Emil Kraepelin (Germany, late 1800s) is the father of modern psychiatric classification. He grouped disorders by observed symptom clusters and course, distinguishing dementia praecox (later renamed schizophrenia) from manic-depressive insanity (mood disorder). His descriptive method is the direct ancestor of the DSM and ICD.

4.4 Etiological models: why disorders arise

"Etiology" means cause. Several theoretical models each explain psychopathology through a different mechanism.

  • Biological – genetics, neurotransmitter imbalance (e.g. serotonin in depression, dopamine in psychosis), hormones, brain structure and injury.
  • Psychodynamic – symptoms express unconscious conflict, usually rooted in early childhood, as defended-against impulses break through (Sigmund Freud's legacy).
  • Behavioural – disorders are learned: maladaptive responses acquired through classical conditioning, operant reinforcement or modelling. A phobia is a conditioned fear.
  • Cognitive – distorted, irrational or negative thinking drives the disorder; change the thought and the emotion follows (Aaron Beck, Albert Ellis).
  • Humanistic – distress arises when growth toward one's potential is blocked, often by conditions of worth imposed by others (Carl Rogers, Abraham Maslow).
  • Sociocultural – family systems, poverty, discrimination, culture and social stress shape and label disorder.

Integrative models

Modern clinical psychology rarely picks just one lens.

  • Biopsychosocial model (George Engel) – every disorder reflects an interaction of biological, psychological and social factors; assessment and treatment must address all three.
  • Diathesis-stress model – a person carries a predisposition or vulnerability (the diathesis, e.g. genetic risk) that only becomes a disorder when enough environmental stress is applied. Neither alone is sufficient; disorder emerges from their interaction.
Why. The diathesis-stress idea explains why two people facing the same trauma diverge: one develops PTSD, the other does not. Their thresholds differ because their underlying vulnerabilities differ. It is the single most examined integrative concept in this topic.

4.5 Anxiety disorders

Anxiety disorders share excessive, persistent fear or worry that is out of proportion to real threat and impairs functioning. In DSM-5-TR the anxiety chapter was slimmed down: OCD and PTSD were moved out into their own chapters (see below).

OCD and related disorders (now a separate DSM-5 chapter)

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) combines obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges that cause anxiety, e.g. contamination fears) with compulsions (repetitive acts or mental rituals performed to reduce that anxiety, e.g. washing, checking, counting). The compulsion briefly relieves distress, which reinforces it. Related disorders in this chapter include body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder and trichotillomania (hair-pulling).

Trauma- and stressor-related disorders (also a separate DSM-5 chapter)

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) follows exposure to a life-threatening or horrifying event and is defined by four symptom clusters: intrusion (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance of reminders, negative changes in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal (startle, hypervigilance), lasting more than one month. Acute stress disorder is the same picture within the first month.

Trap. In DSM-5-TR, OCD and PTSD are not anxiety disorders – each has its own chapter. In the older DSM-IV they were housed under anxiety, and ICD keeps them nearby. Many exam items hinge on this reclassification.

4.6 Mood (affective) disorders

Mood disorders centre on disturbance of emotional state – either depressed, elevated, or swinging between the two.

DisorderCore feature

Key. The line between Bipolar I and II is the presence of full mania (I) versus only hypomania (II). Mania causes marked impairment or psychosis or hospitalisation; hypomania does not. Dysthymia is to MDD what cyclothymia is to bipolar – the chronic, milder shadow.
Who → what. Aaron Beck proposed the cognitive triad of depression: the depressed person holds automatic negative views of the (1) self ("I am worthless"), (2) world/experience ("everything is against me"), and (3) future ("it will never get better"). These distortions, not external reality, sustain the low mood, and correcting them is the target of cognitive therapy (CBT).

A 30-year-old man reports two weeks of not being able to enjoy anything, sleeping twelve hours yet exhausted, feeling he has "ruined everything" and that his family would be better off without him. → Major depressive episode with the cognitive triad clearly active.

4.7 Schizophrenia spectrum

Schizophrenia is a severe disorder of thought, perception and behaviour in which contact with reality is impaired (psychosis). Symptoms are grouped as positive and negative.

  • Positive symptoms (added to normal experience): delusions (fixed false beliefs), hallucinations (usually auditory), disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour.
  • Negative symptoms (normal functions lost): flat affect, alogia (poverty of speech), avolition (lack of drive), anhedonia, social withdrawal. These respond poorly to medication and predict worse outcome.
Who → what. Eugen Bleuler coined the term "schizophrenia" (splitting of psychic functions, not "split personality") and defined its core fundamental symptoms as the 4 A's: Association disturbance (loosened, illogical thought), Affect disturbance (blunted or inappropriate emotion), Ambivalence (contradictory feelings/impulses at once), and Autism (withdrawal into an inner private world). Hallucinations and delusions he regarded as secondary.
Who → what. Kurt Schneider listed first-rank symptoms he considered strongly suggestive of schizophrenia: audible thoughts, voices arguing or commenting on one's actions, thought insertion, thought withdrawal, thought broadcasting, made (externally controlled) feelings, impulses and acts, and delusional perception. Useful clinically, though not disorder-specific.

Subtypes: a historical note

DSM-IV and ICD-10 named subtypes – paranoid, disorganised (hebephrenic), catatonic, undifferentiated and residual. DSM-5 abolished these subtypes because they were unstable and had poor predictive value, replacing them with a dimensional rating of symptom severity.

Cause and course

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that positive symptoms arise from overactivity of dopamine transmission (especially D2 receptors) in mesolimbic pathways; antipsychotics block D2 receptors, and dopamine agonists like amphetamine can trigger psychosis. It is an incomplete picture (glutamate and serotonin also matter) but a heavily tested one. Prognosis is better with acute/sudden onset, clear precipitating stress, good pre-morbid functioning, prominent mood/positive symptoms and late onset; it is worse with insidious onset, prominent negative symptoms and early onset.

Trap. "Schizophrenia" does not mean "split/multiple personality" – that is dissociative identity disorder. Bleuler's "splitting" referred to the fragmentation of thought, emotion and will within one mind, not several personalities.

4.8 Personality disorders

A personality disorder is an enduring, inflexible, pervasive pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from cultural expectation, is stable from adolescence/early adulthood, and causes distress or impairment. DSM-5 groups the ten into three clusters.

OCD is the anxiety-related disorder of obsessions and compulsions and is ego-dystonic (the person is tormented by it). Different diagnoses, easy to confuse.

4.9 Other major categories (briefly)

  • Dissociative disorders – disruption of memory, identity or awareness: dissociative identity disorder (two or more distinct personality states, formerly "multiple personality"), dissociative amnesia (inability to recall important, often traumatic personal information), depersonalisation/derealisation disorder (feeling detached from oneself or that the world is unreal).
  • Somatic symptom & related disorders – distressing bodily symptoms with disproportionate thoughts and behaviour: somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety disorder (health anxiety), and conversion disorder (functional neurological symptoms such as paralysis or blindness with no organic cause).
  • Feeding & eating disorders – anorexia nervosa (restriction, very low weight, intense fear of gaining, distorted body image), bulimia nervosa (binge–purge cycles at normal or above weight), binge-eating disorder (recurrent binges without compensatory purging).
  • Neurodevelopmental disorders – onset in the developmental period: intellectual disability (deficits in intellectual and adaptive functioning), autism spectrum disorder (social-communication deficits plus restricted, repetitive behaviours), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity).
  • Substance-related and addictive disorders – problematic patterns of substance use (tolerance, withdrawal, craving, loss of control, use despite harm) plus behavioural addictions such as gambling disorder.

4.10 Clinical process basics

Diagnosis is only part of clinical work. Two foundational tools:

The Mental Status Examination (MSE)

The MSE is a structured snapshot of the patient's current mental functioning at the time of interview – the psychological equivalent of a physical exam. Its components:

  1. Appearance and behaviour – grooming, dress, eye contact, psychomotor activity, cooperativeness.
  2. Speech – rate, volume, tone, fluency.
  3. Mood and affect – mood is the patient's reported emotional state; affect is the observed, moment-to-moment expression (its range, appropriateness, congruence).
  4. Thought process – the form/flow of thinking (logical, tangential, loosened, flight of ideas).
  5. Thought content – delusions, obsessions, suicidal or homicidal ideation.
  6. Perception – hallucinations, illusions.
  7. Cognition – orientation (time, place, person), attention, memory.
  8. Insight and judgement – awareness of illness and quality of decision-making.
Key. Distinguish mood (subjective, reported, the "climate") from affect (objective, observed, the "weather"). And distinguish thought process (how thoughts flow) from thought content (what the thoughts are about). Both pairs are classic viva and MCQ traps.

Formulation and regulation

Case formulation goes beyond the diagnostic label to build a working explanation of this person's problems – often organised as predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors (the "4 Ps") – and it directly guides the treatment plan. Where diagnosis says "what", formulation says "why, for this person, now".

Key. In India, the practice of clinical psychology is regulated by the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) under the RCI Act, 1992. Only professionals with an RCI-recognised qualification (such as the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology, now transitioning to an RCI-approved MA route) registered on the Central Rehabilitation Register may practise as clinical psychologists. This is why entrance exams like SGT's feed into RCI-recognised programmes.
Recap. Abnormality is judged by the 4 Ds (deviance, distress, dysfunction, danger), read through statistical, sociocultural, biological and psychological models, on a road paved by Philippe Pinel's moral treatment and Emil Kraepelin's classification. Two manuals classify disorders – the APA's DSM-5-TR (detailed, criteria-based, categorical drifting dimensional) and the WHO's ICD-11 (global, guideline-based, more dimensional). Causes are framed biologically, psychodynamically, behaviourally, cognitively, humanistically and socioculturally, integrated by the biopsychosocial and diathesis-stress models. The major families: anxiety (GAD, panic, phobias, agoraphobia, social anxiety) with OCD and PTSD now split off; mood disorders (MDD, dysthymia, bipolar I vs II, cyclothymia, with Beck's cognitive triad); the schizophrenia spectrum (positive vs negative symptoms, Bleuler's 4 A's, Schneider's first-rank symptoms, the dopamine hypothesis); ten personality disorders in Clusters A, B and C; and dissociative, somatic, eating, neurodevelopmental and substance-related disorders. Clinical work rests on the MSE and case formulation, and in India is governed by the RCI.

5 Psychological Assessment & Testing

A psychological test is a standardized sample of behaviour. Two words in that sentence do all the work. Sample means we never measure the whole of a person; we measure a slice and infer the rest. Standardized means the slice is taken the same way every time: same items, same instructions, same scoring, same comparison group. If either word fails, the number the test spits out is meaningless. This chapter builds the machinery that lets us trust a test score, then walks through the actual instruments a clinical psychologist in India uses, and closes on who is legally allowed to use them.

5.1 Psychometric foundations: reliability

Reliability asks one question: if I measured again, would I get the same answer? A bathroom scale that reads 60 kg, then 64 kg, then 58 kg for the same person in one minute is unreliable. It is not that the readings are wrong in any deep sense; they are simply inconsistent, and inconsistency alone makes a test useless. Reliability is quantified as a correlation coefficient running from 0 to 1; for a clinical test we usually want it above 0.80, and for high-stakes decisions above 0.90.

There are several kinds of reliability because there are several sources of inconsistency, and each method isolates one source.

  • Test–retest reliability. Give the same test to the same people twice, some weeks apart, and correlate the two sets of scores. This isolates instability over time. Its enemy is the carry-over effect: people remember their earlier answers, or genuinely change between sittings. Best suited to stable traits (intelligence), poorly suited to fluctuating states (mood).
  • Split-half reliability. Split the test into two halves (usually odd items versus even items), score each half, and correlate them. This isolates internal consistency: do the items hang together? Because a half-test is shorter and shorter tests are less reliable, the raw correlation is corrected upward with the Spearman–Brown formula.
  • Cronbach's alpha. Devised by Lee Cronbach, alpha is the average of all possible split-halves at once. It is the standard modern index of internal consistency for a scale with many items scored on a range (like a Likert questionnaire). High alpha means the items are measuring one common thing.
  • Inter-rater (inter-scorer) reliability. Do two independent examiners, watching the same performance, give the same score? This isolates examiner subjectivity. It matters enormously for tests where scoring involves judgement, such as the Rorschach or a projective drawing, and much less for a machine-scored multiple-choice test. Cohen's kappa is the usual index.
  • Parallel (alternate) forms reliability. Build two equivalent versions of the test and correlate them; this isolates the accident of which particular items were chosen, and sidesteps memory effects that plague test–retest.
Who → what. Lee Cronbach gave us coefficient alpha (1951), the single most-cited index of internal consistency in all of psychology.

5.2 Validity

Validity asks a completely different question: does the test measure what it claims to measure? A scale can be perfectly reliable and still invalid. My bathroom scale might read exactly 60 kg every single time (perfectly reliable) while I actually weigh 70 kg, because it is miscalibrated (invalid). Reliability is about consistency; validity is about truth.

  • Content validity. Do the items cover the whole domain they claim to? A depression scale that asks only about sadness but ignores sleep, appetite, and suicidal thought has poor content validity. Judged by expert review of the item pool, not by a statistic.
  • Criterion validity. Does the test agree with an external, real-world benchmark (the "criterion")? Two flavours by timing:
    • Concurrent validity – test and criterion measured at the same time. A new short depression screener is given alongside a full clinical interview today; do they agree today?
    • Predictive validity – test now predicts a criterion in the future. An entrance exam taken in June predicts first-year marks the following March.
  • Construct validity. The deepest and most abstract kind. A "construct" is an unobservable theoretical entity (intelligence, anxiety, extraversion). Construct validity asks whether the test truly taps that entity, established by a web of evidence: convergent validity (it correlates with other measures of the same construct) and discriminant validity (it does not correlate with measures of unrelated constructs). An anxiety scale should correlate with other anxiety measures and should not correlate with, say, shoe size.
Trap. Reliability and validity are constantly confused. Memorise the two arrows. Reliability = consistency (same answer on repetition). Validity = accuracy (the right answer). A test must be reliable to be valid (an erratic instrument can't be measuring anything real), but it can be reliable without being valid (a consistently miscalibrated scale). So reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. Reliability sets the ceiling: a test's validity can never exceed the square root of its reliability.

5.3 Standardization, norms, and error

Standardization is the process of fixing the administration and scoring, and of building the yardstick against which any individual is compared. Norms are that yardstick: the distribution of scores obtained by a large, representative standardization sample. A raw score of 34 on a test means nothing by itself; it becomes meaningful only when we say "34 is at the 70th percentile for 25-year-old Indian graduates". This is why Indian norms matter so much (Section 5.5): a test normed on American children mislabels Indian children.

Key. A raw score is mute. It speaks only after being placed against a norm group. Always ask: normed on whom?

No measurement is perfect, so every observed score is the true score plus error: Observed = True + Error. The standard error of measurement (SEM) quantifies that error band. It lets us build a confidence interval around a score: an IQ of 100 with an SEM of 3 really means "true IQ is very likely between roughly 94 and 106". The SEM shrinks as reliability rises, tying the two foundations together: reliability tells us how much to trust the number, and the SEM turns that trust into a range.

Before scoring, we must know what kind of number we hold. Stanley Smith Stevens' four levels, from least to most information, govern which statistics are even legal.

Once we have a raw score we convert it into a derived score that locates the person in the norm group.

  • Percentile rank. The percentage of the norm group scoring at or below you. Intuitive but ordinal: the gap between the 50th and 55th percentile is tiny, while the gap between the 90th and 95th is large, because scores bunch in the middle.
  • z-score. How many standard deviations you are above or below the mean. Mean = 0, SD = 1. A z of +1.5 is one and a half SDs above average. The parent of all standard scores.
  • T-score. A rescaled z with mean = 50 and SD = 10, chosen to avoid negatives and decimals. Used on the MMPI and many clinical scales; a T of 70 (two SDs up) is the usual clinical cut-off.
  • Stanine. "Standard nine": a 1–9 scale with mean = 5 and SD = 2. Coarse but simple.
  • Deviation IQ. A standard score with mean = 100 and SD = 15 (Wechsler) or 16 (Stanford–Binet). It has replaced the old ratio IQ (see 5.5).

5.5 Intelligence testing: the historical spine

Who → what. Alfred Binet and his collaborator Theodore Simon, commissioned by the French government in 1905 to identify schoolchildren needing special help, built the first practical intelligence scale. Their genius idea was mental age: a child who solves the tasks that an average 8-year-old solves has a mental age of 8, whatever the birthday says.

The story moves in four leaps:

  1. Binet & Simon (1905, Paris) introduce mental age and an age-graded task ladder. Purely practical, atheoretical, aimed at placement.
  2. Lewis Terman (1916, Stanford University) imports and re-standardizes the scale on American children as the Stanford–Binet, and popularises the ratio IQ: IQ = (Mental Age ÷ Chronological Age) × 100. A 10-year-old with a mental age of 12 gets an IQ of 120.
  3. David Wechsler (from 1939, New York) attacks the ratio IQ's fatal flaw (see trap) and replaces it with the deviation IQ, which simply asks how far you deviate from your own age group. He also splits intelligence into verbal and performance scales, so a non-English-speaker can still show ability. His family of tests dominates clinical practice today.
  4. John Raven (1938, London) builds the Progressive Matrices, a pattern-completion test with no words at all, designed to be culture-fair and to tap Spearman's g directly.
Trap. The ratio IQ breaks down in adulthood. Mental age plateaus in the late teens while chronological age keeps climbing, so the ratio MA÷CA falls with every birthday and would make everyone look like they are losing intelligence. This is exactly why Wechsler's deviation IQ (comparison within one's own age band) replaced it, and why modern Stanford–Binet editions also use deviation IQ.

Theories of intelligence (brief)

  • Charles Spearman – two-factor theory: a general factor g underlies all mental performance, plus test-specific factors s. Raven's Matrices are the purest g test.
  • Louis Thurstone – rejected a single g; proposed seven primary mental abilities (verbal comprehension, word fluency, number, space, memory, perceptual speed, reasoning).
  • Howard Gardner – multiple intelligences: eight relatively independent intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic).
  • Robert Sternberg – triarchic theory: analytical, creative, and practical intelligence.
  • Raymond Cattell – fluid vs crystallized intelligence. Fluid (Gf) is on-the-spot reasoning in novel situations and declines with age; crystallized (Gc) is accumulated knowledge and holds or grows with age.

5.6 Indian intelligence adaptations

A Western test dropped unchanged into India fails twice: its items assume unfamiliar objects and its norms describe the wrong population. India's answer was two-pronged: build performance tests that lean on doing rather than on English, and re-standardize verbal scales on Indian samples.

Who → what. Chandra Mohan Bhatia built Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests (1955), the classic Indian performance battery, precisely so that illiterate and rural testees could be assessed without any reading.
  • Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests (Chandra Mohan Bhatia, 1955). Five sub-tests: Koh's Block Design, Alexander's Pass-along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory, and Picture Construction. Non-verbal, timed, suited to the illiterate; a workhorse in Indian clinics for age 11+.
  • Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC) – Lalitha Malin's Indian adaptation of the WISC, with Verbal and Performance sub-tests and Indian norms; the most widely used individual child IQ test in India.
  • Binet–Kamat Test of Intelligence – V. V. Kamat's Indian adaptation of the Stanford–Binet (into Kannada and Marathi), yielding mental age and IQ for Indian children.
  • Seguin Form Board Test – Edouard Seguin's shape-fitting board, used in India as a quick non-verbal test of speed and eye-hand coordination and a rough screen for intellectual disability in young children.
  • Draw-A-Man Test – Florence Goodenough's drawing test, adapted for Indian children by Pramila Pathak; the child draws a person and the score reflects cognitive maturity through the detail of the drawing.
Why. Culture-fair and performance tests matter in India because a large share of testees are non-literate or speak a language the test was never written in. A verbal, English-normed test would confound lack of schooling with lack of intelligence, systematically mislabelling the poor and rural as impaired. Performance batteries (Bhatia, Seguin) and re-normed scales (MISIC, Binet–Kamat) remove that confound.

5.7 Personality assessment

Personality tests split into two philosophies. Objective tests use fixed items with fixed scoring (true/false, Likert); they are reliable and easy to score but transparent, so they can be faked. Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli on the theory that the person will "project" unconscious material onto them; they are rich and hard to fake but weak on reliability and validity.

Objective personality tests

  • MMPI (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory) – Starke Hathaway & John McKinley. The giant of clinical personality assessment: hundreds of true/false items yielding clinical scales (depression, paranoia, schizophrenia, etc.) plus built-in validity scales (L, F, K) that detect lying and faking. Empirically keyed: items were kept if they statistically separated patient groups, not on theory.
  • 16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire) – Raymond Cattell, derived by factor analysis into sixteen source traits. A normal-range trait test.
  • NEO-PI / Big Five – Paul Costa & Robert McCrae. Measures the five-factor model: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism (OCEAN). The dominant modern trait framework.
  • Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) – Hans Eysenck. Three broad dimensions: Extraversion–Introversion, Neuroticism–Stability, and Psychoticism, grounded in biological theory.

Projective personality tests

  • Rorschach Inkblot Test – Hermann Rorschach (1921). Ten symmetrical inkblots; the person says what each might be, and responses are coded for location, determinants (form, colour, movement), and content. Exner's Comprehensive System later standardized scoring.
  • Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) – Henry Murray & Christiana Morgan. The person tells a story about ambiguous pictures of people; the stories reveal needs, drives, and conflicts (Murray's theory of needs).
  • Sentence Completion Test – the person finishes stems like "My greatest fear is…"; semi-structured, easier to score than the Rorschach (e.g. Rotter's Incomplete Sentences Blank).
  • Draw-A-Person Test – the person draws a human figure; features are interpreted for self-image and conflict (Machover). Also used as a rough cognitive screen in children.
  • Indian TAT adaptation – Uma Chowdhury's Indian adaptation of the TAT, redrawing the cards with Indian figures, dress, and settings so the pictures actually resemble the testee's world.

5.8 Neuropsychological and clinical rating scales

Neuropsychological tests infer the integrity of the brain from behaviour, most classically through visuo-motor tasks where organic damage shows up as distortion.

  • Luria–Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery – a standardized battery based on Alexander Luria's clinical methods, surveying motor, tactile, visual, language, memory, and intellectual functions to localise deficits.
  • PGI Battery – the Post Graduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh developed Indian neuropsychological instruments, notably the PGI Memory Scale and PGI Battery of Brain Dysfunction, standardized on Indian samples.

5.9 Ethics of testing

A test is an act of power over a person's life, so its use is bound by ethics.

  • Informed consent. The testee (or guardian) must understand the purpose, procedure, and use of results and agree freely before testing.
  • Competence. A tester must use only tests they are trained and qualified to administer and interpret; using a test beyond one's training is malpractice.
  • Test security. Test items and manuals must be kept confidential. If items leak into the public domain, the test is compromised and its norms invalidated.
  • Confidentiality. Results are private and shared only with proper consent and on a need-to-know basis.
  • Cultural fairness. The test and its norms must fit the testee's language and culture; using an inappropriate norm group is itself an ethical failure (this is the ethical face of Section 5.6).
Key. In India the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) regulates who may use clinical psychological tests. Only professionals holding an RCI-recognised qualification (the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology and RCI registration as a Clinical Psychologist) are legally entitled to independently administer and interpret clinical assessments. This is why the qualification matters so much: it is not a formality but a legal licence to test.
Recap. Every test is a standardized sample of behaviour that is only trustworthy if it is reliable (consistent: test–retest, split-half, Cronbach's alpha, inter-rater) and valid (accurate: content, criterion [concurrent/predictive], construct) – reliability being necessary but not sufficient for validity. Raw scores mean nothing until placed against norms and expressed as derived scores (percentile, z, T, stanine, deviation IQ), with the SEM giving the error band; and the score's kind is fixed by its level of measurement (nominal → ordinal → interval → ratio, with IQ being interval). Intelligence testing runs Binet & Simon → Terman's Stanford–Binet (ratio IQ) → Wechsler (deviation IQ) → Raven (culture-fair), theorised by Spearman, Thurstone, Gardner, Sternberg, and Cattell, and Indianised by Bhatia, Malin (MISIC), Binet–Kamat, Seguin, and Pathak's Draw-A-Man. Personality splits into objective (MMPI, 16PF, NEO-PI, EPQ) and projective (Rorschach, TAT with Chowdhury's Indian cards, Sentence Completion, Draw-A-Person); neuropsychology adds Bender-Gestalt, Luria–Nebraska, and the Indian PGI Battery; and severity is tracked with Beck (self-report) versus Hamilton (clinician-rated) scales, Y-BOCS, and the MMSE. All of it is bound by ethics – consent, competence, security, fairness – and, in India, by RCI regulation of who may test at all.

6 Psychotherapy & Counselling

Psychotherapy is the planned, systematic use of a trained relationship and psychological methods to relieve emotional distress, change unhelpful thoughts, feelings and behaviour, and help a person live more fully. It is sometimes called "the talking cure," a phrase that goes back to one of Sigmund Freud's early patients. Counselling is a close cousin: it also uses a helping relationship, but tends to focus on specific problems, decisions and adjustment rather than deep personality change. This whole section builds each major approach from the ground up, always naming who developed what, because "match the therapy to its founder" is a favourite question type in this paper.

6.1 What psychotherapy is, and what all therapies share

There are hundreds of named therapies, yet research repeatedly shows they work partly through the same underlying ingredients. These shared ingredients are called the common factors. Saul Rosenzweig first argued in 1936 that different therapies succeed for reasons they hold in common, and he jokingly used the Dodo bird from Alice in Wonderland ("Everybody has won, and all must have prizes") to name the finding that most bona fide therapies produce roughly similar outcomes. This is called the Dodo bird verdict.

  • The therapeutic alliance (working alliance). The bond of trust between client and therapist, plus their agreement on the goals and tasks of therapy. Edward Bordin described its three parts as bond, goals and tasks. The alliance is one of the strongest predictors of good outcome, regardless of the school of therapy.
  • An emotionally charged, confiding relationship with a helping person.
  • A healing setting that raises the client's expectation of being helped.
  • A rationale or "myth" – a believable explanation of the problem.
  • A ritual or procedure that both parties believe in and carry out.
  • Hope, catharsis (emotional release) and new learning.
Key. Common factors (especially the therapeutic alliance) explain a large share of why therapy works. The specific techniques matter too, but no technique works well without the relationship carrying it.

Therapies also differ on two practical dimensions you should be able to name:

  • Directive vs non-directive. A directive therapist actively guides, teaches, assigns homework and challenges thoughts (behaviour therapy, Aaron Beck's and Albert Ellis's cognitive work). A non-directive therapist mainly follows the client, reflecting and clarifying rather than steering (Carl Rogers's person-centred therapy is the classic example).
  • Individual vs group. Individual therapy is one client with one therapist. Group therapy treats several clients together, adding unique healing forces such as universality and peer feedback (see Irvin Yalom below). Related formats include couples and family therapy.
Why. Examiners love the directive/non-directive split because it cleanly separates the humanistic camp (non-directive) from the behavioural and cognitive camps (directive). Anchor Carl Rogers at the non-directive pole and you can place everyone else relative to him.

6.2 Psychodynamic therapy: Sigmund Freud and after

Who → what. Sigmund Freud developed psychoanalysis, the first formal system of psychotherapy, in Vienna around the 1890s–1900s.

Freud held that much of mental life is unconscious and that current symptoms grow from unresolved childhood conflicts, especially around sexual and aggressive urges. The aim of psychoanalysis is to make the unconscious conscious, so that buried conflict can be understood and worked through. The mind, in his structural model, has three parts:

  • Id – the primitive, unconscious reservoir of drives, ruled by the pleasure principle (wants satisfaction now).
  • Ego – the reality-based executive that mediates between id, superego and the outer world, ruled by the reality principle.
  • Superego – the internalised moral conscience and ideals, largely from the parents.

Core techniques of psychoanalysis:

  • Free association. The client says whatever comes to mind, without censoring, so hidden material surfaces.
  • Dream analysis. Freud called dreams "the royal road to the unconscious." The remembered dream (manifest content) is interpreted for its disguised meaning (latent content).
  • Transference. The client unconsciously transfers feelings about important early figures (often parents) onto the therapist. Analysing it is central to the cure.
  • Resistance. Anything that blocks the work – falling silent, missing sessions, changing the subject – signals a defended conflict worth exploring.
  • Interpretation. The analyst offers tentative explanations that link present feelings to unconscious past conflict, timed for when the client can accept them.
  • Working through. Repeatedly re-examining the same conflict across many situations until insight becomes lasting change.

Defense mechanisms are the ego's unconscious strategies for keeping anxiety-provoking material out of awareness. Freud named them and his daughter Anna Freud catalogued them systematically:

  • Repression – pushing threatening thoughts out of consciousness (the master defense).
  • Denial – refusing to accept an external reality.
  • Projection – attributing one's own unacceptable impulses to someone else.
  • Displacement – redirecting an impulse onto a safer target (shouting at the dog after a bad day at work).
  • Sublimation – channelling an impulse into a socially valued activity (the healthiest defense).
  • Reaction formation – turning an impulse into its opposite.
  • Rationalisation – inventing acceptable reasons for unacceptable behaviour.
  • Regression – retreating to an earlier, more childish stage.
  • Intellectualisation – dealing with a threat through abstract thought, stripped of feeling.

The neo-Freudians kept the idea of the unconscious but broke from Freud's heavy emphasis on sexuality:

Who → what. Carl Jung founded analytical psychology, adding the collective unconscious and its archetypes, plus the ideas of introversion/extraversion and individuation.
Who → what. Alfred Adler founded individual psychology, stressing the inferiority complex, striving for superiority, birth order and social interest.
Who → what. Melanie Klein pioneered object relations and child play therapy, focusing on the infant's inner world of internalised "objects" (people). Later object-relations and attachment thinkers include Donald Winnicott and John Bowlby.
Trap. Do not confuse the founders. Freud = psychoanalysis; Jung = analytical psychology; Adler = individual psychology. "Individual psychology" is Adler's label and has nothing to do with individual (one-to-one) therapy format.

6.3 Behaviour therapy

Behaviour therapy applies learning theory to change behaviour directly, holding that maladaptive behaviour is learned and can therefore be unlearned. It is present-focused, directive and measurable. It has two engines: classical conditioning and operant conditioning.

Who → what. Mary Cover Jones is called the "mother of behaviour therapy." Working with the boy "Peter," she reduced a child's fear of a rabbit by gradually pairing the feared object with pleasant eating – an early demonstration of counter-conditioning that inspired later desensitization.

Classical-conditioning based methods:

Who → what. Joseph Wolpe developed systematic desensitization, built on reciprocal inhibition (you cannot be relaxed and anxious at the same time).
  1. Teach deep relaxation (often progressive muscle relaxation).
  2. Build an anxiety hierarchy, ranking feared situations from mild to terrifying.
  3. While relaxed, the client imagines each step, moving up the ladder only when the lower step no longer triggers anxiety. Relaxation replaces fear at every rung.
  • Exposure therapy. Facing the feared stimulus (in imagination, in vivo, or via virtual reality) until anxiety fades through habituation and extinction.
  • Flooding. Intense, prolonged exposure to the most feared situation at full strength (no gentle ladder), continued until the fear burns out.
  • Aversion therapy. Pairing an unwanted behaviour (drinking, smoking) with an unpleasant stimulus so the behaviour loses its appeal (a drug that causes nausea with alcohol).

Operant-conditioning based methods rest on B.F. Skinner's principle that behaviour is shaped by its consequences (reinforcement increases behaviour, punishment decreases it):

Who → what. B.F. Skinner established operant conditioning, the basis for token economies, shaping and contingency management.
  • Token economy. Desired behaviours earn tokens that are later exchanged for rewards; widely used on wards and in classrooms.
  • Shaping. Reinforcing successive approximations that get closer and closer to a target behaviour.
  • Contingency management. Systematically arranging rewards and consequences so they follow (are contingent on) specific behaviours; a contingency contract writes this down.
Why. Classical-conditioning methods change reflexive, emotional responses like fear; operant methods change voluntary behaviour through its consequences. Sort any technique by asking: is it treating an automatic fear (classical) or a chosen action being rewarded/punished (operant)?

6.4 Cognitive therapy and CBT

Cognitive approaches say it is not events themselves but our interpretations of events that drive our feelings. Change the thinking and the emotion changes. Two founders dominate.

Who → what. Aaron Beck developed cognitive therapy, originally for depression, later expanded into Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (CBT).

Beck argued that depressed people are trapped in the cognitive triad: negative views of the self, the world, and the future. These are fed by automatic thoughts (quick, involuntary negative appraisals) and sustained by cognitive distortions, systematic errors in thinking such as:

  • All-or-nothing (black-and-white) thinking.
  • Overgeneralisation – one setback means "always."
  • Catastrophising – expecting the worst.
  • Mental filter – focusing only on the negative.
  • Personalisation – blaming oneself for things outside one's control.
  • "Should" statements and mind-reading.

Beck's therapist uses Socratic questioning (guided discovery through gentle questions), collaborative empiricism (client and therapist test beliefs together like scientists), thought records and behavioural experiments to examine and revise these thoughts.

Who → what. Albert Ellis developed Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT), built on the ABC model.
  • A – Activating event. Something happens.
  • B – Belief. An irrational belief about the event ("I must be approved by everyone").
  • C – Consequence. The emotional and behavioural result.

Ellis added D – Disputing the irrational belief and E – the new Effect. His signature target is "musturbation" – rigid demands phrased as musts and shoulds. His style is far more forceful and confrontational than Beck's gentle questioning.

Trap. Beck vs Ellis is a classic exam contrast. Both are cognitive and both use the idea that beliefs cause distress, but: Aaron Beck uses gentle Socratic questioning / collaborative empiricism and speaks of automatic thoughts and the cognitive triad (therapy: cognitive therapy / CBT). Albert Ellis is directive and confrontational, uses the ABC(DE) model and attacks irrational beliefs / "musts" (therapy: REBT). Remember Ellis came first (1950s) and Beck a little later (1960s).

6.5 Humanistic and existential therapies

These approaches reject both the determinism of psychoanalysis and the mechanism of behaviourism. They see people as basically good, free and driven toward growth. The therapist's job is to create conditions for that growth rather than to fix or condition the client.

Who → what. Carl Rogers developed person-centred (client-centred) therapy, the model non-directive humanistic therapy.

Rogers believed every person has an actualizing tendency, an inbuilt drive to grow toward their full potential, which unfolds when the relationship offers three core conditions:

  • Unconditional positive regard – warm, non-judgemental acceptance of the client as a person.
  • Empathy – accurately sensing and reflecting the client's inner world.
  • Congruence (genuineness) – the therapist is real and transparent, not hiding behind a professional mask.
Key. Rogers's three core conditions – unconditional positive regard, empathy, congruence – are among the most tested facts in this whole topic. Memorise all three by name.
Who → what. Fritz Perls (with Laura Perls) developed Gestalt therapy, focused on the here-and-now and taking responsibility for present experience.

Gestalt therapy aims for awareness of what one is feeling and doing right now, completing "unfinished business." Its best-known technique is the empty chair, where the client speaks to an imagined person (or part of the self) seated in an empty chair, then switches seats to reply.

Who → what. Victor Frankl developed logotherapy, the "third Viennese school," centred on the will to meaning – the idea that finding meaning, even in suffering, is the primary human drive. Frankl drew on his own survival in Nazi concentration camps.

Existential therapy (associated with Rollo May and, in the group/existential blend, Irvin Yalom) confronts the "givens" of existence – death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness – and helps the client live authentically in the face of them. Logotherapy is one existential branch.

6.6 Third-wave and newer approaches

The "third wave" of behaviour therapy keeps CBT's rigor but adds acceptance, mindfulness and values rather than only trying to change thoughts.

Who → what. Marsha Linehan developed Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), first for chronically suicidal clients and borderline personality disorder. It balances (the "dialectic" of) acceptance and change, and teaches four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness.
Who → what. Steven Hayes developed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), which teaches psychological flexibility: accept inner experiences, defuse from thoughts, and commit to action guided by personal values.
  • Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT). Developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale to prevent relapse in recurrent depression; it blends CBT with the mindfulness training of Jon Kabat-Zinn's MBSR.
Who → what. William Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed Motivational Interviewing (MI), a collaborative, client-centred style for strengthening a person's own motivation to change, especially in addictions. It works with ambivalence and "rolls with resistance" rather than arguing.
Who → what. James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente developed the Transtheoretical Model / Stages of Change, often paired with Motivational Interviewing.
  1. Precontemplation – not yet considering change.
  2. Contemplation – weighing it up.
  3. Preparation – getting ready to act.
  4. Action – actively changing.
  5. Maintenance – sustaining the change (with possible relapse and recycling).

6.7 Family and group therapy

Systemic and family therapies treat the family as the unit, not the individual. The idea is that a symptom in one member (the "identified patient") often expresses a problem in the whole family system.

Who → what. Salvador Minuchin developed structural family therapy, mapping the family's structure – its subsystems, boundaries (too rigid or too enmeshed) and hierarchies – and actively restructuring them.
Who → what. Murray Bowen developed Bowenian (intergenerational) family systems therapy, with key ideas of differentiation of self, triangulation, and the multigenerational transmission of patterns (mapped on a genogram).
Who → what. Irvin Yalom is the key name for group therapy, cataloguing the therapeutic factors that make groups heal.

Yalom's therapeutic factors include universality ("I am not the only one"), instillation of hope, imparting information, altruism (helping others helps you), group cohesiveness, interpersonal learning, catharsis, and the corrective recapitulation of the primary family group (the group re-plays and repairs old family dynamics).

Trap. Match the family-therapy founders precisely: Minuchin = structural (boundaries, enmeshment); Bowen = intergenerational (differentiation, triangles, genogram). For group work, the name to give is Irvin Yalom and his therapeutic factors (universality is the most-quoted one).

6.8 The counselling process, and counselling vs psychotherapy

Counselling typically unfolds in identifiable stages. A common way to remember them:

  1. Rapport / relationship building. Establish trust, warmth and the working alliance.
  2. Assessment / exploration. Understand the problem, history and context; clarify what the client wants.
  3. Goal-setting. Agree clear, realistic goals together.
  4. Intervention / working phase. Apply the chosen techniques and support change.
  5. Termination and follow-up. Consolidate gains, prepare for ending, and plan for maintenance and relapse prevention.

Effective counselling rests on micro-skills (systematised by Allen Ivey and echoing Carl Rogers). Learn these named skills:

  • Attending – posture, eye contact and presence (often taught with the SOLER acronym).
  • Active listening.
  • Open and closed questions.
  • Reflection of feeling – naming the emotion the client expresses.
  • Paraphrasing – restating content in your own words.
  • Summarising.
  • Clarifying, confronting (gently), and silence.

How counselling differs from psychotherapy. The two overlap heavily and use many of the same skills, but examiners expect you to state the classic distinctions:

7 Statistics & Research Methodology

Clinical psychology is an empirical science. Every claim it makes – that a therapy works, that a scale measures anxiety, that two symptoms co-occur – rests on data. Statistics is the toolkit for turning messy numbers into defensible conclusions, and research methodology is the discipline of designing studies so those numbers mean what we think they mean. This section builds both from zero, then flags the traps that entrance papers love.

7.1 Why psychology needs statistics

Human behaviour is variable: no two people, and no one person on two days, respond identically. Because of this variability we can never study everyone we care about, and we can never read a result off a single case with certainty. Statistics does two jobs. Descriptive statistics summarize the data we actually collected (averages, spread, shape). Inferential statistics let us reason from a small studied group to the larger group we care about, while honestly quantifying how uncertain that leap is.

Why. Without statistics a therapist could not tell whether clients improved because of the treatment or just by chance (regression to the mean, natural recovery, placebo). Statistics is how psychology separates real effects from noise.

Population vs sample; parameter vs statistic

A population is the entire set of people (or observations) we want to draw conclusions about, for example "all adults with generalized anxiety disorder in India". A sample is the subset we actually measure. A numerical fact about a population is a parameter (usually unknown, written with Greek letters: the population mean is mu, the population standard deviation is sigma). The matching numerical fact computed from a sample is a statistic (the sample mean is x-bar, the sample standard deviation is s). Inferential statistics is essentially using statistics to estimate parameters.

Key. Parameter → population (mu, sigma, rho). Statistic → sample (x-bar, s, r). We compute statistics because parameters are almost always unknowable in full.

Types of variables

A variable is any characteristic that can take different values. How we may analyse a variable depends first on its scale of measurement, a scheme owed to the psychophysicist Stanley Smith Stevens.

Trap. A true zero means "you can say twice as much". 40 kg is twice 20 kg (ratio), but 40 °C is NOT twice as hot as 20 °C because 0 °C is arbitrary (interval). IQ and most psychological test scores are treated as interval, not ratio: an IQ of 0 does not mean "no intelligence", so "twice as intelligent" is meaningless.

By role in a study: the independent variable (IV) is the presumed cause, the one the experimenter manipulates or selects (therapy type). The dependent variable (DV) is the presumed effect, the outcome measured (symptom score). A confounding variable is a third variable that rides along with the IV and offers a rival explanation for changes in the DV (if the therapy group is also younger, age confounds the result). By values a variable can take: a discrete variable takes separated, usually countable values (number of panic attacks); a continuous variable can take any value within a range, limited only by measurement precision (reaction time, height).

Who → what. Stanley Smith Stevens → the nominal / ordinal / interval / ratio taxonomy of measurement scales (1946), the backbone of choosing a statistical test.

7.2 Descriptive statistics: centre and spread

Two questions describe almost any batch of scores: where is the middle, and how spread out are they?

Measures of central tendency

  • Mean (arithmetic average): add all scores, divide by the number of scores. Uses every value, so it is the most informative for symmetric data, but it is pulled toward extreme scores.
  • Median: the middle value when scores are ordered (average of the two middle values if the count is even). It ignores the size of extreme scores, so it is robust to outliers and skew.
  • Mode: the most frequent value. The only centre usable for nominal data; a distribution can have two modes (bimodal) or none unique.
SituationBest centreReason
Symmetric, interval/ratio, no outliersMeanUses all information, feeds later tests
Skewed data or outliers (income, reaction time)MedianNot dragged by the tail
Nominal / categorical dataModeOnly "average" that is meaningful for categories

Effect of skew. In a symmetric distribution mean = median = mode. In a positively (right) skewed distribution (a long tail of high scores), the order is mode < median < mean: the mean is dragged rightward by the tail. In a negatively (left) skewed distribution the order reverses: mean < median < mode. A useful memory hook is that the mean chases the tail.

Measures of dispersion

  • Range: highest minus lowest score. Trivial to compute but decided entirely by the two most extreme scores, so it is unstable.
  • Interquartile range (IQR): the spread of the middle 50% of the data (third quartile minus first quartile, Q3 – Q1). Robust to outliers; it is the "box" in a box plot and the natural partner of the median.
  • Variance: the mean of the squared deviations from the mean. Squaring removes the sign so deviations do not cancel, but it leaves the units squared (points-squared), which is hard to interpret.
  • Standard deviation (SD): the square root of the variance, so it returns to the original units. It is the single most used index of spread: roughly, the typical distance of a score from the mean.
Why. We square deviations rather than just averaging them because raw deviations from the mean always sum to zero (the positives and negatives cancel exactly). Squaring, then taking the root at the end, gives a spread measure in the original units.
Trap. Mean vs median under skew. Report the median, not the mean, for skewed variables such as income, hospital length-of-stay, or reaction time. Newspapers quoting "average income" as the mean overstate the typical person because a few very high earners inflate the mean; the median (the person in the middle) is the honest "typical" value. Same logic in clinical data: one client with a 900-day illness duration can make the "average" duration meaningless.

7.3 The normal distribution

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the bell-shaped curve that approximates many natural and psychological variables (height, IQ, measurement error). Its properties:

  • Symmetric about the mean; mean, median, and mode coincide at the centre.
  • Bell-shaped and asymptotic: the tails approach the horizontal axis but never touch it, so extreme scores are rare but never strictly impossible.
  • Fully defined by just two parameters: the mean (location) and the standard deviation (width).
  • Total area under the curve equals 1 (100% of cases).

The 68–95–99.7 rule (the empirical rule)

In any normal distribution, about 68% of scores fall within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% within 2 SD (more precisely 1.96 SD), and about 99.7% within 3 SD. On an IQ test with mean 100 and SD 15, roughly 95% of people score between 70 and 130, and a score above 145 is beyond 3 SD, seen in well under 1% of people.

z-scores and standardization

A z-score restates a raw score as the number of standard deviations it lies above or below the mean: z = (score – mean) / standard deviation. Converting a whole distribution to z-scores is standardization; the result is the standard normal distribution, which has mean 0 and SD 1. Standardizing lets us compare scores from different scales (a z of +1.5 on a depression scale and +1.5 on an anxiety scale are equally extreme) and lets us look up the exact proportion of the curve beyond any point.

Key. A z-score is a location on the curve, not a probability. Its sign gives direction (above / below the mean) and its magnitude gives how unusual the score is. z = 0 is exactly average; |z| > 2 is unusual (top / bottom ~2.5% each tail).

Skewness and kurtosis

Skewness measures asymmetry: positive skew has a long right tail, negative skew a long left tail, zero skew is symmetric. Kurtosis measures tail-heaviness / peakedness: a leptokurtic curve is tall and thin with heavy tails (more extreme scores than normal), a platykurtic curve is flat with thin tails, and a mesokurtic curve matches the normal. These shape indices tell us whether it is safe to assume normality before running parametric tests.

Other distributions in brief

The binomial distribution models the number of successes in a fixed number of yes/no trials (correct vs incorrect items). The Poisson distribution models counts of rare events in a fixed interval (relapses per year). The t distribution is bell-shaped but with fatter tails than the normal and is used for small samples; as sample size grows it converges to the normal. The chi-square and F distributions are skewed and underlie tests of frequencies and of variances, respectively.

7.4 Correlation

Correlation quantifies how strongly two variables move together, on a scale from –1 through 0 to +1. The sign gives direction: positive means they rise together, negative means one rises as the other falls. The magnitude gives strength: 0 means no linear relationship, and ±1 means a perfect straight-line relationship. A scatterplot (each case a dot with its two scores as coordinates) is the first thing to inspect: it shows direction, strength, linearity, and outliers at a glance.

Karl Pearson's product-moment correlation (r)

Pearson's r measures the strength of the linear association between two interval or ratio variables that are roughly normally distributed. Rough interpretation of magnitude (after Jacob Cohen's conventions): about 0.1 is small / weak, about 0.3 is medium / moderate, about 0.5 and above is large / strong. Squaring it gives the coefficient of determination (r-squared), the proportion of variance in one variable explained by the other; r = 0.4 means only 16% shared variance.

Who → what. Karl Pearson → the product-moment correlation coefficient r, the standard index of linear association for continuous data.

Charles Spearman's rank-order correlation (rho)

Spearman's rho is the correlation computed on the ranks of the scores rather than the raw values. Use it when the data are ordinal, when the relationship is monotonic but not straight-line, or when outliers or non-normality make Pearson's r untrustworthy. Because it works on ranks, it is a non-parametric cousin of Pearson's r.

Who → what. Charles Spearman → rank-order correlation (rho) for ordinal / non-normal data (and, separately, the two-factor theory of intelligence, the "g" factor).
Trap. Correlation is not causation. A strong r tells you two variables travel together, not that one causes the other. Three rival explanations always remain: reverse causation (B causes A), a lurking third variable causing both (ice-cream sales and drownings both rise with summer heat), and pure coincidence. Only a controlled experiment can establish causation. Also beware: r near 0 rules out a linear relationship only; a strong U-shaped (curvilinear) relationship can have r near 0.

7.5 Inferential logic: from sample to population

Inference is the reasoned leap from the sample we measured to the population we care about. The machinery has a few core ideas.

Sampling distribution and standard error

Imagine drawing many samples of the same size and computing the mean of each. Those means themselves form a distribution, the sampling distribution of the mean. The Central Limit Theorem states that, as sample size grows, this sampling distribution becomes approximately normal regardless of the shape of the original population, which is why so many tests can assume normality. The standard deviation of that sampling distribution is the standard error (SE): it measures how much a sample mean typically bounces around the true population mean. Crucially, SE shrinks as sample size grows (SE = SD divided by the square root of n), which is the mathematical reason larger studies give more precise estimates.

Null and alternative hypotheses

We frame a testable question as two rival statements. The null hypothesis (H0) says there is no effect / no difference / no relationship (the therapy does nothing; the two group means are equal). The alternative hypothesis (H1) says there is an effect. Significance testing is a proof-by-contradiction: we assume H0 is true and ask how surprising our data would be under that assumption.

Alpha, the p-value, and confidence intervals

  • The significance level (alpha) is the threshold of surprise we set in advance for rejecting H0, conventionally 0.05 (5%). It is the risk of a false alarm we are willing to tolerate.
  • The p-value is the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as ours if H0 were true. If p is less than alpha, we reject H0 and call the result "statistically significant".
  • A confidence interval (CI) is a range of plausible values for the parameter. A 95% CI means that if we repeated the study endlessly, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true value. A 95% CI for a difference that excludes 0 corresponds to a significant result at alpha 0.05.
Trap. The p-value is NOT the probability that H0 is true, and it is NOT the probability the result was due to chance. It is the probability of the data given H0. A result can be statistically significant (small p) yet trivially small in real-world terms if the sample is huge, which is exactly why we also report effect size. Conversely, "p > 0.05" means "failed to reject H0", NOT "H0 is proven true": absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Type I and Type II error

H0 is really TRUEH0 is really FALSE
We reject H0Type I error (false positive), probability = alphaCorrect: true detection (power = 1 – beta)
We keep H0Correct: true negative (1 – alpha)Type II error (false negative), probability = beta

A Type I error is a false alarm: claiming an effect that is not real (convicting an innocent H0). A Type II error is a miss: failing to detect an effect that is real. Lowering alpha to guard against Type I error raises the risk of Type II error, so the two trade off. In drug trials a Type I error approves a useless drug; a Type II error shelves a working one.

Statistical power and effect size

Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting a false H0, that is, of detecting a real effect (power = 1 – beta). Convention aims for power of at least 0.80. Power rises with a larger sample, a larger true effect, a higher alpha, and lower measurement noise. Effect size is a scale-free measure of how big an effect actually is, independent of sample size. Jacob Cohen formalized this; Cohen's d expresses the difference between two means in standard-deviation units, with rough benchmarks of 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), and 0.8 (large). Correlation r is itself an effect size, and eta-squared serves the same role for ANOVA.

Who → what. Jacob Cohen → effect size and power analysis in psychology; Cohen's d, and the small / medium / large conventions that let us judge practical (not just statistical) importance.
Key. Significance answers "is there likely an effect?"; effect size answers "how big is it?"; power answers "was my study large enough to find it?". A complete result reports all three, plus a confidence interval.

The t-test family (William Gosset)

The t-test compares means when the population standard deviation is unknown and the sample is small; it uses the fatter-tailed t distribution to stay honest under that extra uncertainty. Its three forms are the one-sample (one mean vs a fixed value), the independent-samples (two unrelated groups), and the paired-samples (two measurements on the same or matched subjects).

Who → what. William Gosset (Student) → the t distribution and t-test, developed while doing quality control at the Guinness brewery; he published under the pen name "Student" because his employer forbade staff from publishing.

Analysis of variance (Ronald Fisher)

ANOVA extends the logic of the t-test to three or more groups in one test, avoiding the inflated Type I error you would get from running many t-tests. It works by partitioning total variability into between-groups variance (differences among group means) and within-groups variance (noise inside each group); the F ratio is between-groups over within-groups. A large F says the group means differ by more than chance noise predicts. A significant ANOVA says "at least one group differs" but not which, so a post-hoc test (Tukey's HSD, Bonferroni, Scheffe) then pinpoints the specific pairs.

Who → what. Ronald Fisher → analysis of variance (ANOVA), the F distribution, the null-hypothesis testing framework, and the principle of randomization in experimental design.

Chi-square

The chi-square test works on frequencies / counts of categorical data. Goodness of fit checks whether one variable's observed frequencies match an expected pattern (are diagnoses evenly distributed across seasons?). The test of independence checks whether two categorical variables are associated (is treatment response related to gender?). Both compare observed counts to the counts expected if H0 were true.

Parametric tests (z, t, ANOVA, Pearson's r) assume the data are interval/ratio, roughly normally distributed, and (for group comparisons) have similar variances (homogeneity of variance) and independent observations. 7.7 Research methodology: the scientific method and designs

The scientific method and steps of research

  1. Identify the problem and review existing literature.
  2. Formulate a clear, testable hypothesis and define concepts operationally (state exactly how each variable will be measured).
  3. Choose a research design and sampling method.
  4. Collect data using reliable, valid instruments.
  5. Analyse the data with appropriate statistics.
  6. Interpret results, draw conclusions, and relate them back to the hypothesis and theory.
  7. Report and disseminate; replication and peer review test the finding's durability.
Why. An operational definition (for example, "depression = score above 13 on the Beck Depression Inventory") is what makes a study repeatable and testable. Without it, two researchers studying "depression" might measure completely different things.

Research designs

  • True experiment: the researcher manipulates the IV and uses random assignment to conditions. This is the only design that supports strong causal claims, because randomization equalizes confounders across groups on average.
  • Quasi-experiment: has an IV manipulation or group comparison but lacks random assignment (comparing existing intact groups such as two schools). Causal inference is weaker because groups may differ at the start.
  • Correlational: measures variables as they naturally occur and assesses association without manipulation. Good for prediction and for variables that cannot ethically be manipulated, but cannot establish cause.
  • Case study: intensive, in-depth study of a single person, group, or event. Rich and hypothesis-generating (Sigmund Freud's cases, patient H.M.), but not generalizable and open to observer bias.
  • Survey: self-report from a sample via questionnaire or interview; efficient for attitudes and prevalence, but vulnerable to response biases (social desirability, acquiescence).
  • Observational: watching and recording behaviour, either naturalistic (in the field, no interference) or controlled (in a set-up like a lab playroom); high realism but limited control.
  • Longitudinal: the same people followed over time (tracks true within-person change and development, but slow and prone to participant drop-out / attrition). Cross-sectional: different age or cohort groups compared at one time point (fast and cheap, but age differences can be confounded with cohort / generation effects).

Internal vs external validity

Internal validity is the degree to which we can be sure the IV, and not something else, caused the change in the DV. External validity is the degree to which the findings generalize beyond the study to other people, settings, and times. The two often trade off: tight laboratory control boosts internal validity but the artificial setting can hurt external validity.

Common threats to internal validity include history (an outside event during the study), maturation (natural change over time), testing (practice effects from being measured before), instrumentation (the measuring tool drifting), regression to the mean (extreme scorers drifting toward average on retest), selection (pre-existing group differences), and attrition / mortality (non-random drop-out). Threats to external validity include unrepresentative samples (over-reliance on WEIRD or undergraduate samples) and artificial settings.

Control techniques

  • Randomization (random assignment): allocating participants to conditions by chance so that confounders are spread evenly across groups; the single most powerful control.
  • Blinding: keeping participants (single-blind), or both participants and the researchers who interact with them (double-blind), unaware of condition, to block placebo and experimenter-expectancy effects.
  • Counterbalancing: in within-subjects designs, varying the order of conditions across participants so that practice and fatigue (order effects) do not systematically favour one condition.
  • Standardization: giving every participant the same instructions, environment, and procedure to hold extraneous variables constant.

7.8 Sampling

How we pick the sample decides whether it fairly represents the population. Methods split into two families.

Probability sampling (every member has a known, non-zero chance of selection; supports generalization and error estimation):

  • Simple random sampling: every individual has an equal chance (names from a hat, random number generator). The gold standard for representativeness.
  • Systematic sampling: pick every k-th person from a list after a random start. Easy, but risky if the list has a hidden periodic pattern.
  • Stratified sampling: divide the population into meaningful subgroups (strata) such as age or gender, then sample randomly within each, guaranteeing each stratum is represented in proportion.
  • Cluster sampling: divide the population into naturally occurring clusters (schools, clinics), randomly select whole clusters, and study everyone (or a sample) inside. Cheaper for geographically spread populations, but less precise.

Non-probability sampling (selection chances unknown; convenient but hard to generalize from):

  • Convenience sampling: whoever is easiest to reach (psychology undergraduates). Fast but biased.
  • Purposive (judgmental) sampling: the researcher hand-picks cases fitting a criterion (experts, a specific diagnosis).
  • Snowball sampling: existing participants recruit others they know; used for hidden or hard-to-reach populations (people who use illicit drugs).
  • Quota sampling: fill preset quotas for subgroups (50 men, 50 women) but choose within each non-randomly.
Trap. Do not confuse the two errors. Sampling error is the ordinary, random gap between a sample statistic and the true parameter simply because a sample is not the whole population; it shrinks as sample size grows. Sampling bias is a systematic tilt from a flawed selection method (a convenience sample, or non-response); it does NOT shrink with a larger sample – a bigger biased sample is just a bigger biased sample. Only better sampling design, not more people, fixes bias.

7.9 Reliability, validity, qualitative methods, and ethics

Reliability and validity of research and measures

Reliability is consistency: does the measure give the same result under the same conditions? Its forms are test-retest (stable across time), inter-rater (different observers agree), internal consistency (items hang together, indexed by Cronbach's alpha), and parallel-forms (two versions agree). Validity is accuracy: does the measure actually assess what it claims? Its forms include content validity (items cover the whole construct), criterion validity (correlates with an external benchmark, split into concurrent and predictive), and construct validity (fits the theoretical network, with convergent and discriminant evidence).

Key. A measure can be reliable without being valid, but not valid without being reliable. A miscalibrated scale that always reads 5 kg heavy is perfectly reliable (consistent) yet invalid (wrong). Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

Qualitative methods in brief

Where quantitative research counts and measures, qualitative research seeks meaning in words and experience. Thematic analysis codes textual data and groups the codes into recurring themes. Grounded theory builds a theory upward from the data through iterative coding, letting categories emerge rather than testing a pre-set hypothesis. Phenomenology aims to describe the essence of participants' lived experience of a phenomenon from their own point of view. These methods prize depth, context, and the participant's voice over generalizable numbers.

Research ethics

  • Informed consent: participants agree to take part after being told the study's purpose, procedures, risks, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Special provisions cover minors and those unable to consent.
  • Confidentiality and anonymity: personal data are protected, and identities are kept private or removed from records.
  • Protection from harm: physical and psychological risk is minimized and justified by the study's value.
  • Debriefing: after participation (especially if deception was used and justified), participants are fully informed of the true purpose and any deception is explained.
  • Institutional review: an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or ethics committee must approve the study before data collection, weighing risks against benefits.
  • Professional guidelines: the American Psychological Association (APA) Ethics Code (and in India the Rehabilitation Council of India norms) set standards for integrity, avoiding harm, and honest reporting of results.
Trap. Deception is permitted only when a study cannot be done otherwise, the risk is low, and it is followed by a full debrief; it never overrides the duty to protect participants. Consent obtained by hiding real risks is not informed consent. And confidentiality has limits: a clinician-researcher who learns of imminent danger to self or others may have a duty to breach it.
Recap. Statistics turns variable behaviour into defensible conclusions. Describe data with the right centre (mean for symmetric, median for skewed, mode for categories) and spread (SD, IQR). The normal curve plus z-scores let us locate and compare scores (68–95–99.7). Correlation (Karl Pearson's r for continuous, Charles Spearman's rho for ranks) measures association but never proves causation. Inference rests on sampling distributions and the standard error; we test a null hypothesis against an alpha, read a p-value and confidence interval, and weigh Type I against Type II error, always reporting effect size (Jacob Cohen's d) and power alongside significance. Pick the test by scale and design: William Gosset's t-tests for one or two means, Ronald Fisher's ANOVA for three or more, chi-square for frequencies, and non-parametric equivalents when assumptions fail. Sound methodology (true experiments with randomization, blinding, and counterbalancing), representative probability sampling, reliable and valid measures, and firm ethics (informed consent, confidentiality, debriefing, IRB approval) are what make the numbers trustworthy in the first place.

Key. The examiner is testing associations: founder → school, experiment → theorist, effect → discoverer. Learn psychology as a set of linked pairs and the general-awareness block becomes free marks.

8.1 History & Schools of Psychology

Psychology as a formal science is usually dated to 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt opened the first psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig, Germany. Before that, questions about the mind belonged to philosophy and physiology. Wundt made the mind a subject for controlled experiment, which is why he is called the "father of experimental psychology". After Wundt, psychology split into competing schools of thought, each with a founder and one core idea. The table below is the single most examined item in this whole section.

FunctionalismWilliam JamesStudy what the mind is for – how mental processes help us adapt. His book "The Principles of Psychology" (1890) launched it.
GestaltMax Wertheimer, Wolfgang Kohler, Kurt KoffkaPerception is organised into wholes: "the whole is other than the sum of its parts". Studied insight and perceptual grouping.

Trap. Wundt is the founder of the lab, but the word "structuralism" and the word "introspection" as a formal method are most tightly associated with his student Edward Titchener. If a question asks who named structuralism, the answer is Titchener, not Wundt.
Trap. The Gestalt slogan is "the whole is other than the sum of its parts" (Kurt Koffka's careful wording), often loosely quoted as "greater than". Either phrasing points to Gestalt, never to structuralism, which does the opposite by breaking wholes into elements.
Why. These schools are not just history. Modern clinical practice inherits directly from them: behaviourism → behaviour therapy; cognitive → cognitive therapy; psychoanalysis → psychodynamic therapy; humanistic → person-centred therapy. Knowing the parent school helps you place any therapy question.

8.2 Learning: Classical Conditioning

Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour due to experience. The oldest experimental model is classical conditioning, discovered by the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov while studying dog digestion. A neutral stimulus that is repeatedly paired with a biologically important one comes to trigger the same response.

  • Unconditioned stimulus (US): food, which naturally causes salivation.
  • Unconditioned response (UR): salivation to food (unlearned).
  • Conditioned stimulus (CS): the bell, after pairing with food.
  • Conditioned response (CR): salivation to the bell alone (learned).

Four processes are examined constantly:

  • Acquisition: the CS and US are paired and the CR is built up.
  • Extinction: the CS is presented without the US, and the CR fades.
  • Spontaneous recovery: after a rest, an extinguished CR briefly reappears.
  • Generalization: stimuli similar to the CS also trigger the CR (a bell of a slightly different pitch).
  • Discrimination: the organism learns to respond only to the exact CS, not to similar ones.

John B. Watson extended classical conditioning to humans in the famous "Little Albert" experiment (1920). He paired a white rat (neutral) with a loud, frightening noise (US) until the infant Albert became afraid of the rat (CR), and the fear generalized to other furry objects. This showed emotions themselves can be conditioned.

Trap. Pavlov → dogs, salivation, classical conditioning (involuntary reflex). Skinner → rats/pigeons, levers, operant conditioning (voluntary behaviour + consequences). Do not swap them. Watson used classical conditioning on Little Albert.

8.3 Learning: Operant Conditioning

Operant conditioning explains how consequences shape voluntary behaviour. Its groundwork was laid by Edward Thorndike, whose law of effect states that behaviours followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened, and those followed by unpleasant ones are weakened (from his puzzle-box experiments with cats). B.F. Skinner formalised this using the "Skinner box", coining the terms reinforcement and punishment.

ConsequenceAdd a stimulus (positive)Remove a stimulus (negative)
Reinforcement (behaviour increases)Positive reinforcement: give a reward praise, food pelletNegative reinforcement: remove something unpleasant seatbelt beep stops
Punishment (behaviour decreases)Positive punishment: add something unpleasant a scoldingNegative punishment: take away something pleasant lose phone privileges
Trap. "Negative" does not mean bad, and "positive" does not mean good. Positive = add a stimulus; negative = remove a stimulus. Negative reinforcement increases behaviour (it is still reinforcement); only punishment decreases behaviour.

Schedules of reinforcement govern how often a response is rewarded:

  • Fixed ratio: reward after a set number of responses pay per 10 items made.
  • Variable ratio: reward after an unpredictable number of responses; produces the highest, most persistent responding slot machines, gambling.
  • Fixed interval: reward for the first response after a set time a weekly paycheck.
  • Variable interval: reward for the first response after an unpredictable time checking for a text reply.
Key. Variable-ratio schedules give the highest and most extinction-resistant response rate. This is the classic MCQ answer for "which schedule is hardest to extinguish".

8.4 Learning: Observational Learning

Not all learning needs direct reinforcement. Albert Bandura showed we learn by watching others, in his Bobo doll experiment: children who watched an adult act aggressively toward an inflatable Bobo doll later imitated that aggression. This is the basis of his social learning theory (later "social cognitive theory"), which adds thinking and expectation to behaviourism.

Bandura identified four steps for observational learning: attention (notice the model), retention (remember it), reproduction (be able to perform it), and motivation (a reason to do it, often via vicarious reinforcement – seeing the model rewarded).

Who → what. Pavlov → classical conditioning. Thorndike → law of effect (puzzle box). Skinner → operant conditioning + schedules. Bandura → observational learning + Bobo doll.

8.5 Memory

Memory is examined through its models and its failures. The dominant framework is the multi-store model of Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin (1968), which proposes three stores information flows through.

  • Sensory memory: very brief, large capacity; holds raw sensory input for under a second (iconic for vision, echoic for sound).
  • Short-term memory (STM): holds about 7 ± 2 items (George Miller's "magical number seven") for ~15–30 seconds; kept alive by rehearsal.
  • Long-term memory (LTM): effectively unlimited capacity and duration.

Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch (1974) replaced the passive STM idea with working memory, an active system with a central executive directing two slave systems: the phonological loop (verbal/acoustic material) and the visuospatial sketchpad (visual/spatial material); Baddeley later added the episodic buffer.

Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart (1972) proposed the levels of processing theory: memory strength depends on depth of processing, not on stores. Shallow processing (how a word looks) gives weak memory; deep semantic processing (what a word means) gives strong memory.

For types of LTM, Endel Tulving distinguished:

  • Episodic memory: personal events, tied to time and place your last birthday.
  • Semantic memory: general facts and meanings the capital of France.
  • Procedural memory: skills and how-to knowledge riding a bicycle; part of implicit (non-declarative) memory.

Forgetting was first measured by Hermann Ebbinghaus, whose forgetting curve showed memory drops sharply soon after learning, then levels off. Explanations include decay (traces fade with time), interference (proactive: old learning blocks new; retroactive: new learning blocks old), and retrieval failure (cues missing).

Trap. STM capacity (7±2) is George Miller; the multi-store model is Atkinson & Shiffrin; working memory is Baddeley & Hitch; levels of processing is Craik & Lockhart; episodic/semantic memory is Tulving; the forgetting curve is Ebbinghaus. These five pairs are a favourite matching question.

8.6 Motivation & Emotion

Motivation is what energizes and directs behaviour. Key theories:

  • Maslow's hierarchy of needs (Abraham Maslow): needs are arranged in a pyramid – physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem, and self-actualization at the top. Lower needs must be reasonably met before higher ones drive behaviour.
  • Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull): a physiological need creates a drive (tension); behaviour aims to reduce the drive and restore homeostasis.
  • Henry Murray's needs: a list of psychological needs (achievement, affiliation, power, autonomy). Murray also created the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) to measure them.
  • Intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation: intrinsic = doing something for its own satisfaction; extrinsic = doing it for an external reward or to avoid punishment.

Emotion theories are a guaranteed table question. They differ on the order of the physical arousal, the felt emotion, and the thinking.

TheoryTheorist(s)Core claim (order of events)

Cannon-BardWalter Cannon & Philip BardArousal and emotion happen at the same time and independently.

8.7 Intelligence & Cognition (capstone)

Assessment is covered in its own section, so here we only need the concepts. Intelligence is broadly the capacity to learn, reason, and adapt to the environment. Charles Spearman proposed a general factor, "g", underlying all mental abilities, plus specific factors ("s"). The g debate pits this single-factor view against multiple-intelligence views (Howard Gardner's multiple intelligences, Robert Sternberg's triarchic theory). The nature–nurture debate asks how much intelligence is inherited versus shaped by environment; the modern answer is that both interact.

Thinking and problem-solving use mental shortcuts called heuristics. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed these shortcuts produce systematic biases:

  • Availability heuristic: judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind.
  • Representativeness heuristic: judging by how well something matches a stereotype, ignoring base rates.
  • Anchoring: relying too heavily on the first piece of information.
  • Confirmation bias: seeking evidence that supports what we already believe.
Key. Heuristics and biases → Kahneman & Tversky. Kahneman's "System 1" (fast, automatic) versus "System 2" (slow, effortful) thinking is a common one-liner.

8.8 Social Psychology Core

Social psychology studies how people influence one another. It contributes several of the most famous experiments in the discipline, each welded to one name.

  • Attitudes & persuasion: an attitude has cognitive, affective and behavioural components. Persuasion is analysed via the elaboration likelihood model (central route = arguments; peripheral route = cues like attractiveness).
  • Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger): the discomfort of holding two conflicting beliefs, or acting against a belief, drives us to change an attitude to restore consistency.
  • Conformity (Solomon Asch): in the line-judgment study, people gave obviously wrong answers to match a unanimous group – normative social influence.
  • Obedience (Stanley Milgram): participants delivered what they believed were dangerous shocks because an authority figure told them to; showed obedience to authority.
  • Bystander effect (John Darley & Bibb Latane): the more bystanders present, the less likely any one person helps, due to diffusion of responsibility.
  • Attribution theory (Fritz Heider): we explain behaviour by internal (dispositional) or external (situational) causes. The fundamental attribution error is overusing internal causes for others' behaviour while excusing our own as situational.
  • Prejudice: a negative attitude toward a group; linked to stereotyping and discrimination.
  • Groupthink (Irving Janis): the drive for group harmony overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives, leading to poor decisions.
Trap. Asch = conformity (lines, peer group). Milgram = obedience (shocks, authority). Do not swap them: conformity is yielding to peers, obedience is following an authority's command.
Who → what. Festinger → cognitive dissonance. Asch → conformity. Milgram → obedience. Darley & Latane → bystander effect. Heider → attribution. Janis → groupthink.

8.9 Indian Psychology & Context

SGT is an Indian university, so a few Indian-context items appear. Modern scientific psychology in India began with the first laboratory and department at Calcutta University (1916). The figure most examined is Durganand Sinha, a pioneer who charted the growth of psychology in India, pushed for indigenization (psychology rooted in Indian realities rather than borrowed wholesale from the West), and wrote on the psychology of poverty and rural life.

Papers also test survey-level familiarity with concepts drawn from Indian thought:

  • The three gunas (from Samkhya philosophy and the Bhagavad Gita): sattva (purity, balance, clarity), rajas (activity, passion, restlessness), and tamas (inertia, dullness, darkness). Personality and mental states are seen as a mix of these qualities.
  • Yoga psychology: Patanjali's Yoga Sutras describe the mind (chitta) and its modifications (vrittis), and a path of mental discipline and self-regulation toward samadhi; a forerunner of ideas about attention and consciousness.
  • The self in Indian philosophy: the distinction between the empirical self (ego, ahamkara) and the deeper true self (atman), and the goal of self-realization – a more spiritual, holistic view of the person than Western trait models.

Finally, the regulation of the profession. The Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI), a statutory body under the Ministry of Social Justice & Empowerment, regulates the training and practice of clinical psychologists in India. It maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register, and a clinical psychologist must hold an RCI-recognised qualification (such as the M.Phil in Clinical Psychology, now transitioning to RCI-approved MA/PsyD routes) and register with the RCI to practise legally.

Key. For the Indian block, lock in three answers: Durganand Sinha → indigenization / growth of Indian psychology; three gunas → sattva, rajas, tamas; RCI → the statutory body that licenses clinical psychologists in India.
Recap. Psychology became a science with Wilhelm Wundt's 1879 lab, then split into schools – structuralism, functionalism, Gestalt, behaviourism, psychoanalysis, humanistic, cognitive – each pinned to a founder. Learning runs from Pavlov's classical conditioning through Skinner's operant conditioning (positive/negative reinforcement and punishment, variable-ratio being hardest to extinguish) to Bandura's observational learning. Memory is the Atkinson-Shiffrin stores, Baddeley-Hitch working memory, Craik-Lockhart levels of processing, Tulving's episodic/semantic/procedural, and Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve. Motivation spans Maslow's hierarchy, Hull's drive reduction and Murray's needs; emotion is the James-Lange / Cannon-Bard / Schachter-Singer / Lazarus / Ekman table. Cognition adds the g debate and Kahneman-Tversky heuristics. Social psychology is a set of name-experiment pairs: Festinger dissonance, Asch conformity, Milgram obedience, Darley-Latane bystander, Heider attribution. For India, remember Durganand Sinha, the three gunas, and the RCI. Learn every idea as a name-attached pair and this section becomes your strongest scoring block.

  1. Lock the founder pairings.
