1 · DSM-5-TR vs ICD-11

Both are diagnostic systems; they classify the same disorders but differ in author, purpose and structure. Know the contrasts, not just the names.

High-yield contrasts
• PTSD: ICD-11 is tighter — only re-experiencing in the here-and-now, avoidance, and a sense of current threat. ICD-11 also adds Complex PTSD (PTSD + disturbances in self-organisation: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship problems).
• Schizophrenia duration: DSM-5 needs 6 months (incl. prodrome/residual); ICD-11 needs 1 month of active symptoms.
• New in ICD-11: Gaming disorder, prolonged grief disorder, compulsive sexual behaviour disorder. New in DSM-5-TR: prolonged grief disorder (added too), unspecified mood/suicidal-behaviour codes.
• Bereavement: DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion for MDD (you can diagnose depression even during grief).
Trap: "Which system is dimensional for personality?" → ICD-11. DSM-5's main text is still categorical (the dimensional Alternative Model lives in Section III, not the official one).

2 · Dissociative disorders

Core theme: a disruption of the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception or body representation. DSM-5 recognises three:

  • Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID) — two or more distinct personality states + recurrent gaps in recall. (Formerly "multiple personality disorder".)
  • Dissociative Amnesia — inability to recall important autobiographical info, usually traumatic. With dissociative fugue = amnesia + purposeful travel/bewildered wandering.
  • Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder — persistent detachment, with intact reality testing.
Trap: Depersonalization = detachment from your own self (feeling unreal, robotic, watching yourself). Derealization = detachment from surroundings (the world feels dreamlike, foggy, unreal). Reality testing stays intact — that's what separates it from psychosis.

Positive vs negative symptoms. Positive = added on (delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech/behaviour). Negative = taken away (the 5 A's: Avolition, Alogia, Anhedonia, Affective flattening, Asociality). Negative symptoms predict worse prognosis and respond least to antipsychotics.
NIMHANS: Know Schneider's first-rank symptoms (thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcasting, audible thoughts, running commentary, delusional perception, passivity). DSM-5 dropped the classic subtypes (paranoid, catatonic, hebephrenic, residual) — they're now specifiers/dimensions, not separate diagnoses.

4 · Neurodevelopmental disorders

Onset in the developmental period. The DSM-5 cluster: Intellectual Disability, Communication disorders, Autism Spectrum Disorder, ADHD, Specific Learning Disorder, Motor disorders (incl. tics / Tourette's).

DSM-5 changes to know: Asperger's, autistic disorder and PDD-NOS were merged into one ASD with severity levels 1–3 (1 = "requiring support", 3 = "requiring very substantial support"). ID severity is now set by adaptive functioning, not IQ score alone.
Trap: ADHD presentations are predominantly inattentive / predominantly hyperactive-impulsive / combined — not "subtypes". Onset of several symptoms must be before age 12 (DSM-5 raised it from 7).

6 · Factor analysis

A data-reduction technique: takes many correlated variables and finds a smaller set of underlying latent factors that explain the shared variance.

  • EFA (exploratory) — you don't know the structure, you let the data reveal it. CFA (confirmatory) — you test a hypothesised structure (a measurement model, via SEM).
  • Extraction: Kaiser criterion keeps factors with eigenvalue > 1; cross-check with the scree plot (keep factors above the "elbow").
  • Factor loadings = correlation of a variable with a factor (≥ .30–.40 is meaningful).
  • Rotation: Varimax = orthogonal (factors assumed uncorrelated); Oblimin/Promax = oblique (factors allowed to correlate). Rotation makes loadings interpretable, it doesn't change fit.
  • Adequacy checks: KMO (> .60 ok) and Bartlett's test of sphericity (must be significant).
Trap: Factor analysis vs PCA — PCA explains total variance and is pure data reduction; factor analysis models only the shared (common) variance and assumes latent causes. Exams love this distinction.

8 · t-tests

Compares two means and asks whether the difference is bigger than you'd expect by chance. Three flavours:

  • One-sample — sample mean vs a known/hypothesised value. df = n − 1.
  • Independent-samples — two separate groups. df = n₁ + n₂ − 2.
  • Paired / dependent — same people twice (pre/post) or matched pairs. df = n − 1 (n = number of pairs).
Assumptions: interval/ratio data, normality, homogeneity of variance (Levene's test; if violated use Welch's t), independence of observations. Violate them badly → use the non-parametric cousin (Mann–Whitney U for independent, Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired).
Trap: ≥ 3 groups → not a t-test, use ANOVA (running many t-tests inflates Type I error). A t-test is just ANOVA with two groups (t² = F).

9 · Sampling distributions

The distribution of a statistic (usually the mean) over all possible samples of size n from a population. It's the bridge from one sample to a probability statement about the population.

  • Standard error (SE) = SD / √n — the SD of the sampling distribution. Bigger n → smaller SE → more precise estimate.
  • Central Limit Theorem: as n grows, the sampling distribution of the mean approaches normal regardless of the population's shape (rule of thumb n ≥ 30). This is why parametric tests work even on non-normal data with big samples.
Trap: Don't confuse SD (spread of raw scores) with SE (spread of sample means). SE is always smaller and shrinks with √n; SD doesn't shrink as you collect more data.

10 · Regression

Predicts a continuous outcome (Y) from one (simple) or more (multiple) predictors (X), via the least-squares line that minimises the squared residuals.

  • Equation: Ŷ = a + bX — a = intercept, b = slope (change in Y per 1-unit change in X).
  • R² = proportion of variance in Y explained by the model (the coefficient of determination; = r² in simple regression).
  • Standardised β lets you compare predictors' strength on a common scale.
Assumptions (LINE): Linearity, Independence of errors, Normality of residuals, Equal variance (homoscedasticity). Multiple regression adds: no severe multicollinearity (check VIF).
Trap: Correlation ≠ causation, and a high R² doesn't mean the model is causal or that predictions extrapolate beyond the data range.

11 · CBT · operant conditioning · behaviour modification

CBT

Targets the link between thoughts → feelings → behaviour; change maladaptive cognitions and you change the emotion and behaviour. Two roots:

  • Beck (Cognitive Therapy): the cognitive triad (negative views of self, world, future), cognitive distortions (catastrophising, all-or-nothing, overgeneralisation, mind-reading), automatic thoughts, schemas. Technique: cognitive restructuring, thought records, behavioural experiments.
  • Ellis (REBT): the ABC model — Activating event → Belief (irrational) → Consequence. Dispute the irrational belief (D) to a new effect (E).

Operant conditioning (Skinner)

Add (positive)Remove (negative)
Increase behaviour (Reinforcement)+ give a reward− take away something aversive
Decrease behaviour (Punishment)+ apply something aversive− remove something pleasant (response cost)
Schedules of reinforcement: Ratio (per responses) vs Interval (per time); Fixed vs Variable. Variable Ratio (VR) = highest, steadiest response rate and most resistant to extinction (gambling). Fixed Interval gives the "scalloped" pattern.
Trap: Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment. Negative reinforcement increases behaviour by removing something nasty (taking a painkiller to stop pain). Punishment decreases behaviour.

Behaviour modification

The applied use of operant/classical principles to change behaviour: token economy, contingency management, shaping (reinforcing successive approximations), extinction, time-out (from positive reinforcement), and the Premack principle (a high-frequency behaviour reinforces a low-frequency one — "eat your veg, then dessert"). Sits under the umbrella of ABA (Applied Behaviour Analysis).

NIMHANS: Keep the classical-conditioning techniques distinct from operant ones — systematic desensitisation, flooding, aversion therapy are based on classical (Pavlovian + reciprocal inhibition / Wolpe); token economy and shaping are operant.

12 · Dissertation thread — pornography & aggression (for 11 Jun)

This is the box for your interview. It ties §5 (BPAQ) and §10 (regression) straight to your dissertation. Be ready to state your research question, design, measure and the framework you're situating it in.

The frameworks to name-drop

  • General Aggression Model (GAM — Anderson & Bushman, 2002): media/situational inputs → internal state (cognition, affect, arousal) → appraisal → aggressive behaviour. Repeated exposure builds aggression-related knowledge structures/scripts. The dominant integrative model.
  • Confluence Model of sexual aggression (Malamuth): two converging paths — hostile masculinity and impersonal sex — predict sexual aggression; pornography use interacts with (doesn't single-handedly cause) these risk factors.
  • Sexual script theory: repeated media exposure shapes the cognitive "scripts" people bring to real encounters.
  • Desensitisation vs the catharsis hypothesis: evidence supports desensitisation/priming; the old "porn releases the urge so it lowers aggression" catharsis idea is largely unsupported.

What the evidence actually says

Meta-analyses (e.g. Wright, Tokunaga & Kraus, 2016) link pornography consumption — especially violent content — to attitudes supporting aggression and to actual sexual aggression, with effects stronger for verbal than physical aggression and stronger for violent than non-violent material. The relationship is correlational and moderated (by trait hostility, content type), not a simple cause.

Measurement & method (own your limitations)

  • Measure: BPAQ for trait aggression (4 subscales) — you can correlate subscale scores with consumption frequency/type.
  • Analysis: correlation → multiple regression with porn-use variables predicting BPAQ, controlling for confounds (trait hostility, sensation-seeking, prior victimisation).
  • Limitations to pre-empt: self-report & social-desirability bias, the third-variable problem (a confound drives both), cross-sectional ≠ causal, ecological validity, sampling.
One-line answer if they ask "so does porn cause aggression?": "The evidence is a consistent but modest association, strongest for violent content and aggression-supportive attitudes; it's moderated by individual risk factors and the design is correlational, so I'm careful to claim a link, not direct causation."
