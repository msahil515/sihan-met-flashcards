Intelligence Tests — Complete Reference

Intelligence Tests: A Complete Reference

This note maps the full landscape of intelligence testing an RCI-pattern MPhil/MA Clinical Psychology aspirant must know: individual scales, group tests, infant/developmental scales, non-verbal and culture-fair batteries, and (highest yield for Indian exams) the Indian adaptations. For every test the author and year are named, and age ranges are given precisely.

1. Individual Intelligence Tests

Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales, Fifth Edition (SB5)

  • Origin: Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon devised the first Binet-Simon Scale (1905) in France; Lewis Terman of Stanford University adapted it for America as the Stanford-Binet (1916), introducing the term "Intelligence Quotient" (IQ) using William Stern's ratio IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100.
  • Current edition: SB5 by Gale H. Roid (2003).
  • Age range: 2 years to 85+ years (whole lifespan).
  • Structure: Five factors, each with a Verbal and Nonverbal domain: Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing, Working Memory. Grounded in the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model. Uses a routing subtest to set start points. Yields Full Scale IQ, Verbal IQ, Nonverbal IQ (mean 100, SD 15).
  • Exam note: SB5 abandoned the ratio IQ in favour of the deviation IQ (introduced by David Wechsler).

The Wechsler Scales (author: David Wechsler)

Wechsler introduced the deviation IQ and rejected the single global-score/mental-age model, splitting performance into Verbal and Performance (older editions) and now into index scores. All modern Wechsler scales have a mean of 100 and SD of 15.

  • WISC-V yields five primary index scores: Verbal Comprehension, Visual Spatial, Fluid Reasoning, Working Memory, Processing Speed (plus Full Scale IQ). This five-index structure replaced the older VIQ/PIQ dichotomy.
  • WAIS-IV yields four indices: Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory, Processing Speed.
  • Historical lineage: Wechsler-Bellevue Scale (1939) was the first; it evolved into the WAIS. Remember the boundary overlaps: WPPSI and WISC overlap at ages 6:0-7:7; WISC and WAIS overlap at age 16.

Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children, Second Edition (KABC-II)

  • Authors: Alan S. Kaufman and Nadeen L. Kaufman (KABC-II, 2004; original K-ABC 1983).
  • Age range: 3 years to 18 years.
  • Theory: Dual theoretical basis, chosen by the examiner: Luria's neuropsychological model (sequential vs simultaneous processing, learning, planning) or the CHC model. Designed to minimise cultural and language bias, useful for minority and bilingual children.

Das-Naglieri Cognitive Assessment System (CAS) — PASS theory

  • Authors: Jagannath Prasad (J.P.) Das and Jack A. Naglieri (CAS, 1997; CAS2, 2014). J.P. Das is an Indian-origin psychologist, a point often tested in Indian exams.
  • Age range: 5 years to 17 years (CAS2: 5 to 18).
  • Theory: Based on the PASS theory of intelligence, itself derived from A.R. Luria's model of brain function. PASS = Planning, Attention, Simultaneous processing, Successive (sequential) processing. It measures cognitive processes rather than a single g-factor, and is strong for diagnosing ADHD and learning disabilities.

Woodcock-Johnson Tests of Cognitive Abilities (WJ IV)

  • Authors: Richard Woodcock and Mary Bonner Johnson (original 1977); current WJ IV by Frederick Schrank, Kevin McGrew, Nancy Mather (2014).
  • Age range: 2 years to 90+ years.
  • Theory: The purest applied embodiment of the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory. Has three co-normed batteries: Cognitive Abilities, Oral Language, and Achievement, allowing ability-achievement discrepancy analysis for learning disability diagnosis.

2. Group Intelligence Tests

  • Army Alpha and Army Beta (1917): Developed by a committee under Robert Yerkes (with Arthur Otis's group-testing methods) for US Army recruit screening in World War I. Army Alpha is verbal/literate (for English-reading recruits); Army Beta is non-verbal/pictorial (for illiterate or non-English-speaking recruits). These were the first large-scale group intelligence tests.
  • Otis-Lennon School Ability Test (OLSAT): Descends from Arthur Otis's group-test work. Current authors: Arthur Otis and Roger Lennon. School-based group test for grades K-12 (roughly ages 5 to 18), measuring verbal, quantitative, and figural reasoning.

Raven's Progressive Matrices (author: John C. Raven, 1938)

A non-verbal, culture-reduced test of eductive ability / abstract reasoning (Spearman's g), based on Spearman's theory. The examinee selects the missing piece that completes a matrix pattern. Three versions by difficulty and age band:

CPMColoured Progressive MatricesChildren 5 to 11 years, elderly, and people with intellectual disability
SPMStandard Progressive MatricesGeneral population, roughly 6 to 80+ years (classically 8 to 65)
APMAdvanced Progressive MatricesHigh-ability adolescents and adults (approx. 11+ to adult) for ceiling discrimination

3. Infant and Developmental Scales

  • Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (BSID-III): Author Nancy Bayley (BSID-III, 2006). Age range: 1 month to 42 months. Five domains: Cognitive, Language, Motor, Social-Emotional, Adaptive Behaviour. Yields a Developmental Quotient (DQ), not an IQ. Gold standard for infant assessment.
  • Gesell Developmental Schedules: Author Arnold Gesell (1925). Age range: 4 weeks to 6 years. Assesses four areas: motor, adaptive, language, and personal-social behaviour. Gesell pioneered the concept of the Developmental Quotient (DQ) and maturational milestones.
  • Denver Developmental Screening Test (DDST / Denver II): Authors William Frankenburg and Josiah Dodds (1967; Denver II, 1992). Age range: birth to 6 years. A screening (not diagnostic) tool across four domains: personal-social, fine motor-adaptive, language, and gross motor.

4. Non-Verbal and Culture-Fair Tests

  • Cattell Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT): Author Raymond B. Cattell (1949). Designed to measure fluid intelligence (Gf) free of cultural and language influence. Three scales: Scale 1 (ages 4-8 and adults with disability), Scale 2 (ages 8-14 and average adults), Scale 3 (high-school and above-average adults).
  • Leiter International Performance Scale-Revised (Leiter-R): Original by Russell Leiter (1929); Leiter-R by Gale Roid and Lucy Miller (1997). Age range: 2 years to 20 years 11 months. Entirely non-verbal (no spoken instructions or verbal response required), ideal for deaf, autistic, ESL, or non-verbal children.
  • Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests: See Indian adaptations below (it is simultaneously a non-verbal/performance battery and an indigenous Indian test).

5. Indian Adaptations (HIGH YIELD)

This is the single most frequently tested cluster in NIMHANS/RML/IHBAS/CIP entrance papers. Learn the author, year, age range, and the parent (Western) test each was adapted from.

Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC)

  • Author / year: Arthur J. Malin (1969), Nagpur.
  • Adapted from: The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC).
  • Age range: 6 years to 15 years 11 months.
  • Structure: 11 subtests split into a Verbal scale (Information, Comprehension, Arithmetic, Similarities, Vocabulary, Digit Span) and a Performance scale (Picture Completion, Block Design, Object Assembly, Coding, Mazes). Gives Verbal IQ, Performance IQ, and Full Scale IQ. The most widely used individual children's intelligence test in India.

Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence

  • Author / year: Chandra Mohan Bhatia (1955).
  • Age range: 11 years to 16 years (widely used, and often extended down to test illiterate and rural populations because it is performance-based).
  • Five subtests: (1) Koh's Block Design Test, (2) Alexander's Pass-Along Test, (3) Pattern Drawing Test, (4) Immediate Memory (digit span, forward and reverse), (5) Picture Construction Test. Designed specifically for Indian conditions and for illiterate/semi-literate examinees, since it needs little language.

Seguin Form Board Test (SFB) — Indian norms

  • Original author: Édouard Séguin (1866). Indian norms standardised by researchers at institutions such as NIMHANS/Nagpur.
  • Age range: Approx. 3 years to 10 years (used as a quick performance/psychomotor speed test).
  • Use: The child fits ten geometric blocks into matching recesses; the fastest of three trials (shortest time) is used to estimate mental age. A rapid screening test for intellectual disability and eye-hand coordination, very common in Indian clinical practice.

Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS) — Indian adaptation

  • Original author: Edgar A. Doll (1935). Indian adaptation: A.J. Malin (1965), Nagpur adaptation (often called the Indian/Nagpur VSMS).
  • Age range: Birth to 15 years (some use up to adulthood).
  • Measures: Social maturity, not IQ. Yields a Social Age (SA) and a Social Quotient (SQ) = (Social Age / Chronological Age) × 100. Assessed via informant interview across eight domains (self-help general, self-help eating, self-help dressing, self-direction, occupation, communication, locomotion, socialisation). Central to diagnosing intellectual disability when paired with an IQ test.

Binet-Kamat Test of Intelligence (BKT)

  • Author / year: V.V. Kamat (1934, revised 1967).
  • Adapted from: The Stanford-Binet Scale (Terman's revision).
  • Age range: 3 years to 22 years.
  • Structure: Age-scale format yielding Mental Age and ratio IQ. Standardised on Kannada- and Marathi-speaking children. One of the earliest indigenous Indian intelligence tests; still used clinically at NIMHANS.

Draw-A-Man Test (Goodenough-Harris) — Indian norms

  • Original authors: Florence Goodenough (Draw-A-Man, 1926), revised by Dale B. Harris (Goodenough-Harris Drawing Test, 1963). Indian adaptation and norms by Pramila Phatak (Baroda norms).
  • Age range: 3 years to 15 years (best discrimination 3 to 10 years).
  • Use: The child draws a man; the drawing is scored for detail and proportion to yield a non-verbal estimate of intelligence. Quick, language-free, and culture-reduced.

Raven's Progressive Matrices — Indian norms

  • Both the CPM and SPM have Indian standardisation studies. The SPM Indian adaptation is widely used for adolescents and adults; the CPM is used for younger children (5-11) and for those with intellectual disability. Because it is non-verbal and culture-reduced, RPM is heavily favoured for the linguistically diverse Indian population.

7. High-Yield Exam Pointers

  • Ratio IQ vs Deviation IQ: Ratio IQ (Terman/Stern) = MA/CA × 100 and is used in age-scale tests (Binet-Kamat, old Stanford-Binet). Deviation IQ (Wechsler, mean 100, SD 15) compares the person to same-age peers and is used in all modern scales.
  • Quotients to distinguish: IQ (intelligence), DQ (development — Gesell, Bayley), SQ (social maturity — Vineland). Do not confuse them.
  • For diagnosing intellectual disability in India: the standard clinical pairing is an IQ test (MISIC / Binet-Kamat / SFB) plus an adaptive-behaviour measure (VSMS for SQ). ID diagnosis requires deficits in both IQ and adaptive functioning with onset before age 18.
  • Name the theorists: Luria → PASS (Das & Naglieri); Cattell → fluid vs crystallised and CFIT; CHC (Cattell-Horn-Carroll) → WJ IV and SB5; Spearman's g → Raven's Matrices; Wechsler → deviation IQ.
  • Indian firsts: Binet-Kamat (Kamat, 1934) is among the earliest Indian intelligence tests; Bhatia's Battery (1955) is the classic indigenous performance battery; MISIC (Malin, 1969) is the most-used children's scale.
