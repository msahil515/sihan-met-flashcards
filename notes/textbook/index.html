1.2 The action potential (the single most testable neurophysiology topic)

An AP is a ~1 ms electrical pulse travelling down the axon. The whole story is "Na+ in, K+ out": Na+ rushing in depolarises; K+ leaving repolarises.

  • Resting membrane potential ~ −70 mV. Set by K+ leak channels (membrane ~50× more permeable to K+), NOT by the Na/K pump. Threshold ~ −55 mV.
  • Na/K pump: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per ATP. Electrogenic. Maintains the gradient but does not set RMP.
  • Phase 1 Depolarisation: voltage-gated Na+ channels open → Na+ in → spike to ~ +30 mV.
  • Phase 2 Repolarisation: Na+ channels inactivate, voltage-gated K+ channels open (slower) → K+ out.
  • Phase 3 Hyperpolarisation: K+ channels stay open briefly → undershoot below −70 mV.
  • Phase 4 Return: K+ channels close, Na/K pump restores ions.
Ion concentrations: "K is in, Na is out, Cl is out, Ca is way out." Equilibrium potentials: K+ −90 mV, Na+ +60 mV, Cl− −65 mV, Ca²⁺ +120 mV.

All-or-none law: once threshold is reached the AP fires at constant amplitude; stimulus intensity is coded by frequency, not amplitude.

Refractory periods: Absolute = Na+ channels inactivated, no stimulus however strong can fire another AP (ensures one-way propagation). Relative = membrane hyperpolarised (K+ still open), a stronger stimulus can fire one.

TRAP: Inactivated ≠ closed. The Na+ channel has 3 states: closed (resting), open (during AP), inactivated (after, "ball and chain" blocks the pore). Resets only after repolarisation.

Propagation: continuous (unmyelinated C fibres, 0.5–2 m/s) vs saltatory (myelinated, AP jumps between Nodes of Ranvier, up to 120 m/s in A-alpha). Conduction velocity rises with myelination > axon diameter > temperature. Demyelination (MS, Guillain-Barré) slows/blocks conduction.

Synaptic transmission: AP arrives → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, Ca²⁺ enters → vesicles fuse (SNARE proteins, synaptotagmin = Ca²⁺ sensor) → NT released → binds postsynaptic receptors → EPSP (Na+/Ca²⁺ in, depolarising) or IPSP (Cl− in or K+ out, hyperpolarising) → cleared by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion. Summation (spatial + temporal) at the axon hillock decides if a new AP fires.

TRAP: Ca²⁺ is the trigger ion for NT release at the terminal. Without Ca²⁺ entry, no transmitter is released.

1.7 Sensory systems

  • Vision: rods (peripheral, low light, no colour) + cones (foveal, colour, acuity). Cones densest at the fovea centralis. Optic disc = blind spot. Pathway: retina → optic nerve → chiasm (nasal fibres cross) → optic tract → LGN → V1. Theories: trichromatic (Young-Helmholtz, retinal) + opponent-process (Hering, post-retinal). Dorsal stream "where/how" (parietal); ventral stream "what" (temporal).
  • Audition: outer → middle (malleus, incus, stapes) → cochlea → hair cells on basilar membrane → CN VIII → cochlear nuclei → MGN → Heschl's gyrus. Place theory (Békésy): base = high frequencies, apex = low; best for high. Frequency theory: firing rate = frequency, works <500 Hz. Volley principle bridges mid-range.
  • Somatosensation: dorsal column-medial lemniscus (touch, vibration, proprioception, decussates at medulla) vs spinothalamic (pain, temp, decussates at spinal level of entry).
  • Taste: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami. CN VII / IX / X. Insula = primary gustatory cortex.
  • Smell: olfactory epithelium → bulb → piriform cortex (no thalamus relay), closest to limbic system.

1.11 Sleep (full primer)

Sleep = NREM (3 stages) + REM. One cycle ~ 90 min; 4–6 cycles/night. N3 dominates the first half; REM dominates the second half and lengthens through the night.

StageEEG / signatureNotes
NREM 1 (~5%)Theta (4–8 Hz)Light, hypnic jerks, hypnagogic images
NREM 2 (~45–55%)Sleep spindles + K-complexesMost of the night; spindles gate sensory input
NREM 3 (~15–20%)Delta (0.5–4 Hz, high amplitude)Deep/slow-wave. Growth hormone. Parasomnias (sleepwalking, night terrors, enuresis) come OUT of N3
REM (~20–25%)Mixed, low-voltage (looks awake)Paradoxical: brain active, body in atonia. Vivid dreams, eye bursts, autonomic instability, thermoregulation fails. NOT delta-dominant.
  • Neural control: sleep pressure from adenosine (caffeine blocks adenosine receptors); VLPO = GABAergic "sleep switch"; wake driven by the ARAS (histamine, orexin, NE, 5-HT, ACh). SCN = master circadian clock, entrained by light via the retinohypothalamic tract → pineal → melatonin in darkness. REM-ON = cholinergic (PPT/LDT); REM-OFF = aminergic (NE, 5-HT).
  • Memory: declarative consolidated in NREM (delta replay); procedural in REM + spindles; emotional in REM.
  • Disorders: Narcolepsy = orexin/hypocretin deficiency; cataplexy is pathognomonic. RBD = loss of atonia in REM, older men, α-synucleinopathy warning (Parkinson/Lewy body). RLS = urge to move legs at rest (dopamine/iron). Kleine-Levin = the 3 H's (hypersomnia + hyperphagia + hypersexuality), young males. Insomnia DSM-5: 3 nights/wk × 3 months.

1.12 Emotion, arousal, learning & LTP

  • Theories of emotion: James-Lange (body first, "we feel sad because we cry"); Cannon-Bard (body + emotion simultaneously, thalamic); Schachter-Singer (two-factor: arousal + cognitive label); Lazarus (appraisal precedes emotion).
  • Yerkes-Dodson law: inverted-U of arousal vs performance. Optimum is lower for complex tasks, higher for simple/well-learned tasks.
  • Memory substrates: hippocampus (declarative consolidation), amygdala (emotional/fear), striatum (procedural/habit), cerebellum (motor conditioning). LTP strengthens synapses (NMDA receptor critical, Ca²⁺ entry, AMPA insertion); LTD weakens them.
  • Papez circuit: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary → anterior thalamus → cingulate → entorhinal → hippocampus.

  • Cephalocaudal (head → toe) · Proximodistal (centre → periphery).
  • Critical period = rigid biological window, lost if missed (Lorenz imprinting). Sensitive period = optimal but flexible (language acquisition).
  • Continuity vs discontinuity; nature vs nurture (interactionist consensus); active vs passive child (bidirectionality).

Prenatal stages: Germinal (0–2 wk), Embryonic (2–8 wk, most vulnerable to teratogens, organogenesis), Fetal (8 wk–birth, growth). Teratogens: alcohol (FAS, most common preventable ID), tobacco, thalidomide (limbs), rubella (1st trimester), lead/mercury, Zika (microcephaly).

2.3 Vygotsky — sociocultural

  • ZPD = gap between what a child can do alone vs with help from a more knowledgeable other (MKO).
  • Scaffolding = temporary tailored support, withdrawn as competence grows.
  • Private speech → inner speech, guides cognition. Language is the master tool, internalised from social interaction.
TRAP: "Scaffolding" is Bruner's term, applied to Vygotsky's ZPD. Vygotsky's own terms are ZPD and MKO.
One line: Piaget = child as lone scientist (development → learning). Vygotsky = child as apprentice (learning → development, mediated by culture).

4. Psychological Assessment & Tests MET 15 Q · NIMHANS 10 Q

The whole map: a psychological test is a measuring instrument that must be (a) consistent = reliability and (b) accurate = validity. Everything else is a way to estimate one of those, interpret scores against norms, or an example of a real test.

4.1 Item analysis

  • Difficulty index p = proportion who got the item right (0–1). Sweet spot 0.50 for free-response, ~0.625 for a 4-option MCQ (halfway between guessing 0.25 and ceiling). p > 0.80 too easy, < 0.20 too hard.
  • Discrimination index D = p(top 27%) − p(bottom 27%), range −1 to +1. D > 0.40 good. Negative D = mis-keyed/ambiguous item, discard immediately.

4.2 Reliability (consistency)

Classical test theory: Observed = True + Error. Reliability = proportion of variance that is true.

  • Test-retest (across time; standard window 2–4 wks).
  • Parallel forms (across forms).
  • Split-half + Spearman-Brown correction (r_full = 2·r_half / (1 + r_half)); Cronbach α (≥0.7 acceptable, ≥0.8 good) = mean of all possible split-halves; KR-20 for dichotomous (0/1) items.
  • Inter-rater: Cohen κ (categorical, Landis & Koch: 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81+ almost perfect), ICC (continuous).
  • SEM = SD·√(1 − r_xx). Higher reliability → smaller SEM → tighter confidence band around a score.
TRAP: Cronbach α is NOT a measure of unidimensionality — a test can have high α and still measure two constructs. And κ is NOT internal consistency.

4.3 Validity (accuracy)

  • Content: covers the domain (expert panels, Lawshe's CVR). Face validity = looks valid to a layperson, the weakest form.
  • Criterion: Concurrent (criterion now) vs Predictive = Postponed (criterion later).
  • Construct: convergent (correlates with similar) + discriminant (not with unrelated) + nomological network. Campbell & Fiske's multitrait-multimethod matrix (MTMM, 1959).
Validity ceiling: max validity = √(reliability). Unreliable tests cannot be strongly valid. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

6.6 Research designs & diagnostic stats

DesignDirection / measure

Diagnostic stats: Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN), high-sensitivity test rules a condition OUT when negative (SnNout). Specificity = TN/(TN+FP), rules a condition IN when positive (SpPin). PPV/NPV depend on prevalence; likelihood ratios don't. ROC plots sensitivity vs (1−specificity).

Threats to internal validity: history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression to the mean, selection, attrition, Hawthorne effect, demand characteristics, experimenter (Rosenthal) effect. RCT phases: I (safety, 20–100) → II (efficacy, 100–300) → III (confirmatory, 1000+) → IV (surveillance). Blinding: single/double/triple. ITT vs per-protocol. Reporting: CONSORT (RCTs), PRISMA (systematic reviews/meta), STROBE (observational), COREQ (qualitative). Ethics: Belmont 3 (respect, beneficence, justice), Nuremberg Code, Declaration of Helsinki.

TRAPS: Cohort can be prospective or retrospective; case-control is always retrospective. Random sampling (external validity) ≠ random assignment (internal validity). r = 0 means no linear relationship (a U-shape has r ≈ 0). A large biased sample is precisely biased.

7. Language & Communication Skills MET 15 Q · NIMHANS 5 Q · highest fast-marks ratio

7.1 Subject-verb agreement

The verb agrees with the subject in number, not the nearest noun.

  • Each, every, either, neither, one, anyone, everyone, nobody → singular ("Each of the participants was given…").
  • Either/or, neither/nor → verb agrees with the nearer subject ("Neither the patient nor the family members were informed").
  • "A number of" = plural; "the number of" = singular. None defaults singular in formal writing.
  • "Data are" — data is technically plural (datum singular); treat as plural in exam contexts.
  • Singular-looking: news, mathematics, physics, politics, statistics. Plural-only: scissors, trousers, glasses.

7.2 Voice & conditionals

Active → passive: object becomes subject; verb → "be" + past participle. "The committee will announce the results" → "The results will be announced by the committee."

"Had she taken the medication, she would have recovered sooner" = third conditional (inversion is formal).

7.3 Punctuation & prepositions

  • Restrictive vs non-restrictive clauses: non-restrictive (extra info, removable) → commas + "who"; restrictive (defines which) → no commas, "that". "The patient, who was anxious, refused" (one patient) vs "The patient who was anxious refused" (picks out one of several).
  • Semicolon joins two independent clauses; colon introduces a list/explanation. It's = it is; its = possessive.
  • Prepositions: insist on, depend on, comply with, consist of, refer to, suffer from, object to, accuse of, married to (not with), prefer X to Y, superior/inferior to (not than), discuss (no preposition). "Between you and me."

7.4 Modifiers, parallelism & common corrections

  • Dangling modifier: "Walking down the street, the building looked tall" (the building isn't walking). Parallelism: "reading, writing, and swimming" (not "to swim").
  • Like (before a noun) vs as (before a clause). Who (subject) vs whom (object). Less (uncountable) vs fewer (countable). Between (two) vs among (3+). "The reason is that" (not "because"). Predicate adjective follows a linking verb ("The data were inconclusive").
