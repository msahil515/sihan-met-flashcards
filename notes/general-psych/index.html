1. Perception & Sensation

Sensation is detecting raw stimulus energy at the receptors; perception is the brain organising and interpreting it. Transduction is the conversion of stimulus energy into neural signals.

Psychophysics (thresholds)

ConceptMeaning
Absolute thresholdSmallest intensity detectable 50 percent of the time
Difference threshold (JND)Smallest change between two stimuli that is detectable 50 percent of the time
Weber's lawThe JND is a constant proportion of the stimulus, not a fixed amount
Fechner's lawSensation grows with the logarithm of stimulus intensity
Stevens' power lawSensation is a power function of intensity (often fits better than Fechner)
Subliminal / primingStimuli below the absolute threshold can still prime responses

Signal detection theory

Detection is a decision under uncertainty, not a fixed threshold. Four outcomes: hit, miss, false alarm, correct rejection. d-prime (d') measures true sensitivity; beta is the response criterion (how willing you are to say "yes"). Motivation and expectation shift beta, not sensitivity.

Theories of perception

The two camps: Gibson's direct / bottom-up theory (the stimulus itself carries enough information, perception is data-driven) versus Gregory's constructivist / top-down theory (perception is an active hypothesis built from prior knowledge and inference). Most modern views combine both.

Gestalt laws of organisation

"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts." Proximity, similarity, closure, continuity (good continuation), common fate, figure-ground, pragnanz (simplicity). Founders: Wertheimer, Koffka, Kohler.

Depth & constancy

  • Binocular cues: retinal disparity, convergence.
  • Monocular cues: relative size, interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, motion parallax, aerial perspective.
  • Perceptual constancies: size, shape, colour, brightness, the object looks stable despite changing retinal image.
  • Feature integration theory (Treisman): a preattentive stage detects individual features in parallel, then focused attention binds them into a whole object. Failure of binding produces illusory conjunctions.

2. Attention

ModelClaim
Broadbent (early selection)A filter blocks unattended input early, based on physical features, before meaning is processed
Treisman (attenuation)Unattended input is turned down, not fully blocked; important words (your name) still get through
Deutsch-Norman (late selection)All input is processed for meaning; selection happens late, at the response stage
Kahneman (capacity / resource)Attention is a limited pool of resources allocated across tasks; arousal changes the pool size

Types: selective (focus on one source), divided (multitasking), sustained (vigilance over time), alternating. Automatic vs controlled processing: automatic is fast, parallel, effortless (Stroop interference happens because reading is automatic); controlled is slow, serial, effortful.

TRAP: Cocktail-party effect (hearing your name in an unattended conversation) supports late/attenuation selection, not Broadbent's early filter. Inattentional blindness (missing the gorilla) and change blindness show attention is required for conscious perception.

3. Memory

Multi-store model (Atkinson-Shiffrin)

Sensory memory → short-term/working memory → long-term memory, with attention and rehearsal as the gates.

StoreCapacity / duration
Sensory: iconic (visual)large; about 0.25 to 1 second (Sperling's partial-report)
Sensory: echoic (auditory)about 2 to 4 seconds
Short-term memory7 plus or minus 2 items (Miller), about 15 to 30 seconds; chunking expands it
Long-term memoryeffectively unlimited, durable

Working memory (Baddeley & Hitch)

Four parts: central executive (control), phonological loop (verbal/acoustic), visuospatial sketchpad (visual/spatial), and the episodic buffer (added 2000, integrates information across systems).

Types of long-term memory

  • Explicit / declarative (conscious): episodic (events, "what I did") and semantic (facts, meanings).
  • Implicit / non-declarative (unconscious): procedural (skills), priming, classical conditioning.

Processes, forgetting & distortion

  • Encoding → storage → retrieval. Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart): deep semantic encoding beats shallow.
  • Forgetting: decay; interference (proactive = old disrupts new; retroactive = new disrupts old); cue-dependent / retrieval failure; encoding failure.
  • Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: rapid early loss then levelling; spacing and overlearning slow it.
  • Serial position effect: primacy (LTM) + recency (STM). A delay wipes recency, not primacy.
  • Reconstructive memory: Bartlett (schemas distort recall, "War of the Ghosts"); Loftus (misinformation effect, leading questions reshape memory).
  • Amnesia: anterograde (cannot form new memories, e.g. H.M.) vs retrograde (cannot recall old). Korsakoff = thiamine deficiency + confabulation.

4. Learning

Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

A neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus (UCS) becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that triggers a conditioned response (CR). Phases: acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation, discrimination, higher-order conditioning. Watson's Little Albert = conditioned fear. Garcia effect = taste aversion learned in one trial over long delay (biological preparedness).

Operant conditioning (Thorndike, Skinner)

Thorndike's law of effect: responses followed by satisfaction are stamped in. Skinner formalised it.

Add (positive)Remove (negative)
Increase behaviour (reinforcement)Positive reinforcement (give a reward)Negative reinforcement (remove an aversive)
Decrease behaviour (punishment)Positive punishment (add an aversive)Negative punishment (remove a reward)
Schedules of reinforcement: Fixed Ratio, Variable Ratio, Fixed Interval, Variable Interval. Variable Ratio gives the highest, steadiest response rate and is the most resistant to extinction (gambling). Ratio schedules beat interval; variable beats fixed.

Shaping = reinforcing successive approximations. Premack principle = a high-frequency behaviour reinforces a low-frequency one ("eat your vegetables, then dessert").

Cognitive & observational learning

  • Tolman: latent learning + cognitive maps (rats learned the maze without reinforcement).
  • Kohler: insight learning (chimps solved problems suddenly, the "aha").
  • Bandura: observational learning / modelling (Bobo doll); steps = attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
  • Seligman: learned helplessness; biological constraints (preparedness) limit what can be conditioned.

5. Emotion

TheorySequence
James-LangeStimulus → body arousal → you label the emotion ("I tremble, therefore I am afraid")
Cannon-BardStimulus → arousal AND emotion at the same time (thalamus routes both)
Schachter-Singer (two-factor)Arousal + a cognitive label = emotion; same arousal, different label, different emotion
Lazarus (appraisal)Cognitive appraisal comes first, then emotion
ZajoncSome emotional reactions occur before/without cognition ("preferences need no inferences")
Facial feedback hypothesisFacial expressions feed back and intensify the felt emotion

Ekman: six basic universal emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust). Components of emotion: physiological arousal, expressive behaviour, conscious experience. Yerkes-Dodson law: performance peaks at moderate arousal (inverted U); harder tasks peak at lower arousal.

TRAP: James-Lange = body first. Cannon-Bard = simultaneous. Schachter-Singer = body + label. This ordering is the single most common emotion-theory exam item.

6. Motivation

  • Instinct theory: behaviour driven by innate biological programs.
  • Drive-reduction (Hull): a need creates a drive; behaviour reduces the drive to restore homeostasis.
  • Arousal theory: we seek an optimal level of arousal (links to Yerkes-Dodson).
  • Incentive theory: external rewards pull behaviour.
  • Maslow's hierarchy: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualisation (later: cognitive, aesthetic, transcendence).
  • Intrinsic vs extrinsic: intrinsic = for its own sake; extrinsic = for a reward. The overjustification effect: paying for an already-enjoyed activity can reduce intrinsic motivation.
  • Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan): autonomy, competence, relatedness.
  • McClelland: needs for achievement, affiliation, power.

7. Intelligence

TheoristModel
Spearman (1904)Two-factor: general intelligence g + specific s; invented factor analysis
Thurstone (1938)7 Primary Mental Abilities (no single g)
Cattell & HornFluid intelligence (Gf, reasoning) vs crystallised (Gc, learned knowledge)
GuilfordStructure of Intellect (up to 180 cells); convergent vs divergent thinking
Gardner (1983)Multiple intelligences (8): linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalist
SternbergTriarchic: analytical, creative, practical
Carroll / CHCThree-stratum hierarchy (g → broad → narrow); the dominant modern model

Tests: Wechsler (WAIS/WISC, deviation IQ, SD 15), Stanford-Binet (SD 16, SB-5 uses 15), Raven's Progressive Matrices (culture-reduced g). Ratio IQ = (mental age / chronological age) × 100. Flynn effect = rising IQ scores across generations. Heritability estimates are population statistics, not individual destiny.

8. Cognition (thinking, problem-solving, language)

  • Concepts & prototypes: we categorise by comparison to a best example (prototype).
  • Problem solving: algorithms (guaranteed, slow) vs heuristics (fast rules of thumb). Barriers: fixation, mental set, functional fixedness.
  • Heuristics & biases (Tversky & Kahneman): representativeness, availability, anchoring; confirmation bias; framing effect; overconfidence.
  • Decision making: System 1 (fast, intuitive) vs System 2 (slow, deliberate).
  • Language: phonemes → morphemes → syntax → semantics. Stages: cooing, babbling, one-word (~12 mo), telegraphic (~24 mo). Chomsky: language acquisition device + universal grammar (nativist). Skinner: language is learned by reinforcement (behaviourist). Whorf: linguistic relativity (language shapes thought).

9. Landmark experiments (the detail)

StudyMethod & finding
Milgram (1963) obedienceParticipants gave what they thought were escalating shocks on an experimenter's orders; 65 percent went to the maximum 450 V. Obedience to authority over conscience.
Asch (1951) conformityLine-judgement task with confederates giving wrong answers; about 75 percent conformed at least once. Normative social influence.
Zimbardo (1971) Stanford PrisonStudents randomly assigned guard/prisoner roles; the situation produced abusive behaviour, stopped early. Power of roles and situation (deindividuation).
Darley & Latane (1968) bystanderThe more bystanders present, the less likely and slower any one person helps. Diffusion of responsibility (sparked by the Kitty Genovese case).
Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) dissonancePeople paid 1 dollar to lie about a boring task later rated it more enjoyable than those paid 20 dollars. Less external justification → more attitude change.
Sherif (1954) Robbers CaveBoys' camp; competition created intergroup hostility, shared superordinate goals reduced it. Realistic conflict theory.
Tajfel (1971) minimal groupArbitrary group labels alone produced in-group favouritism. Social identity theory.
Bandura (1961) Bobo dollChildren who watched an adult hit a doll imitated the aggression. Observational learning.
Harlow (1958) monkeysInfant monkeys preferred a soft cloth "mother" over a wire one with food. Contact comfort drives attachment.
Seligman (1967) helplessnessDogs exposed to inescapable shock later failed to escape when they could. Learned helplessness (model of depression).
Rosenhan (1973) "being sane in insane places"Healthy pseudopatients admitted to psychiatric hospitals; diagnoses stuck. Critique of psychiatric labelling.

10. Personality

Full version: this is the compact pass. The dedicated Personality Theories page has all of it in depth — every defence mechanism, the neo-Freudians side by side, type theories, assessment pairings, and the collected MCQ traps.

Psychodynamic (Freud)

  • Structure: id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle), superego (morality/conscience).
  • Topographic: conscious, preconscious, unconscious. The iceberg.
  • Psychosexual stages: oral → anal → phallic (Oedipus/Electra) → latency → genital. Fixation at a stage shapes the adult personality.
  • Defence mechanisms: repression, denial, projection, displacement, reaction formation, rationalisation, sublimation, regression. (Anna Freud catalogued these.)

Neo-Freudians

Jung: collective unconscious, archetypes, introversion/extraversion. Adler: inferiority complex, striving for superiority, birth order. Horney: basic anxiety, womb envy (countered Freud). Erikson: psychosocial stages (see Development).

Trait theories

TheoristModel
AllportCardinal, central, secondary traits
Cattell16 Personality Factors (16PF), via factor analysis
EysenckThree dimensions: extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism (PEN)
Costa & McCraeBig Five / OCEAN: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism

Humanistic & social-cognitive

  • Rogers: self-concept, congruence (real vs ideal self), unconditional positive regard, the fully functioning person.
  • Maslow: self-actualisation (see Motivation).
  • Bandura: reciprocal determinism (behaviour ↔ person ↔ environment), self-efficacy.
  • Rotter: locus of control (internal vs external).
Assessment: projective tests (Rorschach inkblots, TAT) tap the unconscious; objective inventories (MMPI, 16PF, NEO-PI) are self-report and standardised.

11. Social psychology (concepts)

  • Attribution: dispositional vs situational. Fundamental attribution error = overweighting disposition for others' behaviour. Actor-observer bias, self-serving bias (credit success, blame the situation).
  • Attitudes & persuasion: ABC components (affective, behavioural, cognitive). Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo): central route (argument quality) vs peripheral route (cues). Cognitive dissonance (Festinger): conflicting cognitions create discomfort that drives attitude change.
  • Conformity & obedience: normative (to fit in) vs informational (to be right) influence. Compliance techniques: foot-in-the-door, door-in-the-face, low-balling.
  • Group processes: social facilitation (others improve easy tasks, worsen hard ones), social loafing, deindividuation, groupthink (Janis), group polarisation, risky shift.
  • Prejudice: stereotype (cognitive), prejudice (affective), discrimination (behavioural). Realistic conflict theory; social identity theory (Tajfel); contact hypothesis (Allport) reduces it.
  • Prosocial & aggression: bystander effect + diffusion of responsibility; altruism vs reciprocity. Frustration-aggression hypothesis; social learning of aggression.
  • Attraction: proximity, similarity, mere-exposure effect, reciprocity, physical attractiveness.

12. Developmental psychology

TheoristStages / idea
Piaget (cognitive)Sensorimotor (0-2, object permanence) → preoperational (2-7, egocentrism, no conservation) → concrete operational (7-11, conservation) → formal operational (11+, abstract). Schemas, assimilation, accommodation.
Erikson (psychosocial)8 stages, each a crisis: trust v mistrust → autonomy v shame → initiative v guilt → industry v inferiority → identity v role confusion → intimacy v isolation → generativity v stagnation → integrity v despair.
Kohlberg (moral)Preconventional (punishment/reward) → conventional (social approval, law and order) → postconventional (social contract, universal ethics).
Vygotsky (sociocultural)Zone of proximal development, scaffolding, the social origin of thought (more-knowledgeable other).
Freud (psychosexual)See Personality.

Attachment (Bowlby; Ainsworth's Strange Situation): secure, insecure-avoidant, insecure-resistant/ambivalent, and disorganised (Main & Solomon). Harlow = contact comfort. Temperament (Thomas & Chess): easy, difficult, slow-to-warm-up.

TRAP: Conservation (understanding quantity stays the same despite shape change) appears in concrete operational, not preoperational. Object permanence is the sensorimotor milestone.

13. States of consciousness & sleep

  • Sleep stages: NREM 1 (theta, hypnagogic) → NREM 2 (sleep spindles, K-complexes) → NREM 3 (delta, deep/slow-wave) → REM (paradoxical: brain active, body paralysed, dreaming). ~90-minute cycles; REM lengthens toward morning.
  • Circadian rhythm: ~24-hour cycle, governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus; melatonin from the pineal gland.
  • Dream theories: Freud (wish fulfilment, manifest vs latent content); activation-synthesis (Hobson & McCarley, brain makes sense of random REM activity); information-processing/consolidation.
  • Sleep disorders: insomnia, narcolepsy, sleep apnoea, night terrors (NREM) vs nightmares (REM), somnambulism.
  • Altered states: hypnosis, meditation, and psychoactive drugs (depressants, stimulants, hallucinogens, opioids) altering consciousness via neurotransmitters.

14. Stress, coping & health

  • Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome: alarm → resistance → exhaustion.
  • Lazarus & Folkman: primary appraisal (is it a threat?) then secondary appraisal (can I cope?). Problem-focused coping (change the stressor) vs emotion-focused coping (manage the feeling).
  • Physiology: the HPA axis and sympathetic "fight-or-flight" (Cannon); cortisol as the chronic-stress hormone.
  • Type A (competitive, hostile, time-urgent) vs Type B; hostility links to heart disease.
  • Buffers: social support, perceived control, optimism, hardiness.
