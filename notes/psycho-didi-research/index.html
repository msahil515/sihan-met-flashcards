1 · Terms to Know

  • Random assignment: giving every participant an equal chance of being in an experimental or control group.
  • Internal validity: extent to which an experiment investigates what it is supposed to.
  • External validity: generalisability.
  • Demand characteristics: factors that influence a participant's behaviour based on what they think the purpose of the experiment is.
  • Observer expectancy effect: experimenter communicates expectations unintentionally (Pygmalion / Rosenthal effect).
  • Descriptive statistics: the branch of statistics which deals with descriptions of obtained data.
  • Inferential statistics: deals with drawing conclusions about a large group of individuals on the basis of observations of a few participants.
  • Parameter: a measurable characteristic of a population.
  • Confidence interval: the mean of your estimate plus and minus the variation in that estimate.
  • Null hypothesis: the claim that the effect being studied does not exist.
  • Alternate hypothesis: statement that suggests there is a relationship between the variables being studied.
  • Significance level: the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true, i.e., the probability of making a Type I error.
  • Effect size: a quantitative measure of the magnitude of the experimental effect. The larger the effect size, the stronger the relationship between the two variables.
  • Skewness: a lopsided and asymmetrical curve because of extreme data.
  • Kurtosis: the term used to identify the degree of steepness or shallowness of a distribution.
  • Post hoc test: "like a post mortem." Requires two conditions: a directional hypothesis and a significant F ratio.
Trap: Type I error = null hypothesis is true but we reject it (alternate is false but we accept it) → "false alarm." Type II error = null hypothesis is false but we accept it (alternate is true but we reject it) → "miss." The significance level is the probability of making a Type I error.
Key: Test power is the probability that the test will reject the null hypothesis when the alternate hypothesis is true, i.e., it will not make a Type II error.

The decision table (conclusion vs true state of nature)

The small red and green table in the diagram crosses what you conclude from your data (rows) with what is actually true in the population (columns). You never know the true state; the table lists the four possible outcomes. The framework of Type I and Type II errors comes from Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson (papers of 1928 and 1933).

Your conclusionH0 is actually trueHa is actually true (H0 false)
Support H0 / reject HaCorrect conclusion (probability 1 − α)Type II error (probability β), a "miss"
Support Ha / reject H0Type I error (probability α), a "false alarm"Correct conclusion = power (probability 1 − β)

Example: H0 = "the new therapy has no effect." If the therapy really does nothing but your study finds a significant improvement, that is a Type I error. If the therapy really works but your study finds no significant difference (say, because the sample was too small), that is a Type II error. Correctly detecting a therapy that works is power.

Trap: Power = 1 − β, not 1 − α (1 − α is the chance of correctly keeping a true H0). Making α stricter (.05 to .01) cuts Type I errors but raises Type II errors, so power falls. Power rises with a larger sample, a larger effect size, a more lenient α and a one-tailed test. Jacob Cohen (1988) suggested .80 as the usual minimum power.

2 · What is Research (Steps & Types)

Research is a systematic, controlled, empirical, critical investigation.

Steps

  1. Identify a research problem
  2. Review of literature
  3. Formulate a hypothesis
  4. Research design
  5. Collect data
  6. Analyse data
  7. Interpret data
  8. Report findings

3 · Variables

A variable in research is a characteristic, number, or quantity that can be measured or counted and can take on different values.

TypeDescription
Independent variable (IV)Manipulated.
Dependent variable (DV)Not manipulated; changes with change in IV.
Extraneous variablesAny variable other than the IV that could affect the DV. Two kinds (as in the diagram): controlled and confounding.
Controlled (extraneous) variablesExtraneous variables the researcher has dealt with: removed, held constant for everyone, matched across groups, or spread evenly by random assignment.
Confounding variablesUncontrolled extraneous variables that affect results.
Stimulus variablee.g. light (always IV).
Organismic variablee.g. age.
Behavioural / response variablee.g. response rate (always DV).

Example: testing whether music (IV) improves memory scores (DV). Room noise is an extraneous variable; testing everyone in the same quiet room makes it a controlled variable. If the music group is tested in the morning and the no-music group in the evening, time of day becomes a confounding variable: you cannot tell whether the music or the time caused the difference.

Trap: "Extraneous" is the umbrella term. A confounding variable is an extraneous variable that was not controlled and changes along with the IV, so it threatens internal validity.

4 · Research Designs

How the flowchart reads: "Types of research" branches into four designs: experiment, observation, correlation and special designs. "Special designs" then branches into three: longitudinal, cross-sectional and cohort-sequential. The first three differ in how much control the researcher has; the special designs are about time and age and are used mostly in developmental research.

Experiment

  • Experiment (lab): complete control on secondary (error) variance, e.g. lab experiment.
  • Field experiment: secondary variance just greater than lab experiment; happens in a real setting; difficult to control extraneous variables.
  • Quasi experiment: secondary variance greatest; no random assignment; non-equivalent groups, e.g. ex post facto studies.

Correlation

  • Not causation established; only helps us understand association.
  • Gives us the direction of the relationship.
  • Can't control extraneous variables.
  • Generalization is possible.
  • Types: positive, negative, zero.
Trap: Correlation is not causation. It shows association and direction but cannot control extraneous variables.

Observation

  • Can be naturalistic or controlled.
  • Can be participant or non-participant.
  • Tools used: survey, interviews, questionnaires, observation.
  • Usually used to check attitude towards something.

In the diagram, "Observation" is used broadly for non-experimental, descriptive research: the researcher records behaviour or opinions as they are, without manipulating any variable. The four kinds of observation:

KindMeaningExample
NaturalisticBehaviour watched in its real setting, without interfering.Recording how children share toys in a school playground.
Controlled (laboratory)Behaviour watched in a set-up, standardised setting.Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation, run in a lab playroom.
ParticipantThe observer joins the group being studied.A researcher living in a village to study its customs.
Non-participantThe observer stays outside the group.Watching a classroom through a one-way mirror.
Survey vs observation: standard textbooks (e.g. Baron; Ciccarelli & White) treat the survey method as its own descriptive method: a large group is asked questions through questionnaires or interviews about their attitudes, opinions or behaviour. That is why attitude measurement sits in this box. Observation records what people do; a survey records what people say. Survey traps: the sample must be representative, and answers can be distorted by social desirability (people giving the "nice" answer).

Special Designs

Longitudinal design

  • Involves taking a single group and studying it for a long period; the group is called a cohort.
  • Founder note: Lewis Terman — eugenics (Francis Galton, 1883).
  • Prospective study: looking at the future.
  • Retrospective study: looking at the past.

Cross-sectional design

Various age groups are compared at the same time.

Cohort-sequential (cross-sequential)

Cross-sectional + longitudinal; also known as cross-sequential.

Who → what: Lewis Terman — associated with eugenics and long-term longitudinal cohort study.

Variance / Dispersion

How the scores are spread around the mean.

  • Range = highest score − lowest score (scores, not frequencies: marks from 12 to 47 give a range of 35).
  • Standard deviation: spread of scores around the mean.
  • Variance: standard deviation squared.
  • Average deviation: mean of absolute deviations.
  • Quartile deviation (for median values): QD = (Q3 − Q1) / 2.
Formulas: Standard deviation σ = √[ Σ(xi − μ)² / N ]. Average (mean) deviation AD = Σ|xi − μ| / N. Here xi = each score, μ = the mean, N = number of scores, Σ = "add them all up", and | | means ignore the minus sign.

Worked example: scores 2, 4, 6. Mean = 4. Deviations from the mean: −2, 0, +2. SD: squares are 4, 0, 4; sum = 8; 8 / 3 = 2.67 (this is the variance); √2.67 = 1.63. AD: absolute deviations 2, 0, 2; sum = 4; 4 / 3 = 1.33.

Heads up: the diagram writes the average deviation as Σ(xi − μ) / N, without the absolute-value bars. Raw deviations from the mean always add up to 0, so the bars are essential: AD = Σ|xi − μ| / N. Also, σ with N in the denominator is the population SD; the sample SD (s) divides by n − 1.

Reading the degrees-of-freedom columns

  • Main = df of an IV's effect (between groups or between conditions) = K − 1, where K = number of groups or levels.
  • Interaction = df of the A × B effect = (K1 − 1)(K2 − 1). It exists only when there are two or more IVs, which is why it is blank for one-way and repeated-measures ANOVA.
  • Error = within-groups (residual) df, the denominator of the F ratio. One-way: N − K.
  • Total = N − 1, where N = total number of scores. The parts (main + interaction + error, plus subjects or covariate where present) always add up to the total.

Why repeated measures has no "interaction" column entry: each person is measured in every condition, so the total variability (N − 1) splits into between-subjects (n − 1) and within-subjects; the within-subjects part splits into conditions (K − 1) and error (K − 1)(n − 1). That error term is technically the subjects × conditions interaction, which is why some books call it "interaction", but it is the error term (the F denominator), so in this table it belongs in the Error column, as the diagram shows.
Trap: in the diagram's repeated-measures row, the N inside (K − 1)(N − 1) means the number of participants, while the N under Total means the number of scores. With 10 people and 3 conditions, error df = (3 − 1)(10 − 1) = 18, but total df = 30 − 1 = 29, not 10 − 1. This page writes participants as n to keep the two apart.

7 · Factor Analysis & PCA

Who → what: Factor analysis was given by Charles Spearman while creating his theory of intelligence. It uses a correlational design.

Types

TypePurposeDetails / Methods
ExploratoryFor creating a new theory.Unique variance: one factor is enough to explain variance; method = principal component method (unrotated factors given). Common variance: gives factors that explain variance together but not individually; methods = principal axis method, maximum likelihood estimation, generalized least squares.
ConfirmatoryFor verifying another theory.Changes cannot be made.

The arrow in the diagram runs from "Method: principal component method" (under Exploratory, unique variance) to the PCA box. It means the PCA box describes that same method: PCA is the most common way to extract factors in an exploratory analysis (it is the default extraction in SPSS), and the PCA box explains how to decide how many to keep.

Unique vs common variance: the variance of each item has a part it shares with the other items (common variance) and a part special to that item, including measurement error (unique variance). PCA (Karl Pearson, 1901; developed by Harold Hotelling, 1933) analyses the total variance, common plus unique, and condenses many variables into a few components. The common-factor methods (principal axis, maximum likelihood, generalized least squares) analyse only the shared variance, looking for hidden factors that make the items correlate. That is the logic of the diagram's split: the method that keeps unique variance in (PCA) versus the methods that use common variance only.
Trap: strictly, PCA is a data-reduction technique rather than "true" factor analysis, even though it is run from the factor analysis menu. Every extraction method first gives an unrotated solution; rotation (below) comes afterwards.

PCA (Principal Component Analysis)

  • Uses Cattell's scree plot (for a large number of factors) and the Kaiser–Guttman criterion (for a small number of factors, < 20).
  • Eigenvalues indicate how much variance we expect each factor to explain; an eigenvalue of 1 is the minimum expectation from each factor.
  • In a scree plot: x-axis = order of factors; y-axis = unreduced eigenvalues.
  • Identity matrix: all the factors have 0 correlation with each other except with themselves.

Reading the scree plot

A scree plot (Raymond Cattell, 1966) graphs each factor's eigenvalue (y-axis) against its order (x-axis). The first factors explain a lot of variance, so the line drops steeply; later factors explain little, so it flattens out. "Scree" is the loose rubble at the foot of a cliff: the flat tail is the rubble you discard. Rule: find the elbow, where the steep slope turns into the flat tail, and keep the factors before it.

In the example plot (18 components), the eigenvalues fall from about 5.5 for the 1st to about 1.1 for the 5th, drop below 1 from the 6th onwards, and the line is almost flat from about the 8th. So the two arrows mark two possible cut-offs: the 5th factor is the last one with an eigenvalue above 1 (the Kaiser–Guttman rule would keep 5), and the 8th factor is where the curve levels off into scree (the elbow).

Trap: the Kaiser–Guttman rule (Louis Guttman, 1954; Henry Kaiser, 1960) keeps every factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1; the scree test keeps the factors above the elbow. The two rules can give different numbers, as in this plot, so researchers also check whether the factors make theoretical sense. Spotting the elbow is a judgement call, which is the scree test's main weakness.

8 · Regression & Correlation

  • Simple (bivariate) regression: Y = a + bx + E.
  • Multiple regression: Y = a + b1x1 + b2x2 + b3x3 + E, where b = partial slope.
  • The regression coefficient is indicated by β.
  • R = geometric mean of partial correlation (range 0–1).
  • Variance = R × R × 100%.

Partial correlation

Studying a single correlation in a multiple correlation matrix by removing the impact of other correlations.

9 · Reliability

Reliability checks if our test is consistent. It can be external and internal.

MethodDescriptionNotes / Formula
Test–retestGive the test to the same group after some time gap, and correlate the two tests (2 administrations).Source of error: practice effect, time sampling error, carryover effects.
Parallel / equivalent formsCorrelate the new test with a previous version of the test or a similar test.Can be done with or without a time gap.
Split halfComparing the first half of the score with the 2nd half; odd-even method may be used.Formula: r = 2r / (1 + r), i.e. full-test r = 2 × (half-test r) / (1 + half-test r). This is the Spearman–Brown formula with K = 2: each half is only half as long as the real test, so the raw correlation between halves underestimates reliability. Example: halves correlate .60, so full-test reliability = 1.20 / 1.60 = .75. Works for any item format.
Internal consistencyCorrelating performance on each item with overall performance across participants.Methods (as in the diagram): Cronbach's alpha, Kuder–Richardson, Spearman–Brown correction.
Kuder–Richardson methodInternal consistency for dichotomous items.Similar to Cronbach's alpha but can only be used for dichotomous items.
Inter-raterAgreement of raters with regards to performance of participants.Cohen's kappa is used (r should be > .8).
Key — Spearman–Brown correction: used to correct for the effect of the length of the test. Formula: Kr / (1 + (K − 1)r), where K = test length; r = correlation.
Key — Cronbach's alpha: average of all possible split halves; applicable to dichotomous and polytomous items.

The three internal consistency methods

In the diagram, Cronbach's alpha, Spearman–Brown and Kuder–Richardson all branch off internal consistency as its methods: each estimates reliability from a single administration by checking whether the items of one test hang together.

MethodWho / whenItem typeWhat it does
Cronbach's alphaLee Cronbach, 1951Any: dichotomous or polytomous (e.g. Likert)Average of all possible split halves; the most commonly reported reliability coefficient.
Kuder–Richardson (KR-20, KR-21)G. Frederic Kuder and M. W. Richardson, 1937Dichotomous only (right/wrong, yes/no)Same idea as alpha: KR-20 is what alpha becomes when items are scored 0/1. KR-21 is a shortcut that assumes all items are equally difficult.

Trap: "similar to Cronbach's alpha but only for dichotomous items" describes Kuder–Richardson, not split half; split half works with any item format. Most testing textbooks also count split half itself as an internal consistency method, since it needs only one administration.

10 · Validity & Threats

Validity (the centre of the wheel) refers to the accuracy of the test: whether it measures what it claims to measure. A test can be reliable (consistent) without being valid, but it cannot be valid without being reliable. Example: a bathroom scale that always reads 2 kg too heavy is reliable but not valid. The four quarters of the wheel are four kinds of evidence for that accuracy.

ValidityMeaning
Face validityWhat a judge or expert thinks about the test; how much a test appears to be accurate (MMPI (Starke Hathaway and J. Charnley McKinley, 1943) has poor face validity).
Content validityItems are relevant and representative of the target construct.
Construct validityUsed to validate a theory. Comparing a new test with another test that correlates to the same construct. The extent to which the test measures the theoretical trait (construct) it claims to, such as anxiety or intelligence (Lee Cronbach and Paul Meehl, 1955).
Criterion validityThe extent to which the test fulfills the criteria made by another source.
Trap: "refers to the accuracy of the test" defines validity as a whole (the centre of the wheel), not construct validity alone. The confusion has a source: the modern unified view (Samuel Messick, 1989) treats all validity evidence as ultimately evidence for construct validity. For an MCQ asking for the general definition, answer "validity".

Sub-types

  • Concurrent validity (criterion): the extent that a measure simultaneously relates to another measure that it is supposed to relate to, e.g. comparing a new personality questionnaire with the 16PF (Raymond Cattell).
  • Predictive validity (criterion): the extent that a measure predicts another measure, e.g. predicting a real exam through mocks.
  • Convergent validity (construct): the extent that different measures of the same construct converge or strongly correlate with one another.
  • Divergent validity (construct): the extent that measures of different constructs diverge or minimally correlate with one another.

12 · Research Ethics

Research ethics involve a subjective evaluation of the risk-benefit ratio.

  • Informed consent: a person's explicitly expressed willingness to participate in a research project based on a clear understanding of the nature of the research.
  • Privacy: the rights of individuals to decide how information about them is to be communicated to others.
  • Confidentiality: information obtained from a research participant will be kept secret and private.
  • Minimizing deception.
  • Debriefing: explaining to participants the need for deception, addressing misconceptions, and removing harmful effects resulting from deception.
