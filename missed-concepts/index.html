1. Vaillant's ladder of defences (Q36: dissociation)

George Vaillant (Adaptation to Life, 1977), working from the Harvard Grant Study, ranked the defences Sigmund and Anna Freud (1936) described into four levels by maturity. Your Q36 asked where dissociation sits: neurotic.

LevelDefencesWhat it means
I. PsychoticDelusional projection, psychotic denial (of external reality), distortionReality testing lost; psychosis, dreams, early childhood
II. ImmatureProjection, schizoid fantasy, hypochondriasis, passive aggression, acting outNormal in adolescence; in adults, personality disorders and substance misuse
III. NeuroticRepression, displacement, reaction formation, intellectualization (isolation, undoing), dissociation (neurotic denial)Private distress, reality testing intact, the person functions
IV. MatureAltruism, humour, sublimation, suppression, anticipationHealthy adults; predicted better health, work and marriage in the Grant Study

The DSM-IV Defensive Functioning Scale (1994) uses seven levels instead; dissociation is at the mental inhibitions (compromise formation) level, with repression, displacement, intellectualization, isolation of affect, reaction formation and undoing. Same neighbours, same verdict.

Trap: Suppression is conscious and mature; repression is unconscious and neurotic. Projection is immature, but delusional projection is psychotic. Dissociation is not immature, however dramatic dissociative disorders look.

2. Systematic desensitization and its neighbours (Q37)

Joseph Wolpe, Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition (1958). Principle: reciprocal inhibition (a term from Charles Sherrington): a response incompatible with anxiety, made in the presence of the feared stimulus, weakens the stimulus-anxiety bond. That is counterconditioning. Steps: (1) relaxation training (Edmund Jacobson's progressive relaxation, 1938); (2) anxiety hierarchy rated on SUDS, 0 to 100 (Wolpe, 1969); (3) imagine each scene while relaxed, moving up only when calm. Forerunner: Mary Cover Jones (1924), little Peter and the rabbit.

TechniqueWhat is doneMechanism
Systematic desensitizationRelaxation paired with a graded hierarchy, usually imaginedCounterconditioning
In vivo desensitizationSame, with the real stimulusCounterconditioning
FloodingMost feared stimulus at full strength, no escape, until anxiety fallsExtinction
Implosive therapy (Stampfl and Levis, 1967)Flooding in imagination, exaggerated scenes with psychodynamic themesExtinction
Aversion therapyUnwanted behaviour paired with an emetic or a shockClassical conditioning of an aversion
Exposure and response prevention (Meyer, 1966)Face the obsession's trigger, block the compulsion (OCD)Extinction plus blocking negative reinforcement

Trap: Reciprocal inhibition is the principle; systematic desensitization is the technique. 'Gradual' and 'relaxation' mean desensitization; 'full intensity' and 'no escape' mean flooding.

3. Habit reversal training (Q38: awareness training)

Nathan Azrin and Gregory Nunn (1973). For tics, Tourette's and body-focused repetitive behaviours: trichotillomania, skin picking, nail biting, also stuttering. Original package, four phases:

  1. Awareness training: response description, response detection, early warning (the urge or first movement), situation awareness. Your Q38.
  2. Competing response training: a physically incompatible, inconspicuous action (clench fists for hair pulling) held about a minute, or three minutes in the original, or until the urge passes.
  3. Habit control motivation: inconvenience review, social support, public display.
  4. Generalization training: rehearsing the routine across situations, often in imagination.

Simplified HRT (Raymond Miltenberger, Wayne Fuqua and Douglas Woods, 1998): awareness training, competing response training, social support. CBIT (John Piacentini and colleagues, 2010) is HRT plus function-based work, first-line for tics.

Trap: HRT is not the treatment for OCD (that is ERP), even though trichotillomania and excoriation sit in DSM-5's obsessive-compulsive and related chapter. Negative practice (Knight Dunlap, 1932), performing the habit on purpose until fatigue, is the opposite idea.

4. Flashbulb memory (Q39: confidence)

Vivid, detailed memories of the circumstances in which you heard shocking news. Canonical details: place, ongoing activity, informant, own feeling, others' feeling, aftermath.

StudyEventFinding
Roger Brown and James Kulik (1977)Kennedy assassination, 1963Coined the term; proposed a special 'Now Print!' mechanism (Livingston, 1967)
Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch (1992)Challenger explosion, 1986Re-tested about 2.5 years later: accuracy poor, about a quarter wrong on every major detail, confidence still high
Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin (2003)9/11, against an everyday memoryBoth lost consistency at the same rate; only flashbulb memories kept vividness and belief in accuracy. 'Confidence, not consistency'
Kathy Pezdek (2003)9/11Most students 'remembered' seeing the first plane hit live on TV; that footage aired only the next day
William Hirst and colleagues (2009, 2015)9/11, national sampleConsistency dropped in year one, then levelled off; confidence stayed high

Why vivid: emotional arousal at encoding (amygdala boosting hippocampal consolidation, James McGaugh) plus heavy rehearsal through media and retelling. No special mechanism needed.

Trap: Subjective confidence is very high and stays high; accuracy (consistency) falls like any memory. 'Confidence falls in step with accuracy' is wrong.

5. Paradoxical intention and Frankl's toolkit (Q40)

Viktor Frankl, logotherapy, the 'third Viennese school' after Freud and Adler. Primary motive: the will to meaning. Lack of meaning: existential vacuum, noogenic neurosis.

TechniqueTargetsInstruction
Paradoxical intention (first described 1939)Anticipatory anxiety, fear of a symptomIntend or wish for the feared thing, with humour: 'try to stay awake', 'show them how much you can sweat'
DereflectionHyper-reflection, excessive self-watchingTurn attention away from yourself, to the partner, a task, a meaning
Socratic dialogueLoss of meaningQuestioning that helps the client find meaning

Neighbours: negative practice (Knight Dunlap, 1932: perform the habit on purpose until fatigue); symptom prescription in family therapy (Milton Erickson, Jay Haley, Paul Watzlawick, 1974); for insomnia, stimulus control (Richard Bootzin, 1972: get up if not asleep in about 20 minutes) and sleep restriction (Arthur Spielman, 1987: limit time in bed).

Trap: 'Doing the opposite of the goal to reach the goal' is paradoxical intention. 'Stop watching yourself' is dereflection. Sensate focus is Masters and Johnson (1970), not Frankl.

7. The three gunas (the Bavisha question)

From Samkhya (Kapila; Ishvarakrishna's Samkhya Karika) and the Bhagavad Gita (chapters 14, 17, 18). Prakriti, matter including the mind, is made of three gunas, always present together; the dominant one shapes the person. Purusha is pure consciousness, the witness.

GunaQualityIn a personClinical mapping
SattvaLight, clarity, balanceCalm, truthful, content, wiseMental health
RajasActivity, passionRestless, ambitious, craving, angry, greedyAnxiety, agitation, mania
TamasDarkness, heaviness, inertiaDull, lethargic, withdrawn, deluded, oversleepingDepression, apathy

Bavisha, jobless, stressed, not eating, unable to act: shutdown, so tamas alone. Research measure: the Vedic Personality Inventory (David Wolf, 1998).

Trap: Yama is a limb of yoga (ethical restraints), not a guna or a state, so 'yama' and 'both yama and tamas' are wrong. Stressed and racing would be rajas; stuck and inert is tamas.

8. Patanjali's eight limbs (dharana)

Yoga Sutras: yoga is 'chitta vritti nirodhah', stilling the mind's fluctuations (1.2). The eight limbs, ashtanga (2.29). Mnemonic: Yogis Never Argue, Peaceful People Don't Disturb Silence.

#LimbMeaning
1YamaRestraints: ahimsa, satya, asteya, brahmacharya, aparigraha
2NiyamaObservances: shaucha, santosha, tapas, svadhyaya, ishvara pranidhana
3AsanaPosture, steady and comfortable (2.46)
4PranayamaBreath regulation
5PratyaharaWithdrawal of the senses (2.54)
6DharanaConcentration: binding the mind to one point (3.1)
7DhyanaMeditation: unbroken flow of that focus (3.2)
8SamadhiAbsorption, only the object remains (3.3)

Dharana + dhyana + samadhi on one object = samyama (3.4). Limbs 1 to 5 are bahiranga (outer), 6 to 8 antaranga (inner). Jnana yoga is a path in the Gita (with karma and bhakti), not a limb.

Trap: Senses in is pratyahara; mind fixed on one point is dharana; focus flowing on its own is dhyana. Pranayama before pratyahara, dharana before dhyana.

9. Kegels and the pelvic floor

Arnold Kegel, American gynaecologist, 1948: exercises for urinary incontinence after childbirth. Target: the pelvic floor, mainly the levator ani and its pubococcygeus (PC) part, running from the pubic bone to the tailbone and supporting the bladder, bowel and uterus. Method: squeeze and lift for a few seconds, relax, repeat in sets.

  • Uses: stress urinary incontinence, postpartum recovery, pelvic organ prolapse, incontinence after prostate surgery, faecal incontinence.
  • Early biofeedback: Kegel's perineometer, a pressure gauge the patient watched. Biofeedback types: EMG (tension headache, pelvic floor), thermal (migraine, Raynaud's), GSR (arousal), heart rate variability, EEG or neurofeedback (ADHD, epilepsy).
  • Sex therapy: part of directed masturbation for female orgasmic disorder (Joseph LoPiccolo and W. Charles Lobitz, 1972); an add-on for premature ejaculation; pelvic floor relaxation with graded dilators for vaginismus.

Trap: Bed-wetting in a child is not a Kegel problem. First-line is the enuresis alarm, the bell-and-pad (O. Hobart Mowrer and Willie Mae Mowrer, 1938), or desmopressin. Squeezing the buttocks or abdomen is not a Kegel.
