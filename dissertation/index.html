The 30-second pitch (lead with this)

"Tell me about your dissertation."

"We looked at how pornography consumption relates to personality, aggression and sexual compulsivity in young adults in India. It was a quantitative, cross-sectional survey of 214 people aged 18 to 28, run through a Google Form using the BFI-10 for the Big Five, the Buss-Perry Aggression Questionnaire Short Form, and the Sexual Compulsivity Scale, plus a short screening section on consumption habits. We analysed it in SPSS with Spearman's rank correlation and logistic regression. The headline findings: men were about 2.75 times more likely to consume porn, sexual compulsivity was the strongest predictor of both whether and how often people consumed, higher neuroticism predicted lower consumption, and aggression and sexual compulsivity were positively correlated. So personality and impulse-control variables, not aggression on its own, drove consumption patterns."

Keep it to four sentences if they want it shorter: what (porn × personality/aggression/compulsivity), how (214, cross-sectional survey, three validated scales, Spearman + logistic in SPSS), found (sex + sexual compulsivity are the big predictors; neuroticism negative), so what (screen for compulsivity, not moral panic).

Why this topic

"Why pornography? Why did you pick this?"

"Porn is everywhere in the digital age, especially among young adults, but it's under-studied in the Indian context because it's taboo, and most research treats it through a moral lens rather than an empirical one. We wanted to look at it the way you'd look at any behaviour with addictive potential: which personality traits, which impulse-control patterns, which behavioural tendencies actually relate to it. The gap we were filling was the interplay between personality, aggression and sexual compulsivity together, in Indian young adults, which hadn't really been mapped."

"Why does it matter clinically?"

"Because if you can identify which traits flag someone at risk of problematic or compulsive use, you can screen and intervene early, the same logic as any behavioural addiction. Our finding that sexual compulsivity predicts consumption supports treating problematic use as part of a behavioural-addiction framework, not a moral failing."

Why these scales (know each one cold)

"Why the BFI-10 and not the full BFI-44 or NEO-PI-R?"

"The BFI-10 (Rammstedt & John, 2007) is a 10-item short form of the Big Five — two items per trait, 5-point Likert, some items reverse-scored, measures OCEAN: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism. We chose it because the survey was already long and on a sensitive topic, so keeping personality to under a minute reduced fatigue and drop-out. The honest trade-off is that two items per trait gives lower reliability than the 44-item version, and I'd flag that as a limitation."

"Why the Buss-Perry Short Form?"

"The BPAQ-SF (Bryant & Smith, 2001) is a 12-item condensed version of the Buss-Perry, three items each across four factors: physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger and hostility. Bryant and Smith moved it to a 6-point scale, removing the neutral midpoint to force a direction. Confirmatory factor analysis upheld the four-factor structure, and it has concurrent validity against the Personality Assessment Inventory. Short, validated, four-factor — right fit for a multi-scale survey."

"Why the Sexual Compulsivity Scale?"

"The SCS (Kalichman & Rompa, 1995) is a 10-item scale assessing sexual compulsivity and hypersexuality — preoccupation with sexual thoughts, failure to control sexual behaviour. Its items came from self-reports of people who identified as having a sexual addiction. It has high internal consistency, alpha between .85 and .91. We used it as our index of the compulsive/impulse-control dimension, and it turned out to be our strongest predictor."

"What was the screening tool?"

"A short author-built section that confirmed whether someone had ever consumed pornography, and asked when they started, the type of content, the platform, and duration of use. It set up the consumption variables (ever-consumed yes/no, and frequency) that the scales were then related to. Because it's not a standardized instrument, I'd treat it as a limitation on the measurement side."

Why Spearman, not Pearson — and why logistic regression

"Why Spearman's rank correlation instead of Pearson?" (the classic viva question)

"Pearson assumes both variables are continuous, interval-level, normally distributed and linearly related. Several of our variables broke those assumptions: sex is dichotomous, ever-consumed is binary, frequency of consumption is an ordinal category, and on a stigmatised topic the data are skewed, not normal. Spearman's rho is the non-parametric, rank-based equivalent — it doesn't assume normality or interval data, works on ordinal variables, captures monotonic (not strictly linear) relationships, and is robust to outliers. So Spearman was the correct, assumption-safe choice for this data."

"Why logistic regression and not linear/multiple regression?"

"Because our outcomes were categorical, not continuous. 'Ever consumed pornography' is binary (yes/no) so we used binary logistic regression. 'Frequency of consumption' and 'perceived control' are ordered categories, so we used ordinal logistic regression. Linear regression needs a continuous, normally-distributed outcome and would be invalid here. Logistic regression models the log-odds of the outcome and gives us odds ratios — Exp(B) — which tell us how each predictor changes the odds."

"What's the difference between your two regressions?"

"Binary logistic models a yes/no outcome (did they ever consume). Ordinal logistic models an ordered multi-level outcome (how frequently, and perceived control levels), and instead of one cut-point it estimates a set of thresholds between the ordered categories while assuming the predictors act the same way across them — the proportional-odds assumption."

Your actual findings (with the real numbers)

Examiners love "what did you actually find?" Vague answers sink people. Give the direction and the statistic. Significance flags: ** p<.01, * p<.05. N = 214 throughout.

Sex and consumption

Males consumed more. Spearman ρ = .264** between sex and ever-consumed. Logistic regression: sex predicted frequency with Exp(B) = 2.752, p = .004 — males about 2.75× more likely to consume more frequently. Lines up with the literature (Perry & Schleifer, 2018) and the evolutionary visual-stimuli argument (Lykins, Meana & Strauss, 2008).

Sexual compulsivity — your biggest result

Strongest, most consistent predictor. Correlated with frequency at ρ = .397**. In binary logistic it predicted ever-consuming with B = .438, p < .001, Exp(B) = 1.549; in ordinal logistic it predicted frequency with B = .115, p = .001, Exp(B) = 1.158; and it predicted lower perceived control with B = .126, p = .002. So compulsivity drove whether, how often, and loss of control.

Neuroticism — the counter-intuitive one

Higher neuroticism predicted less consumption. Spearman ρ = −.240**; logistic B = −.465, p < .001, Exp(B) = .628. We read this as guilt/shame and emotional regulation in high-neuroticism people deterring use, or under-reporting (Laier & Brand, 2014).

Conscientiousness and perceived control

Conscientiousness was the personality trait that mattered for control: B = −.349, p = .009, which we interpreted as higher conscientiousness going with a greater sense of control over consumption — consistent with conscientiousness being the self-discipline / impulse-control trait.

Aggression

Aggression correlated with consumption (ρ = .152*) and strongly with sexual compulsivity (ρ = .376**), and had a small significant effect on reduced perceived control (B = .059, p = .038). But aggression did not significantly predict consumption in the regression once the other variables were in the model. So the aggression link is real but indirect — likely working through sexual compulsivity.

What didn't matter

Extraversion, agreeableness and openness showed no significant effect on consumption. Agreeableness specifically did not predict consumption, against what we hypothesised.

Limitations (have these ready before they ask)

Every panel asks for limitations. Volunteering them first is the strongest move. Frame each as "here's the limit, here's why, here's what I'd do differently."

Ethics (the sensitive-topic question)

"This is a sensitive topic. How did you handle the ethics?"

"Four things. One, informed consent — participation was voluntary, and the form covered consent, instructions, and what the study was about up front. Two, full anonymity — we collected no personal or identifying information, which is the key protection when you're asking about a stigmatised behaviour. Three, a written debrief plus contact details were provided in case anyone had questions or concerns after. Four, it was restricted to adults 18 and over, so no minors were asked about sexual content. We also gave an undertaking that the data wouldn't be used for publication without our guide's and the director's written approval."

"What would you add ethically if you ran it again?"

"An explicit right-to-withdraw statement, a clearer trigger warning given the content, and signposting to support resources for anyone who recognised compulsive patterns in themselves while answering. On a topic touching sexual compulsivity, pointing distressed respondents somewhere is the responsible thing."

Stats follow-ups they'll drill (the why-Spearman question never comes alone)

"What does Exp(B) mean? Interpret one of yours."

"Exp(B) is the odds ratio — how the odds of the outcome change per one-unit rise in the predictor. My sexual-compulsivity Exp(B) of 1.549 means each one-unit increase in compulsivity multiplied the odds of having consumed porn by about 1.55, roughly 55% higher odds. My neuroticism Exp(B) of .628 is below 1, so each unit of neuroticism multiplied the odds by .63 — about 37% lower odds. Above 1 increases odds, below 1 decreases them."

"What does ρ = .397 actually tell me?"

"It's a moderate positive monotonic relationship — as sexual compulsivity ranks go up, consumption frequency ranks tend to go up too. It's rank-based, so it's about order, not raw distances. And ** means it's significant at p < .01, so unlikely to be chance."

"What's a p-value? What does p < .001 mean here?"

"The probability of getting a result this extreme if the null hypothesis — no real relationship — were true. p < .001 means under a tenth of a percent, so we reject the null and treat the effect as real, not sampling noise."

"Why reverse-score items on the BFI?"

"Because some items are worded in the opposite direction to the trait, so a high agreement actually means a low trait score. You flip them before summing so every item points the same way and the total reflects the trait correctly. It also helps counter response-set bias."

"What's Cronbach's alpha and why does the SCS's .85–.91 matter?"

"Alpha is internal-consistency reliability — how well the items hang together measuring one thing, from 0 to 1. Above .8 is good, above .9 excellent. The SCS sitting at .85 to .91 tells me my compulsivity measure was reliable, which matters because it carried my main finding."

Where people fumble (read this last)

Trap 1 — claiming causation. It's cross-sectional. Never say porn "causes" aggression or compulsivity. Say "associated with," "correlated with," "predicted." Your own discussion flags that the direction is unknown.
Trap 2 — the conscientiousness sign. The B is negative (−.349) but you interpret it as higher conscientiousness → more perceived control. If they push on the negative sign, the honest line is: the direction depends on how the perceived-control variable was coded/ordered, and the substantive reading is that the self-discipline trait goes with a greater sense of control. Don't pretend it's obvious; explain the coding.
Trap 3 — aggression "predicting" use. Aggression correlated with consumption but was not a significant predictor in the regression. The reconciliation: once sexual compulsivity is in the model, it absorbs the shared variance, so aggression's link is likely indirect, through compulsivity. Correlation ≠ unique predictive power.
Trap 4 — "why a short scale?" Don't get defensive. The answer is fatigue and drop-out on a long sensitive survey, then own the reliability cost as a limitation. Trade-off acknowledged beats trade-off hidden.
Trap 5 — your own role. It's a three-person dissertation (you, Jewela Vaz, Lecia Debbarma). Be ready to state specifically what you did — data collection, which analyses you ran, which sections you wrote. "We did it together" is a weak viva answer.
