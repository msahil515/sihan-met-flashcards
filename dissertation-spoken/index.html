Dissertation: answers to say

Practice scenario: assume Shapiro-Wilk was used.

Open a selectable plain-text copy

1What was your study about, and what were the variables?

“My study examined associations between personality, aggression, sexual compulsivity and pornography use in 214 young adults. I measured the Big Five with the BFI-10, aggression with the BPAQ-SF and sexual compulsivity with the SCS; pornography-use measures were ever-use, frequency and perceived control. Age and sex were also recorded.”

2What were your hypotheses?

“The revised hypotheses ask whether frequency differs by sex; whether compulsivity relates to frequency and control; and whether conscientiousness relates to control. They also examine neuroticism and agreeableness separately in relation to ever-use, aggression in relation to each use measure, and aggression with compulsivity among ever-consumers.”

3What were your main findings?

“In the regression models, compulsivity and neuroticism were significant for ever-use; only compulsivity was significant for frequency; and conscientiousness, compulsivity and aggression were significant for perceived control. Aggression and compulsivity also had a positive correlation in the full sample, rho=.376. These are associations, not causal effects.”

4Why did you use Shapiro-Wilk, and how do you interpret it?

“I used Shapiro-Wilk, alongside Q-Q plots, to assess the distributions of the approximately continuous scale scores. Its null hypothesis is normality: at alpha=.05, p<.05 indicates evidence against normality, while p>=.05 means insufficient evidence to reject it, not proof of normality.”

5Why did you use Spearman's correlation?

“I used Spearman's rho to assess monotonic associations using ranks, without requiring bivariate normality. It shows the strength and direction of a rank association, but does not establish causation.”

6Why did you use binary logistic regression?

“Ever-use was a yes-or-no outcome, so I used binary logistic regression to examine its association with several predictors together. It estimates odds for the modeled event and does not require normally distributed predictors.”

7Why did you use ordinal logistic regression?

“Frequency and perceived control had ordered categories, so ordinal logistic regression used that order without assuming equal gaps between categories. A key assumption is proportional odds: the predictor effects are the same across the cumulative category splits.”

8What does the Wald test tell you?

“The Wald test assesses whether an individual regression coefficient differs from zero while accounting for the other predictors in the model. For a one-parameter test, its statistic is approximately the squared ratio of the coefficient to its standard error.”

9How do you interpret B, p, confidence intervals and odds ratios?

“B is the coefficient on the model's logit scale, and exp(B) gives the corresponding odds ratio, interpreted using the outcome coding and model convention. The p-value indicates how incompatible the data are with a zero coefficient under the model; a 95% confidence interval shows the estimate's precision. For a matching two-sided .05 test, the null value is zero for B and one for the odds ratio; odds are not probabilities.”

10What can you conclude about the revised hypotheses?

“At the unadjusted .05 level, the corresponding regression coefficients were significant for R2, R3, R4a, R5 and R6c, but not for R4b, R6a or R6b. R1 needs a sex-by-frequency comparison, and R7 needs the correlation within ever-consumers. A nonsignificant result does not prove that there is no association.”
