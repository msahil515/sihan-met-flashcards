1 · Types of intelligence forum dig

Why this leads. "Types of intelligence" was the single most-reported topic out of the entrance. They like it because one MCQ can test theorist + the structure of his model + a trap pairing. Learn each theory as who → what's the structure → one keyword, and you can answer almost anything they throw.
Spearman — two-factor theory (g and s).Charles Spearman, via factor analysis (he invented it for this). Every mental task draws on a single general intelligence (g) plus a task-specific factor (s). The positive correlation between all cognitive tests is the evidence for g.Trap: crediting factor analysis or "g" to anyone else. g = general; s = specific. The classic distractor is Thurstone, who argued the opposite (no single g).
Thurstone — Primary Mental Abilities (PMA).L.L. Thurstone said intelligence is not one g but 7 independent primary abilities: Verbal comprehension, Word fluency, Number, Spatial, Memory, Perceptual speed, Reasoning (V, W, N, S, M, P, R).Trap: the number is 7. Thurstone is the anti-Spearman, "multiple independent factors," not a hierarchy.
Cattell (& Horn) — fluid vs crystallised.Raymond Cattell split g into fluid intelligence (Gf) = reasoning with novel problems, peaks young, biology-based; and crystallised intelligence (Gc) = accumulated knowledge/vocabulary, grows with age/experience. Horn extended it; later merged into the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model.Trap: which one declines with age. Fluid declines; crystallised holds/rises. Mnemonic: "fluid drains."
Guilford — Structure of Intellect (SOI).J.P. Guilford, a 3-D cube: Operations × Contents × Products, originally 120 abilities (later 150/180). Source of the convergent vs divergent thinking distinction (divergent = creativity).Trap: the cube has 3 dimensions, not 3 abilities. Convergent = one right answer; divergent = many answers (creativity) — they swap these.
Gardner — Multiple Intelligences.Howard Gardner, originally 7 (later 8-9) relatively independent intelligences: Linguistic, Logical-mathematical, Spatial, Musical, Bodily-kinaesthetic, Interpersonal, Intrapersonal (then Naturalistic, and debated Existential).Trap: counting (start from 7/8) and the inter- vs intra- pair. Interpersonal = understanding others; intrapersonal = understanding yourself.
Sternberg — Triarchic theory.Robert Sternberg, three parts: Componential/Analytical (academic problem-solving), Experiential/Creative (novel situations), Contextual/Practical ("street smarts," adapting to environment). Later the WICS / "successful intelligence" model.Trap: matching label to meaning. Practical = real-world/street smarts; Analytical = the testing kind. Don't confuse with Gardner (his is 7-9 separate ones, Sternberg's is 3 processes).
Vernon — hierarchical model.Philip Vernon: g at the top → two major group factors, verbal-educational (v:ed) and practical-mechanical (k:m) → minor factors → specifics. A compromise between Spearman and Thurstone.Trap: it's a hierarchy with g on top, so it does keep g (unlike Thurstone).
Thorndike — multifactor / three kinds.E.L. Thorndike denied a single g and proposed intelligence as many independent factors, popularly summarised as three: Abstract (ideas/symbols), Mechanical/Concrete (objects), Social (people).Trap: "Social intelligence" originates with Thorndike (1920), a common single-fact MCQ. Don't credit it to Gardner or Goleman.
Emotional intelligence — Salovey & Mayer vs Goleman.Salovey & Mayer (1990) coined and modelled EI as an ability (perceive, use, understand, manage emotions). Daniel Goleman (1995) popularised it in the bestseller and added the mixed/competency model (self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills). Bar-On gave the EQ-i and the term "EQ."Trap: Goleman popularised it; Salovey & Mayer coined/originated it. They test "who first proposed EI" (Salovey & Mayer) vs "who made it famous" (Goleman).
Binet, Stern, Terman — the IQ lineage.Binet & Simon (1905) built the first practical intelligence test (mental age). William Stern coined the Intelligence Quotient = (MA/CA) × 100. Terman brought it to Stanford → the Stanford-Binet. Wechsler later gave deviation IQ + separate verbal/performance scales (WAIS/WISC).Trap: who coined "IQ" (Stern, not Binet, not Terman). Ratio IQ = Stern/Terman; deviation IQ = Wechsler.
One-line recall ladder (say it out loud): Spearman = g+s · Thurstone = 7 PMA · Cattell = fluid/crystallised · Guilford = SOI cube/divergent · Gardner = 7-9 multiple · Sternberg = 3 (analytical/creative/practical) · Vernon = hierarchy · Thorndike = abstract/mechanical/social + coined social intelligence · EI = Salovey-Mayer coined, Goleman popularised · IQ = Stern coined.

2 · Introspection & structuralism forum dig

Why it shows up. Introspection is the entry point to the whole "schools of psychology" question. If they ask about introspection, the surrounding MCQs are usually structuralism vs functionalism, Wundt vs Titchener, and why introspection failed. Learn the cluster, not the single word.
What introspection is.The systematic self-observation and reporting of one's own conscious experience (sensations, feelings, images) under controlled conditions. It was the core method of structuralism, the attempt to break consciousness into its basic elements.Trap: "introspection = just thinking about yourself / casual reflection." No, it's a trained, systematic laboratory method with specific stimuli, that rigor is the point.
Wundt — father of experimental psychology.Wilhelm Wundt opened the first psychology lab, Leipzig, 1879 (the date of psychology becoming a science). His method was experimental introspection, his goal was to find the elements of consciousness. His broader project also included Völkerpsychologie (cultural/folk psychology).Trap: 1879 + Leipzig + Wundt is a single high-frequency fact. Don't credit the first lab to Titchener (his was at Cornell) or James.
Titchener — structuralism.Edward Titchener, Wundt's student, brought the school to the US and named it structuralism. He pushed introspection hardest, mapping the elements of consciousness (he listed thousands of sensations) into sensations, images, affections.Trap: Wundt vs Titchener. Wundt = founder/lab/experimental introspection; Titchener = structuralism as a named school. The term "structuralism" is Titchener's, not Wundt's.
Why introspection failed.It was unreliable and unverifiable: results couldn't be reproduced across trained observers, it couldn't study children/animals/abnormal minds, and the "imageless thought" debate (Würzburg school) showed observers disagreeing on whether thought has sensory content. This opened the door for behaviourism (Watson) to reject internal states entirely.Trap: the criticism is "not objective / not replicable," not "too difficult." Watson's behaviourism is the direct reaction against it.
Structuralism vs functionalism.Structuralism (Wundt/Titchener) asks what the mind is made of (its structure/elements). Functionalism (William James, Dewey, Angell; influenced by Darwin) asks what the mind is for (its function/purpose in adaptation). James wrote Principles of Psychology (1890) and gave the "stream of consciousness."Trap: structure = what it's made of; function = what it does. James = functionalism + stream of consciousness; Titchener = structuralism + introspection.

3 · Linguistic relativity (Sapir-Whorf) forum dig

Why it's a favourite. One named hypothesis, two versions, a clean strong/weak distinction, and a famous (and partly debunked) example. That's a perfect MCQ generator. Nail the strong-vs-weak split and you've got it.
The hypothesis.The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (linguistic relativity): the language you speak shapes how you think and perceive the world. Named for Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf.Trap: spelling/attribution, Sapir was the teacher, Whorf the student. It's also just called "linguistic relativity."
Strong vs weak version.Strong = linguistic determinism: language determines thought, you literally cannot think what your language can't express. Weak = linguistic relativity: language influences thought and perception, but doesn't fully determine it. The strong version is largely rejected; the weak version has some support.Trap: the whole question usually hinges on determines (strong) vs influences (weak). "Determinism" is the strong one and the discredited one.
The classic examples.The (overstated) "Eskimo words for snow" claim; colour-term studies (languages that don't separate blue/green); and grammatical gender affecting object descriptions. Berlin & Kay's colour research is the standard counter-evidence (universal focal colours).Trap: the "Eskimo snow words" example is famously exaggerated, if they ask what undermines strong Whorfianism, colour universals (Berlin & Kay) is the answer.
Who's the opposition.Noam Chomsky (universal grammar, innate language faculty) and the nativist view sit against strong linguistic relativity, language is largely universal/innate, not the thing that builds thought. Pinker ("The Language Instinct") popularised the critique.Trap: Chomsky = universal grammar / nativism = the opposite camp from Whorf. Don't mix Chomsky (innate universal grammar) with Skinner (verbal behaviour, learning) — that's a separate famous debate.

C · Research methods & stats one-liners

  • IV vs DV: IV = manipulated/cause; DV = measured/effect. (They reliably flip these.)
  • Reliability vs validity: reliability = consistency; validity = measures what it claims. A test can be reliable but not valid, not the reverse in the useful sense.
  • Mean / median / mode: mode = most frequent; median = middle (robust to outliers); mean = average (pulled by outliers). Skew: in a positive (right) skew, mean > median > mode.
  • Central tendency vs dispersion: SD/variance/range = dispersion, not central tendency.
  • Type I vs Type II error: Type I = false positive (reject a true null, α); Type II = false negative (accept a false null, β).
  • Normal curve: 68-95-99.7% within 1/2/3 SD; mean = median = mode at centre.
  • Correlation ≠ causation; r ranges −1 to +1; 0 = no linear relationship.
  • Sampling: random (probability) vs purposive/convenience (non-probability); stratified = divide into strata then sample each.

D · Abnormal & clinical anchors

  • DSM-5-TR (APA, US) vs ICD-11 (WHO, international). Know it's DSM-5 now, not IV.
  • Schizophrenia: positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) vs negative (flat affect, avolition); Schneider's first-rank symptoms.
  • Mood: MDD vs bipolar (mania/hypomania); unipolar = depression only.
  • Anxiety cluster: GAD, panic, phobias, (OCD & PTSD moved OUT of anxiety in DSM-5 into their own chapters, a classic update trap).
  • Defence mechanisms (Freud/Anna Freud): repression, projection, denial, displacement, sublimation, reaction formation, rationalisation. Sublimation = the "mature/healthy" one.

5 · The English / aptitude section forum dig

The consistent forum line: "For the entrance you'll be answering some basic English grammar paper, that's it." Multiple Amity MA-Psych alumni describe the verbal/English chunk as the part you can't skip. It's bachelor's/CAT-lite level, not GRE-hard. You won't out-study this in a week, but you can stop losing easy marks.

The grammar/vocab traps worth memorising

Subject-verb agreement with intervening phrases.The verb agrees with the subject, not the nearest noun. "The box of chocolates is (not are) on the table." "Each / every / either / neither / one of" → singular verb.Trap: a long phrase between subject and verb to pull you to the wrong number.
Affect vs effect.Affect = verb, to influence ("it affects me"). Effect = noun, the result ("the effect was clear"). (Rare: "effect" as verb = to bring about; "affect" as noun = emotion, the clinical term you already know.)Trap: the one place psychology helps you, "affect" as a noun is the emotion term.
Fewer vs less / fewer countable.Fewer for countable nouns (fewer errors), less for uncountable (less time).Trap: "less mistakes" is wrong; it should be "fewer mistakes."
Who vs whom.Who = subject (who called?), whom = object (to whom did you speak?). If you can answer "him/her," use whom.Trap: formal-register sentences designed to make "who" sound right when "whom" is correct.
Para-jumble strategy.Find the independent opening (no pronoun referring back, no "this/therefore/however" with nothing before it). Then chain by connectors and pronoun antecedents. The last sentence usually concludes ("thus," "finally").Trap: a sentence that looks like an intro but contains "it/this/such," it can't be first.

6 · The interview — scripted Amity gates on this

The hub covers what the PI probes and what's confirmed vs inferred. This is the study-pack version: actual model answers you can adapt, built around the rule you asked for, show your reasoning, don't just state a conclusion.

Honest note: there's no verified first-hand transcript of the Amity Clinical PI (exact questions, panel, length). These are model answers for the questions the rubric + analogous psych interviews make near-certain. Adapt them to your own facts, don't recite them, a memorised answer reads as memorised.
"Why clinical psychology?"
Weak answer
"I've always found the human mind fascinating and I like helping people."
Strong answer (show reasoning)
"Three things made it specifically clinical rather than general or counselling. One, I'm drawn to the assessment side, the structured work of using psychometric tools and DSM/ICD criteria to actually formulate a case, not just talk. Two, I want to work with diagnosable conditions and real patients, which is the clinical scope, not the wellness/coaching end. Three, I understand it's the RCI-licensing path, the M.Phil-replacing MA, that lets me practice as a Clinical Psychologist, and that's the registration I'm aiming for. So it's a deliberate choice of the regulated clinical track, not psychology in general."
"Tell us about a psychological concept you find interesting."
Show reasoning, don't dump facts
Pick one you own (e.g. attachment, or the diathesis-stress model). Structure: what it is → why it matters clinically → a limitation or open question. Example: "The diathesis-stress model, that disorders come from a predisposition interacting with environmental stress. I like it because it explains why two people with the same trauma have different outcomes, and clinically it's hopeful: you can't change the diathesis, but you can target the stress and coping. Its limit is that it can be vague about how much of each, which is where gene-environment research is now pushing."
"Why Amity / why this campus?"
Strong answer
"I looked at the program shape specifically, internship every semester and a dissertation, so it's hands-on across both years rather than theory then a thesis at the end. For a clinical license you need supervised practice hours, and a placement-every-semester structure builds that early. I also went in knowing the load is heavy [foreign language + 12-13 subjects at Noida], and I'd rather a demanding program that prepares me for RCI work than a light one."
"Walk us through your background / why the switch (if non-psych UG)."
Strong answer
Frame any gap as deliberate and show the self-study. "My UG was X, but I've been preparing seriously for clinical psychology, I've worked through abnormal psychology on DSM/ICD, the therapy schools, research methods and stats, and I keep my own structured revision system. So the switch isn't a whim; I've already covered the bachelor's-level clinical core and I can speak to it." (Then be ready, they may test a concept on the spot.)

The 2-3 minute micro-presentation (RCI track)

The RCI/PsyD track adds a short on-the-spot talk. Have one topic you can present cold, structured. A reliable shape:

  1. Hook + definition (15s): name the topic and define it in one clean sentence.
  2. Structure / key model (60-90s): the 3-4 parts, with one example each. (Therapy schools or psychological assessment are good picks, you've got cheatsheets for both.)
  3. Clinical relevance (30s): why a practitioner cares.
  4. One limitation / current debate (15s): shows you think, not just recite.

Re-skim your own cheatsheets the night before, they are the interview's "core knowledge" probe: therapy schools, abnormal/DSM-ICD, stats.

Three questions to ask them

  • "Is this specific program the RCI-regulated MA, and does completing it make me eligible to register with RCI as a Clinical Psychologist?" (signals you understand the licensing game)
  • "Which hospitals / clinics do the semester placements run with?"
  • "What's the typical onward path, M.Phil/PsyD or direct practice?"

Drill plan

If you've got limited runway, do it in this order, it's sequenced by yield:

  1. Section 1 (intelligence) + Section 2 (introspection/schools) + Section 3 (Whorf) first, these are the three the forums named most and they're tightly testable. Use the one-line recall ladders.
