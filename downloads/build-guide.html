Build It From Scratch

How someone with a brand-new Claude account could build everything on this site and in these apps, without knowing any code.
Written 23 November 2026, from what was actually built for Sihan between May and November 2026, including what went wrong.

You will never write code. Claude writes all of it. Your job is three things: know what to ask for, give it the right material (the real syllabus, the real papers, the real books), and check what comes back before you trust it. This guide tells you how to do each one, in the order that works.

0 · What you end up with

By the end you have one private study system that lives on the internet and on your devices. These are the parts, in plain words:

MocksTests you take on screen: 77 mocks with about 6,350 questions, most of them real past papers, grouped by college. Every question explains the concept from scratch and why each wrong option is wrong.

You do not build all of this at once. It grew one ask at a time over six months. Build the first mock first. Everything else is the same move repeated: ask, check, publish.

1 · What you need before you start

  • A Claude subscription. Pro is enough to start. Max if you will use it for hours every day. Even Max has a weekly usage limit, and big jobs (writing a whole textbook) can hit it. When it hits, work stops until the limit resets, so plan big jobs early, not the week before an exam.
  • Claude Code. This is the part of Claude that can make files, run things and publish them. The normal Claude chat can only talk. You can use Claude Code inside the Claude desktop app or in a terminal window. Install it from Anthropic's own instructions, then open it in an empty folder.
  • A free GitHub account. GitHub stores your files and puts the site on the internet for free. Pick a username you do not mind being part of your site's address.
  • A laptop. Mac is easiest. Claude Code runs there and does the work. For the phone apps, Claude installs the Android build tools on it for you.
  • Your phone or tab, to open the site and install the apps.
  • Your material: the official syllabus, every past paper you can find, answer keys, and the books the exam is set from. Chapter 4 covers this.
Rule for the whole build: one folder on the laptop, one GitHub project, one site. Do not let it split into three half-copies. Everything new goes into the same place.

2 · How the pieces fit together

You do not need to understand the code, but you do need this picture, because it tells you what to ask for and why things break.

  • The site is just a folder of pages. No server, no database, no monthly hosting bill. GitHub shows that folder to the world at a fixed address. When Claude "pushes", it uploads the changed pages, and about a minute later the site shows them.
  • Your answers stay on your device. Scores and progress are saved inside the browser or the app on that phone. Nothing is stored on a server, which is why sync (chapter 12) has to be set up on purpose.
  • The apps are the site in a wrapper. An Android app here is the same pages packed inside an app, so it opens fast and works without internet.
  • The apps update themselves. Every time an app opens, it checks a short list on the site of which files changed, downloads just those, and uses them from the next launch. That is why a new mock shows up after you close and open the app twice, without installing anything.
  • Scheduled jobs keep the trackers fresh. GitHub can run a small job on a timer. Twice a day it checks the official college pages for changes and updates the tracker. No one has to do it by hand.

3 · Day 1: an empty site, live

Do not start with content. First prove the pipe works end to end: you ask, Claude builds, it goes live, you open it on your phone.

Say this to Claude CodeI want to build a private study website for my psychology entrance exams. Keep it simple: plain web pages, no frameworks, nothing that needs a server or a monthly bill. Host it free on GitHub Pages. Each test should work offline, and my scores should stay on my device. Set up the empty site with a home page and a passphrase lock screen, publish it, and give me the live link so I can check it on my phone before we build anything real.

Claude will ask you to log in to GitHub once. After that it publishes by itself.

Check before you move on

  • The link opens on your phone, not just on the laptop.
  • The passphrase screen comes up, and the right passphrase gets you in.
  • Ask Claude to change one word on the home page and publish again. Refresh the phone. If the word changed, the pipe works. If it did not, ask Claude to check that the publish actually finished, not just that it was sent.
Then sayNow give the site a top menu: Home, Tests, Notes, Results, Plan, Downloads. Make one look that every page shares, with a day and night mode, so it feels like one app and not a pile of pages.

4 · Collect the raw material

This is the step people skip, and then wonder why everything feels generic. Depth comes from the source. If Claude teaches from memory you get a summary. If it teaches from the real syllabus, the real papers and the real books, you get something that matches the exam.

What to gather

  • The official syllabus for each exam, straight from the college's own site or brochure.
  • Every past paper you can get, with the answer key if one exists. Photos and scans are fine.
  • The standard books the papers are set from (here: Pinel for biopsychology, Schultz for personality, Eysenck and Keane for cognition, Barlow and Durand for abnormal, and so on).
  • Coaching mock papers, but keep them labelled as practice, never as real papers.

How to hand it over

  • Put the files in one folder on the laptop and tell Claude where it is. Or send it a Google Drive link.
  • Do not rely on WhatsApp for files. WhatsApp deletes photos and PDFs from its servers after a while. Here, a whole paper and about 15 photos were lost because nobody downloaded them in time. Drive links do not expire.
  • If the files sit in iCloud, make sure they are actually downloaded to the laptop, not just showing as icons. Claude cannot read a file that is only a placeholder.
Say thisHere is the official syllabus PDF. Give me its exact structure first: every section and topic, word for word as printed, with the marks for each. Do not reorganise it into your own categories yet.
Scans come out garbled. Two columns get mixed together and tick marks vanish. Ask Claude to look at scanned pages as pictures, not just pull the text. Then always check the count: if the paper has 100 questions and Claude found 92, make it find the missing 8 before anything else.

5 · Turn a past paper into a mock

This is the heart of the whole system. Do the first one slowly and get it right, then every paper after it follows the same recipe.

Say thisHere is the 2024 paper for [college] and its answer key. Turn it into a mock on the site: every question word for word, all four options, the key from the official answer key. Group the questions by subject. Count them against the paper before you publish. If any question is unreadable, list it instead of guessing.

The keys can be wrong. Check them.

Answer keys are often worse than you think. Here, one printed key turned out to be almost random: only about a quarter of its answers matched the questions. Another printed key marked "ignoring a child's tantrums" as operant escape when the answer is extinction. So:

  • Ask Claude where the key came from: official, printed in a book, or worked out by Claude. Say which one on the mock.
  • Ask it to spot-check the key against the books before publishing, and to flag every answer it disagrees with.
  • When the key is wrong, the mock should score the right answer and show a short Key note saying what the paper printed and why it is wrong. Then you learn the right answer but still know the trap.

Then make it findable

  • Each mock belongs to a college group (JSS, IHBAS, RML, St John's and so on), so the list does not become an endless wall.
  • A question that comes up in more than one paper gets a badge naming every paper it appeared in.
  • A short page per college lists which papers exist, which are live, and whose key each one uses.

6 · Explanations that actually teach

A mock that only says "answer: b" is a quiz. A mock that teaches is a study tool. The bar used here, for every single question:

  • The concept from scratch, as if you had never heard of it. Not a one-line reminder.
  • Who it comes from, with the full name and the year (for example Burrhus Frederic Skinner, not just "Skinner"). Interview panels ask for names.
  • Why each wrong option is wrong, one by one. Most marks are lost to the option that sounds right.
  • The trap the paper setter used, if there is one.
  • A link to the chapter in the textbook where it is taught properly.
Say thisFor every question in this mock, write an explanation that teaches the concept from scratch, names the theorist in full with the year, then says why each wrong option is wrong. Use the standard books, not memory. Then have a second, separate check go through every explanation for wrong facts before it goes live.
Always ask for the second check. One pass writes, a different pass checks. Mistakes caught here are mistakes you never learn.

7 · The test screen and the Tests app

Once a few mocks exist, the screen you take them on matters as much as the content. Things worth asking for, all of which exist here:

  • A paper picker by college: Continue (half-done papers) at the top, then the next exam, then one row per college that opens to its papers. Search by college or year.
  • Flag, bookmark, and a "redo the wrong ones" pass after you finish.
  • Reset this mock, which really wipes your answers, on every device.
  • A results file you can send back, so the next mock can aim at your weak spots.
  • The Safehouse: every question you got wrong collects there on its own until you master it, with flip cards, "cross questioning" (defend why each wrong option is wrong in your own words), "spot the trap", and one boss battle per topic.
  • Tag the options you do not know, then get a briefing sheet and a drill built from those tags, marked red, amber or green.
  • A look you actually want to open. Ask for a redesign as often as it feels flat. Say what you want it to feel like ("sunset", "heist", "game level") and let Claude show you options first.
Say thisMake an Android app of the Tests section for my Tab S6, held in portrait. Bigger text, options stacked full width. Keep the phone version as it is. Test it on an Android emulator at the S6 screen size before you send me the link.

8 · Name Lock: names and answers out loud

Multiple choice teaches you to recognise an answer. Interviews and viva need you to say it. Name Lock is a separate app for that. Each "world" is one set:

  • Who Said What: theorist to theory, both ways.
  • Biopsych: brain areas, neurotransmitters, with a brain map.
  • Dissertation: the questions a panel asks about your own study, with answers in plain words you can say out loud. Built from your final draft so the numbers match what you wrote.
  • JSS Interview and St John's Interview: the panel questions for that college, including questions on your own work history.
  • Stats and Research and Concept Viva: the core ideas, explained out loud.
Say thisAdd a world to Name Lock for my [college] interview on [date]. Use my CV and work history: I have worked as [your roles]. Give me the questions their panel is likely to ask, each with a short model answer I can say out loud in under a minute. Include a practice mode.

9 · The books and the print PDFs

This is the reading half. It is also the biggest job, so it needs the most structure.

How a book gets written properly

  1. Plan from the sources. Claude reads the syllabus and every past paper for that subject and makes a chapter plan. Every topic that has ever been asked gets a place.
  2. One writer per chapter. Big books are split so many chapters are written at the same time. Each chapter teaches from scratch, names every theorist, and ends with its traps.
  3. A second reader checks each chapter against the books: names, years, criteria.
  4. A blind test. Real past-paper questions are answered using only the book. Here that came out at 1,140 right out of 1,155. Every miss is either a fixed gap or a paper whose key is wrong.
  5. Then the PDFs. Colour and black and white, same page numbers, margins that swap sides for double-sided printing, every chapter starting on a right-hand page, nothing that only works in colour.
Say thisWrite a final exam textbook for all nine JSS subjects, from the official syllabus and every past paper on the site. Explain every topic from scratch, name the theorist with every theory, and end each topic with its traps. Put it in the Library and also make colour and black-and-white A4 PDFs for double-sided printing. Do General and Clinical first so I can start reading.
Ask for a short edition first. A full book of every subject is thousands of pages and takes days of work. Here the short edition (all nine subjects) was ready before the exam, and the full-length editions follow after. Read one chapter of a new book and say if the depth is right before it writes the other 200.

10 · Admissions tracker and news board

A tracker is only useful if you can trust every cell. The rule that made this one trustworthy: official sources only.

  • Start from the regulator's own list (here, the RCI list of approved courses and seats), not a coaching blog.
  • Fill each college's dates, fees and hostel from its own prospectus or notice, with a link to that document in the cell.
  • Where the college prints nothing, the cell says unknown and why. Never a guess, never "approx".
  • Add the columns you actually decide with: quality (with the reason), road distance from home, language fit, whether it pays a stipend.
  • Make it refresh itself twice a day, and show a "last refreshed" time so you know it is not stale.
Say thisBuild an admissions tracker of every college with an RCI-approved course for [course]. For each: application dates, exam date and pattern, seats, fees, hostel, stipend, with a link to the official document for every number. Mark anything not printed officially as unknown. Make it check the official pages twice a day and tell me when something new appears.
Aggregator sites get things wrong. Here they had a fee off by nearly four times (they quoted a different course), exam dates the college itself never published, and a test listed as leading to a hospital that it never fed into. Every one was only caught by going to the college's own page.

11 · Phone and tab apps, and updates

  • One app per job. Here: Tests (phone, tab, Tab S6 portrait), Name Lock (phone, tab), and MET Prep, which carries the whole site offline and is rebuilt every day.
  • Installing: download the app file from the Downloads page on the phone, allow "install unknown apps" for the browser once, and open it.
  • Updates come by themselves. New mocks and fixes reach an installed app the next time it opens. Close it fully and open it twice. You only reinstall when the app itself changes.
  • Make Claude test every app before it sends it. It can run an Android emulator on the laptop at your exact screen size, open the app, take a screenshot, look at it, and check for crashes. Ask for this every time. "Built" is not the same as "works".
Say which device and which way you hold it. "Tab S6, portrait" got a build made for exactly that. "Make it work on my tab" gets a guess.

12 · Sync between devices

Because your progress lives on each device, a phone and a tab do not know about each other until you link them. Here it is one QR scan: the phone shows a QR code under Sync, the tab taps Scan QR, and from then on scores, wrong answers and progress follow you.

Say thisI use the site on my phone and the Tests app on my tab. Make my scores and progress sync between them with one QR scan, no account or login. A reset on one device must reset on the other too.

That last line matters. Here, "Reset this mock" first came back from the dead because the other device synced the old answers back in. It was fixed so a reset wins.

13 · Keeping the work going on its own

The biggest problem in this whole build was not quality. It was that work stopped whenever nobody pushed it. What fixed it:

  • One task list. Every ask goes on one list, in order. Not spread across chats. When you ask for something, it goes on the list; when it ships, it gets ticked with the link.
  • A scheduled work run. Claude Code can be set to run on a timer. Each run takes the top item it can do without asking you, builds it, tests it, publishes it, and sends you a short message: what went live, the link, and what is next. Ask Claude to set this up for you.
  • A progress page that shows the same list: live, still owed, waiting on you.
  • "Waiting on you" is real. Some things cannot move until you answer one line ("Calcutta, yes or no?"). Answer those together in one message and they all move.
Say thisKeep one task list of everything I ask for, in order. Set up a work run every hour that takes the top item you can do without me, ships it, and messages me what went live with the link and the next three items. Never tell me something is live unless you opened the live link and checked it.
Usage limits stop the work run. Here it switched off for weeks after hitting the weekly limit, and a bug kept it off. If you stop getting status messages, ask "is the work run still on?" instead of waiting.

14 · How to ask so you get what you want

  • Say the exam and the date. "This is for JSS on the 27th" changes the order of everything.
  • Say the depth. "From scratch, not revision notes" got a very different book from "notes".
  • Say the device. "Tab S6, portrait." "S11 Ultra, landscape."
  • Say "rush" when it is urgent, and say what can wait.
  • When something is not good enough, say exactly what is wrong. "Looks flat and boring" got a redesign. "Do better" with nothing else got a guess. "The reset button does not clear the answers" got a fix the same day.
  • Ask for the link only once it is live. Not "building", not "nearly". Live, opened, checked.
  • Cut what you do not need. Here, once the exam targets changed in June, a lot of older work was dropped. Saying "drop X" is as useful as asking for something new.

15 · What went wrong here, so you skip it

What happenedHow to avoid it
Photos and a mock PDF sent on WhatsApp were never downloaded, and WhatsApp deleted them.Send files by Google Drive link. Ask "did you get all of them?" with the count.
A printed answer key was mostly noise.Spot-check every key before building a mock on it. Mark where each key came from.
Aggregator sites had wrong fees and dates.Official documents only, with the link in the cell. Unknown is better than a guess.
Things were called "done" that were not live.Done means: published, live link opened and checked, and on the phone it shows after a refresh.
Rebuilding the Library wiped pages that were not on its book list.Ask Claude to add every new page to the official list of books, and to check the old pages still open after a rebuild.
A notes-only app opened to blank pages.Every app gets opened on the emulator and screenshotted before the link is sent.
The weekly usage limit stopped work in the middle of a big job.Start big jobs (whole books) early. Save progress as each chapter finishes, so a cut-off loses one chapter, not the book.
A reset came back after sync.Test any change that touches saved progress on two devices, not one.
The list of open asks drifted between the chat, the board and the site.One list. Everything else (progress page, status messages) reads from it.
A new page looked right on the laptop and broke on the tab.Ask for a check at your real screen size, both ways round, in day and night mode.

16 · The whole build, as a checklist

  • Claude subscription, Claude Code installed, free GitHub account.
  • Empty site live, with a passphrase screen, opened on your phone.
  • Top menu and one shared look, day and night.
  • All syllabi, papers, keys and books in one folder (or Drive), counted.
  • First mock: every question checked against the paper, key checked, full explanations, second check done.
  • All other papers, grouped by college, with key notes where keys are wrong.
  • Test screen extras: picker, flag, reset, redo-wrong pass, results file.
  • Tests app for your phone and tab, tested on the emulator.
  • Safehouse and the learning drills for your wrong answers.
  • Name Lock worlds for theorists, interview, dissertation.
  • Short edition of the exam textbook, then print PDFs, then full editions.
  • Admissions tracker from official documents, refreshing twice a day, plus news board.
  • Sync between phone and tab with one QR scan.
  • Offline app and laptop zip.
  • One task list, a progress page, and a scheduled work run that messages you.
