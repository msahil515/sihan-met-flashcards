2 · The paper and St John's Mock 1

Start St John's Mock 1 (100 Qs)

The notice gives the syllabus as "psychology subjects at Bachelor's and Master's level". In practice that is the core of an MA in Psychology: general and cognitive psychology, biopsychology, developmental, social, personality, assessment and psychometrics, psychopathology, clinical psychology and therapy, and research methods and statistics. Mock 1 is built on those nine.

Mock 1What it is
Questions100 real past-paper questions, the real paper's length. No St John's papers exist anywhere yet (the course is new), so these come from the real papers already on the site: NIMHANS 2024 and 2025, RML 2020 to 2025, IHBAS 2020, Amity 2023, JEMAS(PG) 2025.
FreshNothing from JSS Mock 1, JSS Mock 2 or the SGT Master Mock. IHBAS 2024 and 2025 left out, you have sat both.
CheckedEvery candidate question was rated for subject, difficulty and yield, and any with a doubtful or wrong key was dropped.
MixMixed difficulty like a real paper, mostly concept questions, very little pure who-proposed-it. The result splits by the nine subjects.
Each cardSource paper, the concept from scratch, why each wrong option is wrong, and the Study this button.
Pace for 100 in 90 minutes: 54 seconds a question. Be at Q25 by 22 minutes, Q50 by 45, Q75 by 67, finish the first pass by 80, and use the last 10 for the ones you marked. Anything you can't answer in a minute: mark it, pick your best guess, move on.

Negative marking: the notice does not say. Check the hall ticket and the instructions on the paper. If there is none, answer all 100. If there is, skip only true blind guesses; if you can strike out two options, answer it.

4 · St John's interview cards

Drill mode hides every answer. Say it out loud first, then tap the card to check yourself.

Only what is different at St John's is here. The rest of the panel (your work, ethics, the law, concepts) is already in the Interview world in Name Lock, section 5.

Tell us about yourself.

"Good morning sir, good morning ma'am. I'm Mohammed Sihan. I did my M.Sc in Clinical Psychology at Jain Deemed-to-be University here in Bangalore, from 2022 to 2024. I've worked in four settings: de-addiction and psychiatric rehabilitation centres; as an ABA therapist with children, mostly with autism and ADHD; in special education; and as a counsellor on YourDOST, an online platform, where I've seen over 250 clients across about 473 sessions. My dissertation was a quantitative study of 214 young adults on pornography use, personality, aggression and sexual compulsivity. What I don't have yet is supervised assessment and diagnosis in a hospital on an RCI programme. That's why I'm here."

"You said ABA with autistic children. What exactly did you do?" That is the opening for S4. Have the Pragyan level in Name Lock ready.

Forty-five seconds: training, work, research, then why here. It is morning on the 25th, so "good morning", and it ends on the gap the course fills, which hands them the next question.

Why St John's?

"Three reasons, sir. First, the training sits in a psychiatry department of a teaching hospital that covers the whole range, adult, child and adolescent, geriatric, perinatal, addiction and liaison work, so in two years I'd see presentations I've only read about. Second, the department runs services that connect to what I've already done and want to learn properly: the autism programmes, CARE ADD and COM-DEALL, connect to my ABA work with autistic children, and the BIG service and ROSHNI connect to my rehab work. Third, it's five seats, RCI-recognised, so the supervision is close and it ends in registration."

"Which of those would you want to work in first?" Go to S4.

Every fact is from the department's own page. Name two services that link to your work, not all seven: reciting the full list sounds rehearsed, linking two to your own hours sounds like a reason.

What do you know about our department?

"I know Psychiatry here goes back to 1966, and that beyond the OPD and wards there are special services: BIG for alcohol dependence, AMAS for people after a suicide attempt, ROSHNI for day-care rehabilitation, MAANASI for community psychiatry at Mugalur, the Child Guidance Clinic, and the autism programmes, CARE ADD and COM-DEALL. What I'd like to understand better is how trainees rotate through them over the two years."

Facts, then a question about training. Do not name faculty to their faces or guess at bed numbers. If they ask whose work you know, only name something you have actually read.

Which of our services would you most want to work in?

"The child and autism services, the Child Guidance Clinic and CARE ADD or COM-DEALL. I've done ABA with children with autism and ADHD, so I know the behavioural side: finding the function of a behaviour, teaching skills step by step, prompting and fading, getting the parents to carry it on at home. What I haven't done is the assessment side: the developmental assessment, telling autism apart from intellectual disability or a language disorder, and the feedback to parents. That's the part I'd come here to learn."

"And adults?" "The BIG service. I've worked in de-addiction after detox, motivational interviewing and relapse prevention, but never with the medical side alongside, withdrawal and complications. A hospital service is where I'd see that."

A reason tied to real hours, and an honest gap the course fills. Only claim the parts of the ABA and rehab work you actually did.

This is a Catholic institution. Does that matter to you?

"It matters in a good way, sir. As I understand it, St John's was built around service, not only treatment, and a service ethos means seeing people who can't afford private care, the people clinical psychology in India reaches least. In the room, my job is the same with every patient, whatever their faith or mine: respect their beliefs, work within their values, and keep my own out of the session. And when someone's faith is part of how they cope, it goes into the formulation as a strength."

They are checking two things: that you value working with people who can't pay, and that you keep your own beliefs out of clinical work. Say that much and stop. Don't discuss religion beyond it.

The stipend is ₹7,000 a month. Can you manage for two years?

"Yes, sir. I've planned for the two years."

One calm line, and only if it is true. The course fee is not in the notice: get it from the bulletin or the admissions office before the 25th, never from the panel. Don't ask about fees, hostel or results in the room.

You already have an M.Sc in Clinical Psychology. Why this course?

"The M.Sc gave me the theory and the research training, and I've used both. What it didn't give me is supervised clinical practice in a hospital and RCI registration. Without registration I can't practise as a clinical psychologist in India, however much I know. So I'm not repeating a degree, I'm adding the supervised training and the licence my present qualification doesn't carry."

Own it in the first sentence. Any hesitation here reads as "he couldn't get in anywhere else".

Do you speak Kannada? Many of our patients do.

If yes: "Yes, sir, I can take a history in Kannada." If partly or no: "Not fluently yet, sir." Name the languages you do speak, then: "I'd start learning clinical Kannada from the first week, history-taking phrases and the MSE questions first, and use an interpreter or a co-therapist until then. A history taken across a language gap loses things, so I'd treat it as a priority, not an extra."

A practical question at a Bengaluru hospital, not a trick. They want a plan, not an apology. MAANASI at Mugalur is rural, so this matters more for the community postings.

How did the paper go?

"It was a fair paper, sir. Some of it I was sure of, some I had to think through." If they ask which part: "There was a question on [topic] I wasn't sure about. I'll be going back to it."

This one is likely, because the paper was an hour ago. They are watching how you carry a bad moment, not grading the paper. Never say it went badly, never blame the paper, never argue an answer key. The same holds for JSS if they ask: one line, then stop.

There are five seats and a hall full of candidates. Why should we pick you?

"I can't compare myself with people I haven't met, sir. What I can say is that I come in with three things already done: a master's in this subject, a quantitative dissertation, and real client hours in four settings, rehab, ABA with autistic children, special education, and over 250 clients online. So what I need from you is the supervised hospital training, not the basics. And the services here, the autism work especially, are where my hours already point."

No comparison with others, three facts, and one line that ties you to this department. The last line is what makes it a St John's answer and not the JSS one.

What are the professional responsibilities of a clinical psychologist?

"Five, sir. Competence: work only within what I'm trained and supervised to do, and refer beyond it. Confidentiality and its limits, told to the client at the start: risk to self or others, child sexual abuse, which POCSO makes mandatory to report, and court orders. Informed consent, for assessment and for therapy. Accountability: practising as a clinical psychologist needs RCI registration, and the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 counts clinical psychologists as mental health professionals. And the patient's rights under that Act: dignity, confidentiality, and the right to make their own decisions, including advance directives."

"What does the Act say about someone who attempts suicide?" "Section 115: a person who attempts suicide is presumed to have severe stress unless shown otherwise, is not to be punished for it, and the government has a duty to provide care and rehabilitation."

The notice names "professional responsibilities" as something the panel assesses, so expect it in some form. Five headings, one line each, then stop.

Vignette (child and autism services): a 4-year-old boy is brought because he isn't talking and doesn't respond to his name. The parents ask, "Is he autistic?"

"I wouldn't answer that on the first visit. First, a hearing test, because a child who doesn't respond to his name may simply not hear. Then a developmental history: milestones, whether he had words and lost them, eye contact, pointing and showing, pretend play, repetitive movements, routines, sensory reactions. Then I'd watch him play and with his parents. For assessment, a developmental scale and an autism rating, the Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism or CARS-2, and adaptive behaviour with the VSMS. The rule-outs are hearing loss, global developmental delay or intellectual disability, a language disorder, and neglect. Then plain feedback to the parents, early intervention without waiting for a perfect label, and ISAA certification under the RPwD Act if he meets criteria."

"Why not the M-CHAT?" "The M-CHAT-R is a screener for 16 to 30 months. At four he's past its range."

Hearing first is the mark most candidates miss. ISAA: 40 items in 6 domains, each rated 1 to 5. A total under 70 is no autism, 70 to 106 mild, 107 to 153 moderate, above 153 severe.

Vignette (BIG service): a 42-year-old man is brought by his wife. He has drunk daily for ten years and missed work, and his last drink was yesterday morning. He is shaking and sweating.

"Medical first. He's in alcohol withdrawal, and withdrawal can go on to seizures or delirium tremens, so the psychiatrist manages detox now, with thiamine to protect against Wernicke's. My part starts alongside: a history of quantity, pattern, past attempts to stop and complications; screening for depression and suicide risk, which run high in alcohol dependence; and how the family is coping. Withdrawal severity is tracked on the CIWA-Ar. Once he's through detox and thinking clearly, motivational interviewing to find where he is in the stages of change, then relapse prevention: his high-risk situations, a coping plan for each, refusal skills, and the family brought in."

"Stages of change?" "Prochaska and DiClemente: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance, with relapse as part of the cycle, not the end of it."

The trap is starting counselling on a man in withdrawal. Saying "medical first, and who manages it" is what shows you can work in a hospital team.

Vignette (AMAS): a 19-year-old college student is in casualty after taking an overdose of her mother's tablets. She is medically stable now. You are asked to see her.

"Somewhere private, sitting down, no rush. Then the attempt itself: what she took and how much she thought it would do, whether it was planned, whether she tried not to be found, any note, and how she feels about having survived. Then now: thoughts of dying today, a plan, intent, access to means, hopelessness. What led up to it, any depression or substance use, and what holds her: people, reasons, future plans. Then a written safety plan with her, the tablets at home locked away, and her family involved, with her consent where possible, because risk is a limit to confidentiality. Follow-up within days, and admission if the risk stays high."

"Is attempted suicide a crime?" "Under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, Section 115, a person who attempts suicide is presumed to be under severe stress and is not to be punished for it. The government has a duty to provide care and rehabilitation."

Regret at surviving and taking precautions not to be found are the two strongest signs of high intent. Asking directly about suicide does not plant the idea; panels like hearing you say so.

Vignette (perinatal): three weeks after delivery, a 26-year-old mother cries most days, can't sleep even when the baby sleeps, and says she is a bad mother.

"This is past the baby blues. The blues start in the first few days, peak around day 3 to 5 and settle within about two weeks. Three weeks of low mood, insomnia even when the baby is asleep, and guilt about being a bad mother point to postpartum depression, which DSM-5-TR codes as depression with peripartum onset. I'd screen with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, ten items, where the last item asks about self-harm. I'd ask her directly about thoughts of harming herself and about thoughts of harming the baby. I'd check for psychosis, confusion, hallucinations or strange beliefs about the baby, because that's an emergency. Thyroid and anaemia go to the doctors. Treatment is CBT or interpersonal therapy, practical support with the baby and sleep, the family brought in, and the psychiatrist if medication is needed, keeping breastfeeding in mind."

"What is postpartum psychosis?" "Rare, about 1 to 2 per 1,000 births, usually starting in the first two weeks, often on the bipolar spectrum: confusion, rapid mood swings, delusions. It's a psychiatric emergency because of the risk of suicide and infanticide, so she's admitted."

Blues, depression and psychosis on a timeline is the whole answer. Asking about harm to the baby, gently and directly, is the mark.

Vignette (geriatric clinic): a 68-year-old retired teacher is brought by her son. She has been forgetful for six months, has lost interest in things, and sleeps badly. He asks, "Is it dementia?"

"It could be depression, dementia, or both, and the history tells them apart. Depression in older people can look like dementia, which is called pseudodementia: the onset is quicker, she complains about her memory herself, answers 'I don't know' rather than trying, and mood and sleep changes came first. Dementia is slower and steady, the family notices more than she does, she covers up or gives near-miss answers, and it keeps getting worse. Delirium, sudden and fluctuating with poor attention, is a medical problem and has to be ruled out first. I'd take the history from both of them, do a cognitive screen like the MMSE or the Hindi Mental State Examination, a depression scale like the Geriatric Depression Scale, and ask about daily functioning. Thyroid and B12 go to the doctors. If depression is there, treat it, then test her memory again."

"Delirium versus dementia?" "Delirium: sudden onset, fluctuates through the day, attention is impaired, usually reversible once the cause is treated. Dementia: gradual, progressive, attention fairly well kept early on."

Saying "treat the depression, then retest" shows you know a cognitive score in a depressed patient isn't the final word.

Do you have any questions for us?

"Two, sir. How do trainees rotate across the department's services over the two years, for example the child and autism work, the addiction service and the community programme at Mugalur? And how is supervision set up, and how early do trainees start doing assessments themselves?"

Two questions, both about training, both showing you read about the department. Never fees, hostel or results.
