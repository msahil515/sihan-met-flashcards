Studying during a flare-up

Start with one familiar concept at a time, in whichever format feels easiest today.

These are study-format suggestions to try, not a treatment or a proven best format for fibromyalgia. There is no clinical evidence here that cards, audio, or any other format is best. Fibromyalgia can involve fatigue and problems with concentration or memory; sensitivity to noise or light can also happen. Audio is always optional. NIAMS: Fibromyalgia

A small card can be enough

Try one short card on a topic you already recognize. Keep the main part simple:

  • Definition: one clear sentence.
  • Example: one concrete example.
  • Optional: a quick comparison with a confusable idea, or one recall question with its answer visible.

Begin with familiar topics. If it feels comfortable, you could briefly read the card aloud or listen to audio while keeping the same text available as a transcript. Skip audio if sound is uncomfortable. Audio files may not be available for every card.

Eight high-yield cards

Selected core distinctions for quick revision; not a verified ranking of exam frequency. Definitions and examples are shown up front. Distinctions and recall prompts are optional; every answer stays visible.

1 · Learning

Positive and negative reinforcement

Definition: Reinforcement increases future behavior. Positive reinforcement adds a consequence; negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant condition.

Example: A car’s seatbelt alarm stops when you buckle up. If this increases future buckling, it is negative reinforcement. The sound being removed alone does not establish reinforcement.

Optional distinction: Punishment decreases behavior. “Negative” means removal, not decrease.

2 · Psychometrics

Reliability and validity

Definition: Reliability is consistency of measurement. Validity is whether a test measures what it claims to measure.

Example: A bathroom scale that gives the same wrong reading each time is consistent, but it is not accurate for your weight.

Optional distinction: A measure can be reliable without being valid; validity depends on reliable measurement.

3 · Research methods

Type I and Type II errors

Definition: Type I error rejects a true null hypothesis (false positive). Type II error fails to reject a false null (false negative).

Example: Concluding two groups differ when they do not is Type I; missing a real difference is Type II.

Optional recall: Which error is beta? Answer: Type II; power is 1 − beta.

4 · Personality and disorders

OCD and OCPD

Definition: OCD involves obsessions and/or compulsions. OCPD is a longstanding pattern of perfectionism, orderliness and control.

Example: Repeated unwanted checking that causes distress suggests an OCD-type symptom; insisting that a preferred way of organizing is the only right way may fit an OCPD trait pattern.

Optional distinction: OCD symptoms are often unwanted and resisted; OCPD traits are often experienced as fitting the person’s standards. They can co-occur.

5 · OCD

Obsessions and compulsions

Definition: Obsessions are recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges. Compulsions are repetitive behaviors or mental acts a person feels driven to perform, often to reduce anxiety or prevent something feared.

Example: Fear of leaving the gas on can be an obsession; repeatedly checking the knobs can be a compulsion.

Optional distinction: A compulsion can be mental, such as repeating a phrase, not only a visible action.

6 · Older adult assessment

Delirium and dementia

Definition: Delirium is typically sudden and fluctuating, with impaired attention. Dementia is generally gradual and progressive, with attention often relatively preserved early on.

Example: A sudden, variable change in attention over a day contrasts with a gradual decline in memory and daily abilities over time.

Optional recall: Which pattern should be considered first when change is sudden? Answer: Delirium; the source describes it as a medical problem to rule out first.

7 · Formulation

Diathesis–stress model

Definition: A narrower causal model in which vulnerability (diathesis) and a trigger or stressor combine in relation to a disorder.

Example: A hypothetical person has a vulnerability and then faces a major stressor; the model asks how those factors relate, without assuming either alone explains the outcome.

Optional distinction: The biopsychosocial model is a broader framework for considering biological, psychological and social factors.

8 · Psychosis

Negative symptoms

Definition: Negative symptoms are reductions or losses in usual functions, such as blunted affect, reduced speech (alogia), reduced drive (avolition), reduced pleasure (anhedonia) and reduced social interest (asociality).

Example: A person may show little facial expression, speak very little and struggle to initiate ordinary activities.

Optional distinction: Hallucinations and delusions are positive symptoms, experiences added to the person’s usual functioning. Depression and medication effects can resemble negative symptoms.

Choose your stopping point

After one card, stop and rest, or continue if it feels comfortable. There is no fixed study quota. Skip recall if it takes too much effort. There is no need for timed mock exams or streaks. Longer or difficult new learning can wait for a time when you have more capacity; that is a pacing option, not a promise that symptoms will improve or go away.

The existing Concept Viva cards are one optional source of familiar concepts. Card length may vary. This page is a standalone guide; no flare-up study mode has been built.
